## Answer explanation

Ans: A

![[TO17142046-1.jpg]]

The Charcot Restraint Orthotic Walker, or CROW, is a rigid boot designed to accommodate and support a foot with Charcot neuroarthropathy.

Neuropathic joint was first described by Charcot in tabes dorsalis in 1868. The condition is a progressive destructive (but painless) arthritis that arises as a result of loss of pain and proprioceptive joint sensations. To be precise the pathogenesis is poorly understood, with two speculated theories. Neurovascular theory points that joint destruction is secondary to an autonomic stimulated vascular reflex causing hyperemia and periarticular osteopenia with contributory trauma. The neurotraumatic theory suggests that Charcot Neuropathy is an overuse injury in which insensate joints are subjected to repetitive microtrauma.

Clinically, the condition is characterized by the classic diagnostic Charcot’s triad includes gross joint swelling, exaggerated movements and yet a “painless” presentation. Patient usually comes with single joint involved. Crepitus is particularly peculiar finding owing to presence of multiple loose bodies in joint. X-rays often show marked destructive changes of arthropathy.

Classifications systems: They are predominantly based on radiographic findings and anatomical locations involved.

The most popular staging system is one proposed by Eichenholtz.

Stage I is the fragmentation phase (acute stage) where plain radiographs demonstrate osteopenia, periarticular fragmentation, and subluxation or frank dislocation of joints. Clinically, the foot continues to be warm and edematous and may demonstrate increased ligamentous laxity.

Stage II, coalescence stage, subacute Charcot (reparative stage) represents the early healing phase. Edema and warmth decrease and there is absorption of debris, fusion of bony fragments, new bone formation, and/or sclerosis of bone ends are other typical changes

Stage III, consolidation stage, chronic Charcot (reconstructive or bone healing stage) is characterized by an absence of inflammation and progression to a more stable, although often deformed foot or ankle

Brodsky described an anatomical classification based on the four areas most commonly affected.

Brodsky Type I (midfoot) includes the naviculocuneiform and metatarsocuneiform joints, which is the most common.

The second most common type, type II (hindfoot) includes the subtalar, talonavicular, or calcaneocuboid joints. Type II approximately accounts for 30–35% of anatomical incidence.

Type III is divided into ‘A’ and ‘B’. Type IIIA involves the tibiotalar joint and associated bones, and type IIIB involves a pathologic fracture of the tuberosity of the calcaneus.

TREATMENT

Treatment for Charcot neuropathy depends on the stage and clinical presentation. With joint replacement being relatively contraindicated due to absent protective mechanisms, the aim of treatment is to decrease the foot deformity (to prevent formation of chronic foot ulcers) and achieve a stable plantigrade foot with primarily with offloading, immobilization and bracing.

Eichenholtz stage 0: frequent follow-up with serial radiographs to monitor the development of disease.

Eichenholtz stage I and II (coalescence-subacute phase): The first-line treatment is nearly always total contact casting for 2-4 months (acute stage) for offloading followed by use of moulded total contact ankle foot orthosis. A particularly popular brace is the CROW boot.

In Charcot arthropathy, CROW is used to prevent the development of ulcers by evenly distributing pressure to the entire leg and foot. The CROW effectively controls limb edema, returns the patient to ambulatory activities, and prevents significant progression of deformity. When there might be a severe sole deformity in the midfoot, it may be necessary to limit or eliminate the movement of the midfoot and hindfoot to restrict the entire foot and ankle joints. In this case, the CROW eliminates the movement of the foot and ankle joints, reduces the load and shear force applied to the foot, and protects the deformed foot from further damage. The CROW orthosis is similar to a bivalved TCC and consists of a rigid polypropylene anterior and posterior shell with a dorsiflexion stop, heel lift, and rocker sole to counteract the nutcracker effect. Since the CROW orthosis is fastened with a Velcro strap, it is easy to detach, making it convenient for wound management, including wound cleaning. However, as the CROW orthosis is bulky, it potentially limits patient compliance and sometimes leads to knee and lumbar pain due to immobility of the lower extremities.

In Stage III (reconstruction-chronic), if the foot is plantigrade, the patient can use custom inlay shoes (CROW). But if the patient has a non-plantigrade foot or there is recurrent history of ulcerations, joint debridement +/- an arthrodesis with internal fixation or external fixation may be necessary. While using internal fixation one may have to stage, external fixation on the other hand can mostly be done in single stage.

With the presence of recurrent osteomyelitis and troublesome ulcerations, an amputation may be opted. According to a study by Saltzman et al., amputation rates of the lower extremity have ranged from 3 to 9% depending primarily on avoidance of ulceration.

Role of pharmacotherapy is largely theoretical with Bisphosphonates (Pamidronate in particular) being the foremost tried drugs. Osteoclastic activity is increased in Charcot and hence there is some rationale of giving Bisphosphonates, as per surgeon’s desire.
