## Question

A 65-year-old male with a history of hypertension and controlled type II diabetes undergoes a right total knee replacement without any complications. He is able to return to his prior warehouse job 3 months later and returned to playing pickleball on the weekends. He is doing well for two years until he slips in the garage and sustains a hyperflexion moment to his right knee. He has a palpable defect below his patella with the inability to perform a straight leg raise. His presenting radiograph is shown in Figure A. Which of the following is true regarding treatment for this patient?

## Options

1. Drop lock braces serve as the most appropriate definitive treatment for high functioning patients
2. Primary repair of the patellar tendon has shown to have the lowest failure rates
3. Freeze dried allografts have better outcomes than fresh frozen allografts for reconstruction
4. Any allograft used during reconstruction should be secured with minimal tension placed through graft
5. Synthetic mesh has shown equivalent outcomes in reconstruction when compared to allografts

## Correct option

**5. Synthetic mesh has shown equivalent outcomes in reconstruction when compared to allografts**

## Answer explanation

Extensor mechanism disruption after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a rare, devastating complication that is best managed with operative intervention to achieve the highest functional outcomes. In cases of tendinous disruption, reconstruction has been shown to be superior to primary repair with allografts and synthetic mesh alternatives demonstrating equivalent outcomes (Answer 5).

The extensor mechanism consists of the quadriceps tendon, patella bone, and patellar tendon. Disruption to any portion of the extensor mechanism after TKA has been attributed to trauma, unrecognized iatrogenic injury during initial TKA, mal-positioned implants (i.e. elevated joint line, internally rotated femur/tibia components, lateralization of the patellar component), cutting the patella too thin (<12mm), and/or local vascular disruption in the multiple-revision patient. Patellar fractures should undergo open reduction internal fixation, and grossly malpositioned components should be revised. In cases of tendinous disruptions, primary repair has consistently been shown to result in high rates of failure due to re-rupture or persistent extension lag. Therefore, reconstruction serves as the current standard of care. Achilles tendon or whole extensor mechanism allografts are most often utilized and have demonstrated acceptable results. Unfortunately, even with reconstruction, there remains a considerable risk for failure or persistent extensor lag (~25% of cases). *In 2011, Browne and Hanssen described a novel technique utilizing synthetic mesh as an alternative to allograft purporting its lower cost, averted risk for disease transmission, and equivalent outcomes.* *Larger retrospective case series and systematic reviews have confirmed their results using synthetic mesh.* *However, there remains a void in prospective, randomized trials demonstrating superiority in reconstruction technique or graft/mesh utilization.* At the time being, both allograft and synthetic mesh serve as acceptable surgical options for tendinous extensor mechanism disruptions after TKA.

Bates and Springer provided a comprehensive review article on the management of extensor mechanism disruption after TKA. They recommend nonoperative management and drop lock bracing for minimally ambulatory patients or those who have too many comorbidities to undergo surgery. They note the high rate of failure in primary repair and discuss the various grafts and techniques utilized for reconstruction. They conclude that no reconstruction method has reliably demonstrated superiority or optimal outcomes and more research is necessary in this patient population.

*Browne and Hanssen described their novel technique and outcomes utilizing knitted monofilament polypropylene graft (synthetic mesh) to reconstruct the disrupted patellar tendon in patients with a prior TKA.* *Out of 13 patients, 1 patient had a recurrent infection and was fused, 3 patients’ reconstruction failed, and 9 (70%) had successful reconstruction with minimal (<10 degrees) extension lag.* **They also reported** knee flexion was maintained in all successful reconstruction patients with significant improvements in Knee Society scores. **The authors concluded** that utilizing synthetic mesh for patellar tendon reconstruction after TKA was straightforward, cost-effective, and eliminates the risk of disease transmission associated with using an allograft.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Drop lock braces are essentially hinged knee braces that allow patients to ambulate with the knee locked in extension and provide a quick release button to unlock the brace when they go to sit down. These braces should be reserved only for patients who are contraindicated to undergo reconstruction (i.e. too sick to undergo surgery).
Answer 2: Primary patellar tendon repair has consistently been shown to demonstrate a high rate of failure when performed after total knee arthroplasty. The medial and lateral inferior genicular arteries contribute to the vascular pedicles perfusing the patellar tendon. It is postulated that the surgical approach from the original/revision TKA and subsequent dissection to perform patellar tendon repair completely disrupts these pedicles perfusing the patellar tendon thus decreasing the ability to heal.
Answer 3: Freeze-dried allografts have been shown to have a higher rate of failure compared to fresh frozen allografts. For this reason, fresh frozen Achilles tendon and fresh frozen whole extensor mechanism serve as the two most utilized allografts for reconstruction.
Answer 4: The knee should be positioned in full extension when performing reconstruction with the graft secured in maximal tension. Minimally tensioned allografts have been shown to result in significant extensor lag, whereas maximally tensioned allografts have demonstrated minimal extension lag.
