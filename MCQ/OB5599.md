## Question

A 6-year-old boy presents with difficulty with abduction and external rotation of the arm. His mother recalls prolonged labor with forceps-assisted delivery. Examination shows a prominent scapula on shoulder adduction (arrowhead, Figure A). An MR image of his shoulder is shown in Figure B. What is the next best treatment step?

## Options

1. Osteotomy of the humerus
2. Osteotomy of the glenoid
3. Latissimus dorsi transfer
4. Teres major transfer
5. Subscapularis release

## Correct option

**1. Osteotomy of the humerus**

## Answer explanation

This child has brachial plexus birth palsy (BPBP) complicated by residual motor deficits and internal rotation contracture of the GH joint evidenced by the clinical picture of the Putti Sign and posterior subluxation of glenohumeral joint radiographically.

With BPBP internal rotation contracture, there is glenohumeral (GH) dysplasia. Derotational osteotomies will place the arm in a more functional arc of shoulder rotation. Surgery depends on GH deformity grade. (See Illustration A for Waters classification of GH deformity).
- (1) Waters type II and III: Latissimus dorsi/teres major tendon transfer to the rotator cuff for rotator cuff weakness and minimal GH joint deformity.
- (2) Waters type III and IV: Open/arthroscopic GH reduction, anterior soft tissue contracture release, posterior tendon transfers for weakness, joint contractures and mild-moderate GH joint deformity
- (3) Waters type IV and V: Humeral derotational osteotomy for persistent internal rotation contractures and external rotation weakness, and/or advanced GH deformity

*Abzug et al. retrospectively reviewed 23 children who underwent humeral external rotational osteotomies via a medial approach for brachial plexus birth palsy.* The medial approach is easily concealed and less likely to develop hypertrophic scar. They achieved a mean correction of 43 deg. **They found** a significant improvement in functional scores, external rotation, hand-to-neck and hand-to-mouth functions, and decreased internal rotation. **They recommend** humeral rotational osteotomies for this purpose but caution that there may be loss of midline function (hand to spine function, e.g. zippering, toileting).

*Waters et al. retrospectively reviewed 43 patients with derotational osteotomy for BPBP.* Average patient age was 7.6 years. Average external rotation achieved was 64 deg. Mallet scores showed improvement following osteotomy, with gains in hand-to-mouth, hand-to-neck, and external rotation. **They conclude** derotational osteotomy is an option for advanced glenohumeral dysplasia precluding soft-tissue release and tendon transfers.

Figure A shows the Putti sign (compensatory scapulothoracic motion to adduct the arm, resulting in elevation of the superomedial border of the scapula; arrowhead). Figure B is an axial MR image showing pseudoglenoid formation and progressive posterior subluxation of the left GH joint (type IV). Illustration A (from Waters et al.) shows the Waters classification of the degree of GH joint deformity.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 2: Glenoid osteotomy will not change shoulder external rotation and is not recommended.
Answers 3 to 5: Latissimus dorsi and teres major tendon transfer is adequate for mild GH deformity (types II and III) but will have little effect on late deformity with visible glenoid dysplasia. Anterior subscapularis release (Z-lengthening, musculotendinous lengthening, or elevation off the anterior scapula) is a component of anterior contracture release and performed with posterior tendon transfers for moderate GH deformity (types III and IV) and is less effective when performed in isolation.
