Chapter: General Orthopedics MCQs — 3D Printing in Orthopaedics and Patient Specific Implants

## Question

In the fabrication of porous titanium acetabular cups or metaphyseal cones designed to enhance biological fixation, which architectural and manufacturing combination optimizes osseointegration while minimizing residual thermal stress?

## Options

1. Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) of Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) with a 100% dense infill pattern.
2. Electron Beam Melting (EBM) of Titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V) utilizing a high-temperature vacuum build chamber to produce a fully interconnected porous lattice (pore size 400–600 micro m).
3. Selective Laser Sintering (SLS) of cobalt-chromium powder at room temperature followed by acid etching.
4. Stereolithography (SLA) using a liquid photopolymer resin with post-cure ultraviolet glazing.

## Correct option

**2. Electron Beam Melting (EBM) of Titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V) utilizing a high-temperature vacuum build chamber to produce a fully interconnected porous lattice (pore size 400–600 micro m).**

## Answer explanation

Electron Beam Melting (EBM uses a focused electron beam (rather than a laser) to melt metallic powder in a vacuum chamber. Produces fully dense titanium parts with excellent mechanical properties. The vacuum environment reduces oxidation and contamination. EBM is used by Arcam (GE Additive) to manufacture FDA-cleared orthopaedic implants including acetabular cups with trabecular surface architecture.

EBM operates at elevated build chamber temperatures (typically above 700°C) under a vacuum. This high-temperature environment acts as an in-situ annealing process, significantly reducing residual tensile stresses within the complex geometry and eliminating the immediate need for post-build stress-relief heat treatment.

Why others are incorrect: FDM (A) and SLA (D) are polymer-based techniques unsuitable for load-bearing structural metallic components like acetabular cups. SLS (C) typically involves solid-state sintering rather than full melting, and CoCr processing via powder bed fusion requires intensive support structures and post-processing stress relief due to high thermal gradients when not processed in a heated vacuum like EBM.

3D Printing Technologies Used in Orthopaedics

Several additive manufacturing technologies are employed, each with specific material capabilities, resolution, and clinical applications.

Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM): The most accessible and affordable technology. A thermoplastic filament (PLA — polylactic acid, ABS — acrylonitrile butadiene styrene, PETG, nylon) is heated and extruded through a nozzle, depositing material layer by layer on a build platform. As each layer cools, it solidifies and bonds to the layer below. FDM is widely used for producing anatomical models (for preoperative planning and patient education) and surgical guides/templates. Resolution is moderate (layer thickness 0.1–0.3 mm), surface finish is relatively rough compared to other technologies, and mechanical properties are adequate for non-load-bearing applications.

Selective Laser Sintering (SLS): A high-energy laser selectively fuses powdered material (nylon, polyamide, or metallic powders — titanium, cobalt-chrome, stainless steel) layer by layer. The unfused powder surrounding the sintered object acts as a support structure and is removed after printing. SLS produces parts with superior mechanical properties and finer detail than FDM, and is used for producing functional surgical instruments, custom implants, and load-bearing prosthetic components. SLS with metallic powders is the primary technology for manufacturing patient-specific titanium implants.

Selective Laser Melting (SLM) / Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS): Similar to SLS but uses a higher-energy laser to fully melt (rather than sinter) the metallic powder particles, producing fully dense metallic parts with mechanical properties equivalent to wrought or cast metal. SLM/DMLS with titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V) is the standard technology for manufacturing custom orthopaedic implants — acetabular cups, femoral stems, spinal cages, tumour prostheses, and cranial plates. The process can create complex internal geometries (porous structures, lattice architectures) that are impossible to produce by conventional machining or casting.

Electron Beam Melting (EBM): Uses a focused electron beam (rather than a laser) to melt metallic powder in a vacuum chamber. Produces fully dense titanium parts with excellent mechanical properties. The vacuum environment reduces oxidation and contamination. EBM is used by Arcam (GE Additive) to manufacture FDA-cleared orthopaedic implants including acetabular cups with trabecular surface architecture.

Stereolithography (SLA): A UV laser selectively polymerises a liquid photopolymer resin layer by layer, producing objects with the highest resolution and smoothest surface finish of any 3D printing technology (layer thickness 0.025–0.1 mm). SLA models are used for highly detailed anatomical models, dental applications, and moulds for casting. Biocompatible SLA resins are available for producing surgical guides that contact the patient during surgery.

Bioprinting:
A specialised form of 3D printing that deposits living cells (suspended in bioinks — hydrogels containing cells, growth factors, and scaffold materials) in a precise three-dimensional pattern to fabricate biological tissues and constructs. Bioprinting technologies include extrusion-based (depositing bioink through a nozzle), inkjet-based (ejecting droplets of bioink), and laser-assisted bioprinting. This is the most experimental application but holds the greatest long-term transformative potential — the fabrication of living bone, cartilage, meniscus, and ligament constructs for transplantation.

Answer: 2
