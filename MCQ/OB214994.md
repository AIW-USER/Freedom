## Question

A 23-year-old rugby player undergoes surgery for recurrent instability of his shoulder. His postoperative radiograph is depicted in Figure A. The patient was found to have a neuropraxia postoperatively. Which of the following clinical findings would be most associated with this procedure?

![[q214994-a-laterjet.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral forearm numbness
2. Lateral scapular winging
3. Medial scapular winging
4. Shoulder internal rotation weakness
5. Thumb extension weakness

## Correct option

**1. Lateral forearm numbness**

## Answer explanation

A musculocutaneous neuropraxia is one of the most common neuropraxia following a laterjet procedure. Lateral forearm numbness may be seen clinically following a musculocutaneous neuropraxia as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is a terminal branch.

Bony glenoid augmentation, such as a laterjet, is typically indicated in patients with glenoid bone defects >20-25%. When performing a laterjet procedure, the musculocutaneous nerve is commonly injured and typically occurs secondary to nerve traction during glenoid exposure or graft insertion. The musculocutaneous nerve passes through the coracobrachialis muscle and descends between the biceps brachii and brachialis muscles, giving rise to the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LACN). Injury to the musculocutaneous nerve results in weakness of the biceps brachii muscle and a sensory deficit in the distribution of the LACN (ie. lateral forearm). The axillary nerve may also be injured during graft fixation in a laterjet procedure; it is the most commonly injured nerve with this procedure. Both the musculocutaneous and the axillary neuropraxias are common and are best managed by close observation for 3-6 weeks.

*Boardman et al. reviewed neurological complications of shoulder surgery.* **They found** the vast majority of neurologic injuries occur due to traction or contusion, and are rarely the result of direct laceration or nerve entrapment. **They concluded** that due to the predominantly transient nature of these neurologic complications, observation is the treatment of choice. However, if nerve recovery not occur within a 3-6 week period, electrodiagnostic testing is recommended to further define the lesion.

*Delaney et al. reviewed patients who underwent the latarjet procedure with intraoperative neuromonitoring.* **They report** that a “nerve alert” (neurologic signal change) occurred in 77% of cases, and were found most frequently during glenoid exposure and graft insertion. **They concluded** that all patients with a postoperative neurologic deficit had resolution by 6 months.

*Freehill et al. reviewed changes in neurovascular anatomy following the latarjet procedure in cadaveric shoulder specimens.* The report that the axillary and musculocutaneous nerves both moved medial to their original position, and that the musculocutaneous nerve was found to consistently overlap the axillary nerve following the Latarjet procedure. **They concluded** that this may obscure identification of either nerve and increase the risk of neurologic injury in revision surgery.

*Shah et al. reviewed the short-term complications of the latarjet procedure.* **They report** that the overall complication rate was 25%. **They concluded** that of these complications, neurologic injury occurred in 10% of cases, involving the axillary nerve (2), musculocutaneous nerve (2) and radial nerves (1).

Figures A is the AP radiograph demonstrating a coracoid autograft transfer (laterjet) with no evidence of hardware complication. Illustration A demonstrates the neuroanatomy of the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Lateral scapular winging would suggest injury to the spinal accessory nerve, which occurs less commonly than a musculocutaneous neuropraxia
Answer 3: Medial scapular winging would suggest injury to the long thoracic nerve, which occurs less commonly than a musculocutaneous neuropraxia
Answer 4: Shoulder internal rotation weakness would suggest an injury to the subscapular nerve. This is much less common that a musculocutaneous or axillary neuropraxia
Answer 5: Thumb extension weakness would suggest a posterior interosseous neuropraxia, likely secondary to a radial nerve injury at the level of the shoulder. Again, a radial neruropraxia is much less common that either a musculocutaneous or axillary neuropraxia
