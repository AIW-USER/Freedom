## Question

A 25-year-old female with a history of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) on established corticosteroid therapy presents to your clinic with chronic left shoulder pain. On examination, her active left shoulder range of motion is limited in all planes secondary to pain. The patient subsequently undergoes left shoulder hemiarthroplasty (HA). What imaging features may have been present on preoperative imaging to make this the most appropriate treatment option?

## Options

1. Humeral head osteopenia
2. Humeral head collapse and degenerative changes of the glenoid
3. Isolated humeral head collapse
4. Normal radiographs with T2 hyperintensity of the superior aspect of the humeral head on MRI
5. Mottled humeral head sclerosis without collapse

## Correct option

**3. Isolated humeral head collapse**

## Answer explanation

This patient has avascular necrosis (AVN) of the left humeral head. In patients with AVN demonstrating humeral head collapse on radiographs without associated degenerative changes of the glenoid (Cruess Stage IV), HA is an appropriate treatment option (Answer 3).

The Cruess Classification can be used to guide treatment for AVN of the proximal humerus. According to this classification, core decompression is recommended for Stage I and II disease, which is identified by normal radiographs with only MRI changes and mottled sclerosis/osteopenia, respectively. HA may be considered for stages III and IV, characterized by a crescent sign or subchondral fracture for stage III and isolated flattening and collapse for stage IV. Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is reserved for advanced Stage V disease when degenerative changes extend to the glenoid.

*Harreld et al. reviewed osteonecrosis of the humeral head and* **concluded** that overall, AVN of the humeral head is less common than AVN in other locations, such as the hip. However, the pathophysiology is similar, with factors including genetic predisposition and risk factors like corticosteroid use contributing to its development. Radiographic staging and lesion size help predict disease progression and guide treatment. Current treatment options include pharmacologics, core decompression, arthroscopic-assisted procedures, and bone grafting, though further studies are needed to confirm their effectiveness. Resurfacing techniques are used for articular surface loss, while HA and TSA are recommended for advanced cases.

*Wang et al. evaluated the long-term outcomes of HA and TSA for treating AVN of the humeral head.* *The analysis included 16 shoulders (10 HA, 6 TSA) with a mean follow-up of approximately 13 years.* HA and TSA showed reasonable 10-year implant survivorship rates (88.9% for HA, 80% for TSA) and significant improvements in pain, range of motion, and shoulder function. However, TSA provided notably better internal rotation than HA. Overall, both procedures offer durable and practical long-term solutions for AVN of the humeral head.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Degenerative glenoid changes (Stage V) preclude HA and require TSA.
Answer 1, 4&5: Cruess Stage I and II disease can be treated with core decompression.
