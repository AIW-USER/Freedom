## Question

Correct statement regarding biomarkers for prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is

## Options

1. Synovial CRP has a high positive predictive value for the diagnosis of PJIs
2. Serum Alfa Amyloid is a marker that rises very early but also falls very quickly
3. Serum Prolactin is raised in both bacterial and viral infections and hence its use is no longer recommended after COVID
4. MRI is not a sensitive tool to detect PJI due to interference from metal implant

## Correct option

**4. MRI is not a sensitive tool to detect PJI due to interference from metal implant**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

Choice D is best pick. MRI certainly has limited use in diagnosis of Prosthetic joint infections (PJIs) owing to metal artifacts. MRI with metallic artefact reduction has a role in identification. For knee arthroplasty, MRI (with metal artefact reduction) has been shown to be highly sensitive (92%) and specific (99%) for diagnosing PJI. Only one paper has been published in patients with hip arthroplasty showing that the presence of periosteal reaction, capsule edema, and intramuscular edema has a high accuracy for evaluating PJI.

Choice B and D are inappropriate. Serum Procalcitonin not Prolactin may have some role in PJI.

Regarding choice A; Synovial fluid CRP has high “negative” predictive value. Negative predictive value (NPV) is the probability of not being PJI when the test result is negative and positive predictive value (PPV) is the probability of PJI when the test result is positive. CRP could be positive from multiple reasons so PPV will be low; however, if synovial CRP is normal then high chance there is no PJI, so NPV will be high.

Synovial and serum biomarkers for PJI

The incidence rate of PJI after total joint replacement is 1–3%. Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria are widely used for the diagnosis of PJI. These criteria include results from blood/synovial fluid tests, physical examination, and histological and microbiological analyses of intra-operative samples.

![[TO17907866-1.png]]

Since a “gold standard” is missing, diagnosing PJIs always remains challenging and hence a number of serum and synovial fluid biomarkers have been under scanner. Biomarkers have been described as “any substance, structure, or process that can be measured in the body or its products and influence or predict the incidence of outcome or disease”

PJI causes activation of the innate immune system and drives white blood cells (WBCs) to produce certain substances in the serum as well as in the synovial fluid, that can be measured. Furthermore, certain specific gene expression signatures in the WBCs of the infected synovium have been exhibited using microarray techniques and these resulted in the identification of several biomarkers (see Table below) with diagnostic value in PJI.

![[TO17907866-2.jpg]]

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) [Serum biomarkers]

AAOS (American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons) - recommend the use of ESR and CRP as the first line of screening in the diagnosis of PJI. These serum biomarkers have demonstrated a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 72% for ESR and a sensitivity 94% and specificity of 74% for CRP.

A significant drawback of both ESR and CRP is that they are non-specific markers of systemic inflammation and infection. Furthermore, the use of systemic antibiotics and immunomodulatory drugs has been shown to reduce the levels of CRP and ESR.

These parameters are not specific for inflammation of infectious origin because their levels may increase after trauma, autoimmune disease, and surgical procedure and early postoperative period. They are commonly used in the diagnosis of PJI because of their high sensitivity and accessibility, but their efficacy when used alone is poor because of their low specificity and lack of increase in some low-virulence bacterial infections.

ESR and CRP can be elevated for up to six weeks and 2 weeks respectively after surgery.

The MSIS suggests the conventional thresholds of 30 mm/hour and 10 mg/L for ESR and CRP, respectively, which were selected arbitrarily due to lack of studies determining the threshold.

Interleukin-6 [Mostly Serum values used]

Interleukin 6 (IL-6) is a proinflammatory cytokine and an acute phase reactant commonly released by monocytes and macrophages. IL-6 has a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 87% in the diagnosis of PJI (higher than any other serum biomarker). In addition to this, IL-6 also demonstrated good sensitivity (80%) for PJI caused by low-virulent organisms. The combined sensitivity of CRP and IL-6 was 95% in the diagnosis of PJI. This demonstrates a greater potential of CRP and IL-6 detection as a screening test for PJI. Serum IL-6 levels have also been shown to bear strong correlation with other PJI markers, and when combined with synovial fluid WBC, demonstrated a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 90%. Normal IL-6 levels are mostly below 5 pg/ml. Levels may rise within 12 hours of the infection (peaks at 48 hours) and hence may be very useful for early detection.

Alpha defensin [Synovial fluid biomarker]

Human α-defensin is an antimicrobial peptide released by neutrophils. α-defensin has been shown to work directly and indirectly against bacterial and fungal organisms. Two methods have emerged for detecting synovial fluid alpha-defensin in recent years: the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and the lateral flow device. Synovial fluid alpha-defensin has been shown to have the highest sensitivity for the diagnosis of PJI. A notable advantage compared to other synovial fluid markers with alfa-defensin is that, it appears to maintain its diagnostic accuracy even in the presence of ongoing antibiotic therapy. Finally, it exhibits consistent sensitivity in detecting a wide spectrum of pathogens, including organisms with low virulence. This broad pathogen detection capability enhances its diagnostic utility in identifying infections caused by various types of pathogens.

False positivity has been noted in cases of metallosis. However, it remains accurate even when the synovial fluid is contaminated with blood.

Despite the advantages of synovial alpha-defensin testing, its widespread inclusion in routine tests faces challenges due to its high cost and limited accessibility. Consequently, the integration of synovial alpha-defensin as a routine diagnostic marker for PJI remains a challenge.

Synovial CRP and Synovial white blood cell count

While synovial fluid WBC counts have been a time tested way to confirm PJI, synovial CRP has off late been studied extensively for the diagnosis of acute and chronic PJI. When used in conjunction with α-defensin, synovial CRP has a high specificity and sensitivity. However, the varying levels in the postoperative period have made it challenging to determine threshold values for the diagnosis of acute and chronic PJI. Synovial WBC count and CRP levels that are most suggestive of chronic PJI are more than 1,700–3,000 cells/µL and 10 mg/L respectively. Synovial CRP is a highly useful and predictive tool to diagnose infection in the acute setting (1–3 weeks after operation) and the positive and negative predictive values are higher when used in combination with synovial WBC counts. Used alone, synovial CRP has high NPV but low PPV.

Leukocyte esterase [Synovial biomarker]

Leukocyte esterase (LE) is an enzyme secreted by activated neutrophils recruited to areas of infection. LE strip test is a point of care test that can be performed intraoperatively while using as little as 1 mL of synovial fluid. The LE strip test was originally developed for the identification of urinary tract infections and has demonstrated its accuracy in the diagnosis of the PJI and has been subsequently integrated into the ICM criteria.

The leukocyte esterase colorimetric strip test performed by applying fluid to a reagent test strip has the advantage of being quick, easy, cheap and reliable. The alteration in the color of the LE strip is caused by the reaction between the leukocyte esterase, which is secreted by leukocytes, and the substrate present on the test strip. The activity of leukocyte esterase is basically typically considered to be directly proportional to the number of leukocytes.

The LE test strip is widely used as a biomarker in synovial fluid for PJI due to its affordability, convenience, and commercial availability

A potential disadvantage is the invalidation of the result by blood contamination. However, in most cases, this can be addressed by centrifugation prior to application of the fluid.

LE strip test has also been found to successfully differentiate between metal-on-metal failures and PJI.

Sensitivity and specificity of LE have been found to be 81% and 97%. LE has been shown to have comparable accuracy (slightly lower) to alpha defensin.

Leukocyte esterase may, however, pose some problems due to the colorimetric nature of the test. The optimal cut-off is believed to be + 2 for a high positive likelihood, and + 1 (trace) should be investigated further. Even with the above limitations, as reported by a number of studies, leukocyte esterase test has an excellent diagnostic reliability in the outpatient and the operating room settings.

Procalcitonin [Both Serum and Synovial values used]

Procalcitonin (PCT) is stimulated by bacterial lipopolysaccharides and is considered a very a good indicator of identifying bacterial septicemia. For PJI diagnosis, although it has a high specificity (98%), it lacks sensitivity (33%), so is not as commonly used. Synovial fluid PCT fluid may add better clinical support than serum PCT for detecting PJI atleast.

Serum D-dimer

Apart from having utility in diagnosis of pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis, a number of studies reported the role of D-dimer in diagnosis of PJI. D-dimer has a good diagnostic accuracy for PJI, but its specificity is not high. D-dimer outperforms CRP and ESR in the diagnosis of PJI due to indolent organisms. It is widely believed that D-dimer should be used with other conjunct investigations (and not alone) to increase the diagnostic performance of the test.
