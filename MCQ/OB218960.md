## Options

1. Prescribe oral tamsulosin for his benign prosthetic hyperplasia with referral to urology
2. Refer to oncology for neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by resection
3. Refer to radiation oncology for neoadjuvant radiation followed by resection
4. Perform local excision of the osteochondroma that is projected centrally within the pelvis
5. Wide resection with a multidisciplinary team to include general and plastic surgery providers

## Correct option

**5. Wide resection with a multidisciplinary team to include general and plastic surgery providers**

## Answer explanation

This patient has underlying multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE), which increases his risk for secondary chondrosarcoma. The osteochondroma in his pelvis underwent a secondary malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma and should be treated with wide resection.

Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (MHE) is a rare, autosomal-dominant genetic condition characterized by numerous benign osteochondromas throughout the skeletal system. The underlying cause is a deletion of the tumor suppressor gene exostosin-1 (EXT1) or exostosin-2 (EXT2). Some patients have relatively asymptomatic lesions in anatomically inconsequential locations. However, many patients experience shortened stature, limb-length discrepancies, and neurovascular complications secondary to the local osteochondroma mass effect. While solitary osteochondromas scarcely undergo a malignant transformation in the general population, approximately 5% of MHE patients will experience malignant degeneration in one of their osteochondromas. This occurs most commonly in the pelvis. While regular monitoring is not necessarily required, patients complaining of increasing pain, pain at night, or rapidly increasing osteochondroma size warrant further investigation. Pelvic tumors often go unnoticed until they grow large enough to compress visceral organs, as seen in this patient. The gold standard treatment for pelvic chondrosarcomas includes wide resection with negative margins. This treatment is best rendered with a multidisciplinary team to assist with dissection and pelvic floor reconstruction.

*Tsuda and colleagues performed a retrospective analysis of 51 patients with secondary chondrosarcoma at their institution.* **They reported** that most patients were in their 30s, the pelvis was the most common anatomic site, and most tumors (69%) were grade I. They recommended that secondary chondrosarcoma arising from osteochondromas of the pelvis be treated with wide resection margins. However, limb secondary chondrosarcomas could potentially be treated with less aggressive surgical resection.

*Lin and colleagues provided a review article on secondary chondrosarcomas.* **They discuss** that secondary chondrosarcomas are most seen in hereditary conditions such as Maffucci syndrome, Ollier disease, and MHE. **They report** favorable five-year survival rates (>90%) due to the low grade of most secondary chondrosarcomas. **They further highlight** that pelvic secondary chondrosarcomas have a very high local recurrence rate.

*Wells and colleagues provided an overview of conventional cartilaginous tumors and their respective diagnostic workup and treatment.* *They review the spectrum of cartilage tumors (enchondromas, atypical cartilaginous tumors, chondrosarcomas) and their respective treatment options.* *They also review the genetic conditions predisposing patients to secondary chondrosarcoma, including Ollier disease, Maffucci syndrome, and MHE.* **They conclude** that differentiating between chondroid tumors may be difficult, and any ambiguity warrants evaluation by a musculoskeletal oncology multidisciplinary team.

Figure A demonstrates AP and lateral radiographic imaging of a left knee with significant osteochondromas appreciated in the distal femur and proximal tibia/fibula metaphyseal regions. Figure B shows post-operative imaging after this patient’s primary total knee replacement using a posterior stabilized implant. Figure C shows axial CT imaging with a posteriorly projecting osteochondroma adjacent to this patient’s pubic symphysis. There is an intermittently calcified soft tissue mass projecting from this osteochondroma encompassing a significant portion of the intrapelvic volume. This is secondary chondrosarcoma arising from the preexisting osteochondroma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The primary cause of his difficulty urinating is due to the mass effect of the tumor. Tamsulosin will not resolve his underlying problem.
Answer 2&3: While many soft tissue sarcomas can be treated with chemotherapy or radiation, most chondrosarcomas are not responsive to either modality and thus are treated with wide resection alone.
Answer 4: Performing local excision of the osteochondroma that is projected centrally within the pelvis would not be appropriate. Extended intralesional curettage and bone-void filling are appropriate in appendicular (i.e., extremity) low-grade chondrosarcomas due to their low malignant potential. However, axial (i.e., pelvis and spine) chondrosarcomas are treated with wide resection due to their much higher risk for metastasis.
