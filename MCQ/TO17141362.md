Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Dupuytren’s Contracture

## Question

Which is not an indicated treatment for dupuytren’s contracture?

## Options

1. Clostridial collagenase histolyticum (CCH) injection
2. Hyalase injection
3. Manipulation under anaesthesia
4. Needle aponeurotomy

## Correct option

**2. Hyalase injection**

## Answer explanation

Dupuytren’s contracture:

Dupuytren’s disease is a proliferative fibrous metaplasia of the subcutaneous tissue of the palm. It is characterized by fibromatosis (proliferation of fibroblast or myofibroblast). Palmar fascia is replaced by fibrous tissue (palmar fibromatosis) in the form of nodules and cords that may result in secondary progressive and irreversible finger joint flexion contractures. Ring finger is usually first to be involved followed by little and other fingers. Flexion contractures most commonly occur at the MCP joints, followed by proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints and then distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints.

In a few patients of Dupuytren’s disease, similar lesions may be found in the plantar fascia (Ledderhose’s disease) and in the penile fascia (Peyronie’s disease). Garrod nodules, “knuckle pads” are common on the dorsum of the proximal interphalangeal joints.

It commonly occurs in adults in their forties to sixties. Dupuytren contracture occurs 10 times more frequently in men than in women.

Predisposed Individuals

The condition is commonly seen in men of Scandinavian and Celtic origin (Male:Female = 10:1) in their fourth to sixth decade of life. People who are diabetics, alcoholics (alcoholic cirrhosis), smokers and who are on antiepileptic drugs are at greater risk of affection. Although the exact cause remains unknown, hand trauma and heavy manual labor performed by an individual may be a contributing factor. Hereditary predisposition is present in some families.

Pathogenesis: Dupuytren contracture begins with increased fibroblast proliferation followed by type 3 collagen deposition resulting in uncontrolled palmar fascia growth ultimately causing flexion contractures. In addition, growth factors and their receptors have been shown to have increased expression in diseased fascia, especially transforming growth factor-β and basic fibroblast growth factor.

Metacarpophalangeal and distal interphalangeal joint contractures seem to result from pretendinous and lateral cord development. Proximal interphalangeal joint contractures may develop from isolated digital cords, however, in addition to central, spiral, or retrovascular cords.

Poor prognostic factors: positive family history, male sex, bilateral disease, association with knuckle pads and nodules in plantar fascia, patient on insulin, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

Clinical Presentation

The involvement is commonly bilateral (45%), but it is rarely symmetrical. In the early stages, thickening of palmar aponeurosis or nodules may be felt at the base of the ring or little fingers while in later stage flexion deformity of the fingers develops. The nodules typically appear at the distal palmar crease over the metacarpophalangeal joints and distally over the proximal interphalangeal joints but not over the distal interphalangeal joints. Nodules are typically painless, although larger ones may be painful due to associated tenosynovitis. Garrod’s nodules or “knuckle pads” are common on the dorsum of the PIP joints in these patients.

Treatment

Nonoperative:

- External beam radiotherapy: Low dose radiation (less than 30 Gy) is used. Different protocols have been published. It causes regression of the disease in 45% and stops progression in 80% patients.
- Clostridial collagenase histolyticum (CCH) injections have been used with varying success for metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joint contracture release.
- Manipulation of MCP and IP joints under local anaesthesia. This can be combined with CCH injection
- Percutaneous needle aponeurotomy (PNA): multiple contractile cord areas are sectioned in the palm and fingers with a needle.

Operative:

Operative: An elderly patient with minimal deformity needs no treatment. But when flexion contractures of the fingers at PIP joints exceed 15° and at the MCP joints exceed 30°, then surgery is required to release contractures.

Operative procedures commonly used in treating Dupuytren contracture are (1) subcutaneous fasciotomy, (2) partial (selective) fasciectomy, (3) complete fasciectomy, (4) fasciectomy with skin grafting, staged resection preceded by external fixation, (5) joint resection and arthrodesis, and (6) amputation.

The least extensive procedure, subcutaneous fasciotomy, is commonly used for elderly patients who are not concerned with the appearance of the disease or in patients who have poor general health. Partial (selective) fasciectomy usually is indicated when only the ulnar one or two fingers are involved. In this procedure only mature tissue is excised and recurrence rate is about 50%. Complete fasciectomy rarely, if ever, is indicated because it frequently is associated with complications of stiffness, poor wound healing and hematoma.

Answer: 2
