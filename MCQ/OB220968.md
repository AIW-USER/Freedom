## Question

A 15-year-old female presents to your office following an acute ankle sprain one week ago. She still notes pain along the plantar medial aspect of her foot and in her ankle. Mild swelling is still present on exam without plantar ecchymosis. You obtain plain radiographs as shown in Figure A. What is the initial treatment recommendation for this patient's condition?

## Options

1. Cast immobilization for six weeks
2. Excision of the accessory navicular and posterior tibial tendon advancement
3. Activity modification, orthotics, and physical therapy
4. Modified Brostrom-Gould ligament repair
5. First and second tarsometatarsal joint fixation

## Correct option

**3. Activity modification, orthotics, and physical therapy**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with an accessory navicular that has become symptomatic following an acute ankle sprain. The best initial treatment recommendation for this patient would be activity modification, orthotics, and physical therapy (Answer 3).

Accessory navicular is an idiopathic condition in which the tuberosity of the navicular develops from a secondary ossification center that fails to fuse during childhood. This is a normal variant found in 12% of the population and exhibits an autosomal-dominant pattern of inheritance. A majority of these are diagnosed incidentally and remain asymptomatic. These are diagnosed with plain radiographs of the foot demonstrating a plantar medial enlargement of the navicular bone. Treatment is initially conservative with activity modification, orthotics, and physical therapy. If pain is recalcitrant, then cast immobilization can improve symptoms. Surgical excision +/- posterior tibial tendon advancement and/or medializing calcaneal osteotomy is typically reserved for patients who have failed extensive nonoperative treatment.

*Murphy et al. reviewed the evaluation and management of common accessory ossicles of the foot and ankle in children and adolescents.* **They note** that approximately 20 accessory ossicles have been described in the foot and ankle. Five of these, in particular, generate the most concern among pediatric patients. These include an accessory navicular along the plantar medial aspect of the navicular, os trigonum along the posterior aspect of the talus, os subfibulare near the tip of the lateral malleolus, os subtibiale near the tip of the medial malleolus, and os peroneum found in the peroneus longus tendon and just lateral to the cuboid in the foot. These accessory ossicles may contribute to recalcitrant pain and ultimately to surgical excision in pediatric patients.

Campbell and Jeng discuss painful accessory navicular and spring ligament injuries in young athletes. They note that these injuries differ from those in typical patients with pes planus deformities, as they affect running and jumping athletes and cause medial arch pain. Initial treatment consists of rest, orthotics, and restriction from sports. They conclude that orthotics can alleviate medial arch pain by offloading this region of the foot; however, many patients still undergo surgery to expedite recovery and return to sports.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Cast immobilization for 6 weeks is typically reserved for patients with acute tarsal navicular fractures or persistent pain with an accessory navicular bone despite activity modification, orthotics, and physical therapy.
Answer 2: Excision of the accessory navicular bone with posterior tibial tendon advancement is reserved for patients who have failed extensive nonoperative treatment.
Answer 4: A Modified Brostrom-Gould ligament repair would be indicated for lateral ankle pain and recurrent lateral ankle instability.
Answer 5: First and second tarsometatarsal joint fixation would be indicated for a LisFranc injury; however, this is not present in this patient.
