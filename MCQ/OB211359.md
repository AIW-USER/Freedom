## Question

He has a history significant for a slipped capital femoral epiphysis requiring in-situ pinning and then hardware removal several years later. On examination, the patient has a painless range of motion but symptoms reproducible with combined flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. There was no tenderness over the anterior hip joint and no pain with resisted hip flexion. Figures A through D are the current images. What is the most likely cause of this patient's hip pain?

## Options

1. Osteoarthritis
2. Pincer-type femoroacetabular impingement
3. Femoral neck stress fracture
4. Cam-type femoroacetabular impingement
5. Iliopsoas tendonitis

## Correct option

**4. Cam-type femoroacetabular impingement**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with hip pain consistent with femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) with radiographs revealing a cam deformity of the femoral head and neck.

Impingement of the femoral neck on the acetabulum, also known as FAI, can be a cause of groin pain in young athletic patients leading to labral and cartilage damage. Cam and pincer lesions are characteristic variants with most patients having combined pathology. Cam-type FAI is the result of an aspherical head in a hemispheric acetabulum that causes cartilage delamination and labral separation in the anterolateral acetabulum. Cam lesions are best assessed on frog-leg or lateral Dunn radiographs of the hip by measuring the alpha angle with values >50° being diagnostic. Surgical treatment involves arthroscopic osteoplasty or open hip dislocation osteoplasty of the femoral neck.

*Philippon et al. performed a retrospective review of 45 professional athletes undergoing arthroscopic decompression of FAI.* **They reported** that 93% of patients were able to return to a professional level of play, with the 7% that did not were found to have diffuse osteoarthritis at the time of arthroscopy. **They concluded** that arthroscopic treatment of FAI allows professional athletes to return to play.

*Bedi et al. performed a prospective cohort study of 60 male patients matched to either surgical hip dislocation or hip arthroscopy for the treatment of FAI.* **The authors reported** in the arthroscopy group a reduction in the lateral and AP alpha angle by 28.3% and 16.8%, and an increase head-neck offset by 111% while the surgical hip dislocation group experienced a reduction in lateral and AP alpha angle by 30.7% and 25.7% with an increase in head-neck offset by 108%. **The authors concluded** that arthroscopic osteoplasty can have similar restoration of proximal femoral anatomy with anterior and anterosuperior cam and rim impingement, but open surgical hip dislocation may better address the posterosuperior loss of femoral offset.

*Nepple et al. performed a retrospective study of 355 hips in 338 patients undergoing hip arthroscopy.* **They found** Tönnis osteoarthritis grade, alpha angle >50° on frog-leg lateral radiographs, male sex, and older age were independent risk factors for grade 3 and 4 chondromalacia with cam-type FAI being strongly associated with severe labral disease. **The authors concluded** that recognition of patient and radiographic factors is important for patient selection, surgical planning, and counseling for hip arthroscopy.

*Schoenecker et al. reviewed the surgical management of problematic hip pain in adolescents and young adult patients.* *They described cam-type FAI being the result of femoral head posterior tilt leading to an aspheric head being jammed into the anterolateral labrum and acetabular cartilage as the hip is flexed and internally rotated.* **The authors recommended** early diagnosis and treatment of hip instability and impingement in young patients as articular cartilage damage is irreversible.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Given the patient's age and the radiographs, osteoarthritis is unlikely to be the cause of hip pain.
Answer 2: Pincer-type FAI is the result of acetabular over coverage leading to an increased lateral center edge angle. This is not apparent with the patient's radiographs and is not consistent with his history
Answer 3: Femoral neck stress fractures are possible in energy-deficient athletes, but the coronal T1 MRI was unremarkable for edema of the femoral neck.
Answer 5: Iliopsoas tendonitis is a possible cause of groin pain in athletes, which is usually apparent with groin pain reproduced with resisted hip flexion, also known as a Stinchfield test.
