## Question

A 17-year-old high school football player with no prior shoulder injury presents to the emergency department with complaints of right shoulder pain after a tackle during the opening game of the season. Imaging reveals an anterior glenohumeral dislocation. He undergoes closed reduction in the emergency department. An MRI is obtained and demonstrates a tear of the anterior inferior labrum with a small non-engaging Hill-Sachs lesion and no fracture of the glenoid. When considering treatment options, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. Nonoperative management would allow the patient to return to play during this current season
2. Nonoperative management has a similar risk of recurrence as surgical management with an anterior soft-tissue stabilization procedure
3. Nonoperative management with an external rotation brace has a lower risk of redislocation than internal rotation sling
4. Surgical management would allow for quicker return to play than nonoperative management
5. Surgical management with anterior soft-tissue stabilization will have a lower recurrence than a bony augmentation procedure

## Correct option

**1. Nonoperative management would allow the patient to return to play during this current season**

## Answer explanation

Return to play after nonoperative management typically takes 3 to 4 weeks once the player has full painless range of motion and return of strength.

Traumatic anterior shoulder instability, also referred to as TUBS (Traumatic Unilateral dislocations with a Bankart lesion requiring Surgery), are traumatic shoulder injuries that generally occur as a result of an anterior force to the shoulder while its abducted and externally rotated and may lead to recurrent anterior shoulder instability. Diagnosis is made clinically with the presence of positive anterior instability provocative tests and confirmed with MRI studies that may reveal labral and/or bony injuries of the glenoid and proximal humerus (Hill-Sachs lesion). Treatment may be nonoperative or operative depending on the chronicity of symptoms, the presence of risk factors for recurrence, and the severity of labral and/or glenoid defects. In high-risk populations, surgery is often offered after a single dislocation event.

*Owens et al. reviewed shoulder dislocation and subluxation injuries in young athletes.* **They reported** that there is controversy regarding optimal treatment of an athlete with an in-season shoulder dislocation. **They noted** that surgical management with arthroscopic or open Bankart repair reduces the rate of recurrence. **They concluded** that when selecting a management option, the clinician must consider the natural history of shoulder instability, pathologic changes noted on examination and imaging, sport- and position-specific demands, duration of treatment, and the athlete's motivation.

*Burns et al. reviewed shoulder instability in young athletes.* **They noted** that the management of athletes during their competitive season requires an understanding of the natural history of shoulder instability, the specific needs of the injured athlete, and the duration of treatment. **They noted** that rehabilitation can enable an athlete with a shoulder dislocation to return to play within 3 weeks of injury, while surgical stabilization should be considered for the athlete with recurrent instability or inability to perform.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Nonoperative management has a higher rate of recurrence compared with surgical management.
Answer 3: There have been no studies that have demonstrated that bracing helps reduce the risk of recurrence, although bracing is frequently used to limit motion (abduction and external rotation).
Answer 4: Nonoperative management allows for a faster return-to-play.
Answer 5: A bony augmentation (Latarjet) is likely to have an equal or lower recurrence rate than anterior soft-tissue stabilization procedure (Bankart).
