## Question

A 72-year-old female presents to the clinic failing nonoperative management of left tri-compartmental osteoarthritis. She undergoes an uncomplicated total knee arthroplasty and does well postoperatively (Figures A & B). Three years later, she falls down her stairs at home and undergoes surgery for her distal femur periprosthetic fracture at an outside facility. She returns to your clinic approximately 3 months later complaining of continued pain requiring prolonged narcotic use. She has remained on partial weight-bearing status since her most recent surgery but endorses feelings of knee instability/buckling during physical therapy sessions. The incision appears healed and there is a moderate effusion with a notably limited range of motion. Her most recent radiographs are shown in Figures C & D. ## Options

1. Initiate progressive strength training and a return to walk protocol with physical therapy
2. Order inflammatory labs and perform a joint aspiration with synovial fluid analysis
3. Refer the patient to a durable medical equipment provider for a bone stimulator
4. Perform hardware removal, deformity correction, and revision open reduction internal fixation
5. Perform hardware removal and conversion to distal femur replacement

## Correct option

**2. Order inflammatory labs and perform a joint aspiration with synovial fluid analysis**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s history, physical examination, and radiographic findings are collectively concerning for infectious nonunion and periprosthetic infection after undergoing open reduction internal fixation of a periprosthetic distal femur fracture. Underlying infectious etiology should be ruled out prior to any further revision surgeries being pursued (Answer 2).

Periprosthetic femur fractures are often devastating injuries with a detrimental impact on a patient’s quality of life after total knee replacement. The ideal treatment is determined by the fracture pattern, quality of bone stock, and the patient’s likelihood of healing. Some cases may undergo acute revision TKA due to loose implants or lack of adequate bone stock for fixation. However, most of these fractures present with a stable implant and subsequently undergo operative fixation using various techniques (locked lateral plating, retrograde intramedullary nailing, etc). The most common causes for revision surgery thereafter are for the treatment of fracture nonunion or for deep infection. It is imperative that periprosthetic joint infection be ruled out prior to any revision TKA. In this case, the patient has increasing knee pain, a moderate effusion with limited range of motion, and signs of hardware loosening months after her most recent surgery. Periprosthetic joint infection should be ruled out prior to proceeding with any revision surgery. Unfortunately, this patient was found to have a septic nonunion with a periprosthetic joint infection. She underwent an explanation and debridement of nonviable bone necessitating a static spacer (Illustration A) with the eventual plan to convert to a distal femur replacement.

*Kuzyk and colleagues provided a comprehensive review article regarding revision TKA for the management of distal femur periprosthetic fractures.* **They discuss** that when selecting implants, ligamentous stability and residual bone stock must be considered. **They emphasize** utilizing tantalum cones for metaphyseal fixation with long stem implants or, more simply, utilizing a distal femur replacement. **The authors conclude** that these patients often have multiple comorbidities and the ultimate goal of surgery is early mobilization and return of function.

Kancherla and Nwachuku provided a review article on the management of periprosthetic femur fractures after TKA. They discuss that each patient should be treated on a case-by-case basis dependent on their fracture pattern, stability of underlying implants, and potential to heal. The authors further highlight various treatment options with case examples. They conclude that complication rates for each are relatively high, however, careful operative planning and patient optimization can mitigate these concerns.

*Abbas and Morgan-Jones performed a retrospective case-series review on the treatment of periprosthetic femur fracture nonunion in 6 patients treated with revision TKA.* *At a 4.5-year average follow-up, all patients achieved union and were ambulating.* **The authors concluded** that constrained implants, metaphyseal sleeves, and intramedullary stems can appropriately address bone loss, instability, and mal-alignment to obviate the need for distal femur replacement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Given the clinical and radiographic findings, this patient should undergo evaluation for a periprosthetic joint infection rather than be referred back to physical therapy.
Answer 3: A bone stimulator could be considered in patients with delayed osseous union. However, a bone stimulator would not address infectious-etiology nonunion cases.
Answer 4: Hardware removal, deformity correction, and revision open reduction internal fixation would be considered an appropriate treatment if the periprosthetic joint infection workup was negative.
Answer 5: Performing hardware removal and conversion to distal femur replacement would also be considered an appropriate treatment option if the periprosthetic joint infection workup was negative.
