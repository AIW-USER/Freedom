## Question

A 62-year-old male mechanic presents to your office complaining of pain in his middle finger which is located over the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint. There is no evidence of deformity at the PIP joint but radiographs demonstrate primary osteoarthritis. You discuss management options with the patient and he states he has already tried hand therapy and other nonoperative measures and would like surgery. He has heard about the ability to "replace," the arthritic joint. Which of the following is true regarding silicone arthroplasty when used to treat PIP arthritis?

## Options

1. Associated with improved grip strength and DASH score outcomes
2. No correlation noted between age and revision rate
3. May not provide reliable, long-term pain relief
4. Not shown to significantly improve PIP joint range of motion
5. Dorsal approach provides lower revision rate than volar approach

## Correct option

**4. Not shown to significantly improve PIP joint range of motion**

## Answer explanation

Reliable improvements in pain, but no significant differences in grip strength, proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint motion, or outcome scores, can be expected after PIP joint silicone arthroplasty.

PIP joint arthritis can be caused by primary osteoarthritis or erosive arthritis and is diagnosed based on physical examination findings and radiographs. First-line management involves NSAIDS and observation, but if symptoms fail to improve, surgery may be indicated. Fusion of the PIP joint is one option but is more so used for border digits or in cases of middle/ring finger deformity or ligamentous incompetence. Silicone arthroplasty is indicated for the middle and ring finger PIP joints in cases where the collateral ligaments remain intact and there is adequate bone stock with no significant angular deformity. Studies have shown reliable pain relief with PIP joint silicone arthroplasty but less reliable improvements in motion and grip strength.

*Wagner et al. published a JAAOS review article discussing PIP joint arthroplasty in patients < 60 years of age.* **They note** a revision rate of around 25% in patients <60 and lower ten-year implant survival rates in younger patients as well. At follow-up, pain levels had improved but the range of motion and grip strength were maintained.

*Wagner et al. studied outcomes using pyrocarbon PIP arthroplasty in 170 cases.* **They noted** a reoperation rate of 34%, with higher rates in those who had their initial surgery for post-traumatic arthritis. They reinforce improvements in pain compared to pre-operatively, but no significant changes in PIP range of motion.

*Dickson et al. reviewed PIP arthroplasty with a minimum of 5-year follow-up.* *They again noted relatively high rates of revision and loosening but also included patients with inflammatory arthritis in the study.* **They also showed** improved pain but no significant improvements in PIP joint range of motion or DASH outcome scores.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Silicone PIP arthroplasty has not been shown to significantly improve grip strength or DASH scores.
Answer 2: Younger patients <60 years of age have shown a higher revision rate (~25%) after silicone PIP arthroplasty.
Answer 3: Silicone PIP arthroplasty has been shown to reliably improve pain scores.
Answer 5: Volar, not dorsal approach to PIP arthroplasty has lower revision rates and better postoperative range of motion according to some studies.
