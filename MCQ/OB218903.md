## Question

A 32-year-old male presents with a four-month history of loss of sensation along the median nerve distribution of his right hand. He states he wakes up once or twice a night and needs to shake out his hand due to the feeling of pins and needles. He has a positive Phalen test and undergoes a nerve conduction study. The report states his sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) display increased latency with decreased nerve conduction velocity across his wrist compared to the unaffected, contralateral side. The SNAPs amplitudes remain equivalent bilaterally. He undergoes a corticosteroid injection to his carpal tunnel, which provides relief for 2-3 weeks with regression of symptoms thereafter. Given these findings, how should the patient be counseled regarding his nerve conduction studies?

## Options

1. The findings were inconclusive, a trial of nonoperative management is the best option
2. He has mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome; surgery will predictably provide symptom relief
3. He has mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome; surgery is unlikely to provide relief of his symptoms
4. He has severe carpal tunnel syndrome; surgery will predictably provide some relief
5. He has severe carpal tunnel syndrome; surgery is unlikely to provide symptom relief

## Correct option

**2. He has mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome; surgery will predictably provide symptom relief**

## Answer explanation

Increased latency and decreased nerve conduction velocity without changes in amplitude are suggestive of focal demyelination without a change in the number of nerve fibers. These findings are consistent with mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and, having a positive temporary response to corticosteroid injection, are predictive of a positive response to carpal tunnel release.

The clinical usefulness of electrodiagnostic studies (EDS) heavily depends on pretest probability. In other words, when an underlying nerve injury is likely, EDS can be used to measure severity or location. When an underlying nerve injury is unlikely, particularly in the setting of nonspecific symptoms, there is a high likelihood that the EDS results will be inconclusive or misleading. EDS is meant to serve as an adjuvant diagnostic tool only after obtaining a thorough history and physical examination. Therefore, electrodiagnostic studies are most useful to determine the severity of the present disease, hone in on the location of a neurologic lesion, exclude concomitant pathology, and prognosticate potential outcomes with treatment.

EDS encompasses two separate tests: (1) a nerve conduction study (NCS) and (2) electromyography (EMG). The NCS reflects the health of a peripheral nerve’s sensory fibers (sensory nerve action potential, SNAP) and motor fibers (compound motor action potential, CMAP). Both sensory and motor fibers are tested for latency, nerve conduction velocity (NCV), and amplitude. Both latency and NCV reflect the speed of conduction (i.e. integrity of the myelin around an axon) whereas the amplitude reflects the number of functioning fibers within a nerve. In early carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), focal demyelination is reflected by an increase in both SNAP/CMAP latency and a decrease in nerve conduction velocity without changes in amplitude. Chronic, more severe CTS cases show an axonal loss from chronic ischemia with subsequent decreases in motor amplitudes.

*Dy and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of the interpretation of electrodiagnostic studies in the field of orthopaedic surgery.* *They discuss the pathophysiology behind both nerve conduction studies and electromyography with real-world case examples of common peripheral nerve injuries.* **The authors conclude** that a detailed understanding of these electrodiagnostic studies can aid surgeons in localizing neurologic lesions, determining the presence of current or alternative diagnoses, and guiding the selection of nonsurgical and surgical treatments.

Dr. Wang from the University of Washington presented an overview regarding the treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome. They discuss the various techniques utilized in diagnosing and treating carpal tunnel syndrome, stressing that only electrodiagnostic studies provide the information necessary to classify carpal tunnel syndrome from mild to severe. The author concludes that electrodiagnostic studies are not necessary to establish the diagnosis, however, provide useful information for ambiguous patient presentations and prediction of surgical outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1. Latency is a measurement of how long it takes for a nerve transmission from point of stimulus to point of recording. The latency would INCREASE if there was an injury to the nerve’s myelin. Nerve conduction velocity (NCV) is calculated by dividing the conduction time by the distance between the points of stimulus and recording. The NCV would decrease if there was an injury to the nerve’s myelin, making NCV inversely proportional to latency. When correlated to this patient’s history and clinical examination, these findings are corroborated to be conclusive of mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome.
Answer 3: As discussed, this patient has mild-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome. Given a positive response to a corticosteroid injection to the carpal tunnel, this patient would predictably receive relief from operative carpal tunnel release.
Answer 4&5: Amplitude reflects the number of functioning fibers within the nerve and is not reliant on the speed of nerve conduction. If this patient’s nerve conduction study showed decreased SNAPs amplitude, this would be concerning for chronic, permanent damage to the nerve. In those cases of severe carpal tunnel syndrome, response to surgery is less predictable given the permanent loss of nerve fibers already present.
