## Question

A 65-year-old male radio disc jockey complains of right sided shoulder pain with overhead activities for the past eight weeks. The pain started after lifting a heavy bag over his head. He has 5/5 supraspinatus strength, and positive impingement signs. An MRI is demonstrated in Figures A and B. After failure of conservative management, what is the best treatment option?

## Options

1. Subacromial decompression and rotator cuff debridement
2. Single row rotator cuff repair
3. Double row rotator cuff repair
4. Labral repair
5. Biceps tenodesis

## Correct option

**1. Subacromial decompression and rotator cuff debridement**

## Answer explanation

The patient has clinical evidence of subacromial impingement and a low-grade partial articular sided rotator cuff tear visible on MRI. The best treatment option for him is a subacromial decompression and rotator cuff debridement.

Partial rotator cuff tears can be classified as articular- or bursal-sided with outcomes of treatment varying between them. In general, articular sided tears fare better than bursal sided tears, and a higher degree of partial tearing is acceptable prior to needing repair. The traditional teaching is that repair is required for bursal-sided tears of >25% and articular-sided tears of >50%.

*Cordasco et. al. performed a retrospective review of patients with subacromial decompression and concomitant grade 1 or 2 partial thickness rotator cuff tears who underwent subacromial decompression alone.* **They found** that the clinical outcomes for these patients were no different than for patients with impingement and no rotator cuff tear. Patients were more likely to fail (L'insalata score < 70) this surgical intervention in the presence of a grade 2 bursal sided tears. Failure was very low in patients with partial articular sided tears (5%).

*Payne et. al. retrospectively reviewed 40 young athletes with partial thickness rotator cuff tears treated with shoulder arthroscopy.* **They found** that patients with acute rotator cuff tears from traumatic injuries were more likely to have inflamed subacromial bursae, and good post-operative results after arthroscopic subacromial decompression and tear debridement. Patients with insidious, atraumatic shoulder pain tended to have less success with just debridement and decompression, which the authors attributed to an increase in glenohumeral translation in these patients.

Figure A is a T2 weighted coronal MRI which demonstrates a partial articular sided supraspinatus tear. Figure B is a T2 weighted sagittal MRI demonstrating the same tear.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: While a single row rotator cuff repair is an option, there is good evidence to suggest that patients with partial articular rotator cuff tears and impingement will do well with subacromial decompression and cuff debridement alone.
Answer 3: A double row (transosseous equivalent) suture technique would be helpful for large, retracted cuff tears. This patient's tear, however, is not indicated for repair.
Answer 4: The clinical scenario demonstrates rotator cuff pathology. There is no clear suggestion of labral disease.
Answer 5: Biceps tenodesis would be appropriate treatment for long head of biceps/superior labral disease. However, this patient does not present with symptoms or imaging suggestive of this.
