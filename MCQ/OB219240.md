## Question

An 11-year-old male is brought to the trauma bay following an all-terrain vehicle crash with the injury shown in Figure A. He was treated with open reduction and screw fixation. He appears to do well at his two-week postoperative appointment and is lost to follow-up. Seven months postoperatively he returns to your clinic complaining of worsening hip and groin pain. A physical exam reveals significant pain in the left hip with any range of motion, and radiographs are shown in Figure B. ## Options

1. Lateral femoral circumflex artery
2. Medial femoral circumflex artery
3. Femoral artery
4. Superior gluteal artery
5. Artery of ligamentum teres

## Correct option

**2. Medial femoral circumflex artery**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a Delbet Type 1B femoral neck fracture which was treated with open reduction internal fixation and has since progressed to avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head. Injury to retinacular branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery, the dominant blood supply to the femoral head in a patient of this age, is usually responsible for the development of post-traumatic AVN.

*Ogden published a 1974 cadaveric study examining the development and maturation of the vascular supply of the pediatric hip.* *He examined 36 hip joints from pediatric cadavers which had their abdominal aortae injected with dyes, then subsequently dissected to reveal the vascular networks.* *Ogden* **concluded** that in younger children the LFCA and MFCA contribute roughly equal amounts of vascular supply to the proximal epiphysis and physeal plate. This changes as the child ages, as subsequent vascular development is characterized by regression of the lateral circumflex system and concomitant development of the medial circumflex system.

*Moon and Mehlman published a 2006 study on the risk factors for AVN following femoral neck fractures.* *The authors reviewed 25 cases from their own institution and 360 patients through a PubMed meta-analysis.* **The authors found** that fracture type and age were identified as significant predictors of AVN. Older children were 1.14 times more likely to develop AVN for each year of increasing age. Type I to III fractures were 15, 6, and 4 times, respectively, more likely to develop AVN than type IV fractures. AVN rate by Delbet classification was I = 38%, II = 28%, III = 18%, and IV = 5%. Of note, their study does not account for nondisplaced versus displaced fractures (subtype A vs. subtype B).

*Spence et al. published a 2016 study on the risk factors for AVN following pediatric femoral neck fractures.* **The authors identified** 70 patients between the ages of 1.3 and 18.1 years treated for a femoral neck fracture between 2000 and 2011 and followed them until clinical and radiographic union. Multivariable predictors of osteonecrosis included fracture displacement (P = 0.01) and fracture location (P = 0.02). In their study population of 70 patients, 20 patients (29%) developed AVN. When stratifying by fracture classification, 60% of type I fractures developed AVN, 35% of type II, 20% of type III, and 0% of the type IV. Notably, 95% of the cases of osteonecrosis occurred after displaced fractures. The rate of AVN for displaced fractures was 35%, and only 6% for nondisplaced fractures.

*Patterson et al. published a 2018 JAAOS review on the management of pediatric femoral neck fractures.* The ultimate goal of treatment is expedient and anatomic reduction and fracture fixation. Although early reduction of adult hip fractures improves outcomes, the effect of early versus late fracture reduction on outcomes in children remains unclear. Although limited and contradicting evidence exists in the literature concerning the timing of fixation, anatomic reduction should occur as soon as possible. Care must to taken to avoid posterior perforation or screw placement in the anterolateral quadrant of the epiphysis to reduce the risk of iatrogenic vascular injury. Physeal-sparing fixation methods include transphyseal fixation with smooth wires or placement of screws that do not cross the physis; however stable fracture fixation should not be compromised to spare the physis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 3, 4, 5: Post-traumatic AVN in pediatric femoral neck fractures are secondary to disrupted branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery.
