## Question

A 32-year-old male suffers a right distal biceps tendon avulsion while doing biceps curls. He subsequently undergoes right distal biceps tendon repair with a single incision approach. At the 2-week postoperative appointment, the patient reports that the back of his right hand is numb. On exam, the radial aspect of the dorsum of the right hand has decreased sensation compared to the contralateral extremity. What is the most accurate description of the anatomical relationship of the injured nerve to the biceps tendon at the level of the antecubital fossa?

## Options

1. Medial (ulnar)
2. Lateral (radial)
3. Deep
4. Directly superficial
5. Proximal

## Correct option

**2. Lateral (radial)**

## Answer explanation

Regardless of the surgical approach chosen to address a distal biceps tendon avulsion, the antecubital fossa anatomy must be well understood. The superficial radial nerve lies lateral to the biceps tendon at the level of the antecubital fossa.

Distal biceps avulsion injuries typically occur due to excessive eccentric contraction of the biceps brachii. Surgical repair is recommended in young healthy patients who do not want a decrease in functional strength. Both the single incision and dual incision techniques involve dissection in the antecubital fossa. The antecubital fossa is defined by the pronator teres ulnarly and the brachioradialis radially. The radial nerve is lateral to the biceps tendon and bifurcates into its deep motor and superficial branch at the level of the antecubital fossa. The median nerve and brachial artery are medial to the tendon at this level. Shortly after the radial artery separates from the brachial artery, the radial recurrent branch travels superficial to the biceps tendon. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve lies superficial in the subcutaneous tissue and is the nerve most likely injured during a biceps tendon repair. The superficial radial nerve is the second most common nerve injury.

*Eames et al. published a cadaveric study of 17 cadaveric elbows that were dissected to identify the details of the distal biceps tendon and the lacertus fibrosus.* *In 10 of the 17 specimens, the distal biceps had 2 distinct tendons at that level.* **The authors also highlight** the above-mentioned anatomy regarding the pertinent locations of important neurovascular structures.

*Cho et al. published an anatomic study of 25 cadaveric elbows.* In 40% of their specimens, the biceps tendon was 2 distinct tendons at the insertion. **They also highlight** that the short head of the biceps inserts anterior inferior while the long head of the biceps inserts posterior superior on the radial tuberosity. **The authors recommend** further studies to evaluate the clinical significance of the anatomic orientation of the tendon during surgical repair.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: The median nerve and brachial artery are medial to the biceps tendon at the level of the antecubital fossa.
Answers 3 and 4: The superficial radial nerve is lateral and superficial to the biceps tendon at the level of the antecubital fossa.
Answer 5: Proximal to the antecubital fossa, the radial nerve has yet to bifurcate into the deep and superficial branch.
