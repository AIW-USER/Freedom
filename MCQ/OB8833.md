## Question

A 15-year-old boy has recently joined an athletic team and is complaining of constant and progressive pain in the diaphysis of his left tibia. He describes the pain to be worse at night, but mostly relieved with anti-inflammatory medication. Radiographs are shown in Figure A. What would be the most appropriate next step in management?

![[q8833-a-143945687-5e1fc1ddef-z.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Referral to a tertiary oncology specialized centre
2. Biopsy lesion
3. Repeat imaging in 3 months
4. Chest radiograph
5. Fine-cut CT scan

## Correct option

**5. Fine-cut CT scan**

## Answer explanation

This patient has presented with clinical and radiographic features consistent with a cortical stress fracture or benign active bone pathology. A fine cut CT scan would help to best differentiate between these pathologic processes.

The most common painful benign active lesions found in the tibia is osteoid osteoma. It is not uncommon for these lesions to be misdiagnosed as stress fractures in active children and teenagers. Osteoid osteoma can most effectively be differentiated from a stress fracture with high-resolution CT scan. A systemic work-up is not required.

*Bilchik et al. reviewed osteoid osteoma.* **They state** that cross-sectional imaging with a CT scan is the study of choice. MRI is usually not recommended as it can mimic aggressive lesions due to the surrounding bone edema.

Figure A shows a AP and lateral radiograph of the tibia showing a focal area of bone reactivity. Illustration A shows a CT axial cut of the lesion showing a nidus with surrounding bone sclerosis. This is pathognomonic for osteoid osteoma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Referrals to a specialty centre should occur after appropriate work-up and if the lesion is malignant or shows benign aggressive features.
Answer 2: A biopsy should never be performed without first obtaining cross-sectional imaging for planning principles.
Answer 3: Repeating imaging in 3 months may be appropriate if the patient was improving or symptoms were stable. A progressive pain history with correlating pathology on radiographs should be investigated with advanced imaging.
Answer 4: A chest radiograph would be needed as part of the systemic work up, if the lesion had malignant features or the benign aggressive lesion was known to metastasize to the lung (e.g. giant cell tumour).
