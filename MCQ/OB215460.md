## Question

A 5-year-old budding orthopedic surgeon was helping his father around the house repairing wood furniture when his thumb was crushed with a hammer. He sustained a nailbed injury with an obvious displaced nailplate deformity. The urgent care facility read the radiographs (Figure A) as normal and referred the patient to the local hand surgeon as an outpatient. What is the anatomic cause of the deformity seen in the Figure?

![[q215460-a-seymour.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal epiphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar metaphysis
2. The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal epiphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar epiphysis
3. The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal metaphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar metaphysis
4. The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal metaphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar epiphysis
5. The urgent care doctor is correct to say there is no acute pathology on this radiograph

## Correct option

**1. The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal epiphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar metaphysis**

## Answer explanation

A Seymour fracture displaces because of the extensor tendon's insertion proximal to the physis, on the epiphysis, and the flexor tendon's insertion distal to the physis, on the metaphysis. These opposing insertion points cause a flexion moment at the physis.

Seymour fractures are open, hyperflexion, physeal injuries, often with interposed nailbed, and are frequently undiagnosed and mismanaged which can lead to osteomyelitis, nail deformity, and permanent physeal injury. While the AP radiograph may look normal, the lateral is typically flexed because of the insertion of the extensor and flexor tendons. A widened physis may be indicative of interposed soft tissue at the physis. Treatment includes removal of interposed soft tissue at the fracture site, reduction (with or without pinning), irrigation and debridement, and nail bed repair.

*Inglefield et al reviewed 22 pediatric nailbed injuries.* All achieved normal nail growth, 91% achieved a "good" result, and most parents were happy with the result. **The authors recommended** the repair of every nail bed injury.

*Pearce and Colville reviewed 46 pediatric nailbed injuries, the majority of which were caused by way of a door, and most frequently to the middle finger.* Most repairs were done under general anesthetic using absorbable sutures within 24 hours of the injury. After a 15-month follow-up, there was a low (7%) complication rate and a high satisfaction rate (8.9/10), and none required additional surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2-4: The extensor tendon inserts on the dorsal epiphysis, the flexor tendon inserts on the volar metaphysis, which causes a flexion moment at the physis.
Answer 5: The radiograph demonstrates a lateral radiograph of a finger, in a skeletally immature patient, with a displaced physeal fracture of the distal phalanx.
