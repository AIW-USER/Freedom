## Question

A 50-year-old female with type II diabetes is admitted to the hospital for bacteremia. Two days later, she begins to complain of left knee pain for which you were consulted. She is confused but denies any injury to the knee. She is given adequate fluid resuscitation but her blood pressure is currently 80/50. On examination, her knee is red and hot to touch, and motion is limited secondary to pain. Her labs include a HbA1C of 9.0, ESR 40, CRP 10, WBC of 13k, lactate of 3mmol/L,. Her knee is aspirated and blood cultures are sent. Which of the following is the most likely microorganism to be present?

## Answer explanation

This patient is in septic shock secondary to bacteremia and subsequently developed septic arthropathy; a knee aspiration was appropriately performed. Staph aureus (Figure A) is the most likely organism to be present in septic arthropathy.

Septic shock currently refers to a state of acute circulatory failure associated with infection. The knee has clinical signs of infection and was appropriately aspirated, this should be followed immediately by the administration of broad-spectrum IV antibiotics. Patients with septic shock often present with persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) =/> 65 mmHg and have a serum lactate level > 2mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate volume resuscitation. Unfortunately, with these criteria, hospital mortality is in excess of 40%. Staph aureus is the most common organism present in septic arthropathy and this patient will benefit from surgical intervention.

*Shanker-Hari et al. performed a systematic review to developed a new definition and clinical criteria for identifying septic shock in adults.* **They identified** 44 studies reporting on septic shock outcomes and found that septic shock is defined as a subset of sepsis in which underlying circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities are associated with a greater risk of mortality than sepsis alone. **They concluded** that adult patients with septic shock can be identified using the clinical criteria of hypotension requiring vasopressor therapy to maintain mean BP >65 mm Hg and having a serum lactate level >2 mmol/L after adequate fluid resuscitation.

*Horowitz et al. reviewed the diagnosis and treatment of infectious arthritis.* **They report** that synovial fluid WBC in patients with septic arthritis is usually >50,000 per mm3 and that almost any microorganism can be pathogenic in septic arthritis; however, septic arthritis is caused by nongonococcal pathogens (most commonly Staphylococcus species) in >80% of patients. **They concluded** that the evacuation of purulent material with arthrocentesis or surgical methods is necessary.

Figure A is the gram stain of S. aureus (gram-positive organism that is non- motile, non-sporing presenting in a "bunch of grapes" configuration). Figure B is Neisseria gonorrhea (gram-negative diplococci). Figure C is a gram-negative bacillus (E coli). Figure D is streptococcus pyogenes (Group A, gram-positive, non-motile and non-sporing cocci). Figure E is pseudomonas aeruginosa (encapsulated, gram-negative, rod-shaped bacterium).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: N. gonorrhea accounts for ~20% of cases of septic arthritis and is the most common organism in otherwise healthy sexually active adolescents and young adults.
Answer 3: Gram-negative bacilli account for 10-20% of cases. Pathogens include
E. coli, proteus, klebsiella, enterobacter. Risk factors for these pathogens include neonates, IV drug users, elderly, and immunocompromised patients with diabetes. They remain less common causes of septic arthritis than S. aureus.
Answer 4: Strep pyogenes infections are less common causes of septic arthritis than S. aureus
Answer 5: Pseudomonas aeruginosa is commonly seen in patients with a history of IV drug abuse. It remains a less common cause of septic arthritis than S. aureus.
