## Question

A patient with spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy is followed with routine hip surveillance radiographs. Progressive left hip displacement is identified and she undergoes left hip reconstruction consisting of varus derotational femoral osteotomy and soft-tissue releases, with the addition of a left acetabuloplasty, as shown in Figure 1. At 18-month follow-up, surveillance radiographs demonstrate stable hip containment. Which preoperative factor most strongly supported the decision to add an acetabuloplasty to the left hip reconstruction?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Patient age less than 6 years at time of surgery
2. Preoperative acetabular index greater than 25 degrees
3. Gross Motor Function Classification System level V
4. Preoperative neck-shaft angle greater than 160 degrees
5. Prior botulinum toxin injections

## Correct option

**2. Preoperative acetabular index greater than 25 degrees**

## Answer explanation

The patient undergoes combined femoral osteotomy and acetabuloplasty for neuromuscular hip dysplasia. The decision to add an acetabuloplasty in this patient was most strongly supported by the presence of acetabular dysplasia with a preoperative acetabular index greater than 25 degrees (Answer 2).

Neuromuscular hip dysplasia in cerebral palsy results from muscle imbalance and abnormal tone that disrupt regular weight-bearing forces required for proper hip development, leading to progressive femoral head subluxation from the acetabulum. The incidence correlates directly with motor impairment severity, affecting up to 90% of children at GMFCS level V, and can compromise quality of life through pain, positioning difficulties, and reduced mobility if left untreated. In children with cerebral palsy undergoing hip reconstruction, an acetabular index exceeding 25 degrees is a well-established indication for pericapsular acetabuloplasty, as residual acetabular dysplasia is a known risk factor for failure of isolated femoral reconstruction. Severe hip subluxation, typically defined by a migration percentage greater than 40–50%, further supports the use of combined femoral and pelvic osteotomies, which have been shown to reduce recurrent subluxation and improve long-term hip containment compared with femoral osteotomy alone.

*Minaie et al retrospectively studied 291 hips in 179 children with cerebral palsy who underwent hip reconstruction and* **found** a 13.1% failure rate (defined as revision surgery or migration percentage >50% at follow-up). Age under 6 years at surgery significantly increased the risk of failure (26% vs. 6.3%, P<0.001), as did preoperative migration percentage >70% (28.9% vs. 9.9%, P<0.001). **They concluded** that the addition of an acetabular osteotomy was protective against failure (9.1% vs. 16.9%, P=0.048), notably when preoperative acetabular index exceeded 25 degrees.

*Axt and Wadley* **found** that adding acetabuloplasty (Dega osteotomy) to femoral varus derotation osteotomy (VDRO) in children with cerebral palsy and unstable hips resulted in significantly higher rates of midterm hip stability. At final follow-up (mean 49.1 months), 83% of hips were stable in the VDRO + acetabuloplasty group compared to only 54% in the VDRO-alone group. The odds of hip stability at final follow-up were 3.9 times higher in the combined procedure group (95% CI: 1.5-9.7, p=0.004). Migration percentage improved from 64.4% preoperatively to 19.3% at final follow-up in the combined group, compared to 61% to 35.7% in the VDRO-alone group.

*Zakrzewski et al. performed a retrospective study of 197 hips in 108 patients with cerebral palsy.* Over-containment (defined as postoperative migration percentage ≤0%) resulted in significantly lower failure rates in high-risk subgroups. Specifically, over-containment reduced failure rates in GMFCS IV/V hips, in children under age six at surgery, and in those with preoperative migration percentage >50%. While the overall failure rate trended lower in the over-containment group (6.1% vs. 14.0%), this did not reach statistical significance for the entire cohort. **The authors concluded** that over-containment should be considered in hips at high risk of failure, especially nonambulatory children with significant hip subluxation at an early age.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Younger age is a known risk factor for recurrence after neuromuscular hip reconstruction, but is not, by itself, an indication for acetabuloplasty.
Answer 3: A high GMFCS level increases the risk of hip displacement and reconstruction failure but does not independently mandate a pelvic osteotomy.
Answer 4: An increased neck–shaft angle is addressed with varus derotational femoral osteotomy rather than acetabuloplasty.
Answer 5: Prior botulinum toxin injections have not been shown to protect against or predispose to recurrent hip subluxation.
