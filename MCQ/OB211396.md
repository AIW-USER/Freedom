## Question

A healthy 66-year-old female with no significant medical co-morbidities presents to your clinic with long-standing right ankle pain. She enjoys hiking and tries to stay active. She has tried NSAIDs, physical therapy and bracing, none of which provide much relief. Intra-articular injection of corticosteroid and lidocaine did alleviate some of her symptoms albeit temporarily. On exam, she has dorsiflexion to 5°, plantarflexion to 15°, inversion to 10°, and eversion to 10°. She has no pain with subtalar motion but significant pain with tibiotalar motion. Figures A-C are her most recent ankle Xrays. What is the optimal surgical option to maximize functional outcomes and return to comfortable gait on uneven surfaces for this patient?

## Options

1. Tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis with hindfoot fusion nail
2. Total ankle arthroplasty
3. Tibiotalar arthrodesis
4. Distraction arthroplasty
5. Arthroscopic chondroplasty

## Correct option

**2. Total ankle arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

Elderly patients with functionally limiting ankle pain often do well with total ankle arthroplasty (TAA).

Total ankle arthroplasty has improved over time with current implant survivorship around 90% at 10 years. This procedure is a motion-preserving procedure, allowing for a more natural range of motion of the ankle post-operatively. However, it has been shown that survivorship decreases in patients less than 54 years old.

*Jastifer et al. compared ankle arthrodesis to TAA in performance on stairs, ramps and uneven surfaces.* *They had 61 TAA and 16 ankle arthrodesis patients.* TAA patients performed better in walking upstairs, downstairs and walking uphill. These patients also had improved outcomes scores post-operatively compared to ankle arthrodesis patients.

*Haddad et al. performed a systematic review to compare arthrodesis and arthroplasty at intermediate and long-term outcomes.* **They found** at both time points that TAA and ankle arthrodesis patients perform very similarly in regards to ankle-hindfoot scale score, excellent and good outcome percentages, and revision rates. No significant differences were identified.

*Hahn et al. compared TAA to ankle arthrodesis using gait analysis.* *Each group had 9 patients with both pre-operative and 12-month post-operative gait analyses.* **They found** both groups had pain reduction and improved gait post-operatively. The TAA group had a better post-operative range of motion than the ankle arthrodesis patient.

*Coester et al. followed 23 patients treated with ankle arthrodesis for post-traumatic arthritis for a mean of 22 years.* These patients were found to have increased ipsilateral midfoot and subtalar pain, and foot disability. **They also found** that as foot pain and disability increased, there was an association with increased difficulty with activity and knee dysfunction on the ipsilateral side. Radiographically ipsilateral joints showed more degeneration compared to contralateral joints.

Figures A-C are the AP, mortise and lateral of this patient. Note the decreased joint space and subchondral sclerosis of the bone.

Illustrations A and B are the postoperative AP and lateral fluoroscopic images for this patient showing the good position of the tibial and talar components with no coronal malalignment.

Incorrect answer explanation:
Answer 1: Ankle arthrodesis would be a better option in a younger patient. Additionally, she does not have symptoms of subtalar arthritis and this joint should be spared if possible.
Answer 3: Ankle arthrodesis would be the correct option if this were a younger patient.
Answer 4: Distraction arthroplasty can be used to treat moderate ankle osteoarthritis, however, this would be a better option in a younger patient.
Answer 5: This would likely not alleviate the patient's symptoms
