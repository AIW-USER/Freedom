## Question

You performed an aTSA in a 67-year-old male 6 months ago and the patient follows up in your office with complaints of new shoulder pain and popping. He feels like his shoulder clicks with overhead range of motion. On examination there is a palpable click with forward elevation. He has 80º of external rotation in adduction and 5/5 strength with empty can testing. Sensation is intact throughout the upper extremity. His incision is well-healed with no signs of erythema or swelling. His axillary radiograph is seen in Figure A. What is the most likely complication which has occurred?

![[q217830-a-subscap-tear-atsa.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Infection
2. Glenoid loosening
3. Supraspinatus tear
4. Subscapularis failure
5. Axillary neuropraxia

## Correct option

**4. Subscapularis failure**

## Answer explanation

Failure of the subscapularis tendon is likely given examination findings and anterior subluxation of the humeral component on axillary radiograph.

Subscapularis failure is a well-described complication after anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA). The subscapularis is detached during the initial deltopectoral approach to the shoulder and is then repaired at the end of the case. There are different methods of detaching the subscapularis, including a peel, tenotomy, and lesser tuberosity osteotomy. Despite the type of approach, subscapularis failure is a feared complication, as the glenohumeral joint loses its anterior-posterior force couple. Anterior subluxation or gross dislocation can be seen on an axillary radiograph. In some cases, if acute, revision subscapularis repair or augmentation may be performed but otherwise, revision to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty may be required.

*Gerber et al. evaluated subscapularis muscle function and structure after aTSA by way of a lesser tuberosity osteotomy.* **They noted** that the osteotomized tuberosity fragment healed in all 39 shoulders. Interestingly, however, fatty infiltration of the subscapularis increased in most shoulders although patients still had good function.

*Caplan et al. looked at subscapularis function after tendon repair in 45 aTSAs.* *All patients had a negative belly-press test, and 41/45 had a negative lift-off test.* **They concluded** that tendon-to-tendon repair was simpler and quicker than lesser tuberosity osteotomy and decreased the risk for nonunion.

*Buckley et al. performed a comparative analysis of subscapularis integrity/function after lesser tuberosity osteotomy compared to subscapularis peel in aTSA.* **They noted** that subscapularis peel patients had a greater degree of external rotation after repair, while belly press and constant scores were not different. They did find that there were 4 abnormal subscapularis tendons in the peel group and none in the osteotomy group, with the patients with abnormalities having inferior outcomes compared to those who had intact tendons.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There are no signs of infection on examination; rather, his exam and radiographs reflect a tear of the subscapularis.
Answer 2: There is no evidence of glenoid loosening on radiographs or based on the history/examination.
Answer 3: Empty can strength is 5/5, suggestive of an intact supraspinatus.
Answer 5: Sensation over the entire upper extremity is intact which would likely not be the case if there were an axillary neuropraxia. Additionally, the patient may have some weakness in abduction given deltoid innervation of the axillary nerve.
