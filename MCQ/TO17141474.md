Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Mallet Finger

## Question

A case of Mallet finger with partial articular avulsion with no subluxation has come. The ideal management for the case would be (FNB 2022)

## Options

1. Buddy splint
2. ORIF
3. Splinting of distal phalanx in extension
4. Extension block splinting of PIP

## Correct option

**3. Splinting of distal phalanx in extension**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C

A MALLET FINGER is a deformity caused by disruption of the terminal extensor tendon distal to DIP joint. It’s a zone 1 injury as per the Kleinert and Verdant classification of extensor tendon injuries, mostly involving sports persons playing ball sports.

Mostly the dominant hand is involved and middle finger > ring finger are most commonly involved.

The injury is usually caused by a traumatic sudden forced flexion blow to the tip of an extended finger such that the DIP is forced into sudden flexion.

On examination, one classically finds the finger resting in 45o flexion with loss of active DIP extension.

Treatment is dictated by the degree of displacement and acuity of injury.

Acute injuries (< 12 weeks) with minimal displacement and no joint subluxation are treated with extension bracing (volar splinting has fewer complications than dorsal splinting) for 6-8 weeks (24 hours a day). A trial of splinting may also be given for chronic injuries (> 12 weeks old) with a supple, congruent and non-arthritic joint.

ORIF or closed reduction and percutaneous fixation (simple k wire fixation < dorsal extension block pinning by Ishiguro technique) is indicated for chronic injuries that fail splinting trial or acute injuries with volar displacement of the distal phalanx, a >2mm articular step-off, or when a majority (>50%) of the articular surface is involved. with a non-displaced bony mallet finger without joint subluxation. Open injuries also need fixation.

A DIP arthrodesis is preferred when the joint is stiff, painful and arthritic.

A slight residual extensor lag of < 10° may be present in up to 40% after either closed or operative treatment, however, it hardly results in a functional deficit

Some chronic cases land up with a swan neck deformity that occurs due to retracted terminal insertion leading to proportionally excessive pull on P2 from central slip plus attenuation of the PIP volar plate and a dorsal subluxation of lateral bands resulting PIP hyperextension. A contracture of triangular ligament ensues that then maintains the deformity. Such cases may require a Fowler’s central slip tenotomy and lateral band tenodesis.
