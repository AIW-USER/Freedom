## Question

A 34-year-old Olympic sand volleyball player presents to your office complaining of recurrent ankle inversion injuries and feelings of instability. On exam, he has a positive anterior drawer test and tenderness laterally over the anterior talofibular (ATFL) and calcaneofibular (CFL) ligaments. External rotation stress test is negative. Weightbearing view of the foot is seen in Figure A. Coleman block test is negative. He has failed nonoperative management and you recommend a lateral ankle ligament reconstruction. Which of the following would most decrease his risk of recurrent ankle instability?

![[q211968-a-hindfoot-varus-clinical-pic.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy
2. Syndesmotic reduction and fixation
3. Postoperative therapy focused on proprioceptive exercises and peroneal strengthening
4. Use of an ankle orthosis with sporting activities postoperatively
5. Reinforcement of ligament repair with inferior extensor retinaculum

## Correct option

**1. Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with clinical features of recurrent lateral ankle instability in the setting of a cavovarus foot, which should be corrected at the time of surgery with a lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy.

Chronic lateral ankle instability is common after multiple inversion ankle injuries and is usually associated with pain, instability, and persistent swelling over the lateral ankle. Initial treatment is supportive with rest, bracing, and physical therapy. When nonoperative management fails, a modified Brostrom-Gould lateral ankle reconstruction is a reliable surgical option to tighten the ligamentous structures and decrease ankle laxity. Associated peroneal pathology and hindfoot alignment should be considered. In the setting of cavovarus foot alignment, a lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy should be performed at the time of the Brostrom procedure to take stress off the repair and decrease the risk of failure.

*Maffulli et al. review the management of ankle instability in both acute and chronic settings.* **They recommend** initial nonsurgical management with functional rehabilitation for acute injuries, with surgery more likely indicated if these measures fail. They finish by saying, in addition to standard ankle arthroscopy, anatomic ligament repair is preferred, but reconstruction is acceptable if tissue quality is poor.

*Philbin et al. review peroneal tendon injuries,* **noting** their common association with chronic lateral ankle instability. They review the spectrum of peroneal pathology and the different indications for nonoperative and operative management, including specific guidelines for intraoperative decision-making. **They conclude** that if these guidelines are followed, patients normally have good outcomes postoperatively.

Figure A shows a clinical image representative of a cavovarus foot.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: External rotation stress test was negative, and pain was primarily laterally over the ligamentous complex, indicating no syndesmotic injury. Therefore, syndesmotic fixation would not be indicated.
Answers 3-5: These may all be useful tools in improving outcomes and patient satisfaction but are less important in this case than correcting the patient's cavovarus foot alignment to neutral with a calcaneal osteotomy.
