## Question

A 65-year-old female presents for evaluation of a 1-year history of neck pain. She has a history of C6-C7 anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) performed 12 years ago and was doing well until last year. She describes her pain as a dull ache which is made worse by flexion and rotation of her cervical spine without radiation to the arms. Her current imaging is shown in Figure A. Her flexion and extension imaging does not show any listhesis and her MRI studies reveal mild cervical stenosis at C4-C5 and C5-C6 without evidence of cord compression. Her neurologic examination is normal and she has a normal gait with no difficulties with fine motor activities. Which of the following is the next best step in management?

![[q1688-a-lateral-acdf-c6-c7.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Removal of hardware
2. Revision C6-C7 ACDF
3. Posterior instrumentation and fusion C4-C7
4. Epidural steroid injection
5. Patient education and physical therapy

## Correct option

**5. Patient education and physical therapy**

## Answer explanation

The patient has evidence of axial neck pain without adjacent segment disease, myelopathy or other neurological deficits. The next best step in management would be a conservative course consisting of patient education, physical therapy (PT) and non-narcotic pain medication.

Axial neck pain can be difficult to manage. Pain management is not always successful, and surgical approaches may provide disappointing results unless there is discrete pathology which in the patient vignette is unclear. Whereas planning of a surgical approach should consider prior approaches and preexisting laryngeal dysfunction, no compelling case for surgical intervention can be made for this patient. Therefore, management should consist of patient education, exercise, and nonnarcotic medication.

*Ahn et al. describe the course of patients with axial neck pain and cervical radiculopathy.* **They note** that the majority of patients can be managed conservatively and surgical intervention is considered only when conservative management has failed unless the neurologic deficits are very significant. Surgical intervention involves a complete understanding of the disease process both from physical examination and from radiographic studies. **The authors conclude** that if surgery is undertaken without appropriate clinical correlation, poor results often occur.

*Algers et al. reviewed 20 patients who underwent ACDF due to chronic symptoms after whiplash injury with 4 years of follow-up.* The commonest indications for surgery were disabling headache and neck pain and radiographically verified disc protrusion. At follow-up, 11 patients had reduced headache and neck pain, while paresthesia and radicular pain were diminished in 9 patients. **The authors noted** that based on Robinson's criteria for the evaluation of surgical results, 2 patients had good, 9 had fair and 9 had poor results. **The authors concluded** that surgical intervention should only be undertaken in patients that meet appropriate surgical criteria.

Figure A shows a solid arthrodesis of C6-C7 without evidence of adjacent segment disease or spinal malalignment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient will likely not obtain any pain relief from hardware removal without any indication of symptomatic hardware.
Answer 2: The patient has achieved a solid arthrodesis so revision ACDF would be of no use.
Answer 3: There is no evidence of cervical instability, neurological deficits or C6-C7 non-union so there is no indication for a PSIF.
Answer 4: Given the absence of radiculopathy, the patient is unlikely to benefit from an isolated epidural steroid injection.
