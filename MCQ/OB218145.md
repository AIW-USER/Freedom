## Question

In adult traumatic brachial plexus injuries, it is important to distinguish whether the injury is considered pre-ganglionic versus post-ganglionic. Both have differing implications on the overall prognosis, timing of surgery, and treatment modalities available. Which of the following is most suggestive of a post-ganglionic adult traumatic brachial plexus injury?

## Options

1. Involvement of the rhomboids and/or serratus anterior musculature
2. CT myelogram showing pseudomeningoceles of involved levels
3. Advancing Tinel sign along the supraclavicular region
4. Diaphragm paralysis on the affected side
5. Meiosis and drooping eyelid on the affected side

## Correct option

**3. Advancing Tinel sign along the supraclavicular region**

## Answer explanation

As an injured nerve regenerates, the Tinel sign will progress along the course of the nerve, representing the advancing front of regenerative nerve fibers. This finding therefore implies a high rate of recovery and is only seen in post-ganglionic brachial plexus nerve injuries (Answer 3).

In the setting of adult traumatic brachial plexus injuries, post-ganglionic involvement entails nerve disruption distal to the dorsal root ganglion (Illustration A). These injuries retain the capability of at least partial recovery and therefore a delay in surgery is warranted (6 weeks – 6 months) to allow for definitive demarcation and partial recovery to occur. However, it is imperative to perform nerve grafting/transfer procedures within the first year of injury (i.e. prior to motor end plate degeneration). Post-ganglionic injuries may be treated with direct nerve repair, however, most likely require an interposition sural nerve autograft to gap the defect after debridement of nonviable nerve endings. With the leaps made in peripheral nerve surgery, many surgeons will now primarily treat these injuries with peripheral nerve transfers as well, or in combination with, the aforementioned sural nerve grafting.

Pre-ganglionic injuries involve avulsions of the rootlet from the spinal cord and lack any clinically significant recovery potential (Illustration A). Given this lack of regenerative potential, early exploration and reconstruction (within 6 weeks after injury) is indicated. Unfortunately, primary repair or interposition nerve grafting procedures are futile due to the lack of root continuity with the spinal cord. These injuries are often treated with peripheral nerve transfers using viable donors (ulnar fascicle transfer to the musculocutaneous nerve for C5/6 root avulsions) or free functional muscle transfer (pan-plexus injuries).

*Noland and colleagues provided an overview of the management of adult traumatic brachial plexus injuries.* **They highlight** the importance of delineating pre- and post-ganglionic injuries as they have notable prognostic and treatment implications. They also reviewed the various operative procedures available noting that each patient requires individualized treatment tailored to their specific injury and goals of care. **The authors conclude** that adult traumatic brachial plexus injuries can be devastating to patients and require honest discussion regarding expectations with surgical reconstruction.

*Giuffre and colleagues provided an overview of the treatment of adult brachial plexus injuries.* **They highlight** that the restoration of function in upper trunk injuries (C5/6; shoulder and elbow function) with surgery has much more favorable outcomes than the treatment for lower trunk injuries (C8/T1; hand intrinsic function). **The authors highlight** various treatment options and conclude that the goal is to improve the patient’s function by providing elbow flexion, shoulder stability, grasp function, and hand sensibility.

Fox and Mackinnon provided an overview of adult peripheral nerve disorders, including brachial plexus injuries. The authors discuss their well-illustrated techniques for the treatment of common peripheral nerve injuries that are applicable in the treatment of brachial plexus injuries as well. Throughout their work, the authors highlight the importance of surgeon knowledge of individual nerves, the nerve topography, and the pathophysiology of nerve injury in determining the most appropriate treatment for each patient.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The rhomboids are innervated by the dorsal scapular nerve (C4/5), which is the most proximal branch off of the C5 root (proximal to the brachial plexus trunks, divisions, cords, or individual peripheral nerves). Therefore, rhomboid weakness is highly associated with pre-ganglionic avulsion injuries. The serratus anterior has a similar proximal branching innervation (via the Long Thoracic nerve) and is also suggestive of pre-ganglionic avulsion injuries with resultant medial scapular winging.
Answer 2: Pseudomeningoceles are cerebral spinal fluid collections extending from the central spinal canal into the peripheral soft tissues. They are highly associated (>95% predictive) with pre-ganglionic root avulsions.
Answer 4: Diaphragm paralysis, which can be appreciated on chest radiographs, may occur in the setting of upper brachial plexus (C5) avulsions. This is because the phrenic nerve involves direct innervation from the C3-C5 roots and therefore is suggestive of a high (pre-ganglionic) avulsion.
Answer 5: Horner syndrome is an injury to the sympathetic chain, which may occur concomitantly with lower root (C8-T1) avulsions. It is highly associated with pre-ganglionic avulsion injuries.
