## Options

1. Autosomal dominant mutation in the NF1 gene on 17q11.2
2. Autosomal dominant mutation in the NF1 gene on 11q17.2
3. Autosomal recessive mutation in the NF1 gene on 17q11.2
4. Autosomal recessive mutation in the NF1 gene on 11q17.2
5. This condition is not secondary to an underlying genetic aberration

## Correct option

**1. Autosomal dominant mutation in the NF1 gene on 17q11.2**

## Answer explanation

This patient with cafe-au-lait spots, anterolateral bowing of the tibia and Lisch nodules (Figure A), with a family history of surgically-treated scoliosis, most likely has Neurofibromatosis Type I, caused by an autosomal dominant mutation in the NF1 gene on chromosome 17q11.2 (Answer 1).

Neurofibromatosis Type I (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a mutation in the NF1 gene on chromosome 17q11.2 that codes for the neurofibromin protein. This disorder typically presents with skin lesions, upper/lower extremity deformities, and scoliosis. Overall, there are three major subtypes of neurofibromatosis: Type I (also known as von Recklinghausen disease), Type II (NF2), and Segmental. Patients with NF1 often suffer from scoliosis, as the spine is the most common site of skeletal involvement in NF1. Scoliosis in NF1 can be dystrophic and non-dystrophic. Dystrophic scoliosis is typically thoracic, short-segmented (4-6 vertebrae), and has a sharp curve. Non-dystrophic resembles Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis. Patients with NF2 rarely, if ever, have spinal involvement, but are at risk for the development of bilateral vestibular schwannomas. Approximately 1:3,000 births have a mutation in the NF1 gene and the diagnosis of NF1 is made according to the NIH Consensus Development Conference Statement from 1987 (Illustration A).

*Gutmann et al. provide a comprehensive review of NF1.* **They note** that almost all individuals with NF1 develop skin lesions and dermal neurofibromas. **They also note** that patients with NF1 frequently develop scoliosis, and routine surveillance with radiographs and other imaging studies is required for early detection and intervention. **The authors note** that while no single treatment exists, the backbone of management is built upon early detection, complication minimization and quality of life optimization.

*Delucia et al. provide a primer for the management of NF1.* **They note** that careful attention must be paid to screening for scoliosis and tibial pseudarthrosis. **The authors highlight** that intervention is the norm, not the exception, and that 70% of patients with NF1 undergo hospitalization and/or surgery secondary to disease manifestations.

Figure A demonstrates cafe-au-lait (flat areas of hyperpigmentation) spots, anterolateral tibial bowing and Lisch nodules (benign, hyperpigmented hamartomas of the iris). Illustration A is a listing of the NIH Consensus Development Conference Statement (1987), currently used to establish the diagnosis of NF1.
