## Question

You are a spine surgeon performing an anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) at C5-6 for a 66-year-old male with symptoms of cervical myelopathy. The case takes longer than usual due to difficulty with exposure. You are concerned that the prolonged retraction of the longus coli may lead to a complication. What would be the most likely clinical presentation if this patient were to have this complication?

## Options

1. Dysphagia
2. Vocal cord paralysis
3. Abnormal vocal phonation
4. Horner's syndrome
5. Absent brachioradialis reflex

## Correct option

**4. Horner's syndrome**

## Answer explanation

Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, and anhydrosis) can be caused by damage to the cervical sympathetic chain, which lies on the lateral border of the longus coli.

The anterior approach to the cervical spine is utilized frequently to perform anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) as well as anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF). While the approach provides great access to the anterior cervical vertebral bodies and disc spaces, it also comes with a number of risks. These include damage to the trachea, esophagus, neurovascular bundle, recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) and sympathetic chain. The sympathetic chain lies posteromedial to the carotid sheath and runs along the lateral aspect of the longus coli muscles under the prevertebral fascia. Injury to the sympathetic trunk can occur with excessive retraction of the longus coli and create a Horner's syndrome, which causes ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, and enophthalmos. These symptoms are due to a relative increase in parasympathetic activity to the eye/face after injury to the sympathetic chain.

*Traynelis et al. reviewed Horner's syndrome, which they deemed a rare complication of spinal surgery.* *They reviewed the records of 8,887 patients who underwent anterior cervical spine surgery and Horner's syndrome was only identified in 5 patients (0.06%).* All occurred during an ACDF and all involved an operation on the C5 or C6 levels, indicating that the sympathetic chain may be most vulnerable in this position.

*Yee et al. reviewed complications of spine surgery using an anterior approach.* *In reviewing 240 articles,* **they concluded** the most common complications were: adjacent segment disease (8.1%), dysphagia (5.3% and C5 nerve palsy (3%), with Horner's syndrome having a rate of 0.4%. **They concluded** that while most of these complications occur infrequently, it is important to have an understanding of the complex neck anatomy to avoid injury to vital structures.

*Lubelski et al. published a case series and review of Horner's syndrome after ACDF.* **They noted** that out of 1,116 patients at their institution who underwent ACDF, the rate of Horner's syndrome was 0.45% and C4-5/C5-6 were the most common levels involved. They did note that all patients saw at least partial improvement within 6 months to a year postoperatively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Dysphagia is a common side effect after ACDF (usually at higher levels), as the esophagus is being retracted, but does not occur directly as a result of longus coli compression.
Answer 2: Vocal cord paralysis would be related to recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury, which typically occurs secondary to aggressive retraction at the tracheoesophageal junction.
Answer 3: Injury to the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) is less common, but can relate in hoarseness, difficulty breathing and abnormal vocal phonation.
Answer 5: The brachioradialis reflex would be absent with injury to the C6 nerve root, which would not occur secondary to retraction of the longus coli at the midline.
