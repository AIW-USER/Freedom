## Question

An 11-year-old male presents to the emergency department after injuring his right knee when he collided with another player at basketball practice. His mother reports that he has had a cold for the past 3 days, but is otherwise healthy. He has been unable to bear weight following the injury. On exam, he has pain localized over the distal femoral physis, a palpable effusion, and moderate discomfort as you take his knee through a range of motion. Distally, he is neurovascularly intact. Initial workup reveals a temperature of 98.5°F, WBC 9k, CRP 1.7 mg/dL, and an ESR of 33. A radiograph obtained in the ED is shown in Figure 1. Which of the following is the best next step in treatment?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Knee aspiration
2. MRI
3. NSAID challenge
4. Hinged knee brace and physical therapy
5. Open reduction internal fixation

## Correct option

**2. MRI**

## Answer explanation

This patient has acute onset right knee pain following a basketball injury and is unable to bear weight. Of the options, MRI is the best next step to evaluate for physeal and ligamentous injury.

Distal femoral physeal fractures constitute 2-5% of all physeal injuries and are most commonly Salter-Harris II type injuries. Obvious deformities are present in patients with displaced fractures, however, nondisplaced injuries often present with focal tenderness, swelling, and an inability to bear weight. The use of stress radiographs for the diagnosis of nondisplaced Salter-Harris I fractures has fallen out of favor because of patient discomfort and the potential need for sedation. When the diagnosis cannot be made on plain radiographs, an MRI may be used to confirm the diagnosis and to further evaluate the soft tissues about the knee.

*Zionts et al review the diagnosis and management of fractures about the knee in children.* **They report** that an MRI can be especially helpful for the diagnosis of nondisplaced distal femoral physeal fractures in patients who have a preceding traumatic event, swelling and an inability to bear weight. **They conclude** that fractures about the knee in children differ than those in adults and an emphasis should be placed on close follow up and the early evaluation and treatment of any potential complications.

*Mayer et al review pediatric knee dislocations and physeal fractures about the knee.* **They report** that stress radiographs of the distal femur have fallen out of favor, primarily due to discomfort and the need for sedation. **They conclude** that if plain radiographs are normal and the surgeon has concern for physeal injury, an MRI is a prudent next step. However, given the cost and potential need for sedation to obtain the MRI, it should not be routinely ordered as part of the initial imaging series.

Figures 1 is the lateral radiograph of the right knee in a skeletally immature patient that shows no obvious fractures, but a large knee effusion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient had a clear traumatic event, that after, he was unable to bear weight. Additionally, his laboratory markers are not elevated enough to warrant knee aspiration to rule out infection.
Answer 3: NSAID challenge is used as the initial treatment for transient synovitis of the hip. While this may help with the patient’s pain, it will not give you a definitive diagnosis.
Answer 4: Hinged knee brace and physical therapy are not appropriate in a patient who is unable to bear weight following a traumatic event.
Answer 5: Patients with nondisplaced distal femur Salter-Harris I fractures are treated with a long leg cast, not open reduction internal fixation.
