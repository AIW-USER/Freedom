## Question

A 57-year-old male presents with worsening right ankle pain over the previous eight months. The patient has used an ankle gauntlet brace, received several corticosteroid injections, and taken scheduled NSAIDs, but his symptoms continue to worsen. Physical exam reveals limited ankle dorsiflexion and pain with plantar flexion that is limited to 20 degrees. There is no pain with ankle inversion or eversion. He does have a history of diabetes that is complicated by peripheral neuropathy. Current radiographs are depicted in figures A and B. What is the best treatment option for this patient?

## Options

1. Continued bracing and steroid injections
2. Total ankle arthroplasty
3. Ankle arthroscopy with debridement
4. Tibiotalar arthrodesis
5. Subtalar arthrodesis

## Correct option

**4. Tibiotalar arthrodesis**

## Answer explanation

The patient has end-stage tibiotalar arthritis that is complicated by peripheral neuropathy. The best treatment option for this patient would be a tibiotalar arthrodesis.

End-stage tibiotalar arthritis is typically due to post-traumatic degeneration and accounts for >60% of cases. Treatment initially begins with conservative measures including bracing, pain medications, and shoe modifications. However, patients that remain symptomatic can be treated with total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) or ankle arthrodesis. Historically, there have been high failure rates reported with TAA, but current designs have reasonable 10-year survivorship. The theoretical benefits of TAA originate from the preservation of ankle range of motion which allows for better cadence and reduced risk of adjacent joint degeneration. However, patients with comorbidities such as peripheral neuropathy, severe peripheral vascular disease, neuropathic arthropathy, and osteonecrosis of the talus, among other conditions, are best treated with ankle arthrodesis due to increased failure rates of TAA in these patients.

*Morash et al. reviewed ankle arthrodesis and TAA for the treatment of end-stage tibiotalar arthritis.* **They reported** 75-90% survivorship of TAA at 10 years, but note that outcome can be severely limited by several patient factors. **They recommended** performing an ankle arthrodesis over a TAA in patients with underlying neuropathy due to the higher failure rates of TAA in neuropathic patients.

*Hayes et al. provided an instructional course lecture on the treatment of tibiotalar arthritis.* **The authors recommended** that initial treatment begin with bracing, shoe wear modifications, and steroid injections. **They further recommended** arthrodesis or arthroplasty for patients that continue to be symptomatic, but the inherent risks of implant loosening, failure, and adjacent joint degeneration should be weighed on a case-by-case basis.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs of the right ankle with significant joint space narrowing and subchondral sclerosis of the tibiotalar joint without significant subtalar degeneration.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The patient is continuing to worsen with the current treatment regimen.
Answer 2: The patient has underlying peripheral neuropathy, which places him at a higher risk for TAA failure.
Answer 3: An ankle arthroscopy with debridement would not likely improve the patient's symptoms significantly given the degree of arthrosis on radiographs.
Answer 5: The patient does not have radiographic or clinical subtalar arthritis. A subtalar fusion would not address the patient's complaints.
