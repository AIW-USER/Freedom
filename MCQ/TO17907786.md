## Question

## Answer explanation

Ans: B

Since the examiner has specifically mentioned the unconstrained implant, instability is the most expected issue the patient is likely to return back with.

TOTAL ELBOW ARTHROPLASTY DESIGNS

Unconstrained

- Requires competent/ intact collaterals
- Precise component alignment is must to avoid failure
- Instability is the commonest issue with this design

Semiconstrained [Classic example is Conrad Moorey Prosthesis]

- Its semiconstrained because the implant has a sloppy hinge that allows 7° of varus-valgus laxity and 7° of axial rotation
- It’s the commonest used design
- Radial head is not needed for stability
- Component loosening is least with this design. The inherent stability of the design reduces dependence on surrounding capsuloligamentous structures, and the laxity of the hinge system is thought to reduce the incidence of aseptic loosening. Ulnar component loosening is more common. However, with earlier designs humeral component used to be at risk. But in current designs it’s provided with an anterior flange to prevent loosening. Improper placement of the flange can lead to humeral loosening.
- Here also instability is the commonest mode of failure

COMPLICATIONS OF TOTAL ELBOW ARTHROPLASTY

Although complications tend to be specific to the implant design, the most notable complications of Total Elbow Arthroplasty (keeping semiconstrained as standard model) include instability, implant loosening, periprosthetic fracture, infection and wound breakdown, triceps insufficiency, and ulnar neuropathy.

Instability

Instability may be seen in 7-19% cases and is the commonest mode of failure for the commonly used semiconstrained implant. The stability of the replacement relies heavily on soft-tissue balance and ulnar collateral ligament and extensor tendon mass contribute maximum to elbow stability. In general, on table, the joint line should not separate more than 1 to 2 mm with intraoperative distraction in 90° of flexion and pronation.

In instability cases, attempts have been made to reconstruct the collateral ligaments, but most patients eventually required revision with semiconstrained hinged systems for stability.

Implant Loosening

The estimated incidence of clinically significant implant loosening is 7% to 15% that may entail a revision. Highest loosening rates are seen with constrained implants. With current designs, Ulnar component loosening is mostly seen.

Correct implant alignment is critical for limiting loosening rates. The alignment of the implant stem was described by Figgie et al17 as "implanto-medullary canal parallelism” (see Fig). The limit of displacement from the center of rotation with minimal impact on elbow kinematics is estimated to be 5 mm in all directions. According to Morrey and Sanchez-Sotelo, the axis of rotation can be estimated by using the anterior humeral cortex for AP position, the roof of the coronoid fossa for implant depth, and the condylar plane for coronal rotation. The ulnar component should lie at the center of the greater sigmoid notch, with the posterior cortex of the olecranon used for rotational alignment

Fig: Implant alignment

Impingement pistoning of the ulnar stem on elbow flexion is another source of implant loosening. A trial of motion must be carefully assessed intraoperatively, with particular attention paid to the coronoid process, protruding cement, and heterotopic ossification.

The Gruen classification, which was originally designed to evaluate femoral components in total hip arthroplasty, divides the implant into four zones (see Fig below). The periarticular zone is designated as zone 1, and the rest of the stem is divided into thirds (zones 2 through 4). A diffuse pattern of radiolucency encompasses at least three zones, and a focal pattern involves two or fewer zones.

Periprosthetic Fracture

Reported in 5-30% cases of a primary TEA.

Trauma is the commonest cause of periprosthetic fracture. Other causes include osteoporosis, aseptic loosening, stress shielding, poor technique, and patient noncompliance with activity restriction. The Mayo classification of periprosthetic elbow fracture is based on the classification for periprosthetic femoral fractures. The classification includes the site of fracture, implant fixation, and quality of bone stock.

![[TO17907786-5.jpg]]

![[TO17907786-6.jpg]]

Fig: Mayo classification; Type I, fracture of the humeral condyles and olecranon. Type II, humeral and ulnar shaft fracture along the length of the stem. Type III, fracture past the tip of the prosthesis.

Humeral condylar and olecranon fractures (i.e. type I) are commonest. These periarticular regions are exposed to osteolysis from the hinge components and to distracting forces from muscular attachments. While undisplaced fractures can be managed conservatively, displaced ones require ORIF. Management in type II depends on stability of implant and bone loss and accordingly options may vary from ORIF to Revision with allograft supplementation. Type III can be managed in a manner similar to routine fractures in this area.

Humeral bone loss is a particular issue in periprosthetic fracture management. Up to 8 cm of bone loss on the distal humerus can be accommodated with the use of a longer component with an extended anterior flange. Severe bone loss (see Table below) may require Endoprosthetic arthroplasty.

Bushing (PE) wear and implant failure

Bushings are Polyethylene bearings used in the prosthetic hinge (see first pic) in constrained and semiconstrained designs. Clinically significant bushing wear that requires isolated exchange occurs in approximately 1-6% of cases, with an average of 7.9 years between the index arthroplasty and bushing exchange. Patients may report moderate to severe pain, squeaking, and palpable crepitation resulting from acute axis pin-bushing disarticulation. Radiographic evidence of bushing wear is detected on a true AP view with the elbow in full extension. An angle between the humeral yoke and ulnar component measuring >7° is indicative of bushing wear, whereas varus-valgus angulation measuring > 10° is correlated with extensive wear.

![[TO17907786-8.jpg]]

Fig: A semiconstrained total elbow system typically has 7° of varusvalgus laxity, which correlates with 3.5° laxity in one coronal direction. Laxity >7° is indicative of partial wear beyond the constraints of the prosthesis design. Complete wear is likely with >10° of laxity.

Infection

The rate of deep infection after TEA is approximately 3% to 8%. Infection is commonly caused by Staphylococcus aureus and S epidermidis, the latter being particularly more virulent because of the production of unrelenting biofilms. Management principles are same as in hip or knee arthroplasty.

Triceps Insufficiency

The overall prevalence of triceps insufficiency ranges from 2% to 8%. The triceps serves two important functions. First, adequate extension of the elbow to reach overhead is solely dependent on triceps. Second, the muscle coverage around the implant provides a thick soft tissue envelope that can augment the thin coverage around the elbow. This may limit wound complications and exposure to deep infection.

Triceps disruption is typically managed surgically but Prevention of triceps insufficiency is the best thing and can be addressed with meticulous surgical approach and meticulous repair of the triceps.

The triceps-splitting, -reflecting, and -preserving approaches are three primary surgical exposures used to preserve continuity of the extensor mechanism. For the triceps-splitting and triceps-reflecting approaches, the triceps tendon remains attached to the periosteum and superficial fascia of the forearm. The elbow joint is exposed by splitting the triceps or reflecting it as a single unit. A small wafer of olecranon can be detached along with the extensors. Direct repair of the extensor complex to the olecranon, reinforced by soft-tissue repair, allows for faster rehabilitation. Complete triceps disruption occurs in <2% of patients. The triceps-preserving approach is a bilateral tricipital approach that does not involve detachment of the triceps from the olecranon. Adequate exposure to the radial and ulnar sides of the elbow can be obtained through ulnohumeral joint dislocation. This approach is particularly useful in patients with severe rheumatoid arthritis.

No large study has directly compared the three approaches, and there is no consensus on a best approach. All three techniques are popular among elbow surgeons for performing primary TEA.

Ulnar neuropathy

The ulnar and radial nerves are exposed during surgical dissection of the elbow. Ulnar paresthesia is common postoperatively (<40%), but symptoms typically resolve within 2 to 6 weeks. Radial nerve is less frequently injured.
