## Question

A 54-year-old male with a history of uncontrolled diabetes complicated by multiple foot ulcers, treated with several total contact casts presents to your clinic for follow-up. He states that since his last cast was taken off nine months ago, he has noticed that his foot has remained in an abnormal position that makes it difficult to bring the affected foot in front of the other foot without dragging his toes. This problem has persisted despite physical therapy and the use of specialized orthotics. Which of the following physical exam findings, if present, would make a gastrocnemius recession most appropriate?

## Options

1. Diffuse diminished sensation of the foot
2. Diminished ankle dorsiflexion that improves with knee flexion
3. Fixed dorsiflexion positioning of the foot
4. Positive Thompson test
5. Presence of a “too many toes” sign

## Correct option

**2. Diminished ankle dorsiflexion that improves with knee flexion**

## Answer explanation

A Silfverskiold test demonstrating improved ankle dorsiflexion while the knee is flexed suggests the presence of a tight gastrocnemius, which would be best addressed with a gastrocnemius recession if conservative treatment has failed (Answer 2).

The patient has an equinus contracture, as evident in his inability to clear his affected foot during the swing phase of gait. This finding is common in diabetic patients after prolonged periods of immobilization. The Silfverskiold test is useful for determining the exact cause of an equinus contracture and is performed by assessing ankle dorsiflexion in both extended and flexed knee positions. Improved ankle dorsiflexion while the knee is flexed indicates that the primary issue is tightness in the gastrocnemius rather than the Achilles tendon. Conversely, a maintained level of limited ankle dorsiflexion when the knee is positioned in flexion and extension suggests that the Achilles tendon is tight rather than the gastrocnemius. Gastrocnemius tightness recalcitrant to conservative management may be treated with gastrocnemius recession.

*Barouk and Barouk published a review on diagnosing gastrocnemius tightness.* **They highlight** the utility of the Silfverskiold test and offer pearls on performing this test, such as positioning the patient prone, maintaining the foot in a neutral or varus position to assess true ankle dorsiflexion, and limiting the application of force beneath the forefoot to 2 kg. **They conclude** that the Silfverskiold test is useful in diagnosing gastrocnemius tightness but should be performed with careful consideration of potentially confounding factors.

*Anderson et al. reviewed the role of gastrocnemius contractures in foot and ankle pathology and potential treatment strategies.* *They discuss the anatomy of the gastrocnemius, the role of this muscle in arch collapse and resultant pathologies, the Grand Rapids Arch Collapse classification, recommended treatments for differing degrees of arch collapse, indications for increasing ankle dorsiflexion surgically, and different techniques for performing this procedure.* **They conclude** that the importance of recognizing gastrocnemius contractures is critical in various foot and ankle pathologies, and that gastrocnemius recession may serve as an effective intervention when appropriately indicated.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Diffuse diminished sensation of the foot is common in the setting of severe diabetic neuropathy. This finding does not warrant a gastrocnemius recession.
Answer 3. Fixed dorsiflexion positioning of the foot is not consistent with this patient’s report of difficulty with clearance of the foot during the swing phase of gait.
Answer 4: A positive Thompson test suggests insufficiency of the Achilles tendon, which would not be best addressed with a gastrocnemius recession.
Answer 5: The presence of a “too many toes” sign is suggestive of a flatfoot deformity, which is often caused by posterior tibial tendon dysfunction. A gastrocnemius recession alone would not be appropriate for this pathology.
