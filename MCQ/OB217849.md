## Question

A 30-year-old man sustains a laceration over the palmar aspect of his ring finger. He undergoes operative intervention for tendon repair that included trimming of the tendon ends and repair. Three months postoperatively, he complains of an inability to completely flex the long and small fingers. What is the most likely cause?

## Options

1. Over-advancement of the flexor digitorum profundus during tendon repair
2. Over-advancement of the flexor digitorum superficialis during tendon repair
3. Lengthening of the flexor digitorum profundus during tendon repair
4. Lengthening of the flexor digitorum superficialis during tendon repair
5. Tightness of the intrinsic muscles of the hand

## Correct option

**1. Over-advancement of the flexor digitorum profundus during tendon repair**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent over-advancement of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon during repair. This would lead to complete active flexion of the ring finger with incomplete active flexion of the long and small finger, which share a common muscle belly.

Quadriga effect is characterized by an active flexion lag in fingers adjacent to a digit with a previously injured or repaired FDP tendon. The cause is attributable to a common muscle belly among the individual FDP tendons. Unlike the flexor digitorum superficialis, which has independent muscle bellies that are NOT interconnected, the FDP tendons share a common muscle belly proximally, and, in addition, have multiple cross-connections between the tendons distally. Hence if one tendon is shortened during repair, it prevents the others from fully flexing during active flexion (the exception here is that the FDP to the index finger is often independent). Diagnosis is made clinically with the inability to fully flex the fingers of the hand adjacent to the injured finger. Treatment is observation if the patient is minimally symptomatic but may involve the release of FDP tendon of injured digit if symptoms impact patient's activity demands.

*Schreuders et al. reviewed injury to the FDP tendons.* **They reported** that the FDP tendons are markedly interconnected, making them less able to move independently than the tendons of the FDS. This difference is often attributed to the common muscle belly of the profundus, but also, more importantly, to cross-connections between the tendons of the profundus. **They concluded** that the effect of this quadriga phenomenon is important in several clinical situations, including testing for strength, assessing movement of the tendons, and when deciding which exercises to teach the patient after a tendon injury.

*Malerich et al. reviewed injury to the FDP tendons.* **They reported** that lacerations of the profundus tendon distal to the superficialis insertion can be treated by advancement of the proximal cut end of the tendon to its insertion. **They concluded** that their cadaveric model suggested the degree of tendon advancement tolerable was 1 cm.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-4: Over-advancement of the FDP during tendon repair may lead to the Quadriga effect due to a common muscle belly of the profundus and the cross-connections between the tendons of the profundus
Answer 5: Intrinsic muscles of the hand flex the metacarpophalangeal joints and extend the PIP joint. Intrinsic tightness causes decreased PIP flexion when the MP joint is in extension.
