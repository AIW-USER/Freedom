## Question

A 35-year-old male presents with foot pain that has failed to improve with nonoperative treatment. A clinical image is shown in Figure A. When placing a block under the lateral aspect of his foot, his alignment becomes slightly valgus. With knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion is 5 degrees short of neutral and does not change with knee flexion. Which of the following procedures should be included in the treatment of this patient's condition?

![[q3878-a-cavovarus.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. First ray dorsiflexion osteotomy and gastrocnemius recession
2. First ray dorsiflexion osteotomy and Achilles lengthening
3. First ray dorsiflexion osteotomy, calcaneal osteotomy, and gastrocnemius recession
4. First ray dorsiflexion osteotomy, calcaneal osteotomy, and Achilles lengthening
5. Triple arthrodesis

## Correct option

**2. First ray dorsiflexion osteotomy and Achilles lengthening**

## Answer explanation

This patient has forefoot-driven, flexible pes cavovarus. A first ray dorsiflexion osteotomy and Achilles lengthening should be included in this patient's ultimate treatment although other elements such as tendon transfer may be necessary depending upon the patients full clinical exam.

Fixed plantarflexion of the first ray can contribute to hindfoot varus. The Coleman block test (the test described) is used to assess hindfoot flexibility. A supple hindfoot will correct to neutral or slight valgus when the block is placed under the lateral hindfoot. Correction of hindfoot varus implies that the deformity is driven by the forefoot. A first ray dorsiflexion metatarsal osteotomy will correct the forefoot deformity and allow the hindfoot to resume its slight valgus position and avoid a calcaneal osteotomy. The Silfverskiöld test differentiates gastrocnemius tightness from an Achilles contracture. Increased ankle dorsiflexion with knee flexion indicates gastrocnemius tightness. If there is no difference in dorsiflexion with the knee flexed or extended, then the contracture is coming from the Achilles tendon. To correct this patients deformity, he requires a first ray dorsiflexion osteotomy and Achilles lengthening.

*Chen et al. performed a review of Achilles lengthening procedures.* **They report** that the Silverskjold test is the first step to determine if only the gastrocnemius or the entire Achilles tendon is tight. In patients with Achilles contractures, the tendon can be lengthened open or percutaneously. **They conclude** that regardless of the method, the key is to lengthen adequately, but not excessively.

*Younger et al. performed a review of the adult cavovarus foot.* **They report** that a tight heel cord can be addressed with either a gastrocnemius recession or heel cord lengthening. If the Achilles does not feel tight, then a mechanical block to dorsiflexion is likely to be present which may be caused by a tight posterior capsule, anterior osteophytes, or a tight soleus component. **They conclude** that the weight-bearing lateral radiograph should be evaluated for mechanical blocks.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Gastrocnemius recession is not appropriate as the contracture is not isolated to the gastrocnemius as demonstrated by the Silfverskiöld test.
Answers 3 and 4: Calcaneal osteotomy is not needed as the deformity corrects with the Coleman block test and is primarily coming from the forefoot.
Answer 5: Triple arthrodesis is not appropriate at this time as the patient's deformity is supple.
