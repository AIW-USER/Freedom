## Question

A 65-year-old female with peripheral vascular disease and diabetic neuropathy presents to your office with a chronic right foot deformity. She has a history of recurrent plantar forefoot ulcers that were previously treated with debridements, antibiotics, and total contact casting. On physical exam, the recurrent ulcer has completely healed, and palpable pulses are felt in the right foot. Right ankle range of motion demonstrates 40 degrees of ankle plantarflexion to 10 degrees short of neutral dorsiflexion that does not correct with knee flexion. Which of the following statements is true about this patient's condition that can help maintain a stable, plantigrade foot and prevent future ulcer recurrence?

## Options

1. Orthotics are more effective at healing plantar forefoot ulcers compared to total contact casting
2. Based on her physical exam, she would benefit from an ostectomy and gastrocnemius recession
3. Based on her physical exam, she would benefit from an ostectomy and achilles tendon lengthening
4. Debridement of recurrent wound and referral to vascular surgery for a revascularization procedure to prevent recurrence
5. Total contact casting with ankle in neutral dorsiflexion to regain ankle motion

## Correct option

**3. Based on her physical exam, she would benefit from an ostectomy and achilles tendon lengthening**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with chronic diabetic neuropathy and a recurrent plantar forefoot ulcer. Based on this patient's physical examination with a positive Silverskiöld test, an ostectomy of the bony prominences causing increased pressure on this region and an Achilles tendon lengthening (TAL) will help prevent future ulcer recurrence and maintain a stable, plantigrade foot (answer 3).

Diabetic foot ulcers are relatively common lower extremity wounds that affect approximately 12% of patients with diabetes mellitus. In fact, foot ulcers are responsible for nearly 85% of lower extremity amputations. Risk factors include uncontrolled hyperglycemia (A1C>8.0), inability to offload the affected region, poor local or systemic blood flow, infection, and poor nutrition. Patients present with a painless ulceration of varying depth +/- an infection. Physical exam, assessing the depth of the ulcer, presence of an infection, evaluating for Achilles tendon tightness, and a thorough neurovascular exam is important. Transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) is considered the gold standard to assess wound healing potential. Greater than 30mmHg is a sign that there is adequate healing potential in the affected extremity. Radiographic evaluation is important to assess the affected osseous structures, and advanced imaging, like MRI and bone scans, can help to evaluate for a soft tissue infection, osteomyelitis, or Charcot arthropathy. Treatment for infected ulcers includes debridement, antibiotics, and wound care. Treatment following proper debridement and resolution of infection includes total contact casting (TCC), custom orthotics, flexor tendon tenotomy, ostectomy of bony exostoses, Keller resection arthroplasty, or amputation. Additional important treatments that have been shown to help prevent ulcer recurrence are TAL or a gastrocnemius recession.

*Dodd et al. performed a JBJS current concepts review on Charcot neuroarthropathy (CN) of the foot and ankle.* **They report** that a common cause of this condition is diabetes mellitus. Ulceration and deep infection contribute to a 15% rate of major amputation. Common indications for surgical intervention are recurrent ulcers, associated deformities, and deep infection/osteomyelitis. **The authors report** that the primary goal of surgical intervention is to maintain a stable, plantigrade foot free from ulcerations, while managing BMI and hyperglycemia. **They conclude** that a multidisciplinary approach to the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers is important to help prevent recurrent ulceration and improve overall quality of life.

*Jones et al. reviewed the surgical management of Charcot arthropathy.* *They explain that the historical management of Charcot foot arthropathy consisted of immobilization until the active disease resolved, followed by bracing/orthotics to help prevent recurrence.* This historic nonoperative management has not improved the quality of life; therefore, surgical deformity correction has been utilized more recently. **They conclude** that it is important to have a deep understanding of the operative indications and surgical techniques to correct acquired deformities in Charcot arthropathy of foot and ankle disorders to help improve quality of life and prevent ulcer recurrence.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: TCC is the gold standard for healing existing plantar neuropathic ulcers, and studies have demonstrated faster healing rates compared to custom orthotics or cast walkers; likely attributable to compliance.
Answer 2: This patient has a positive Silverskiöld test and would therefore benefit more from a TAL than a gastroc recession.
Answer 4: A debridement and revascularization procedure would not remove the bony prominence that is causing pressure on the plantar forefoot.
Answer 5: Total contact casting with the ankle in neutral dorsiflexion will not remove the bony prominence, causing pressure on the plantar forefoot, and neutral ankle dorsiflexion will be difficult to achieve without a TAL procedure.
