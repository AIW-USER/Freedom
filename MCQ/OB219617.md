## Question

A 55-year-old female presents to your orthopedic oncology clinic as a referral for a painless thigh mass. She cannot recall when the mass was first noticed but thinks it has slowly grown in the past several months. Radiographs are performed and show no bony involvement or soft-tissue calcification. She presents with the MRI shown in Figure A. What is the appropriate next step for this patient?

![[q219617-a-mri.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Wide resection & radiation
2. Marginal resection
3. Core-needle biopsy
4. Excisional biopsy
5. Observation

## Correct option

**3. Core-needle biopsy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a large, heterogenous soft-tissue mass in the deep tissue of the thigh and therefore warrants core-needle biopsy to be performed for the next step (answer 3).

Large (>5cm), sub-fascial, soft-tissue masses are presumed to be sarcomas until proven otherwise. Radiographic imaging and magnetic resonance imaging are the first steps in the workup for a presumed sarcoma. The appropriate next step is a core-needle biopsy of the mass, ideally performed by the potential definitive surgeon. The goal of the biopsy is to establish a tissue diagnosis to determine treatment plans. Biopsy should be performed in a manner that minimizes morbidity and contamination. See Illustration A for examples of biopsy principles. For soft tissue sarcomas, staging is often performed with a CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. Once staging is performed, most sarcomas can be treated with wide resection and radiation, with a few exceptions.

Kransdorf and Murphey provide a review and analysis of imaging techniques for the evaluation of soft-tissue masses. Although MRI is not typically sufficient to suggest a specific diagnosis, the authors note reports of 90% accuracy when using MRI to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions. Criteria for malignant lesions include ill-defined margins, larger size, and heterogenous signal. The authors conclude that MR imaging is the preferred modality, once radiographs are completed, to evaluate soft-tissue masses but reiterate the importance of a thorough workup, including biopsy, in indeterminant lesions.

*Renn et al. review malignant soft-tissue sarcomas in the extremities of adults.* After appropriate history and imaging are performed, an accurate diagnosis is sought by way of a percutaneous core-needle biopsy. The authors of this study recommend using a 14-G needle to obtain at least four core samples for optimal diagnostic yield. Core-needle biopsies may be performed under image guidance by a radiologist or by a suitably trained physician when easily palpable. **The authors conclude** that the benefits of core-needle biopsy of soft-tissue tumors outweigh the potential risks of seeding and should be performed in the workup of any concerning soft-tissue lesion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Initiation of treatment before a tissue diagnosis is inappropriate and could result in inappropriate treatment. While this may be the definitive treatment, a biopsy and staging should be performed prior.
Answer choice 2: Initiation of treatment before a tissue diagnosis is inappropriate and could result in inappropriate treatment. Furthermore, marginal resection (leaving a residual tumor-reactive zone) creates a high risk of local recurrence and is typically not employed for soft tissue sarcomas.
Answer choice 4: Excisional biopsy is a complete removal of the mass at the time of biopsy. This is typically done for small (<3 cm) masses that are confirmed or strongly suggestive of a benign nature. This mass is large with malignant features, therefore excisional biopsy would be inappropriate.
Answer choice 5: Due to the aggressive appearance of the lesion (size >5cm, deep to fascia, heterogenous), observation would not be appropriate for this patient.
