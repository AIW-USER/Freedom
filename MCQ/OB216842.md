## Question

A 16-year-old male running back presents to your clinic after a knee injury during his school's game yesterday. He states he attempted to spin to avoid a defender and his knee gave out, causing a loud pop. Immediate swelling was noted which is observed still today. Examination is difficult given acute pain and guarding but does not appear to demonstrate any varus/valgus laxity. His radiograph is seen in Figure A. Based on the most likely diagnosis, which of the following structures seen in Figure B is also injured in this patient?

## Options

## Correct option

**4. Structure 4**

## Answer explanation

Structure 4 represents the anterolateral ligament (ALL), which is commonly injured at the time of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture and is identified by a bony avulsion at its insertion site on the proximal/lateral tibia.

The understanding of the ALL continues to evolve but its association with ACL rupture and knee stability is more clear at this point. The ALL attaches to the femur near the lateral collateral ligament (LCL), and to the tibia between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head. When the tibial attachment is avulsed, this creates the Segond fracture and is highly sensitive for ACL rupture. The ALL is thought to play an important role in rotational stability of the knee, although its role in anterior/posterior stability is less well understood. ALL augmentation or reconstruction (e.g. lumiere technique) has been suggested to improve rotational stability in association with ACL reconstruction, but there is a void of long-term outcomes data on this.

*Claes et al. described the anatomy of the ALL,* **noting** that out of the 41 cadavers studied, 40 of them (97%) had a well-defined ligamentous structure that was able to be distinguished from the underlying capsule. **They noted** some interconnection of the ALL and LCL at their femoral attachments, with an oblique course of the ALL attaching to the anterolateral proximal tibia between Gerdy's and the fibular head. They hypothesized based on its location that it would function to control internal tibial rotation and decrease the pivot shift.

*Lau et al. reviewed the use of ALL reconstruction to augment ACL reconstruction.* Their indications for use of ALL augmentation included revision ACL reconstruction, grade III pivot shift, generalized ligamentous laxity, age <20, and high-level athlete. **They suggest** that early evidence supports this technique in order to lead to a decreased risk of ACL re-rupture.

*Lubowitz et al. wrote an editorial regarding the, "discovery" of the ALL where* **they noted** the femoral attachment was just posterior to the lateral femoral epicondyle and the tibial attachment was midway between Gerdy's and the tip of the fibular head. **They noted** that the ALL was well situated to help control rotational forces at the knee given its position peripheral to the knee's center of rotation. **They concluded** that given the anatomic features, patients may see additional benefit from reconstruction of the ALL.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the left knee demonstrating a Segond fracture, representative of a tibial avulsion of the anterolateral ligament. Figure B is a cadaveric dissection of the lateral knee demonstrating the: (1) lateral gastrocnemius tendon (2) long head of biceps femoris tendon (3) lateral collateral ligament (4) anterolateral ligament (5) popliteus tendon.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Structure 1 represents the lateral head of the gastrocnemius which attaches to the femur posterior & proximal to the LCL attachment.
Answer 2: Structure 2 represents the long head of the biceps femoris tendon which inserts on the proximal fibula posterior to the LCL and popliteofibular ligament.
Answer 3: Structure 3 represents the LCL which attaches just posterior to the lateral femoral epicondyle and anteriorly on the fibular head.
Answer 5: Structure 5 represents the popliteus tendon which attaches anterior and distal to the LCL on the femur and posteriorly on the proximal tibia.
