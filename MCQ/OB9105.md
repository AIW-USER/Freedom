## Question

A 29-year-old male falls down a flight of stairs while intoxicated and sustains a hyperflexion injury to the cervical spine. During the examination, the patient is obtunded and uncooperative with the physical examination. Motor testing cannot be performed due to the patient's status, but reflexes are 2/4 biceps, triceps, and patellar. Figure A is the cervical spine radiograph. There are no other obvious injuries during primary and secondary surveys. Initial management should include?

![[q9105-a-screen-shot-2019-11-24-at-11-23-12-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Gardner-wells traction, closed reduction, followed by posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation
2. Halo traction, closed reduction, followed by anterior interbody fusion with dynamic plating
3. Immediate open anterior reduction with interbody fusion and static plating followed by MRI of the C-spine
4. Immediate MRI of the C-spine followed by operative treatment
5. Immediate open posterior reduction with posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation, followed by MRI of the C-spine

## Correct option

**4. Immediate MRI of the C-spine followed by operative treatment**

## Answer explanation

The patient is currently intoxicated and unable to cooperate with a neurological examination that is presenting with a bilateral facet dislocation at C4-5. The management would include immediate MRI of the cervical spine followed by reduction, instrumentation, and fusion depending on the pathology present on imaging.

Cervical facet dislocations are severe injuries that have a high incidence of spinal cord injuries that results from a flexion-distractive injury mechanism to the cervical spine. Initial management depends on whether the patient is awake and cooperative. In patients that are obtunded, intoxicated, or unconscious immediate MRI imaging of the cervical spine should be performed to determine if there is a herniated nucleus pulposus or ruptured longitudinal ligaments. Following closed reduction, fixation with either anterior or posterior fusion should be performed.

*Vaccaro et al. performed a prospective clinical study of 11 patients with a cervical facet dislocation that underwent a pre- and post-reduction cervical spine MRI to evaluate for intervertebral disc herniations and spinal ligament injuries.* **They reported** that two patients were found to have disc herniations prior to reduction, whereas five patients were found to have herniated discs after closed reduction. Despite the increase in herniated discs after closed reduction, there were no patients with worsening neurological changes. **The authors concluded** that closed reduction of facet dislocations appears to increase the incidence of intervertebral disc herniations, but this does not appear to pose harm to the awake and alert patient.

*Coe et al. performed a cadaveric study of human spines with a simulated bilateral facet dislocation at C5-6 that was stabilized by eight different constructs.* *Of the constructs tested, there was no significant difference in flexion and torsional stiffness, but there was a greater posterior strain with anterior plating constructs.* **The authors concluded** that Caspar plating is an inferior fixation option for treating distractive injuries.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The patient is presenting in an obtunded state of which reliable neurological exams cannot be performed during the reduction. The patient should undergo an immediate MRI followed by open reduction and posterior fixation.
Answer 2: In the acute setting, Gardner-Wells traction should be applied in an awake and cooperative patient.
Answer 3: A patient that is obtunded, intoxicated, or unconscious should undergo an immediate MRI to evaluate for the presence of a herniated disc, extruded disc or ruptured ligaments. Due to their mental status, neurologic testing is unable to be performed by the treating surgeon. Following MRI, a reduction and operative stabilization should be performed.
