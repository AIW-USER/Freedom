## Question

An 18-year-old male presents to the clinic with a four-month history of right proximal thigh pain without an inciting event. He states that he has noticed that the pain is worse at night and when he is drinking with his friends. He has not tried any other modalities to address his pain. A CT of his lower extremities was obtained at an outside hospital and is shown in Figure A. What is the next best step in the management of this patient?

## Options

1. High-intensity focused ultrasound
2. MRI of the lesion
3. Radiofrequency ablation
4. Surgical resection
5. Trial of NSAIDs and observation

## Correct option

**5. Trial of NSAIDs and observation**

## Answer explanation

An osseous lesion composed of a radiolucent nidus surrounded by sclerotic bone and accompanied by clinical symptoms of pain that is worse at night and with alcoholic beverage intake suggests the presence of an osteoid osteoma. Initial treatment of this condition consists of a trial of NSAIDs and observation (answer choice 5).

Osteoid osteomas are uncommon benign osseous lesions that typically present in males under the age of 20 in lower extremity bones or the spine. Patients characteristically present with pain that is worse at night and with alcohol intake, but that is improved with NSAIDs. It is thought that the pathologic driver of the patients’ pain is the high concentration of prostaglandin E2 and COX at the site of these lesions. Imaging demonstrates sclerotic bone surrounding a radiolucent nidus usually less than 1.5 cm in diameter (lesions greater than 1.5 cm in diameter warrant consideration of alternative diagnoses like an osteoblastoma). Treatment ranges from conservative treatment with NSAIDs to more invasive interventions if medical management fails, such as high-intensity focused ultrasound, radio frequency ablation (RFA), and surgical resection. Recurrence rates typically range from 10 to 15% with radiofrequency ablation.

*Efthymiadis et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the outcomes of minimally invasive surgery addressing osteoid osteomas.* **They identified** 16 total case series and reported over 90% success rates each for CT-guided RFA, CT-guided percutaneous resection drilling, and arthroscopic resection. **They concluded** that osteoid osteomas can be treated with minimally invasive techniques, RFA represents the most effective option, and further research is needed to confirm the efficacy of arthroscopic resection for this indication.

*Napora et al. published a review on 91 osteoid osteomas treated with thermoablation at their institution and discussed the typical and atypical presentations that their patients presented with.* *They reviewed cases where osteoid osteomas were misdiagnosed as other conditions, such as suspected femoroacetabular impingement, Lyme disease, leukemia, fatigue fracture, tuberculosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and chronic hip arthritis.* *They also reviewed unusual presentations such as lesions located in the axial skeleton or in the joint and mimickers of osteoid osteomas, such as Brodie’s abscesses or intracortical chondromas.* **They conclude** that the diagnosis of osteoid osteomas in unusual locations or settings is difficult and requires a high index of suspicion in order to appropriately indicate patients for the optimal treatment.

*Tepelenis et al. published a review on osteoid osteomas, one of the most common benign bone tumors.* *They discuss the most common presentation of these tumors, which typically occur in males between 5 and 30 years of age in the appendicular skeleton.* *They review that radiographs and computed tomography scans are the workhorses for diagnostic imaging.* *They cover treatment modalities which should first consist of conservative measures INSAIDs, salicylates) and then more invasive interventions like radiofrequency ablation, cryoablation, and, rarely, surgical excision if conservative measures fail.* **They conclude** that a comprehensive understanding of the presentation and treatment strategies of this condition is important to adequately manage patients with osteoid osteoma.

Figure A demonstrates an osseous lesion composed of a radiolucent nidus surrounded by sclerotic bone, suggestive of an osteoid osteoma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, and 4: High-intensity focused ultrasound, radiofrequency ablation, and surgical resection should be reserved for patients whose symptoms do not improve with conservative treatment
Answer 2: This patient has clinical and imaging findings consistent with a diagnosis of an osteoid osteoma. An MRI would not provide additional information that would further guide treatment.
