## Options

2. Has higher odds of requiring open reduction
3. Has a more common injury pattern
4. Will require physical therapy

## Correct option

**2. Has higher odds of requiring open reduction**

## Answer explanation

A patient with flexion-type supracondylar humerus fracture has higher odds of requiring open reduction than an extension-type.

Supracondylar fractures are one of the most common traumatic fractures seen in children and most commonly occur in children 5-7 years of age after a fall on an outstretched hand. The flexion-type variant is relatively rare, accounting for less than 5% of supracondylar humerus fractures, and commonly occurs secondary to a direct blow to the elbow. Vascular compromise occurs in 5-17% of all supracondylar humerus fractures. A thorough neurovascular exam is critical in all supracondylar humerus fractures and is especially important in flexion-type supracondylar humerus fractures given that the risk of ulnar nerve injury is higher. Due to the risk of ulnar nerve injury and/or entrapment, flexion-type supracondylar fractures tend have a much higher rate of necessitating an open reduction.

*Flynn et al. reviewed 2,783 consecutive supracondylar humerus fractures at a single facility of which 95 (3.4%) were flexion-type.* *21 of the flexion-type humerus fractures required open reduction, representing a 15.4-fold increase in the odds of open reduction.* The presence of an ulnar nerve injury at presentation resulted in an additional 6.7-fold higher risk of open reduction among flexion-type supracondylar humeral fractures.

*Choi et al. performed a review of 1255 pediatric supracondylar fractures treated at a single institution.* There were 33 (2.6%) pulseless fractures. All of the 24 well-perfused patients did well postoperatively without vascular repair, although 10 remained pulseless. Of the 9 poorly perfused patients, 4 underwent vascular repair and 2 developed compartment syndrome. **They concluded** that patients with poor perfusion were at increased risk of vascular repair and compartment syndrome.

Abzug at al. reviewed the management of supracondylar humerus fractures, including evolving concepts regarding pin placement, complications of a pulseless hand, compartment syndrome, and posterolateral rotatory instability. They report that the extension-type variant accounts for 98% of supracondylar humerus fractures. They also note that ulnar nerve injury is more common with flexion-type fractures.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: Flexion-type supracondylar humerus fractures have not been shown to have higher odds of requiring vascular repair.
Answer 3: Extension-type supracondylar humerus fractures are more common than flexion-type.
Answer 4: Literature does not support the use of physical therapy for supracondylar humerus fractures. Most children will attain their normal arm function and range of motion without therapy.
Answer 5: Most children with a pink pulseless extremity after sustaining a supracondylar humerus fracture will have a palpable radial pulse at final follow-up.
