## Question

A 24-year-old male presents to the emergency room with the injury shown in Figure A after driving his car off of an embankment into a ravine. If circumferential pelvic compression is not applied on initial presentation and the patient becomes acutely hemodynamically unstable, other than venous plexus bleeding, which of the following would be the most likely source of the hemodynamic instability based on the pattern of injury shown in Figure A?

![[q219162-a-screenshot-2023-09-17-at-8-58-10-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. External iliac artery
2. Internal iliac artery
3. Internal pudendal artery
4. Obturator artery
5. Superior gluteal artery

## Correct option

**5. Superior gluteal artery**

## Answer explanation

Though venous plexus bleeding represents the most common overall cause for acute blood loss in pelvic fractures, certain fracture patterns are also more commonly associated with specific arterial injuries. In anterior-posterior compression (APC) type pelvic ring injuries, the origin of arterial bleeding is most commonly from the superior gluteal artery.

Pelvic ring injuries can present with a wide range of severity, including benign, low-energy geriatric fractures through life-threatening, high-energy injuries such as that shown in Figure A. The patient’s presenting condition along with accurate interpretation of the anteroposterior pelvis radiograph is critical to treating these patients when they present to the trauma bay. If presenting with signs of hemodynamic instability and shock, prompt application of circumferential pelvic compression should be performed to minimize hemorrhage. Most commonly, hemorrhage occurs from shearing injury to the thin-walled venous plexus (80% of bleeding), leading to a large retroperitoneal hematoma that may not be suspected initially (can hold up to 4L of blood). Though arterial injuries are less common, they can account for 10-20% of serious bleeding in pelvic ring injuries, with specific patterns of pelvic ring injuries being more commonly associated with specific arterial injuries. Specifically, APC-type injuries are more commonly associated with bleeding from the superior gluteal artery, while LC-type injuries more commonly see bleeding from the internal pudendal and/or obturator arteries.

Langford, et al. provide a review of pelvic fracture evaluation, classification, and resuscitation. The authors note that the initial reduction of pelvic blood loss can be provided by binders, sheets, or some form of external fixation, which serves to reduce pelvic volume, stabilize clot formation, and reduce ongoing tissue damage. Persistently unstable patients, on the other hand, may benefit from angiography with selective embolization, pelvic packing, or a combination of these interventions.

Kleweno, et al. provide an ICL on the management of pelvic fractures. The authors note that prior to the placement of any circumferential device about the pelvis, a complete examination of the soft tissue envelope must be performed, including the posterior soft tissues about the buttock and the groin, to rule out the possibility of an open injury. They conclude that because the volumetric expansion of the unstable pelvis has been described as most similar to a hemi-elliptical sphere, containment and reduction of the bony pelvis diminishes the potential space for hemorrhage and supports any clot or tamponade that has already been formed.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the pelvis showing an APC III-type pelvic ring injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: The internal and external iliac arteries are less commonly injured entities in pelvic ring injuries.
Answers 3 and 4: The internal pudendal and obturator arteries are more commonly injured in lateral compression (LC)-type pelvic ring injuries.
