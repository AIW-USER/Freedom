## Question

A 17-year-old male high school football player takes a hit directly to the thigh during a game. He is unable to return to the game and is taken to the ED, where radiographs are negative for fracture. Orthopedics is asked to evaluate the patient in the ED. On examination the thigh is swollen and firm with overlying ecchymosis. There is point tenderness to palpation over the anterior thigh, but the patient is relatively comfortable at rest. He can perform a straight leg raise, although with difficulty and pain. Sensation is intact to the lower extremity and the leg is well perfused with brisk capillary refill. What is the next best step in management of this patient's most likely injury?

## Options

1. CT scan to evaluate for active arterial extravasation
2. Cryotherapy, NSAIDS, and hyperflexion splinting
3. Emergent thigh fasciotomies
4. Placement of long leg posterior splint in extension
5. MRI to evaluate for extensor mechanism rupture

## Correct option

**2. Cryotherapy, NSAIDS, and hyperflexion splinting**

## Answer explanation

This clinical scenario represents a quadriceps contusion in the setting of a direct, traumatic injury and should be treated with hyperflexion splinting at the knee overnight to minimize the risk of developing myositis ossificans.

Quadriceps contusions are relatively common in sports where direct blows to the anterior thigh may occur, such as football, rugby and soccer. Swelling and hematoma formation take place after the injury, which eventually gives way to muscle and scar generation. Severe quadriceps contusion can put these athletes at risk for myositis ossificans, which is calcification of these lesions in the anterior thigh musculature and causes significant stiffness and pain. Hyperflexion splinting, NSAIDs and ice therapy have all shown benefit in improving recovery times, return to sport, and outcomes in these patients.

*Rossettini et al. review a case report of myositis ossificans in a cyclist with severe anterolateral thigh pain 4 months after a blunt trauma during a fall off his bike.* Knee range of motion was significantly restricted and a hard mass was noted in his thigh and MRI was ultimately obtained, which confirmed the diagnosis. Physical therapy and NSAIDS were used to treat the patient and almost a year later he was found to be pain-free.

*Jackson et al. performed a trial of knee hyperflexion for 24 hours on naval academy midshipmen with quadriceps contusions.* **They noted** that the subjects were able to return to activity quickly (avg 3.5 days) and of the 23 included, only 1 midshipman developed myositis ossificans. **The authors concluded** that immediate hyperflexion splinting of the knee improved outcomes in active patients with quadriceps contusions.

*Ryan et al. studied 117 army cadets with quadriceps contusions over 3 years to evaluate the effectiveness of a therapy program to return them to activity.* *They encouraged knee flexion positioning and flexion exercises and classified the contusions based on knee range of motion at 24 hours (mild >90 deg, mod 45-90 deg, severe <45 deg).* Myositis ossificans developed in 9% and was associated with less knee motion, delay in treatment, and previous quadriceps injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While a hematoma is likely present, there are no signs of active arterial extravasation and this is not part of the initial workup for a quadriceps contusion.
Answer 3: This patient does not appear to have a clinical diagnosis of compartment syndrome based on patient comfort and normal neurovascular exam. Fasciotomies would only be indicated if compartment syndrome was present.
Answer 4: Multiple studies have demonstrated splinting in hyperflexion, not extension, improves outcomes in these patients.
Answer 5: There are no indications of extensor mechanism rupture as the patient can perform a straight leg raise. The associated difficulty with straight leg raise is likely secondary to pain.
