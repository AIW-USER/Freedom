## Question

A 25-year-old male presents to your office with left medial sided knee pain. He is a former college football player who is now working as a medical device representative. He complains of mechanical symptoms and he has an MRI that demonstrates an unstable osteochondral lesion. You recommend surgery and are unable to fixate the osteochondral fragment because it is too fragmented. Therefore, you perform a loose body removal as well as a technique where you penetrate the subchondral bone with an awl. Which of the following is true of the procedure performed?

## Options

1. Content of type I cartilage generated is greater than type II cartilage
2. Improved outcomes are maintained at 5-year follow-up
3. Utilized primarily in uncontained lesions
4. Does not require post-operative weight bearing restrictions
5. Ideal for lesions >4 cm2 in size

## Correct option

**1. Content of type I cartilage generated is greater than type II cartilage**

## Answer explanation

Microfracture generates a much higher concentration of type I fibrocartilage than type II hyaline cartilage.

Articular cartilage defects of the knee are quite common and there is a spectrum of severity with regards to these injuries. Patients typically complain of recurrent swelling, pain and mechanical symptoms can be present in the setting of lesions that are loose or unstable. MRI is most sensitive in evaluating these lesions and treatment options are variable based on location, size, and stability of the lesions. Microfracture is one technique used in smaller lesions <2 cm2, where the subchondral bone is penetrated by a sharp awl which helps to release marrow elements in order to stimulate new cartilage formation. The cartilage that is formed is primarily type I cartilage, which is stiffer and less durable than type II hyaline cartilage. Outcomes tend to improve in the short-term but have been shown to only be maintained for 2-3 years.

*Mithoefer published a review of complex articular cartilage restoration,* **noting** that if these lesions go untreated they may lead to chronic joint degeneration and disability. Specifically in reference to microfracture, they mention it as one of the more frequently used techniques due to its less invasive nature. **They also note** that while knee function initially improves in 58-95% of patients, functional improvements tend to deteriorate between 18-36 months in 50-80% of cases.

*Mithoefer et al. looked at the clinical efficacy of microfracture techniques for articular cartilage repairs in the knee based on 28 studies and 3,122 patients.* **They noted** that there were improvements in knee function during the first 2 years in almost all patients but there were conflicting results regarding the durability of these improvements. **They noted** that post-operative MRI demonstrated a high degree of variability with regards to the amount of the defect filled by microfracture and this variability correlated with outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Most authors have agreed that the improvements in function related to microfracture are not well maintained at long-term follow-up.
Answer 3: Microfracture should not be utilized in an uncontained lesion as this does not provide a stable rim for the marrow elements to embed within.
Answer 4: Weight-bearing is typically restricted for about 6 weeks after microfracture surgery of the femoral condyles or tibial plateau in order to allow maturation of the cartilage.
Answer 5: Microfracture has been demonstrated to have better outcomes in smaller lesions, particularly those <2cm2.
