## Question

A 30-year-old with motor vehicle injury presented to the casualty with pelvic fracture. His vitals are stable but he is unable to pass urine. He has blood at the urethral meatus and a rectal examination showed a superiorly displaced prostate. An RGU was performed as shown below. What is the most likely site of the Urethral injury?

![[TO17137993-1.png]]

## Options

1. Penile Urethra
2. Membranous Urethra
3. Bulbar Urethra
4. Spongy Urethra

## Correct option

**2. Membranous Urethra**

## Answer explanation

EXPLANATION:

Blood at the urethral meatus, inability to void urine and a superiorly displaced prostate that is movable during a digital rectal examination are classic signs of Pelvic fractures associated with Urethral injuries. The RGU shows extravasation of the contrast medium at the Membranous urethra.

Pelvic fracture associated Urethral injury

Pelvic fracture is usually caused by a high-energy injury, such as a traffic accident or fall from heights, and places patients at risk of associated urethral injury, which is known as Pelvic Fracture Urethral injuries (PFUI). PFUIs are relatively rare and are much more common in males, because the female urethra is shorter and more mobile than that of a male, and is almost completely protected by the pubic bone. Early identification and appropriate management are of utmost importance in preventing significant long-term morbidity and providing better outcome.

Site of Injury

In the past, it has been believed that PFUIs occurred at the level of the membranous urethra only and resulted from tearing the prostate from the membranous urethra. However, injury can occur at the prostatic apex, throughout the membranous urethra or distal to the membranous urethra (junction between the bulbar urethra and membranous urethra), and the most common site of injury in adult patients is distal to the membranous urethra.

In children the site of injury tends to be more proximal, including the prostate and the bladder neck.

Presentation

The two classic signs of PFUI are blood at the urethral meatus and a superiorly displaced prostate that is movable during a digital rectal examination. Other signs of urethral injury include difficulty or inability to pass a urethral catheter and a distended palpable bladder due to the inability to void.

Diagnosis

When a PFUI is suspected in the acute setting, retrograde urethrography (RGU) is the standard diagnostic investigation. The site and the extent of urethral injury can be identified by the urethrography.

Management

Initial management should concentrate first on resuscitating and stabilizing the patient, and then on identifying all associated injuries. Urethral injury can cause urinary outflow obstruction, extravasation and secondary sepsis in the acute phase. Once a urethral injury is identified, the most important initial goal in the acutely injured patient is to accomplish bladder drainage promptly for prevention of urinary extravasation and infection.

The most convenient way is to place a Suprapubic tube into the bladder, which can be carried out either in the emergency room or during laparotomy for the repair of concomitant injuries.
