## Answer explanation

The histology describes an enchondroma, which is depicted in the radiograph labeled "E" (Answer 5).

Enchondromas are common benign osseous lesions often discovered incidentally on radiographs after an injury. Enchondromas typically present radiographically as a well-defined, lucent, central medullary lesion that has a propensity to calcify over time. The gross histology of an enchondroma is described as mature hyaline cartilage and chondrocytes, with scattered calcifications. Microscopically, mature hyaline cartilage lobules separated by normal marrow with abundant extracellular matrix and lack of myxoid component are typical of an enchondroma.

*Lubahn et al. reviewed the evaluation and management of enchondromas of the hand.* **They found** that enchondromas are the most common primary bone tumor in the hand, typically presenting as a benign cartilaginous mass. It often manifests as a pathologic fracture, and less common conditions like multiple enchondromatosis must be excluded at the initial presentation. The standard treatment for symptomatic lesions is curettage, however, the timing of surgery for fractures and how best to manage post-curettage defects is still debated. **The authors conclude** that it is critical to distinguish between enchondroma and low-grade chondrosarcoma for effective management. Chondrosarcoma typically exhibits higher cellularity and more binucleated chondrocytes than enchondroma histologically.

*Davies et al. conducted a study on the predilection of enchondroma for the tubular bones of the hand.* Their research challenges the conventional belief that the hand is the most common site for enchondroma. A 10-year retrospective review of hand radiographs showed an enchondroma incidence of 0.07%, while MRI identified a greater number of enchondromas in the proximal humerus, distal femur, and proximal tibia. Only 17% of lesions under 2 cm were visible on radiographs, and detectability improved as lesion size increased. These findings suggest that the higher sensitivity of MRI in detecting small lesions means that the hand may not be the most frequent site for enchondroma as previously thought.

*Tang et al. reviewed the surgical treatment of hand enchondromas.* Curettage is typically implemented, with adjuvant therapies like high-speed burring or alcohol instillation. Allograft and autograft utilization can be employed to fill the osseous defect after curettage, but their impact on outcomes is uncertain. Alternatively, cement injection methods provide immediate stability with low complication rates. Ultimately the authors summarize that small asymptomatic enchondromas can be monitored, while symptomatic lesions should be treated with curettage and cement injection should be reserved for large lesions to increase mechanical stability.
