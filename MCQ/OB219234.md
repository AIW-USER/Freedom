## Question

A 32-year-old female presents to the clinic six weeks after sustaining a dog bite injury to her right dominant hand. She shows you a picture of the injury on her phone (Figure A) and states the pain has somewhat improved, but she continues to have dense numbness to the radial aspect of her index finger. Surgical exploration is performed, and she is found to have a transected digital nerve to the radial aspect of her index finger. After trimming the nerve ends to visualize healthy nerve fascicles, there remains a 1.8cm defect. The surgeon bridges this defect utilizing a decellular nerve allograft. Which of the following best describes the purpose of the graft?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. It provides living cells to aid in axonal regeneration but requires immunosuppression
2. It serves as a bioabsorbable substance that prevents axonal escape reliant on fibrin clot formation
3. It provides a structural scaffold (via microtubules) for axonal regeneration reliant on host Schwann cell migration
4. It provides living cells to aid in axonal regeneration but imparts donor site morbidity
5. It utilizes a redundant functioning nerve to supplement or restore the damaged nerve’s function

## Correct option

**3. It provides a structural scaffold (via microtubules) for axonal regeneration reliant on host Schwann cell migration**

## Answer explanation

Decellular nerve allografts (DNAs) are commercial off-the-shelf grafts that have been processed to remove cellular remnants, myelin, neurofilaments, and native growth inhibitors. However, the structural architect/scaffold for axonal regeneration (specifically microtubular structure) and associated extracellular matrix proteins (i.e. laminin) are retained to aid in neuron regeneration via host Schwann cells (Answer 3).

In the setting of peripheral nerve neurotmesis, primary tensionless nerve repair (via fascicular or epineural approximation) should always be employed in the setting of acute, sharp lacerations. However, many nerve injuries are either chronic or involve small segmental defects. In either case, the nerve should be carefully sequentially trimmed until healthy nerve fascicles are visualized from both nerve ends. Neurolysis can be performed on both proximal and distal nerve stumps to gain excursion, however, there often remain gaps at both nerve endings precluding primary, tensionless repair. For small sensory defects, gap treatment options include utilizing a nerve conduit, allograft, or autograft. While autografts are generally the gold standard due to their biological superiority and outcomes, they impart donor-site morbidity and sacrifice sensation at the donor site for sensation at the recipient site. In noncritical nerves, conduits and DNAs retain similarly good outcomes, are readily available, preclude donor site morbidity, and do not require immunosuppressive therapy. Therefore, they can serve as a powerful tool in a surgeon’s armamentarium.

Tang and Chauhan provided a comprehensive review article regarding the use of decellular nerve allografts. They discuss that improvements in processing technologies allow for these grafts to be utilized without the immunosuppressive therapy required for non-processed nerve allografts. The authors generally recommend its use in sensory nerve gaps <3cm and especially in the setting of multiple nerve injuries with varying diameters. The authors conclude that while these decellular nerve allografts work well for small sensory defects, they are not supported yet for mixed or motor nerve injuries.

*Fernandez and colleagues provided a current concept review on adjuvant therapies for nerve recovery.* *They briefly discuss the distinctions between conduits, allografts, and autografts citing autograft superiority in most clinical studies.* **They highlight** different immunosuppressant pharmaceuticals and other oral medications that may aid in faster and more robust nerve recovery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Non-processed nerve allografts were of great interest twenty years ago, however, required immunosuppression to prevent host-graft immunogenic failure. Given their high cost, immunosuppressive requirement, and alternative options available with similar outcomes, non-processed nerve allografts are seldom utilized today.
Answer 2: Bioabsorbable nerve conduits may be fabricated from synthetic collagen, polyglycolic acids, or other options that rely on fibrin clot formation to support nerve regeneration. They are often manufactured as tubular wraps that can either bridge small (<1cm) gaps or supplement nerve repairs to prevent axonal escape, structurally strengthen the repair site, and offload repair site tension.
Answer 4: Nerve autografts remain the “gold standard” imparting the best outcomes when utilized for nerve gap defects. However, they also come at the cost of potential donor site morbidity, a requirement to prep/drape a separate extremity where the donor nerve lies, and added surgical time to perform donor harvest. Commonly used sensory donor nerves include the sural nerve, the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (MABC), the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (LABC), and the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN).
Answer 5: Peripheral nerve transfers are clinically powerful techniques most often utilized to maintain or restore the motor function of injured critical nerves (i.e. ulnar nerve). They can be utilized to supplement a primary repair (i.e. AIN supercharged end-to-side transfer with ulnar nerve repair) or restore the function of an irreparably damaged nerve (i.e. radial to axillary nerve transfer to restore shoulder abduction in upper trunk brachial plexus injuries).
