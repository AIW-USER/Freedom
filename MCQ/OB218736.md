## Question

A 15-year-old male presents to your office with a several-month history of mid-back pain. He is an active track, football, and weightlifting athlete, and denies any history of trauma or injury. The pain is aggravated primarily by prolonged sitting and is relieved by movement and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication. Physical examination shows limited forward flexion. The patient exhibits a normal gait and a negative straight leg raise. Radiographs are shown in Figure A. Which of the following best describes the patient’s underlying condition?

## Options

1. Complete fracture of the pars interarticularis
2. Anterior wedging of >5 degrees across three consecutive vertebrae
3. Failure of normal segmentation resulting in abnormal fusion of multiple cervical segments
4. Forward translation of one vertebral segment over the one beneath it due to a pars defect
5. Seronegative spondyloarthropathy characterized by bridging spinal osteophytes

## Correct option

**2. Anterior wedging of >5 degrees across three consecutive vertebrae**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a clinical presentation of Scheuermann disease, which is classically described as anterior wedging of >5 degrees across three consecutive vertebrae.

Scheuermann kyphosis (SK) is a form of developmental kyphosis defined radiographically as the anterior wedging of ≥5° of ≥3 consecutive vertebral bodies with or without endplate irregularities and Schmorl’s nodes. The incidence of SK in the US is 0.4-8% and is most often diagnosed in children between the ages of 10 and 12, with a positive correlation between age and kyphosis angle. SK has a male predilection, with male-to-female ratios ranging between 2:1 and 7:1. A majority of patients will present due to progressive deformity, cosmetic reasons, or pain. It is rare to present with any neurologic complaints. Patients will often present in adolescence, with thoracic or thoracolumbar kyphosis.

*Palazzo et al. published a 2014 review on the presentation, pathophysiology, and treatment of SK.* It was first described in 1921 as a painful, fixed, dorsal kyphosis consisting of wedged vertebrae, with disturbances of the vertebral endplates, occurring in adolescents. *Sorensen later proposed the now widely accepted radiographic criteria of three adjacent wedged vertebrae, angled by at least 5.* For some authors, one wedged vertebra is sufficient to conclude a diagnosis of SK if associated with irregular vertebral endplates.

*O’Donnell et al. published an updated 2023 review on the current concepts and management of SK.* Treatment is based on curve magnitude and symptoms - more severe deformity has been associated with progression and pain. Nonoperative treatment consists of physical therapy in symptomatic patients, and bracing can be added for skeletally mature patients. Operative management can be considered in patients with large, progressive, and symptomatic deformities. Although no definitive curve magnitude threshold has been empirically determined, kyphosis greater than 70–80° is generally considered to be approaching the surgical range. Surgical options typically include posterior instrumentation and multi-level osteotomies for appropriate deformity correction.
