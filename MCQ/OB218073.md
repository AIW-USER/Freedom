## Question

A 27-year-old professional violinist falls down a rocky crag while hiking in Yosemite National Park and presents with the injury shown in Figure A. Which of the following would provide the best indication for the use of open reduction and internal fixation with a plate and screw construct in lieu of either cast immobilization or closed reduction and percutaneous pinning with Kirschner wires?

![[q218073-a-screenshot-2023-08-23-at-3-05-43-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Extra-articular nature of the fracture
2. Lack of comminution
3. Lack of rotational deformity or scissoring on exam
4. Less than 10 degrees of angulation and 2mm of shortening
5. Need for initiation of early range of motion

## Correct option

**5. Need for initiation of early range of motion**

## Answer explanation

Open reduction and internal fixation, when compared to either cast immobilization or closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) with Kirschner wires, allows for the initiation of early range of motion.

Phalanx fractures account for up to 10% of all fractures, with distal phalanx fractures being the most common, followed by middle and proximal phalanx fractures. When treating proximal phalanx fractures that are extra-articular, lack rotational deformity or scissoring on exam, and exhibit less than 10 degrees of angulation and 2mm of shortening, treatment can include either CRPP or cast immobilization if adequate alignment is able to be maintained. Long, oblique fractures such as the one in Figure A tend to fail closed reduction and internal fixation is indicated. In a professional violinist who requires expedient return to work with the use of her hands, internal fixation with the use of either lag screws alone or a combination plate and screw construct would allow for the initiation of early range of motion. Complications with plate fixation, however, are not uncommon, most notably that of stiffness, which is why expeditious implementation of aggressive hand therapy is needed.

Guerrero, et al. studied the complications associated with the use of low-profile plate fixation for phalanx fractures. Twenty-three patients were studied, of which 52.2% had a post-operative complication, the most common being return to the operating room for removal of hardware and tenolysis/capsulotomy due to stiffness (30.4%). The authors concluded that ORIF with low-profile anatomic plates consistently achieves union for phalanx fractures, but it does not appear to reduce high complication rates.

Henry, et al. discuss the preferred methods of fixation and stabilization for different types of phalanx fractures. In terms of proximal phalanx fractures that are long and oblique/spiral, the authors note that there is very little help from fracture fragment contact in preventing shortening unless significant interfragmentary compression can be achieved. Not only is this important in resisting the forces of early rehabilitation, but it is also essential in preventing extensor lag, which is predicted to be 12 degrees for each millimeter of shortening. Thus, open reduction internal fixation is recommended in this pattern to be able to initiate early range of motion protocols.

El-Saeed, et al. performed a randomized controlled trial to study the use of Kirschner wires versus titanium plates and screws in the management of unstable phalangeal fractures. Out of 40 patients studied, the authors found that fixation of unstable proximal and middle phalangeal fractures using a titanium plate and screws through a mid-lateral approach is a reliable and safe method for most fracture types and is associated with higher total active motion (TAM) and fewer complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: These are all indications for either closed management or CRPP in proximal phalanx fractures.
