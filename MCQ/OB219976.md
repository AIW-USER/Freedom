## Answer explanation

Given the patient's age, gender, BMI, and mechanism of injury, she most likely sustained a posterior root tear of the medial meniscus (Figure D).

Medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMPRT) are increasingly becoming recognized as degenerative tears of an older, predominantly female population. Associated factors include female gender, increased age, obesity, varus knee alignment, and a sedentary lifestyle. The mechanism of injury more commonly includes descending knee motions such as stairs or downhill slopes, but not necessarily motions with deep knee flexion as in other types of degenerative meniscal tears. Tears of the medial meniscus posterior root are functionally equivalent to a total meniscectomy, resulting in increased contact pressure, external rotation, and lateral tibial translation, all of which can be corrected with surgical repair. Given these factors, there has been an increasing trend toward surgical repair of root tears, even in older populations traditionally treated with partial meniscectomies.

*Hwang et al. reviewed the risk factors for medial meniscus posterior root tears.* *The authors reviewed 476 consecutive patients undergoing an arthroscopic procedure on their medial meniscus and found that 104 had a MMPRT, with female sex being associated with a 5.9-fold increase in risk, a varus mechanical axis angle with a 3.3-fold increase in risk, a BMI more than 30 kg/m2 with a 4.9-fold increase in risk, and a lower sports activity level with a 2.7-fold increase in risk.* **They concluded** that persons with an MMPRT had significantly increased age, female sex predominance, higher BMI, greater varus mechanical axis angle, and lower sports activity level compared with persons with other types of meniscal tears, while oriental postural positions including the lotus position and squatting showed no contribution to increased risk of MMPRT, suggesting that intrinsic risk factors are better predictors.

*Furumatsu et al. reviewed injury patterns associated with medial meniscus posterior root tears.* **The authors note** that MMPRTs typically occur in middle-aged patients who have posteromedial painful popping during light activities. The authors reviewed 100 patients with MRI-confirmed MMPRT after posteromedial painful popping episodes and found that descending knee motion was the most common cause of MMPRTs (38%) followed by a walking injury pattern (18%) and a squatting action related to high flexion activities of the knee (13%). **They concluded** that high flexion activities of the knee are not the greatest cause of MMPRTs, as in other types of meniscus tears, suggesting that the descending action with a low knee flexion angle may trigger minor traumatic MMPRTs in older, less active patients.

Figure A is a coronal STIR MRI sequence showing a bucketed medial meniscus flipped into the intercondylar notch. Figure B is an axial T2-weighted MRI sequence showing an oblique tear of the medial meniscus consistent with a parrot beak-type tear. Figure C is a T1-weighted sagittal MRI sequence showing a "double-PCL" sign, consistent with a bucket-handle tear of the medial meniscus. Figure D is a T2-weighted coronal MRI sequence showing a complete disruption of the posterior root of the medial meniscus. Figure E is a sagittal PD fat-saturated MRI sequence showing a vertical (longitudinal) tear of the peripheral capsular attachment of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus at the meniscocapsular junction, consistent with a ramp lesion. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Bucket-handle meniscus tears are often the sequelae of higher-energy injuries and occur more frequently with concomitant cruciate injuries, rather than as attritional injuries in low-impact individuals.
Answer 2: Parrot beak-type tears are more consistent with acute, twisting injuries, not low-impact sedentary injuries in older patients.
Answers 3 and 5: As above, bucket-handle meniscus tears and ramp lesions, respectively, occur with higher-energy injuries such as ACL tears.
