## Question

A 16-year-old female lacrosse player presents to your office one week after sustaining an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury during a game. On physical examination, the patient demonstrates a grade 2 pivot shift test, has generalized ligamentous laxity with a Beighton score of 6, and 11 degrees of recurvatum is present about her bilateral knees. The patient states that her goal is to return to lacrosse by next season. What additional procedure should be included at the time of this patient's ACL reconstruction to decrease the risk of persistent rotatory instability 2 years after surgery?

## Options

1. Avoidance of vertical tunnel position
2. Utilization of suspensory fixation
3. Repair any coinciding meniscal injury found intraoperatively
4. Perform a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET)
5. Utilize dual fixation with suspension and interference screws

## Correct option

**4. Perform a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET)**

## Answer explanation

This is a 16-year-old female who plays a contact pivoting sport with a Beighton score >4, >10 degrees of knee recurvatum, and a grade 2 pivot shift test indicating that this patient may be at risk for persistent rotatory laxity and/or early graft failure. To mitigate this concern, this patient's ACL reconstruction should be coupled with a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (Answer 4).

Lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) combined with ACL reconstruction significantly reduces failure rates, especially in high-risk patients aged 14–25 with two or more risk factors, including the return to contact pivoting sports, high-grade anterolateral rotatory laxity (pivot shift test of grade 2 or higher), and generalized ligamentous laxity (Beighton Score >4). Additional proposed indications to perform a LET include Segond fractures, chronic ACL lesions, lateral femoral notch signs, and lateral coronal plane laxity. The addition of extra-articular tenodesis to a single-bundle hamstring tendon autograft anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction in young patients at increased risk of failure show shown results of a statistically significant, clinically relevant reduction in graft rupture rates and persistent rotatory laxity at 2 years after surgery.

*Getgood et al. completed a randomized controlled trial (STABILITY Study) investigating the hypothesis that a single-bundle, hamstring ACL reconstruction in combination with a LET would reduce the risk of ACLR failure in young, active individuals.* **They found** that The ACLR+LET group had a lower clinical failure rate (25% vs. 40%) and fewer graft ruptures (4% vs. 11%) over two years. However, the ACLR group reported less pain in the early postoperative period and overall patient-reported outcomes and sports activity levels were similar between groups at two years.

*Hurley et al. reviewed the rates of return to play after ACLR with extra-articular augmentation (EA).* **They state** ACLR + EA leads to high return-to-play rates in both primary (82.8%–100%) and revision (50%–88.4%) surgeries, with many athletes returning to the same or higher level of play. Comparative studies show similar overall return-to-play rates between ACLR with and without EA, though some studies report higher rates of returning to the same level with EA. **The authors conclude** that ACLR + EA results in high levels of return to play in both the primary and revision settings.

*Getgood et al. completed a randomized control trial assessing the functional outcomes of patients with ACLR + LET when compared to ACLR alone.* In the Stability Study, adding LET to ACLR led to slightly increased pain, reduced quadriceps strength, and lower self-reported function compared to ACLR alone during the first 6 months postoperatively. However, these differences were resolved by 12 months, with no significant differences in objective functional outcomes, including the Limb Symmetry Index from hop testing, at 6, 12, or 24 months. Overall, LET did not negatively impact long-term functional recovery compared to ACLR alone.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Avoiding a vertical tunnel position can decrease the risk of persistent rotatory instability, however, this is not considered an additional procedure.
Answer 2: Using suspensory fixation maximizes the amount of graft in the femoral tunnel while providing secure fixation and excellent tensile strength. However, suspensory fixation is not associated with decreasing the risk of persistent rotatory instability greater than 2 years postoperatively from ACLR.
Answer 3: Clinically significant meniscal tears may be repaired at the time of ACLR, however, doing so is not associated with decreasing the risk of persistent rotatory instability greater than 2 years postoperatively from ACLR.
Answer 5: Dual fixation (suspensory fixation + interference screw) provides strong fixation that may minimize the risk of graft slippage or movement. However, dual fixation is not associated with decreasing the risk of persistent rotatory instability greater than 2 years postoperatively from ACLR.
