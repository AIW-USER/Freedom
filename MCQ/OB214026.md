## Question

In Figure A, injury to which labeled structure, paired with the correct Seddon type, will prevent full peripheral nerve recovery?

![[q214026-a-screen-shot-2020-05-03-at-3-30-30-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. A; Axonotmesis
2. A; Neurotmesis
3. B; Neurapraxia
4. B; Axonotmesis
5. C; Axonotmesis

## Correct option

**2. A; Neurotmesis**

## Answer explanation

Letter A corresponds to the endoneurium, which must be intact to allow for a full recovery of an injured peripheral nerve. Neurotmesis refers to an injury that damages the epineurium, perineurium, and endoneurium (3rd-5th degree Sunderland).

Peripheral nerves are arranged so that the outer epineurium encircles fascicles, the fascicles are surrounded by perineurium, and the individual axons are surrounded by endoneurium. The epineurium is typically the focus of surgical repair, the perineuralial layer is the major contributor to tensile strength, and the endoneurium provides nourishment to the axons. Damage to the endoneurium is the most severe form of injury. The Seddon and Sunderland classifications describe these injuries and can help guide treatment and prognosis.

*Pogetti et al. provide a review article comparing autologous nerve grafts, the gold standard for repairing large nerve defects, to nanoscaffold surfaces and magnetic nanoparticles.* *The authors describe the technical limits of autologous nerve grafts, including donor morbidity, surgical access, and finite graft availability.* **They suggest** that nanotechnology holds promise in providing physical stimuli for nerve regeneration support.

*Lee et al. provide a general review of peripheral nerve injury and repair.* *The authors describe the discouraging results associated with immediate epineurial repair, reporting in general only 50% of patients regaining useful function.* Specific to endoneurium disruption, **they highlight** that full recovery is impaired by inevitable intrafascicular fibrosis, which hinders axonal regeneration.
