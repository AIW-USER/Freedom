## Question

A 28-year-old orthopedic resident is rock climbing on his post-call day. He lost his grip, slipped, and suffered a large laceration to the ulnar side of his proximal forearm. As an astute resident, he evaluates his wound and realizes that he has severed his ulnar nerve. He lacks sensation over the ulnar side of his hand. However, he is able to extend his thumb, flex his second interphalangeal joint, and abduct and adduct his fingers. What nerve anastomosis does he most likely have?

## Options

1. Berrettini
2. Linburg-Comstock
3. Martin-Gruber
4. Marinacci
5. Riche-Cannieu

## Correct option

**3. Martin-Gruber**

## Answer explanation

This patient is firing musculature that is typically supplied by the ulnar nerve (intrinsics), despite a high ulnar nerve laceration. This can occur in the setting of a Martin-Gruber anastomosis, the most common anomalous innervation pattern in the forearm (Answer choice 3).

A Martin-Gruber anastomosis (MGA) is a motor nerve interconnection between the median nerve and the ulnar nerve in the proximal forearm. This anastomosis can confound clinical assessment and nerve conduction study interpretations in patients with median or ulnar nerve injuries. In an MGA, median nerve axons will leave the trunk of the median (or anterior interosseous) nerve and join the main trunk of the ulnar nerve, innervating intrinsic muscles of the hand. Sensory fibers are typically spared. The crossover usually occurs in the proximal 1/3 of the forearm. The reported incidence ranges from 15 to 40% of patients, more commonly on the right side. Multiple patterns of innervation have been described and produce variable patterns of innervation of the intrinsic muscles of the hand.

*Smith et al. performed a review of median and ulnar nerve anastomoses in the forearm and hand.* *The authors discussed the Martin-Gruber, Marinacci, Rich-Cannieu, and Berrettini anastomoses.* **The authors report** a prevalence of 19.5% and describe the four subtypes of MGA and describe the varying clinical pictures. The Marinacci anastomosis is reported as the rarest of median-ulnar interconnections (0.7%) and is often described as the reverse Martin-Gruber due to the proximal branching of ulnar nerve fibers to join the median nerve distally. The Riche-Cannieu anastomosis occurs in the hand with fibers from the recurrent branch of the median nerve connecting with the deep branch of the ulnar nerve in three different presentations. The Berrettini anastomosis is a common sensory anastomosis between the common digital nerves of the ulnar and median nerves, most commonly at the fourth and third common digital nerves. **The authors concluded** that knowledge of these four anastomoses is critical for appropriate diagnosis and avoidance of iatrogenic injury.

*Martin et al. performed a review of electrophysiologic findings in median-ulnar nerve connections.* *The authors also reviewed the four main median-ulnar anastomoses in the forearm and hand: Martin Gruber, Marinacci, Rich-Cannieu, and Berrettini.* **The authors concluded** that the presence of these anomalous interconnections can lead to misdiagnosis during electrodiagnostic tests and iatrogenic injuries during surgical procedures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A Berrettini anastomosis is the most common sensory interconnection of the anomalies, with an incidence of over 80% in some studies. The anastomosis features a purely sensory connection of the common digital nerves of the ulnar and median nerves, most commonly the third and fourth digital nerves in the palm. Clinically, this interconnection can be damaged during a carpal tunnel release, resulting in decreased sensation to the third webspace.
Answer 2: Linburg-Comstock variation is not a median-ulnar anastomosis but a tendinous connection between the flexor pollicis longus and the flexor digitorum profundus to the index finger. This variation is typically asymptomatic but has been proposed to increase susceptibility to carpal tunnel syndrome with overuse. The symptoms of Linburg-Comstock would not produce the findings in our vignette.
Answer 4: The Marinacci anastomosis is known as the reverse Martin-Gruber anastomosis. This features ulnar nerve fibers that branch proximally to join the median nerve distally in the forearm. The reported prevalence is 4%. Clinically, this can present as a proximal median nerve lesion but with the retained function of distal median nerve muscles (i.e., abductor pollicis brevis). This would not show continued innervation of ulnar musculature in a high ulnar nerve lesion, as is the case in our patient.
Answer 5: Riche-Cannieu anastomosis (RCA) is an interconnection between the recurrent branch of the median nerve and the deep branch of the ulnar nerve with a prevalence of 55%. This connection can carry motor and sensory fibers. The three possible clinical presentations of RCA include a hand entirely supplied by the ulnar nerve, a hand with motor fibers only from the ulnar nerve, or a hand with normally median-supplied muscles innervated by the ulnar nerve.
