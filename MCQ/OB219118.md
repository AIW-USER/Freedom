## Question

A 33-year-old male is brought to the emergency department following an injury sustained while free solo rock climbing one hour ago. His initial radiographs are shown in Figure A, and he is reduced under conscious sedation in the trauma bay. Following reduction, he complains of motor and sensory deficits in the affected extremity. Given the pattern of his injury, which of the following structures is most likely affected?

![[q219118-a-xray-lat-knee-dislocation-emedicine-moved.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Primary muscle affected in adult-acquired flatfoot deformity
2. Muscle originating from the lateral fibular surface and intermuscular septa of the leg and inserting on the 5th metatarsal base
3. Muscle originating from the lateral surface of the lateral femoral condyle and inserting on the middle 1/3 of the posterior calcaneal surface
4. Nerve providing sensation along the plantar aspect of the heel
5. Nerve providing sensation along the lateral border of the forefoot

## Correct option

**2. Muscle originating from the lateral fibular surface and intermuscular septa of the leg and inserting on the 5th metatarsal base**

## Answer explanation

The patient described above has sustained an anterior knee dislocation, shown in Figure A. Anterior dislocations have a high association with peroneal nerve injury (25%, range 16-40%), with tibial nerve injuries being less common. While pain due to injury may weaken all muscle groups around the knee compared to the contralateral, unaffected extremity, a peroneal nerve injury would affect the peroneus brevis, manifesting as weakness with eversion.

Knee dislocations are one of the few orthopaedic emergencies, and require emergent attention due to the high rate of concomitant vascular injury. Dislocations may be described in a descriptive manner using the Kennedy classification, or based on the pattern of multiligamentous injury using the Schenck classification. Regardless of the classification system used, 50% of knee dislocation spontaneously reduce prior to arrival to the emergency department, so a heightened clinical suspicion and careful physical exam is crucial to diagnosis and management. Ankle-brachial indices (ABI) and subsequent advanced imaging (CTA, angiogram) in the setting of ABI <0.9 are critical to detecting vascular injury. In addition to a high risk of vascular injury, injury to the surrounding major nerves is of concern as well, with the common peroneal nerve (CPN) being the most common. The common peroneal nerve travels posteroinferior along the biceps femoris muscle and courses anteriorly at the level of the fibular neck, and it has several motor and sensory functions. The deep branch (DPN) innervates the muscles of the anterior compartment of the leg and the intrinsic foot extensors while providing sensation to the 1st webspace of the foot, and the superficial branch (SPN) innervates the lateral compartment of the leg while providing sensation to the majority of the dorsum of the foot.

*Woodmass et al. published a 2015 review on peroneal nerve injuries following traumatic knee dislocations.* *The authors sought to determine the rate of CPN palsy following knee dislocation, report the overall and treatment-specific rates of functional recovery following CPN palsy, and identify factors prognostic for CPN recovery.* Functional recovery (motor strength ≥3/5) following complete CPN palsy was 38.4 %, whereas full recovery (motor strength = 5/5) following partial CPN palsy was 87.3 %. Younger age was predictive of neurologic recovery, and recovery following isolated neurologic interventions remained poor, ranging from 0-30 %.

*Poage et al. published a 2016 review on peroneal nerve palsy evaluation and management.* Citing compressive etiologies as the most common cause, traumatic causes such as knee dislocations, direct blunt trauma, lacerations, and severe ankle inversions are associated with poorer outcomes. **The authors recommend** against immediate EMGs in the setting of traumatic injury or postoperative palsy, and note that they should not be performed for 2 to 6 weeks to allow any acute neural shock to resolve completely. The authors describe comprehensive decompression through release of the overlying fascia and the three underlying intermuscular septa for atraumatic CPN palsy; however refractory cases may require transfer of the posterior tibial tendon (PTT) to the lateral cuneiform or cuboid, which removes the primary deforming force to restore ankle dorsiflexion.

*Boyce et al. published a 2015 review on acute management of knee dislocations.* *The authors mention that CPN palsies occur in approximately 25% of knee dislocations, with a generally poor overall prognosis, and the return of nerve function is equivocal at best, with rates averaging 50%.* In most cases the CPN palsy is secondary to a traction neuropraxia rather than complete transection; therefore patients may be fitted with ankle-foot-orthoses (AFOs) and undergo aggressive physical therapy to prevent equinus contractures. In the absence of an obvious transection through an open wound, post-traumatic CPN palsy alone is not an indication for acute exploration. If the CPN palsy persists at the time of definitive reconstruction of the multiligamentous knee injury, nerve exploration and neurolysis may be indicated for adherent hematoma and fibrosis. If no clinical or electrical evidence of nerve continuity exists by 3 months, surgical intervention for direct or intercalary nerve repair is indicated.
