## Question

A 16-year-old soccer player presents to your orthopedic urgent care for evaluation of three weeks of intermittent pain in the bilateral knees, left shoulder, and right elbow. He has been training for his upcoming season, but has not had any specific trauma or injuries. He denies experiencing any pain, clicking, catching, or locking in his knee, or any symptoms of instability. Physical exam and radiographs of the involved joints are unremarkable. However, he does have a rash on the posterior left thigh, as pictured in Figure A. What is the mechanism of action for the most appropriate initial treatment for the most likely diagnosis?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Inhibition of DNA synthesis via disruption of tetrahydrofolate production
2. Inhibition of bacterial cell wall synthesis via direct binding to D-ala-D-ala protein sequences
3. Halting of DNA replication via inhibition of DNA gyrase
4. Inhibition of protein synthesis via prevention of tRNA binding at the 30S ribosomal subunit
5. Inhibition of protein synthesis via binding of the 50S ribosomal subunit and blockage of peptide translocation

## Correct option

**4. Inhibition of protein synthesis via prevention of tRNA binding at the 30S ribosomal subunit**

## Answer explanation

This patient with a large erythematous rash with central clearing and acute polyarthralgia without antecedent trauma likely has acute localized Lyme disease. The first-line treatment for this condition is considered to be oral doxycycline, amoxicillin, or cefuroxime. Only doxycycline is described in the available answer choices; it inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit, thereby preventing binding of tRNA (Answer choice 4).

Lyme Disease is a systemic infection caused by the tick-borne bacterium Borrelia burgdorferi, typically presenting with flu-like symptoms, a characteristic expanding rash called erythema migrans, and potentially progressing to involve the joints, nervous system, or heart. Diagnosis is based on a history of potential tick exposure in endemic areas, the presence of erythema migrans, and is confirmed by serologic testing, typically a two-step process. Treatment generally involves oral antibiotics for early localized or mild disseminated disease, with intravenous antibiotics reserved for more severe cases, recalcitrance to oral antibiotics, or late-stage manifestations. The most common oral agent utilized is doxycycline, although amoxicillin and cefuroxime axetil are also considered appropriate first-line agents. While persistent joint pain can occur, early and appropriate antibiotic therapy is crucial to mitigate the risk of developing chronic Lyme arthritis and associated disabling musculoskeletal issues.

*Nguyen et al. reviewed the 2020 guidelines for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of Lyme Disease, developed as a collective effort by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), American Academy of Neurology (AAN), and American College of Rheumatology (ACR), summarizing major recommendations based on the available evidence.* **They found** strong recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis with a single dose of oral doxycycline within 72 hours of a high-risk tick bite and for treating erythema migrans with a 10-day course of oral doxycycline or a 14-day course of amoxicillin or cefuroxime axetil. **They concluded** that their guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations for managing various presentations of Lyme disease, including acute neurologic manifestations and Lyme arthritis. **They also noted** that additional antibiotic therapy is not recommended for persistent, nonspecific symptoms without evidence of reinfection or treatment failure.

*Schoen reviewed advances and challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of Lyme disease since its recognition,* **noting** its status as the most common vector-borne illness in North America and Europe with well-characterized clinical features. **He found** that Lyme disease occurs in distinct stages, with clinical expression influenced by Borrelial variability, and that early localized disease is often cured with a single course of oral antibiotics, contrasting with Lyme arthritis, which may require other treatment strategies. **He concluded** that successful management begins with accurate diagnosis and an understanding of the disease's emergence, clinical features, and impact over the past half century.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is a two-agent antibiotic that inhibits DNA synthesis via disruption of tetrahydrofolate production. It is sometimes employed for treatment of skin and soft tissue infections, typically in cases where MRSA is suspected.
Answer choice 2: Vancomycin inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis via direct binding to D-ala-D-ala protein sequences. It is typically reserved for intravenous administration, most commonly in cases necessitating MRSA coverage.
Answer choice 3: Fluoroquinolones halt DNA replication by inhibition of DNA gyrase. These provide broad coverage and are utilized for a variety of pulmonary or genitourinary infections, among others; however, they have a less favorable side effect profile and thus have diminished in utilization in recent years.
Answer choice 5: Macrolides inhibit protein synthesis via binding of the 50S ribosomal subunit and blocking peptide translocation. These are not considered first-line therapy, but may be employed in cases where patients are unable to tolerate the aforementioned first-line agents.
