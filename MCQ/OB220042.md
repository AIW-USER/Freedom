## Answer explanation

The skeletally immature patient in Figure B has developed a physeal bar encompassing >50% of the distal radial physis, resulting in an ulnar-positive variance. This condition is best treated with a distal ulnar epiphysiodesis (Answer 2).

Distal radius fractures are the most common fracture in the skeletally immature population younger than 16. Physeal fractures are classified by the Salter-Harris system and are at a higher risk of premature physeal arrest. Risk factors for premature physeal arrest are recurrent injuries, late presentation, late reduction attempts, and higher severity physeal fractures (Salter-Harris III and IV). Changes in ulnar variance can be observed 3-4 months following injury, so close follow-up is critical to detecting physeal bars or complete arrests that may require early intervention. Treatment depends on the degree of physeal involvement but may involve a bar resection or a completion epiphysiodesis of the distal radial physis with or without a concomitant distal ulnar epiphysiodesis. Patients approaching skeletal maturity or who are already skeletally mature may not have enough remaining growth potential to benefit from guided growth, and may instead require an ulnar-shortening osteotomy to offload an ulnar-positive wrist.

*Dr. Samora published a literature review describing the indications for treating ulnar-positive wrists in pediatric patients.* The literature recommends that for high-risk fracture patterns with a high risk of potential growth arrest, repeat radiographs should be obtained 3 to 6 months after the initial injury and every 3 to 6 months thereafter until normal growth is established by a parallel Park-Harris line through the metaphysis. Patients with a growth arrest of the distal radius may present with ulnar-sided wrist pain and sometimes a prominence of the ulnar head. Treatment options include observation, physeal bar resection/interposition, epiphysiodesis (ulna ± radius completion), ulnar shortening osteotomy (USO), radial osteotomy, and distraction osteogenesis. Decision-making is multifactorial and must consider patient age, remaining growth potential, location of injury within the physis, size of physeal bar, angular deformity, and individual patient and family desires.

*Campbell et al. published a case series on the success of distal ulnar epiphysiodesis.* *A chart review was conducted on 31 wrists in 30 patients over a span of 11 years, identifying 2 failures of primary epiphysiodesis, indicating an index success rate of 93.7%.* **The authors concluded** that ulnar epiphysiodesis successfully terminates ulnar physeal growth in 93.7% of cases. Preoperative symptoms were completely resolved with a median physeal closure of just over 4 months, and ulnar variance was corrected on average by 4.1 mm when a radial or ulnar shortening osteotomy was performed at the time of epiphysiodesis.

Figure D is an MRI of ulnar impaction syndrome in a skeletally mature patient. Figure E is a radiograph of Madelung deformity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A skeletally mature patient with ulnar-positive variance would best benefit from an ulnar-shortening osteotomy, as there is no remaining growth potential for guided growth.
Answer 3: Kienbock disease is not treated with ulnar epiphysiodesis.
Answer 4: Ulnar impaction syndrome in a skeletally mature patient is best treated with an unloading procedure such as an ulnar shortening osteotomy.
Answer 5: Symptomatic Madelung deformity failing nonoperative management may be treated either with a release of the pathologic Vickers ligament or a radial ± ulnar osteotomy.
