## Answer explanation

Radial nerve palsies are characteristically common in the setting of distal 1/3 spiral humeral shaft fractures (i.e. Holstein Lewis fracture; Answer 2).

Humeral shaft fractures are well known to present with clinically apparent radial nerve palsy, reported in approximately 10-15% of cases. This common involvement is owed to the ~6.5cm length of diaphyseal bone where the radial nerve lies in direct proximity within the spiral groove. The incidence rate has been reportedly even higher when the fracture is located at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the humeral shaft (~20-25%). This may be due to tensional stress at the junction where the nerve separates from the spiral groove to pierce the lateral muscular septum. It may also be secondary to entrapment of the nerve within the fracture site. For these reasons (and to avoid functionally limiting deformity from fracture displacement of distal fractures), many suggest that Holstein-Lewis fractures serve as a strong indication for ORIF and radial nerve exploration as compared to a standard period of observation employed for most mid-diaphyseal humeral shaft fractures.

*Ring and colleagues performed a case series review regarding radial nerve palsy associated with high-energy humeral shaft fractures at their institution.* *Of the 24 patients included,* **the authors found** that neurotmesis was associated with complex open fractures with nerve repair resulting in poor recovery (likely related to shortened stumps requiring grafting rather than primary neurorrhaphy). **They also found** that even in high-energy injuries with complex closed fractures, nearly all had complete recovery of the radial nerve, albeit over the course of many months. **The authors concluded** by endorsing the refrainment of nerve exploration in closed fracture settings unless operating fixation is also required.

*Shao and colleagues performed a systematic review of radial nerve palsy associated with fractures of the humerus.* *Of the 35 papers (n=1045 patients),* **they found** that fractures at the junction between the middle and distal 1/3 (i.e. Holstein Lewis fractures) had the highest association with radial nerve palsy compared to other anatomic regions. **They also reported** that early vs. late exploration did not result in significantly different long-term patient outcomes and suggested that a period of watchful waiting be employed in the setting of closed humeral fractures with concomitant radial nerve palsy. **The authors concluded** by proposing their recommended treatment algorithm for the management of these injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The most common neuropraxia associated with flexion-type supracondylar fractures involves the ulnar nerve.
Answer 3: While extension-type supracondylar fractures with posteromedial displacement may present with radial nerve palsy, the most common neuropraxia associated with these fractures involves the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN). Further, radial neuropraxia is infrequently encountered (<5% of supracondylar humerus fractures) compared to the incidence seen in Holstein-Lewis distal humerus fractures (20-25%).
Answer 4: The most common neuropraxia associated with Monteggia fractures involves the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN). Of note, the sensory branch of the radial nerve and motor innervation to the extensor carpi radialis muscles are proximal and typically spared.
Answer 5: The most common neuropraxia associated with perilunate dislocations involves the median nerve.
