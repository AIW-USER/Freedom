## Question

When evaluating sub-types and variants of osteosarcoma, which of the following clinicopathological correlations is CORRECT?

## Options

1. Periosteal osteosarcoma is an intermediate-grade surface tumor predominantly composed of a malignant chondroid matrix with a strong propensity for early systemic metastasis.
2. Gnathaic osteosarcomas (arising in the jaw) occur predominantly in the elderly and exhibit a much higher rate of early pulmonary metastasis compared to extremity tumors.
3. Small cell osteosarcoma morphologically mimics Ewing sarcoma but can be differentiated by its uniform, strong diffuse positivity for CD99.
4. Parosteal osteosarcoma is a high-grade surface lesion that typically arises from the anterior aspect of the mid-shaft tibia and carries a poor prognosis.

## Correct option

**1. Periosteal osteosarcoma is an intermediate-grade surface tumor predominantly composed of a malignant chondroid matrix with a strong propensity for early systemic metastasis.**

## Answer explanation

Periosteal osteosarcoma is an intermediate-grade surface tumor dominated by chondroblastic differentiation, but it carries a significantly better prognosis and lower metastatic rate than conventional high-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma.

Types of osteosarcomas:

Osteosarcoma is characterized by a high degree of heterogeneity. The definitive histological hallmark across all variants is the direct production of malignant osteoid (unmineralized bone matrix) or woven bone by highly pleomorphic, malignant stromal cells.

The histopathological features of osteosarcoma are classified based on their anatomic location (intramedullary vs. surface) and their predominant matrix/cellular morphology.

I. Conventional High-Grade Intramedullary Osteosarcoma

This accounts for approximately 75% to 80% of all cases. Within this category, tumors are subclassified based on the dominant extracellular matrix produced by the malignant cells.

1. Osteoblastic Osteosarcoma (Most Common)

 - Matrix: Abundant production of dense, lace-like, mesh-like, or sheet-like unmineralized osteoid. This matrix often directly entraps the malignant cells.

 - Cellular Morphology: Highly pleomorphic, hyperchromatic malignant osteoblasts with frequent, atypical mitotic figures. Cells often display an eccentric nucleus and abundant amphophilic cytoplasm (plasmacytoid appearance).

 - Architecture: The malignant osteoid is deposited along pre-existing lamellar trabeculae, often inciting a destructive, permeative pattern that overrides host bone.

2. Chondroblastic Osteosarcoma

 - Matrix: Dominated by a dense, hyaline cartilage or chondromyxoid matrix.

 - Cellular Morphology: Malignant chondrocytes reside within distinct lacunae, but they exhibit severe nuclear atypia, binucleation, and pleomorphism.

 - Key Diagnostic Nuance: To differentiate it from a primary chondrosarcoma, the pathologist must locate focal areas where highly atypical spindle cells are directly producing lace-like osteoid without an intervening cartilaginous template.

3. Fibroblastic Osteosarcoma

 - Matrix: The matrix is predominantly collagenous, with minimal or very sparse osteoid deposition.

 - Cellular Morphology: Dominated by highly malignant spindle cells.

 - Architecture: The cells are typically arranged in intersecting fascicles, forming a classic herringbone
 or storiform pattern, strongly mimicking a high-grade fibrosarcoma or undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS). Careful sampling is required to identify the diagnostic focal osteoid.

II. Distinct Intramedullary Variants

1. Telangiectatic Osteosarcoma

 - Architecture: Grossly and microscopically characterized by large, cyst-like, blood-filled aneurysmal spaces. It can easily be misdiagnosed as an Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC) on low power.

 - Septal Morphology: The diagnostic fields reside entirely within the thin septa flanking these blood spaces. The septa contain highly malignant, hyperchromatic, pleomorphic cells, bizarre giant cells, and atypical mitotic figures.

 - Matrix: Extremely delicate, thin strands of lace-like osteoid are identifiable within the sarcomatous septa.

2. Small Cell Osteosarcoma

 - Cellular Morphology: Composed of uniform sheets of small, round, blue cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios. It mimics Ewing sarcoma or lymphoma.

 - Matrix: Distinct, fine tracks of lace-like osteoid production can be seen weaving between the sheets of small round cells.

 - Immunohistochemistry (IHC): Unlike Ewing sarcoma, it typically lacks diffuse, strong membranous CD99 positivity and does not possess the EWSR1 gene translocation. It is positive for SATB2.

3. Epithelioid Osteosarcoma

 - Cellular Morphology: Mimics a metastatic carcinoma or melanoma. Cells are large, polygonal, or round, arranged in nests, cords, or cohesive sheets, featuring abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli.

 - Diagnostic Clue: Direct production of osteoid by these epithelioid cells, along with SATB2 positivity and lack of true epithelial ultrastructural markers.

4. Low-Grade Central Osteosarcoma

 - Architecture: A well-differentiated, indolent intramedullary tumor that mimics fibrous dysplasia. It exhibits a permeative growth pattern, replacing normal marrow and entrapping host lamellar bone trabeculae.

 - Cellular Morphology: Composed of bland, uniform spindle cells showing minimal cytologic atypia and rare, normal mitoses.

 - Matrix: Abundant dense, parallel, or irregular woven bone trabeculae.

 - Molecular Genetics: Characterized by MDM2 and CDK4 gene amplification (often on supernumerary ring chromosomes), which reliably differentiates it from benign fibrous dysplasia.

III. Surface (Juxtacortical) Osteosarcomas

Surface tumors develop on the external cortex of the bone and are classified strictly by their grade and specific matrix features.

1. Parosteal Osteosarcoma (Low-Grade Surface)

 - Architecture: A heavily mineralized, exophytic mass arising from the outer cortex. On low power, it shows well-formed, parallel, heavily mineralized lamellar-like bone trabeculae separated by a spindle cell stroma.

 - Cellular Morphology: The intervening stroma consists of bland, uniform spindle cells lacking significant nuclear pleomorphism or atypia.

 - Periphery: Often capped by a cartilaginous cap or hypercellular zone that can mimic an osteochondroma.

 - Molecular Genetics: Similar to low-grade central osteosarcoma, it shows MDM2/CDK4 amplification, distinguishing it from fibrous dysplasia or myositis ossificans.

2. Periosteal Osteosarcoma (Intermediate-Grade Surface)

 - Architecture: Arises beneath the periosteum, typically elevating it. It characteristically exhibits a lobulated architecture separated by thin fibrous bands.

 - Matrix: Strongly chondroblastic
 in nature. It is dominated by intermediate-grade malignant cartilage.

 - Cellular Morphology: The cells within the lacunae are atypical and pleomorphic, but to a lesser degree than conventional high-grade chondroblastic osteosarcoma.

 - Osteoid: Fine, lace-like osteoid matrix is identified strictly in a fine, permeative pattern between the cartilaginous lobules or perpendicular to the cortex.

3. High-Grade Surface Osteosarcoma

 - Histopathology: Morphologically indistinguishable from conventional high-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma (can be osteoblastic, chondroblastic, or fibroblastic).

 - Distinction: It is defined purely by its anatomical restriction to the bone surface, with minimal or no invasion of the underlying medullary canal.

IV. Secondary Osteosarcomas

These arise within pre-existing bone pathology and are histologically high-grade, often showing highly pleomorphic, bizarre cells.

 - Pagetoid Osteosarcoma: Arises in the setting of long-standing Paget’s disease. The background bone shows the characteristic "mosaic pattern" of irregular cement lines, abruptly overtaken by highly anaplastic, osteoid-producing sarcomatous cells.

 - Post-Radiation Osteosarcoma: Develops years after therapeutic radiation. Features a highly pleomorphic, fibroblastic, or osteoblastic high-grade pattern, frequently demonstrating bizarre multi-nucleated tumor giant cells and extensive radiation-induced stromal atypia/necrosis.
