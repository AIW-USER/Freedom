## Question

A 49-year-old woman with stage 4 breast cancer presents for evaluation of thoracic back pain for 2 months. She states that the pain is episodic but is not associated with relief with recumbency or worsening with movement. She is currently undergoing treatment with Trastuzumab-Pertuzumab. Advanced imaging is performed, and a metastatic lesion is noted involving her T6 vertebrae. Which of the following additional combinations of lesion characteristics and imaging findings would MOST likely lead to a surgical referral for possible operative stabilization of the spine?

## Options

1. Blastic metastatic lesion, 30% vertebral body collapse, normal spinal alignment, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle
2. Lytic metastatic lesion, no vertebral body collapse, normal spinal alignment, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle
3. Mixed metastatic lesion, 30% vertebral body collapse, focal kyphosis at T6, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle
4. Mixed metastatic lesion, 30% vertebral body collapse, normal spinal alignment, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle
5. Mixed metastatic lesion, 60% vertebral body collapse, normal spinal alignment, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle

## Correct option

**3. Mixed metastatic lesion, 30% vertebral body collapse, focal kyphosis at T6, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle**

## Answer explanation

A patient with a lytic lesion of the T6 vertebrae with occasional non-mechanical pain, 30% vertebral body collapse, focal kyphosis at T6, and no posterolateral involvement of the facet/pedicle has a Spinal Instability Neoplasia Score (SINS) of 7, which is classified as a potentially unstable spine. Patients with potentially unstable spines should receive a surgical referral for possible operative stabilization (Answer 3).

The SINS is a scoring system designed to classify and categorize spinal instability in the setting of metastatic disease of the spine. Points are assigned in distinct sub-categories, which include location, pain relief with recumbency and/or pain with movement/loading of the spine, type of osseous metastatic lesion, presence of spinal malalignment, degree of vertebral body collapse, and involvement of the posterolateral spinal elements. Scores of 6 and below suggest the presence of a stable spine, scores of 7 to 12 suggest the presence of a potentially unstable spine, and scores of 13 to 18 suggest the presence of an unstable spine. Patients in the potentially unstable or unstable category should be referred for surgical evaluation.

*Fox et al. assessed the reliability of the SINS score in 25 orthopaedic and neurosurgery trainees.* **They found** high levels of inter- and intra-observer reliability suggestive of close to perfect agreement with total SINS scores. All six SINS score components, except bone quality, demonstrated inter- and intra-observer reliability of moderate to near-perfect agreement. **The authors concluded** that the SINS is a reliable tool for trainees to assess instability in patients with spinal neoplasia.

*Campos et al. assessed the reliability of the SINS score in orthopaedic and non-orthopaedic oncology specialists and reviewed the correlation of these cases’ scores with patients’ actual treatment.* **They found** excellent levels of inter- and intra-observer reliability with total SINS scores. Of the six components of the SINS score, location demonstrated the highest level of agreement across study participants. **They found** no difference in the frequency of surgery between patients with scores indicative of stable lesions (SINS > 6), patients with scores indicative of potentially unstable lesions (SINS 7-12), and patients with scores indicative of unstable lesions (SINS > 12) (40% vs 50% vs 100%, p = 0.06) The authors concluded that the SINS score is a useful tool to assess for instability in patients with spinal metastasis and can be utilized by both orthopaedic and non-orthopaedic practitioners.
