## Question

A 28-year-old female was involved in a bike crash and sustained an open talar neck fracture as demonstrated in Figure A. She was taken for debridement and reduction within 24 hours of injury. Due to hemodynamic instability from abdominal trauma, definitive open reduction and internal fixation were not performed until one week later via a lateral approach. At her three-month follow-up, sclerosis and articular collapse are noted on radiographic evaluation. Which of the following factors placed this patient at increased risk of developing this complication?

![[q220558-a-hawkins-4.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Delayed definitive fixation
2. Presence of other traumatic injuries
3. Lateral approach
4. Open fracture
5. Time to initial reduction

## Correct option

**4. Open fracture**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation and radiographs demonstrate an extruded talus with an associated talar neck fracture that subsequently developed talar avascular necrosis (AVN). The presence of an open injury is an independent risk factor for the development of talar AVN (Answer 4).

Fractures of the talar neck typically result from a high-energy dorsiflexion force combined with axial loading of the hindfoot. The presence of an open injury is an independent risk factor for the development of talar AVN. For displaced fractures, closed treatment has a limited role, as optimal management involves open reduction and internal fixation to restore anatomic alignment. Previous studies have shown improved restoration of angulation, length, and rotation with the use of two incisions at the time of definitive fixation. However, surgeons should avoid dissection superiorly and inferiorly to the talar neck, which may compromise the remaining blood supply.

*Alley et al. performed a retrospective, multicenter study assessing talar neck and/or body fractures in 798 patients.* **The authors found** that several factors place the patient at increased risk of developing osteonecrosis, including more severe Hawkins classification (Hawkins IIB, III, or IV), combined neck and body fractures, tobacco use, open fracture, dual surgical approach, increased age, and elevated body mass index. After excluding patients who had a dislocation for >7 days, there was no difference in rates of AVN for patients with Hawkins type IIB-IV neck injury if reduction was delayed longer than 12 hours. Furthermore, staged treatment with or without external fixation was not a risk factor for AVN.

*Vallier et al. retrospectively reviewed 102 talar neck fractures treated with open reduction and internal fixation.* **The authors found** that 19 patients developed radiographic evidence of osteonecrosis, with spontaneous revascularization of the talar dome in 7 of these patients. Talar AVN was associated with comminution of the talar neck and open fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Staged treatment with adequate initial reduction (with or without external fixation) followed by definitive open reduction and internal fixation is not associated with AVN.
Answer 2: Associated injuries and polytrauma status do not place the patient at higher risk for talar AVN.
Answer 3: Use of a dual-incision approach, rather than an isolated lateral approach, is associated with talar AVN.
Answer 5: Excluding patients with peritalar dislocation >7 days, time to reduction greater than 12 hours does not increase the risk of developing talar AVN.
