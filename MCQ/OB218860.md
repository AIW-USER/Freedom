## Question

Late at night while on call, you receive a call from the emergency department about a six-month-old who sustained the injury in Figure A. The father reported the child fell while running after the family dog. The ER provider says it is a closed injury and he already splinted it appropriately but wants help establishing a follow-up visit. What should you tell the ER provider?

![[q218860-a-peds-femur.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Have the patient follow up in my clinic in 1 week when you will transition him to a cast
2. To have the patient admitted for surgical fixation tomorrow morning
3. The patient should be admitted for metabolic bone disease workup
4. That additional imaging and social work consultation are required
5. Have the patient follow up in clinic this week for discussion of surgical intervention

## Correct option

**4. That additional imaging and social work consultation are required**

## Answer explanation

Ambulation, let alone running, is often not achieved before 12 months of age. The patient's injury and reported mechanism are concerning for nonaccidental trauma.

Nonaccidental trauma (NAT) is the most common reason for femur fractures in nonambulatory patients and is the etiology of about 50% of fractures in patients under 1 year of age. NAT should be suspected for any pediatric injury when the reported mechanism is inconsistent, long bone fractures in nonambulatory children, if there is a delay in seeking care, or if specific fracture patterns are present. Workup for nonaccidental trauma is a multidisciplinary effort and should include both a skeletal survey and social work consult. Specific fracture patterns that are concerning are metaphyseal corner fractures, bucket handle fractures, humeral transphyseal separations, and rib fractures.

Drs. Jayakumar, Barry, and Ramachandran review NAT and its common orthopedic presentations. They discuss the importance of the mobility of the patient in identifying NAT and the holistic approach to examining these patients, as a single fracture may not be clearly indicative of NAT. Rib fractures have the highest specificity for NAT. Other highly specific fractures include the scapula, vertebra, skull, and lateral clavicle.

*Dr. Ranade, Allen, and Deutsch reviewed the role of the orthopedic surgeon in the identification and management of NAT.* *Their review starts by discussing the history of NAT from a policy and medical standpoint, including the fact that all states and US territories have reporting mandate laws.* **They further discuss** that fractures are the second most common presenting injury (after skin lesions) and 12-20% of fractures in young persons are due to NAT. They continue on with recommended steps in history taking, physical exam, documentation, further imaging and workup, and management. They include the NAT guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Radiology.

*Pandya et al. retrospectively reviewed prospectively collected data from a Level 1 pediatric trauma center.* *They compared injury profiles of 500 child abuse patients to 985 accidental trauma patients less than 48 months of age.* **They found** abuse patients were on average younger, though no gender differences between groups. Humerus and femur fractures were 2.3 and 1.8 times as common in abused children compared to accidental trauma patients for those less than 18 months of age. There was no difference when comparing groups for all children <48 months of age. Amongst all kids, rib fractures (14.4 times), tibia/fibula (6.3 times), forearm (5.8 times), and clavicle (4.4 times) were disproportionately more common in abuse patients.

Figure A demonstrates a mid-shaft femur fracture in a skeletally immature patient.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1-3,5: This patient is likely too young to walk, let alone run, therefore the reported mechanism is concerning for attempts at covering up NAT. If NAT is suspected, a complete workup with a skeletal survey and social work consultation would be obtained immediately for the safety of the patient. Treatment of this femoral shaft fracture would likely be with spica casting or possibly a Pavlik harness, but this should occur after the completion of the NAT workup.
