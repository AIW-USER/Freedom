## Question

A 19-year-old college rugby player presents to your clinic after being tackled and sustaining a shoulder dislocation the prior day. He states this is his fourth dislocation and he was previously advised to undergo arthroscopic labral repair but declined. Figure A demonstrates a 3D CT reconstruction of his right shoulder. When discussing the most appropriate surgery for this patient, which of the following is true of the associated complications?

![[q217838-a-screen-shot-2022-09-30-at-9-28-40-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral glenoid overhang of graft leads to higher rates of glenohumeral osteoarthritis
2. Axillary nerve palsy is most common nerve injured during graft harvest
3. Stiffness in elbow flexion and shoulder internal rotation are common
4. Risk of subscapularis rupture is as high as 10%
5. Resorption of autograft is uncommon

## Correct option

**1. Lateral glenoid overhang of graft leads to higher rates of glenohumeral osteoarthritis**

## Answer explanation

A latarjet procedure is necessary for this patient given the high degree of bone loss seen on CT. Lateral overhang of the latarjet graft relative to the native glenoid surface is known to increase the risk of glenohumeral osteoarthritis.

Traumatic anterior shoulder instability is a common problem, especially affecting younger, male athletes who participate in contact sports. In combination with a traumatic glenohumeral joint dislocation, tearing of the anteroinferior capsulolabral complex normally occurs. Bony destruction to the anterior glenoid and the posterosuperior humeral head can also occur at the time of injury. Subsequent dislocations increase the risk of bony injury occurring. The degree of bone less termed, "critical," is still debated but it is thought that anterior glenoid bone loss as little as 13% can contribute to recurrent instability. Bone loss, therefore, must be addressed with a bony solution - in this case a latarjet coracoid transfer. Risks of the latarjet procedure include nonunion, graft resorption/failure, external rotation stiffness, nerve palsy, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis.

*Mizuno et al. investigated the long term results of the latarjet procedure in 68 patients.* **They noted** that subjective and objective patient measures remained significantly improved at final follow-up compared to pre-operatively. However, they did note that 20% of shoulders without pre-existing arthritis did progress to having arthritis at final follow-up and odds of this occurring were increased with older age, high-demand sports and lateral coracoid graft overhang.

*Young et al. provided a general overview of the use of the latarjet procedure for bone loss in anterior shoulder instability.* *They referred to Burkhart's findings where there was a 4% risk of recurrence of instability after arthroscopic labral repair without bone loss, which increased to 67% in the setting of bone deficiency.* **They proposed** that with increasing degrees of bone loss the Latarjet was effective in restoring stability to the shoulder.

Figure A depicts a sagittal 3D CT showing significant anterior glenoid bone loss with loss of inverted pear glenoid morphology.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Axillary nerve palsy can occur during graft fixation, but musculocutaneous nerve injury is most common during coracoid harvesting.
Answer 3: Stiffness in external rotation is most common after latarjet coracoid transfer.
Answer 4: Subscapularis split is usually performed during a latarjet procedure. Given that the subscapularis is not detached, risk for failure is low.
Answer 5: Graft resorption is quite common after latarjet, and occurs in up to 90% of cases.
