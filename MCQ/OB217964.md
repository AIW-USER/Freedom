## Question

A 78-year-old female with a history of osteopenia presents to the emergency room after a motor vehicle collision. She is complaining of left hip pain. She is found to have isolated injuries as shown in Figure A. Which of the following would be the most appropriate treatment option?

![[q217964-a-put-it-on-my-tab.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Gibson approach with greater trochanteric osteotomy to perform ORIF of the femoral head
2. Open reduction internal fixation of the posterior column with delayed total hip arthroplasty
3. Total hip arthroplasty with multiple screws placed through the acetabular cup
4. Combined posterior column ORIF with total hip arthroplasty during the same procedure
5. Utilizing a cup-cage construct when performing total hip arthroplasty

## Correct option

**4. Combined posterior column ORIF with total hip arthroplasty during the same procedure**

## Answer explanation

Older patients with concomitant acetabular and femoral head fractures can be treated with acute total hip arthroplasty (THA). Acetabulum column stability is foundational to ensuring the THA acetabular cup component remains in appropriate positioning.

Acute THA in the setting of acetabular fractures has been well described and encouraged for decades. Acetabular fractures may present with additional injuries including traumatic acetabular protrusion from medial wall blowout, femoral head cartilage damage, and femoral neck fracture. In these settings, some have advocated for staged management with ORIF of the acetabulum followed by delayed THA. However, this approach is fraught with complications of mal-reduction, heterotopic ossification, significant scar formation, and other factors making the second stage THA technically more difficult. On the other hand, performing acetabular ORIF and immediate THA has shown promising functional outcomes and obviates the need for second-stage surgery.

*Sierra et al. reviewed the role of total hip replacement in the setting of acetabular fractures.* *They discussed indications to include physiologically older patients with significant posterior-based acetabular fracture and/or significant impaction of the femoral head articular surface.* *They stress the importance of pre-operative planning for the longer operative duration, having blood products on standby, fixing displaced column fractures, and using uncemented acetabular components with supplemental screw fixation.*

*Lin et al. retrospectively reviewed 33 consecutive patients who underwent acute THA in the setting of acetabular fractures.* **They report** a 15% complication rate with only 2 patients requiring revision surgery (94% implant survival). **The authors concluded** that functional outcomes were equivalent to those after primary THA for osteoarthritis.

*LaFlamme reported on 32 patients who sustained an iatrogenic intraoperative acetabular fracture during the placement of a primary THA.* Subgroup analysis revealed that all patients with loss of posterior column integrity treated with acetabular screw augmentation through the cup alone (without acetabular plating) required revision for cup migration and nonunion. **They concluded** that in the setting of a complete posterior column fracture, screw fixation through the acetabular cup alone is inadequate and these patients require separate posterior column fixation.

Incorrect Answers:

Answer 1: A Gibson approach is often utilized in order to perform ORIF of the femoral head. There have been various modifications proposed, however, they all involve performing a trochanteric osteotomy to facilitate surgical dislocation of the femoral head. Once dislocated, the surgeon can then perform ORIF of the femoral head utilizing headless compression screws. This patient is elderly with considerable risk for treatment failure and other complications associated with the non-weight bearing status for 6-8 weeks after surgery. Patients within this age group are more appropriately treated with acute THA (with column fixation in this case).
Answer 2: Acute total hip arthroplasty, even in the setting of additional acetabular fracture requiring fixation, is considered safe and appropriate. While some have advocated for staged management with ORIF of the acetabulum followed by delayed THA, this approach is fraught with complications of mal-reduction, heterotopic ossification, significant scar formation, and other factors making the second-stage THA technically more difficult.
Answer 3: As discussed, columnar stability is necessary before placing an acute THA. In the LaFlamme article, acetabular screw augmentation through the cup alone in the setting of posterior column instability is inadequate and will likely result in cup migration.
Answer 5: A cup-cage construct is overall considered as a revision construct, however, is appropriate in the acute setting when there is an acetabular protrusion or extreme bone loss leading to pelvic discontinuity. Placement of a cup-cake construct is technically difficult and utilizes extended surgical approaches. This patient does not require a cup-cage construct for appropriate treatment.
