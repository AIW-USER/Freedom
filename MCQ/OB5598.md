## Question

A concerned father brings his 11-year-old son into the office and requests a doctor's note to excuse him from a school physical fitness program. The parent complains that while the school claims the program is appropriate for adolescents, it includes weight training and plyometrics, which he believes will provide no benefit and carries high risks of injury and growth disturbance. Which of the following statements is true and can be given as advice to the parent?

## Options

1. Only plyometrics is known to lead to increased risks of injury or growth disturbances in adolescents and should be avoided
2. Only weight training is known to lead to increased risks of injury or growth disturbances in adolescents and should be avoided
3. Both weight training and plyometrics are known to lead to increased risks of injury or growth disturbances in adolescents and should be avoided
4. Weight training and plyometrics are safe and can generate gains in strength secondary to muscle hypertrophy
5. Weight training and plyometrics are safe and can generate gains in strength secondary to improved neuromuscular activation and coordination

## Correct option

**5. Weight training and plyometrics are safe and can generate gains in strength secondary to improved neuromuscular activation and coordination**

## Answer explanation

Adolescents can safely participate in both weight training and plyometric programs when properly designed and supervised. Gains in strength can be achieved largely due to improved neuromuscular activation and coordination.

It was previously thought that children and adolescents should avoid strength training or plyometric (rapid eccentric-concentric shortening) exercise programs for a variety of reasons, including concern for physeal injury and growth disturbance, increasing the risk of injury during other sporting activities, and inability to derive strength gains. New studies have shown, however, that when participating in an age appropriate fitness program, adolescents can safely achieve strength and conditioning gains beyond expected normal developmental gains from age. It is believed these gains are likely related to neuromuscular improvements in activation and coordination, rather than muscle hypertrophy. An age appropriate fitness program will limit the risk to the adolescent by appropriately limiting the amount of weight and number of repetitions being performed.

*Guy et al. provide a review of current concepts in strength training for children and adolescents.* **They note** that gains in strength and conditioning can be seen, and are reversible if the training is discontinued. **They report** that the risk of injury is no different for adolescent athletes who participate in these types of programs compared to those who do not.

*Malina et al. provide a review of 22 reports regarding experimental training routines for pre- and early-pubertal youth.* **They found** that strength gains were routinely seen as a result of these programs. They did not find any effect on growth. Only ten of these studies systematically monitored for injuries, and only 3 injuries were reported. **They concluded** that training programs including resistance programs were safe and effective when properly monitored with low instructor to participant ratios.

*Faigenbaum et al. provide a review of the current literature regarding adolescent strength training and plyometric training.* *They describe that properly designed training programs for adolescents are safe, and can lead to numerous benefits including improvements in strength, improved psychosocial health, improved bone health (contrary to the classic belief that these activities may harm immature bone or physes), and improvements in motor skills performance.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, and 3: Neither weight training nor plyometrics have been shown to lead to higher rates of injury or growth disturbance, and can be safely done by adolescents.
Answer 4: Strength gains in adolescents are believed to be due to improved neuromuscular activation and coordination, rather than muscle hypertrophy.
