## Question

A 60-year-old patient with a history of type 2 diabetes presents to your office with complaints of foot pain and non-healing ulceration which has been present for months, without a rapidly changing appearance. White blood cell count is 15,000/µL. CRP and ESR are 75 mg/L and 120 mm/hr, respectively. On exam, there is no obvious deformity to the foot but sensation is diminished diffusely. Erythema and swelling are noted, which do not improve with elevation of the foot. There is an ulcer over the plantar surface of the midfoot which appears to probe to bone with minimal drainage. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Charcot arthropathy
2. Deep space abscess
3. Necrotizing fasciitis
4. Osteomyelitis
5. Primary bone tumor

## Correct option

**4. Osteomyelitis**

## Answer explanation

Given the fact that this diabetic patient has a non-healing ulcer that probes to bone, the most likely diagnosis is osteomyelitis of the midfoot.

The diagnosis of osteomyelitis in the setting of diabetic neuropathy can be challenging and often confused with charcot arthropathy. Exposed bone, or the ability to probe to bone through an ulcer, is highly suggestive of osteomyelitis and should guide appropriate treatment with antibiotics and/or debridement. MRI may also assist in the diagnosis of osteomyelitis but results can sometimes be equivocal and difficult to interpret. Laboratory values such as WBC count lack specificity to help differentiate osteomyelitis from other inflammatory sources of foot pathology. In summary, a thorough physical examination in patients with diabetic foot wounds is imperative for accurate diagnosis and management.

*Allahabadi et al. sought to provide consensus statements on the diagnosis and management of diabetic foot osteomyelitis.* In these statements, **they note** the importance of deep, exposed ulcers in the diagnosis of diabetic osteomyelitis and reinforce that MRI and bone biopsy should be used only in cases where diagnosis is not initially obvious. They finish by discussing the goals of medical and operative management in these cases and recommend strong multi-disciplinary coordination.

*Van der Ven et al. discuss Charcot arthropathy and its increasing clinical significance in the diabetic population.* **They highlight** that the best treatment is prevention and then discuss diagnostic techniques in addition to examination findings. They finish by reviewing a treatment algorithm for different stages of disease in these patients which involves both pharmacologic and operative intervention.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Charcot arthropathy would demonstrate swelling and erythema that improves with elevation. It can occur concomitantly with diabetic ulceration and osteomyelitis but this patient's exam findings were more consistent with osteomyelitis, given the ulceration that probed to bone.
Answer 2: There were no signs of fluctuance or purulent drainage on examination to suggest deep space abscess.
Answer 3: Necrotizing fasciitis would likely be more progressive, and with this patient's findings being present for several months, this diagnosis is unlikely.
Answer 5: While bony tumors can eventually cause overlying skin ulceration and drainage, there is nothing in the question stem and no radiographic findings to suggest a primary bone tumor.
