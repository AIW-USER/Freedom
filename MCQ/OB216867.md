## Question

You're walking a younger resident through an ulnar nerve decompression at the cubital tunnel on a 45-year-old competitive body builder. During dissection distal to the medial epicondyle, the resident is concerned that they severed the nerve labeled A in the Figure. Which of the following deficits would be expected postoperatively if this nerve was transected at this level?

![[q216867-a-screen-shot-2021-11-30-at-6-21-23-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Inability to flex the thumb IP joint
2. Inability to extend the thumb IP joint
3. Inability to hold middle and index fingers abducted
4. Decreased sensation to radial forearm
5. Decreased sensation to ulnar forearm

## Correct option

**5. Decreased sensation to ulnar forearm**

## Answer explanation

Label A in the figure is consistent with the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve, which crosses the field 3.1 cm distal to the medial epicondyle during cubital tunnel release. If severed, this would lead to decreased sensation over ulnar aspect of the forearm.

Cubital tunnel syndrome results from entrapment of the ulnar nerve at the level of the elbow and most commonly occurs between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnar is (FCU). During surgical release the surgeon must be aware of cutaneous anatomy, specifically the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve distally and the medial brachial cutaneous nerve more proximally. The main branch of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve crosses the field approximately 3 cm distal to the medial epicondyle (Label A in Figure A) and is responsible for anteromedial sensation in the distal forearm. Conversely, the medial brachial cutaneous nerve crosses the field 7 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle and provides pain and pressure sensation to the medial distal third of the forearm.

*Chowdhry et al. present an anatomic study on how to avoid the medial brachial cutaneous nerve during surgery of the medial arm.* **They report** that at about 7 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle, there is an arborization of 2 to 3 cutaneous branches. This nerve sends 3 to 4 branches through the muscular fascia across the ulnar nerve to skin of the medial arm. **They conclude** that this path may be variable and that the surgeon should be aware of cutaneous anatomy when performing surgeries on the medial aspect of the arm.

*Lowe et al. review the position of crossing branches of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve during cubital tunnel surgery in humans.* **They report** that medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve branches were noted to cross distal to the medial humeral epicondyle 100 percent of the time at an average distal distance of 3.1 cm. **They conclude** that inadvertent injury to this nerve my cause a painful neuroma, which could be misdiagnosed as recurrent disease.

In summary, the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve always has its main branch distal to the medial epicondyle, but minor branches can be found at or proximal to the medial epicondyle, and these must be preserved as damage to these will cause numbness, and frequently a painful neuroma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Inability to flex the IP joint would be indicative of an AIN injury, which is a branch of the Median nerve and should not be encountered during cubital tunnel surgery.
Answer 2: Inability to extend the IP joint would be indicative of a PIN injury, which is a branch of the Radial nerve not encountered during cubital tunnel surgery.
Answer 3: Inability to hold the fingers abducted would result from an injury to the ulnar nerve.
Answer 4: The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve supplies sensation to the radial forearm and is not encountered during medial elbow dissection.
