## Question

A six-year-old male presents to your office with a notable deformity in his right leg, as shown in Figure 1. His mother notes that he injured the same leg about 12 months ago and was placed in a cast after his bones were perfectly realigned. After removing his cast two months following the injury, he no longer had pain, and his leg was straight without any residual deformity. They deny any additional injuries. What is the likely cause of this angular deformity seen in Figure 1?

![[q220942-a-screenshot-2025-12-29-at-2-35-40-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Persistent deformity from previous midshaft tibia fracture
2. Persistent deformity from previous proximal tibia fracture
3. Late valgus deformity from previous proximal tibial metaphyseal fracture
4. Vitamin D deficiency
5. Physiologic tibial bowing

## Correct option

**3. Late valgus deformity from previous proximal tibial metaphyseal fracture**

## Answer explanation

This patient with a unilateral valgus deformity presenting months after a previous ipsilateral leg injury is most consistent with Cozen's phenomenon, a late valgus deformity following a proximal tibial metaphyseal fracture (answer 3).

Proximal tibial metaphyseal fractures are most commonly found in children between 3 and 6 years of age. This fracture, in particular, has a high propensity to develop a late valgus deformity, known as Cozen's phenomenon, which must be closely monitored. The etiology of this phenomenon is unknown and can occur regardless of treatment. The incidence of this late valgus deformity is 50-90% and develops within 5-15 months after the inciting injury. Diagnosis is made with plain radiographs and on physical exam. These are typically observed for 1-2 years, with the expectation of spontaneous recovery in most patients; however, some may experience a persistent deformity. Other treatment options for those with persistent valgus deformities >15-20 degrees and near skeletal maturity are a medial proximal tibial epiphysiodesis or varus-producing proximal tibia and fibula osteotomies.

*Yang et al. performed a retrospective review of an institutional orthopedic database from 2014 to 2017 to evaluate the prevalence of Cozen's phenomenon.* *They included 181 proximal tibia fractures in children aged 1-8 years.* **They found** that a majority of fractures (77.3%) had minimal initial valgus (<4 degrees). Among those, 14.3% developed at least 4 degrees of angulation at some point during follow-up (average 0.8 months), and only 4 patients had 5 or more degrees of valgus at final follow-up (none required surgery). **They concluded** that most cases of Cozen's phenomenon with mild early valgus angulation do not progress to clinically significant deformities, and they also noted that routine prolonged radiographic surveillance may not be necessary in all patients.

*Papamerkouriou et al. performed a retrospective review of 33 pediatric patients (mean follow-up of 8.8 years) with proximal tibial metaphyseal fractures treated over 25 years.* *Valgus angulation was measured post-treatment, at peak deformity (8-19 months), and at final follow-up.* **They found** that 45% of patients (15/33) developed a late valgus deformity, which decreased significantly by final follow-up, with a small residual valgus deformity present (average of 1.9 degrees). **They concluded** that late valgus changes occur in a substantial portion of children after proximal tibial metaphyseal fractures and tend to remodel over time to near normal alignment without the need for surgical intervention.

Figure 1 is a plain AP radiograph of bilateral lowextremities,ies demonstrating a mild valgus deformity involving the proximal right tibia.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer 1: The valgus deformity is present in the proximal tibia (not the middle 1/3), and previous radiographs/clinical exams did not demonstrate a malreduction or deformity following the patient's initial injury.
Answer 2: Previous radiographs/clinical exams did not demonstrate a malreduction or deformity following the patient's initial injury. Therefore, it is less likely to be the result of a persistent deformity or malreduction.
Answer 4: Vitamin D deficiency can lead to Rickets and tibial bowing; however, this is typically associated with metaphyseal cupping and widened proximal tibial physes, which are both absent in this patient.
Answer 5: Physiologic tibial bowing can occur in young children; however, this typically resolves by 5-7 years of age and causes bilateral deformities. Since this patient's angular deformity is unilateral and occurred after an injury, it is less likely secondary to physiologic causes.
