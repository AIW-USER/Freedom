Chapter: Trauma MCQs — Fracture of Femur and Tibia Shaft

## Question

A 72-year-old female on long-term alendronate therapy presents with a complete atypical femoral fracture (AFF) in the subtrochanteric region. Preoperative full-length radiographs reveal an exaggerated anterolateral bowing of the femur with a short radius of curvature. The surgeon elects to proceed with cephalomedullary nailing. Which of the following technical strategies is most critical to minimize the risk of iatrogenic intraoperative complications and subsequent mechanical failure?

## Options

1. Insert a standard straight nail utilizing an exact-match reaming technique (line-to-line) to maximize cortical contact and rotational stability within the hyper-sclerotic diaphysis.
2. Utilize a load-bearing lateral locking compression plate (LCP) with prophylactic cerclage wiring to completely avoid the high risk of anterior cortical blowout associated with reaming severely bowed femurs.
3. Utilize a lateralized trochanteric entry point and aggressively over-ream the medullary canal by at least 1.5 to 2.0 mm larger than the intended nail diameter to accommodate the sagittal bow and prevent incarceration.
4. Perform a prophylactic anterior closing-wedge osteotomy at the apex of the bow, followed by the insertion of an under-reamed, smaller diameter nail to preserve the remaining compromised endosteal blood supply

## Correct option

**3. Utilize a lateralized trochanteric entry point and aggressively over-ream the medullary canal by at least 1.5 to 2.0 mm larger than the intended nail diameter to accommodate the sagittal bow and prevent incarceration.**

## Answer explanation

Over-reaming the narrow, sclerotic canal by 1.5 to 2.0 mm is mandatory to allow passage of the implant without incarceration. A lateralized entry point and a nail with a smaller radius of curvature (greater bow) help accommodate the patient's altered anatomy and prevent anterior cortical penetration. Plates (Option B) are strictly contraindicated due to inevitable fatigue failure in these slow-healing fractures. Line-to-line reaming (Option A) in an unyielding canal causes incarceration. While an osteotomy is occasionally used for extreme bowing, one should never intentionally under-ream and use a smaller diameter nail (Option D) in an AFF, as a larger nail is required to maximize the construct's fatigue life.

Technical considerations in nailing of atypical femoral fractures:

Pre-operative Planning & Implant Selection

 - Evaluate the Radius of Curvature (ROC): AFFs are characterized by excessive anterolateral bowing. Standard intramedullary nails have a large ROC (e.g., 2.0 to 3.0 meters) and are too straight for these femurs. Select a newer-generation nail with a smaller ROC (e.g., 1.0 to 1.5 meters) to better match the native sagittal bow.

 - Mandate Cephalomedullary Fixation:
 Always utilize a full-length cephalomedullary reconstruction nail. This protects the femoral neck from future stress fractures and provides optimal load-sharing across the entire compromised diaphysis.

 - Avoid Load-Bearing Implants: Plates are strictly contraindicated due to the prolonged healing timeline of AFFs; a plate will almost inevitably undergo fatigue failure before bridging callus forms.

 Optimizing the Entry Portal

 - Lateralize the Starting Point: A standard piriformis or tip-of-trochanter entry trajectory will often direct the nail straight into the thickened, sclerotic anterolateral cortex. Move your starting point slightly more lateral (and occasionally slightly posterior) on the greater trochanter. This alters the insertion angle, helping the nail avoid lateral impingement and smoothly negotiate the exaggerated anterior bow.

Medullary Preparation: The Sclerotic Canal

 - Aggressive Over-Reaming: The diaphyseal canal in an AFF is notoriously narrow and hyper-sclerotic. "Line-to-line" reaming will result in nail incarceration. You must over-ream by at least 1.5 mm to 2.0 mm larger than the intended nail diameter.

 - Use Sharp Reamers and Advance Slowly: Sclerotic bone generates significant friction. Use sharp, new reamer heads and advance slowly with frequent withdrawal to clear flutes. This prevents thermal necrosis of the already compromised endosteum.

 - Utilize Flexible Reaming Shafts: If the bowing is severe, stiff reamers will eccentrically ream the anterior cortex and potentially cause a blowout. Ensure your reaming shafts are highly flexible to follow the anatomical path.

 Navigating the Bow & Nail Insertion

 - Protect the Anterior Cortex: As the nail passes the apex of the bow (often the fracture site), watch the lateral fluoroscopic view closely. If the tip of the nail is impinging heavily on the anterior cortex, do not force it.

 - Techniques for Extreme Mismatch: If the nail cannot pass without risking an anterior blowout, you have two options:

 - Downsize the nail diameter slightly to increase its flexibility (though this sacrifices some fatigue strength).

 - Perform a corrective closing-wedge osteotomy at the apex of the deformity. While creating a secondary fracture seems counterintuitive, correcting the mechanical axis to allow the nail to pass safely is often necessary in extreme bowing.

Biological Augmentation at the Fracture Site

 - Harvest the "Reamer Dust": The bone you are reaming from the canal is valuable, hyper-mineralized autograft. Do not discard it.

 - Local Grafting: If you are performing an open reduction, or if there is a lateral cortical gap after the nail is seated (due to the straightening effect of the implant), pack the reamer dust directly into and around the fracture site to stimulate the lagging biology.

 - Avoid Fracture Distraction: Ensure there is no gap at the fracture site upon final seating. If the fracture is distracted, back slap the nail or use the internal compression mechanism of the implant to achieve cortical contact. A distracted AFF will proceed to a guaranteed non-union.

Answer: 3
