## Question

A female dancer suffers from pain in left buttock while dancing. Later on pain occurs on rest also. Pain increases with hip extension, adduction and external rotation. MRI shows signal changes/ edema in quadratus femoris. What is the probable diagnosis? (regional orthopedics- hip and pelvis- ischiofemoral impingement)

## Options

1. Ischiofemoral impingement
2. Femoroacetabular impingement
3. Piriformis syndrome
4. Quadriceps tendinopathy

## Correct option

**1. Ischiofemoral impingement**

## Answer explanation

Differentials of hip pain of muscle/tendon origin.

Ischiofemoral impingement syndrome: Ischiofemoral impingement syndrome (IFIS) is a rare clinical entity characterized by chronic groin, buttock or hip pain associated with radiographic evidence of narrowing of the space between the lesser femoral trochanter and the ischial tuberosity. Normal Ischiofemoral distance on MRI axial cut is more than 2 cm and less than 1.3 cm is considered critical. Such narrowing is thought to affect the quadratus femoris muscle, showing an increased signal on T2 images, with no disruption of muscular fibers. Quadratus femoris edema may compress and displace the sciatic nerve causing radicular neuropathic pain.

It is considered an iatrogenic consequence of hip surgical procedures; however, it has also been described in patients suffering from hip trauma, patients with ischial exostosis, and even as a primary condition in young athletes.

Clinical examination: Pain could be reproduced in extension, abduction and external rotation of the hip (Ischiofemoral impingement test). On palpation, tenderness along the ischiofemoral space may be present.

Treatment : Initial conservative trial includes nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications, guided steroidal local infiltrations, and targeted physical therapy. If conservative treatment fails, surgical endoscopic decompression through resection of the lesser trochanter is required.

Piriformis syndrome: Piriformis syndrome is a painful musculoskeletal condition resembling sciatica, secondary to sciatic nerve entrapment in piriformis muscle at the greater sciatic notch. It is also called back pocket sciatica or wallet sciatica.

Anatomy: The sciatic nerve exits the greater sciatic foramen deep along the inferior surface of the piriformis muscle in 96% of the population. The sciatic nerve may pass completely through the muscle belly, or the nerve may split—with one branch (usually the peroneal portion) piercing the muscle and the other branch (usually the tibial portion) running inferiorly or superiorly along the muscle.

Etiology: Primary piriformis syndrome has an anatomic background such as spilt piriformis muscle, split sciatic nerve, or an anomalous sciatic nerve path. Secondary piriformis syndrome occurs as a result of precipitating causes including trauma, leg length discrepancy, cerebral palsy and narrowed sciatic foramen etc. Macrotrauma to the buttocks may lead to inflammation of the soft tissue, muscle spasm, and consequent nerve compression. Microtrauma may result from overuse of the piriformis muscle such as in long distance walking or running, dancing and excessive exercise. It may be due to direct pressure due to keeping the wallet in the right back pocket of trousers or jeans.

Clinical picture: Most common presentation is increasing pain in the buttock especially over the piriformis muscle attachments or lower part of the back when rising after sitting or squatting longer than 15 to 20 minutes. Patients may complain of difficulty in walking and of pain with internal rotation of the ipsilateral leg, such as occurs during cross-legged sitting or ambulation. The pain improves with ambulation & worsens with rest. Spasm of the piriformis muscle may cause stress on the sacrotuberous ligament. This stress may lead to compression of the pudendal nerves or increased mechanical stress on the innominate bones, potentially causing groin and pelvic pain. Compression of the fibular branch of the sciatic nerve may cause pain and or paresthesia radiating from the sacrum through the gluteal area and down the posterior aspect of the thigh.

Clinical tests:

Piriformis sign-In supine position when the patient is relaxed the ipsilateral foot is externally rotated and active efforts to bring the foot in midline results in pain, a positive piriformis sign.

Freiberg sign-Pain is experienced during passive internal rotation of the hip joint.

Pace sign- Pace sign, revealed with the FAIR (flexion, adduction, and internal rotation) test, involves the recreation of sciatic symptoms.

Beatty test- In this test, the patient lies on the unaffected side, lifting and holding the superior knee approximately 4 inches off the examination table. If sciatic symptoms are recreated, the test result is positive.

Lasegue sign-Patient in supine position flexes the hip and knee to 90 degree, then keeping the hip flexed extends the knee, if the patient has posterior thigh pain, a positive lasegue test.

Treatment: Early conservative treatment is the most effective treatment, in patients with piriformis with the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), muscle relaxants, ice, and rest. Stretching of the piriformis muscle and strengthening of the abductor and adductor muscles is also helpful in treatment of patients with piriformis syndrome. Surgery may be required in recalcitrant cases. The goal of surgery in these cases is to reduce any tension under which the piriformis muscle may be placed, as well as to explore the sciatic notch to ensure that there are no fibrous bands or constrictions compressing the sciatic nerve.

Snapping hip syndrome: Snapping hip syndrome (coxa saltans/dancer’s hip) is a rare disease and most commonly seen in ballet dancers and athletes requiring repetitive and extreme hip flexion and abduction. It can be intraarticular or extraarticular. Extra Articular type is more common and further divided into internal and external forms. In the internal type patients complain of pain and audible clicking sounds anterior to hip joint due to impingement of iliopsoas muscle or its tendon against iliopectineal eminence.

External form is more common and patients complain of lateral pain and clicking sounds due to rubbing of iliotibial band (ITB) over the greater trochanter. Intra Articular snapping occurs due to lesions inside the joint, viz. labral tears and loose bodies. Diagnosis is mainly clinical but dynamic USG or MRI may be helpful in clinching the diagnosis of intra-articular snapping. Treatment is mostly conservative and involves NSAIDS, ice application and stretching of involved muscle or tendon. Arthroscopic loose body removal and labral repair is the treatment of choice in intra articular snapping. Some cases of extra articular snapping may require local corticosteroid injection into involved tendon sheath. Resistant cases may require surgical release and lengthening of iliopsoas tendon and ITB.

Iliopsoas tendinopathy: Tendinitis of the iliopsoas usually occurs at the junction of the muscle and tendon. Inflammation of the iliopsoas bursa, the largest in the body and located deep to the tendon, can occur alone or with associated tendinitis. The 3 main causes of iliopsoas bursitis are rheumatoid arthritis, acute trauma, and overuse injury. Acute or chronic occupational or sports activity involving vigorous hip flexion and extension account for the majority of the cases.

A history of constant anterior hip pain aggravated by activity such as active flexion, ascending stairs, or flexing the hip to get out of a car or bed is strongly suggestive of the diagnosis. Tenderness may be felt on deep palpation over the musculotendinous junction. The most commonly reported clinical maneuver is the “snapping hip sign” or “extension test” whereby pain with or without a palpable snap is reproducible by deep manual palpation of the inguinal crease while having the patient flex the hip then abduct and externally rotate the hip. Upon returning to the neutral position, the examiner palpates for a click.

Treatment: Initial non-operative management of iliopsoas tendinitis and bursitis includes rest, stretching and strengthening exercises, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications, and a physical therapy program. In long standing cases iliopsoas tendon release from the lesser trochanter or tendon lengthening may be required.

Iliopsoas tendonitis after THR: It is a cause of groin pain after THR. Diagnosis should be considered in patients complaining of pain in the groin during activities which require active hip flexion, such as walking up stairs, lifting the leg in and out of a car. Physical examinations include difficulty in raising the leg to the examination couch, painful SLR or not possible and passive ROM and passive SLR is pain free. Cross- table lateral view X-ray should be done to confirm prominence of the acetabular component over the anterior aspect of the rim, which may irritate the iliopsoas tendon. Painful snapping of iliopsoas tendon may also occur over the anterior lip of the cup in some cases.

Diagnosis : Cross- table lateral view X-ray should be done to confirm prominence of the acetabular component over the anterior aspect of the rim. Relief after USG/CT guided local anesthetic injection into iliopsoas tendon sheath can confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment: CT guided corticosteroid injection and stretching exercises. If conservative treatment fails then iliopsoas tenotomy relieves the symptoms.

Answer : a
