## Question

A 13-year-old male soccer player presents to your clinic after three months of ankle pain. His pain has progressively gotten worse throughout the recent season, and currently he has continued pain with weight bearing that is preventing him from being able to run. His current radiograph is shown in Figure A. You would like to obtain an MRI of the patient's ankle to better evaluate for which of the prognostic factors below related to his pathology?

![[q218907-a-talar-ocd-lesion.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Peri-lesional stress fracture of the talus
2. Rupture of the anterior talo-fibular and calcaneo-fibular ligaments
3. Incomplete cartilage cap with fluid penetration
4. Complete physeal closure
5. Associated synvoial hypertrophy within the ankle joint

## Correct option

**3. Incomplete cartilage cap with fluid penetration**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a medial talar dome osteochondral lesion. Of the answer choices, an incomplete cartilage cap with fluid penetration is associated with an unstable lesion and a poor prognostic sign.

Talar osteochondral lesions typically present with pain and arise most often on the medial talar dome. They often occur in association with trauma (inversion injuries or fractures) but can occur without a history of a clear traumatic event. Non-traumatic lesions are thought to arise from micro-trauma. X-rays are typically sufficient for diagnosis, but MRI is important to evaluate lesion stability, associated edema, and measuring lesion size. Prognostic factors include the presence of open physes and intact cartilage cap (stable lesions). Unstable lesions have an incomplete cartilage cap and synovial fluid is able to penetrate through the subchondral bone beneath the lesion. Lesion stability is also important in treatment options, as stable lesions can be treated with immobilization or lesion drilling, while unstable lesions often require osteochondral grafting.

*Letts et al. retrospectively reviewed pediatric patients with talar OCDs treated at a major Canadian pediatric referral center.* *The average age was 13 years and 4 months, with medial lesions comprising 73% of lesions (19 of 26).* Surgical intervention was required in 58% of these patients, the rest being treated with immobilization and/or activity modification. **They report** a 96% complete or partial symptom resolution rate with treatment.

*Bruns et al. reviewed osteochondral lesions of the talus.* **They report** an incidence of 4.6 per 100,000 in pediatric patients with the majority occurring on the medial talar dome (53-58%). They further discuss diagnostic modalities including MRI and CT arthrograms, conservative treatment, and surgical options—specifically, lesion debridement, bone marrow stimulation, osteochondral transplantation, and autologous chondrocyte implantation.

Figure A demonstrates an AP X-ray ankle of a skeletally immature person with a medial talar dome osteochondral lesion.

Incorrect choices:
Answer 1: Subchondral fractures of the talus can be associated with these lesions, however, are not associated with any prognostic implication.
Answer 2: Lateral ankle instability may be associated with the development of OCD lesions, both medially and laterally, however, it is not prognostic in the healing of these lesions.
Answer 4: Open physes are a crucial prognostic indicator in healing, however, this can easily be identified on the patient's plain film X-rays.
Answer 5: Synovial hypertrophy can occur with chronic ankle conditions, and may contribute to overall pain and symptoms, however, is not predictive for this patient's OCD lesion.
