## Question

A 75-year-old African American female presents with an insidious onset of atraumatic right shoulder pain. She has a history of a rotator cuff tear repair performed over 20 years ago. She denies fevers or constitutional symptoms. Her WBC is 9, ESR 15 mm/h (rr 0-20 mm/h), CRP 8 mg/L (rr 0-10 mg/L). Figure A is a radiograph of her right shoulder. What is the most likely pathogenesis of her condition?

## Options

1. Intra-articular hemosiderin deposition, synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cytokine cascade and enzymatic cartilage destruction.
2. Intra-articular proliferation of gram positive cocci, activation of polymorphonuclear cells, release of inflammatory cytokines and enzymatic cartilage destruction.
3. Loss of dynamic compression of the humeral head against the glenoid, abnormal joint forces and mechanical cartilage wear.
4. Occlusion of the intraosseous microcirculation, decreased perfusion to humeral head and subchondral osteocyte death.
5. T-cell mediated autoimmune response, synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cytokine cascade and enzymatic cartilage destruction.

## Correct option

**3. Loss of dynamic compression of the humeral head against the glenoid, abnormal joint forces and mechanical cartilage wear.**

## Answer explanation

This patient has rotator cuff arthropathy (RCA), which results from rotator cuff deficiency with loss of humeral head compression against the glenoid, abnormal joint forces, superior humeral head (HH) migration and mechanical cartilage wear.

RCA represents a spectrum of pathology encompassing three key features: (1) rotator cuff insufficiency, (2) degenerative changes of the glenohumeral (GH) joint and (3) superior migration of the HH. The rotator cuff muscles compress the HH against the glenoid, allowing concentric rotation of the HH – a concept termed concavity-compression. Alterations in this compressive force ultimately result in GH instability and superior translation of the HH, with subsequent mechanical joint degeneration.

*Ecklund et al. reviewed the pathogenesis of RCA, which involves both mechanical and biochemical effects of a massive cuff defect.* Loss of dynamic concavity-compression and anterosuperior HH escape results in the mechanical impact of the HH abutting the acromion, producing cartilage fragmentation. This debris incites an enzymatic response that further damages cartilage, causing pain and immobility that ultimately results in disuse deterioration of the articular surface (Illustration A).

*Feeley et al. reviewed the surgical management of RCA.* Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) should be avoided due to a high rate of glenoid loosening requiring component revision. Without the compressive and inferiorly-directed force of the intact rotator cuff, the HH displaces superiorly with motion leading to eccentric loading of the glenoid component (“rocking-horse” phenomenon). The surgical treatment of choice remains the reverse TSA, which moves the center of rotation distally and medially to improve deltoid function and implant stability.

Figure A is the anteroposterior (AP) radiograph of the right shoulder with classic radiographic findings of RCA: superior HH migration and acetabularization (wearing of the undersurface of the acromion and superior glenoid). Illustration A is another AP radiograph showing femoralization of the proximal humerus (erosion of the greater tuberosity due to contact with the acromion), another radiographic finding seen in RCA (Nam et al.). Illustration B depicts the multifactorial etiology of RCA (Feeley et al.). Illustration C shows the Seebauer classification of RCA based on clinical and radiographic parameters (Nam et al.).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Pathogenesis of hemophilic arthropathy, joint destruction due recurrent hemarthroses in the setting of hereditary coagulation factor deficiency. Hemophilic arthropathy in the shoulder can result in massive rotator cuff tears, but is a less likely etiology given this patients gender and clinical history.
Answer 2: Pathogenesis of septic arthritis, which is unlikely given the normal WBC, ESR and CRP. Propionibacterium acnes (P.acnes) can cause a septic arthritis with normal inflammatory markers and may be considered in a patient with a surgical history; however, P.acnes is a gram positive bacillus.
Answer 4: Pathogenesis of avascular necrosis, characterized radiographically by subchondral lucency and/or HH collapse, not superior HH migration.
Answer 5: Pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis, which is less likely given normal inflammatory markers and is characterized radiographically by periarticular osteopenia and central glenoid erosion, not superior.
