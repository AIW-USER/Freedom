## Question

A 24-year-old primigravid female undergoes a difficult vaginal childbirth. The newborn presents with an upper extremity palsy seen in Figure A. Three months later the palsy persists. If left untreated, which pattern of glenohumeral dysplasia would be most expected in this patient?

![[q218680-a-brachialplexusplasy2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Glenoid anteversion, anterior subluxation of the humeral head
2. Glenoid retroversion, posterior subluxation of the humeral head
3. Inferior glenoid bone loss, inferior subluxation of the humeral head
4. Superior glenoid bone loss, high-riding humerus, and acetabularization of the acromion
5. Complete glenoid hypoplasia, multidirectional shoulder instability

## Correct option

**2. Glenoid retroversion, posterior subluxation of the humeral head**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with an Erb’s (C5, C6) brachial plexus palsy following a difficult delivery. Persistent palsies left untreated will most likely lead to glenoid retroversion and posterior subluxation of a flattened humeral head.

Brachial plexus birth palsies are uncommon, with an incidence of 1 to 4 every 1,000 births. While the overall incidence is decreasing due to improved obstetric care, there are several identified risk factors: macrosomia, multiparous pregnancies, difficult presentation, shoulder dystocia, forceps-assisted delivery, breech presentation, and prolonged delivery. Palsies secondary to upper lesions (Erb’s - C5, C6) are more common than lower lesions (Klumpke’s - C8, T1), as newborns tend to experience traction to the plexus through lateral flexion of the head towards the contralateral shoulder far more commonly than longitudinal/abduction traction to the upper extremity itself. Erb’s palsies develop secondary to weak abductors and external rotators and tend to have the best prognosis for spontaneous recovery, with antigravity function of the biceps by 3 months being a good prognostic sign. However, preganglionic lesions and those with concomitant Horner’s syndrome tend to have poorer prognoses, with the former having the worst prognosis for motor recovery.

*Waters et al. published a 2009 study correlating the cross-sectional area of muscle with the degree of glenohumeral deformity in children affected by brachial plexus birth palsies.* *The authors evaluated differences in the cross-sectional area ratios between affected and unaffected shoulders in the same individual and assessed whether an increased internal to external rotator muscle cross-sectional area correlated with greater glenohumeral deformity, ultimately finding that muscle cross-sectional area ratios were significantly correlated with glenohumeral deformity type.* **The authors concluded** that the degree of muscle imbalance between internal and external rotators about the shoulder is measurable by magnetic resonance imaging in children with persistent brachial plexopathy, and the imbalance correlates with the degree of glenohumeral deformity.

*Hale et al. published a 2010 review of the pathophysiology, natural history, evaluation, and current management strategies in brachial birth plexopathies.* With regards to the natural history of chronic birth palsies, the progressive deformity of the glenohumeral joint is described by the Waters classification: normal joint (type I), posterior glenoid deformity (type II), humeral head subluxation and increased glenoid dysplasia (type III), development of a false glenoid with humeral head dislocation (type IV), flattening of both the glenoid and the humeral head (type V), infantile posterior dislocation (type VI), and physeal arrest of the proximal humerus (type VII). **The authors state** that brachial birth plexopathies have a wide range of clinical presentations, and treatment should be tailored. Recent developments in nerve, soft tissue, and osseous procedures have led to better functional outcomes; however, the proper timing of the intervention and type of surgical procedure for children who do not recover motor function spontaneously continue to be controversial topics.
