## Options

2. disproportionate short stature and shortening of the proximal segment
3. disproportionate short stature and shortening of the middle segment
4. disproportionate short stature and shortening of the middle and distal segments
5. disproportionate short stature and shortening of the distal segment

## Correct option

**2. disproportionate short stature and shortening of the proximal segment**

## Answer explanation

This child has achondroplasia, which is a form of rhizomelic dwarfism. There is disproportionate short stature and shortening of the proximal limb segment (humerus and femur).

Achondroplasia is the most common skeletal dysplasia. It is characterized by short stature, cranial findings (frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia), spine findings (foramen magnum stenosis, thoracolumbar kyphosis, spinal stenosis, decreasing interpedicular distance from L1 to L5), pelvic findings (champagne glass pelvis with squared iliac wings), limb findings (genu varum, metaphyseal flaring, trident hands, V-shaped physis, long fibulae). Mutations of the fibroblast growth factor 3 receptor gene on the short arm of chromosome 4 lead to shortening of long bones formed by endochondral ossification. The most common mutation is the G380R mutation.

*Shirley et al. reviewed achondroplasia.* For foramen magnum stenosis, a screening sleep study is suggested for all infants. Thoracolumbar kyphosis decreases in frequency with age (87% at 1 to 2 years; 39% from age 2 to 5; 11% from age 5 to 10). Management is controversial with(1) prophylactic prohibition of unsupported seating for infants and (2) bracing for kyphosis >30° with anterior wedging or posterior vertebral displacement at the apex recommended by some authors. Spinal fusion is indicated for kyphosis >50° or if there is neurologic compromise though unless there is rapid progression or a neurologic issue this is generally observed until the age of 4. Limb lengthening is controversial and can have up to 43% have complications.

*Schiedel et al. reviewed limb lengthening in achondroplasia.* The indications are controversial in the short stature community. There are 4 possible approaches for 4 segment lengthening: (1) all 4 segments at once, (2) longitudinal simultaneously (ipsilateral femur-tibia), (3) cross-leg inverse (R femur/L tibia, then L femur/R tibia), (4) transverse parallel (both femurs first, then both tibiae). **They recommend** the bilateral transverse parallel approach as the treatment is provisionally complete after each step and the next step can be possibly omitted, it does not create a discordance in knee height (such as in 3), or lead to leg length discrepancy (such as in 2).

Figures A through C show characteristic findings in achondroplasia. Figure A is an AP radiograph of the lumbar spine showing progressive narrowing of the interpedicular distance (from L1 to L5). Figure B is an AP radiograph of the knees showing inverted-V shaped physis at the knee, genu varum, and relative fibular overgrowth. Figure C is a T2-weighted sagittal MRI of the cervical spine and brain showing foramen magnum stenosis. Illustration A shows the locations of rhizo-, meso- and acromelia.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Achondroplasia is a short limb skeletal dysplasia with disproportionate short stature.
Answer 3: An example of mesomelic dwarfism (shortened middle segment) is Leri-Weill dyschondrosteosis.
Answer 4: An example of mesoacromelic dwarfism (shortened middle and distal segments) is Ellis van Creveld chondroectodermal dysplasia.
Answer 5: An example of acromelic dwarfism (shortened distal segment) is peripheral dysostosis.
