## Question

A 62-year-old patient suffered the complication shown in Figure A. The stem in the figure was placed in a revision setting for pain and osteolysis approximately two years prior. What is the biggest risk factor for the development of this complication?

![[q218088-a-stem-fracture.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Smaller diameter of femoral stem
2. Inadequate bony exposure during implantation
3. Inadequate proximal bone stock
4. Increased offset
5. Older age

## Correct option

**3. Inadequate proximal bone stock**

## Answer explanation

This patient suffered a fracture through a fully porous-coated femoral stem. The most important risk factor for this failure is inadequate proximal bone stock (answer 3).

Fractures through non-cemented, fully porous-coated femoral stems are not commonly reported. Only a handful of cases have been published on the matter. Extensively porous-coated stems, typically reserved for revisions, provide the advantage of proximal and distal bony ingrowth for better diaphyseal stability. With greater diaphyseal fit and stability, fully coated stems can be employed when proximal bone stock is lacking. However, in the event of significant proximal bone loss and distal bony ingrowth, cantilever force is placed through the mid stem, and the implants can eventually fail secondary to this cyclic bending moment. Many authors have proposed risk factors for fully coated stem failure, including inadequate proximal bone stock, higher body weight (Charnley reported a high risk of failure in patients > 88 kg), smaller stem size (some authors recommend implanting stems > 13.5 mm), younger age, or extended trochanteric osteotomies (ETOs). Most authors would agree that inadequate proximal bone stock is the most common reason for failure. To revise a failed fully-coated stem, most find an ETO is needed to access the diaphyseal aspect of the stem, as this will likely be well fixed. Revision requires addressing the lack of proximal bone with either grafting or proximal femur replacement.

*Lu et al. presented four cases of uncemented, fully porous-coated femoral stem fractures from their own institution.* The primary surgeon in this study implanted three 12 mm stems and one 11 mm stem. Three patients were below the age of 55 and only one had a body weight over 88 kg. Three of the four patients had a previous trochanteric osteotomy with signs of nonunion at the osteotomy site. **The authors suggested** this, paired with good diaphyseal ingrowth, created cyclic bending stress and fatigue fractures at the femoral stem. **The authors concluded** that common risk factors were inadequate bone stock in the proximal femur (either secondary to ETO nonunion or osteolysis), stem diameter <12 mm, high body weight, and relatively young age.

*Landa et al. provide a case series of fractured uncemented, fully porous-coated femoral stems.* *The study included three cases of stem failure in the revision setting.* The three patients fractured at a mean of 21 months after revision, with a mean age of 51 years at the time of fracture, and the mean weight was 90 kg. The authors saw fractures through 13 mm, 15 mm, and 16 mm diameter stems and noted significant bone loss in the proximal segments of all three cases. As a result, **the authors concluded** that the most important risk factor for fatigue failure of these stems is inadequate proximal femoral bone stock.

Figure A is a fully porous-coated femoral stem with a transverse fracture through the mid stem.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Some authors have suggested that implantation of a stem < 13.5 mm will increase the risk of fracture through the stem. Stems with smaller radii are more susceptible to fatigue fracture, however, this has not been found to be the leading factor in stem failure. Smaller stems are often implanted and have been shown to have satisfactory outcomes long-term.
Answer 2: Inadequate bony exposure has not been shown to increase the risk of fracture through fully coated stems. Studies have found a higher risk of fracture through porous-coated stems when a trochanteric osteotomy is performed. Likely secondary to nonunion of the osteotomy site.
Answer 4: Increased offset is often used in the revision setting to appropriately tension the abductors. The increased offset would increase the stress at the neck-shaft junction but is less likely to cause enough stress at the distal aspect of the stem to create a fracture.
Answer 5: Older age has not been associated with a fracture through fully-coated stems. In contrast, younger age (some authors suggest < 60 years of age) has been associated with increased rates of fatigue failure. With younger age, authors propose the implant is undergoing more cycles and likely higher activity leading to more fatigue failure.
