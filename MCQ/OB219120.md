## Question

A 22-year-old civilian male presents with a mangled lower extremity below the knee. He elects to attempt limb salvage. Despite heroic attempts made with various operative procedures, he ultimately decides to proceed with below-knee amputation a year later. The surgeon is planning to perform a standard modified Burgess below-knee amputation, however, the patient inquires about the modified Ertl technique (Figure 1). When compared to the traditional modified Burgess transtibial amputation, which of the following is true regarding this modified Ertl technique?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Can only be performed with a posteriorly-based flap closure
2. Significantly lower risk for subsequent surgeries
3. Significantly less time to perform in the operating room
4. Lacks definitive improvement in functional outcomes
5. Improved overall quality of life only shown in the military population

## Correct option

**4. Lacks definitive improvement in functional outcomes**

## Answer explanation

The modified Ertl technique involves performing a BKA and utilizing an osteomyoplastic bridge to fuse the distal tibia/fibula for the purported improved weight-bearing capabilities through a prosthesis. At this time, no definitive improvement in functional outcomes has been reported other than in small, retrospective case series.

Below knee mangled extremity injuries pose a clinical challenge for providers and patients alike. It is generally regarded that the functional outcomes of below-knee amputation are similar to that of limb salvage in the civilian population with both cohorts incurring a persistent, life-long functional disability. The military population appears to have improved outcomes with amputation owed to the enhanced rehabilitation protocols through dedicated treatment centers specializing in amputee care. When patients undergo below-knee amputation, the standard Burgess approach is utilized by most surgeons. This involves leaving adequate tibial length to facilitate eventual prosthesis fitting, cutting the fibula 1-2 cm shorter than the tibia, and utilizing a posterior-based flap for gastrocnemius fascial closure. The modified Ertl technique is similar in the approach however involves utilizing a tibial-based periosteal flap, fibular cortical bone +/- suture/screw fixation, and peroneal muscular coverage to create an osteomyoplastic bridge to fuse the distal tibia/fibula prior to gastrocnemius fascial closure. Advocates for this technique cite its usefulness in preventing symptomatic instability of the distal tibia/fibula (i.e. “chopsticking”), earlier allowance for ambulation, and improved osseous support to facilitate prosthetic-assisted ambulation. Unfortunately, there is highly conflicting evidence on outcomes with the majority of studies involving small, retrospective case series. While it cannot be firmly supported at this time, the results of the Transtibial Amputation Outcomes Study (TAOS; NCT01821976) will hopefully resolve discrepancies in reported outcomes.

Taylor and Poka provided a comprehensive review of the surgical technique and outcomes of the Ertl osteomyoplastic transtibial amputation procedure. The authors describe the meticulous elevation of the tibial periosteal flap, discuss controversies in fixation methodology of the fibula-tibia osteoperiosteal flap prior to peroneal musculature coverage, and the outcomes of larger studies. They argue that despite the limited and often conflicting evidence on the Ertl technique, it remains a safe and viable option for patients requiring transtibial amputation.

*Keeling and colleagues performed a questionnaire-based study utilizing their prospective military amputee database to identify and contact patients.* With a 42% response rate, they obtained 27 Ertl and 38 Burgess amputees’ responses regarding Short Form-36, Prosthesis Evaluation Questionnaire, and functional outcome data. **They found** that the Ertl cohort was more likely treated in a delayed manner (>6 weeks) and had significantly longer residual limbs compared to Burgess patients. However, there was no significant difference between the functional outcomes of both groups, similar to their prior studies. **The authors concluded** that the modified Ertl and Burgess techniques offer similar outcomes when employed in the young, active-duty military population.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: So long as tension-free skin closure can be obtained, the modified Ertl technique can be performed with traditional posterior flaps, vascular-based skew flaps, fish mouth flaps, long medial flaps, or sagittal flaps.
Answer 2: The modified Ertl technique has a significantly higher risk for subsequent surgeries owed to potential symptomatic hardware and nonunion procedures. The risk for wound dehiscence and deep infection appears equivalent between both techniques.
Answer 3: The modified Ertl technique takes a significantly longer time to perform in the operating room owing to the increased technical demands in creating the osteomyoplastic bridge between the residual tibia and fibula.
Answer 5: The United States military service members who sustained major lower-extremity trauma provided higher self-reported functional outcome scores when treated with below-knee amputations over limb salvage (METALS Study). However, multiple questionnaire-based and propensity score matching studies within this patient population have not shown improvement in functional outcomes or quality of life when comparing the traditional Burgess to modified Ertl BKA techniques.
