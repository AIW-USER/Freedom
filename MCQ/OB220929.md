## Question

Figure A is a weight-bearing radiograph of a 58-year-old woman with progressive medial forefoot pain, forefoot fatigue after long walks, and difficulty wearing shoes. Her exam demonstrates a pronated hallux with lateral angulation of the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint, and there is tenderness with palpation of the tibial eminence and a plantar forefoot callus. First-ray motion is mildly increased; percussion posterior to the medial malleolus does not produce any radiating pain or paresthesia. Which of the following associated findings is most likely present?

![[q220929-a-screen-shot-2025-11-20-at-6-55-01-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Plantar focal tenderness and keratosis under the second metatarsal head, improves with metatarsal pad
2. Posterior heel pain with tenderness at the calcaneus tuberosity and pain on passive ankle dorsiflexion
3. Burning dysesthesia between the third and fourth toes with a palpable click on squeeze test
4. Limited ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended that persists when the knee is flexed
5. Fixed hindfoot varus without correction during Coleman block testing

## Correct option

**1. Plantar focal tenderness and keratosis under the second metatarsal head, improves with metatarsal pad**

## Answer explanation

Transfer metatarsalgia presents as plantar tenderness and callus beneath the second metatarsal head (answer choice 1) and is commonly associated with hallux valgus. A shift toward more lateral weight-bearing and loss of the windlass mechanism can overload the lesser rays; symptoms typically improve with shoe modifications and metatarsal pads.

Hallux valgus is a valgus/pronation deformity of the first ray; lateral sesamoid displacement and diminished first-ray function can cause transfer metatarsalgia by shifting plantar pressure to the lesser metatarsal heads. Initial management of hallux valgus and metatarsalgia is nonoperative with shoe modifications, orthoses, and metatarsal pads; surgery is reserved for persistent symptoms. Ankle equinus and a relatively long second metatarsal can further increase forefoot load; these factors should be considered when determining nonoperative and operative treatment modalities, as appropriate offloading (using pads or a rocker sole) and addressing pathomechanics are crucial for successful management.

*Besse reviewed metatarsalgia, classifying etiologies as primary, secondary, and iatrogenic, distinguishing “static” from “propulsive” forms, and* **emphasized** assessment with physical exam plus weight-bearing radiographs, with ultrasound/MRI as needed. They described first-line conservative care (stretching, footwear modification, insoles, lesion debridement), with soft-tissue procedures (gastrocnemius recession, tendon transfers, plantar plate repair) and metatarsal procedures such as the Weil osteotomy as reliable treatment modalities, while percutaneous methods require further evaluation; management can follow an algorithm keyed to whether the hallux is normal or abnormal, and inflammatory disease warrants a specific strategy. **They concluded** that integrating biomechanical and anatomical considerations with a stepwise algorithm optimises treatment selection.

*Espinosa et al. reviewed metatarsalgia through the lens of gait mechanics (rocker phases), relating symptoms to anatomy, deformity, and first-ray function.* **They found** that most patients improve with individualized nonsurgical care—shoe modification, metatarsal pads/rocker soles, and stretching—whereas surgery (e.g., Weil osteotomy and modifications) should be reserved for clearly defined second- versus third-rocker problems, given risks such as stiffness and floating toe. **They concluded** that mapping pain patterns and keratoses to gait rockers and correcting contributors like first-ray insufficiency or gastrocnemius tightness enables precise diagnosis and stepwise treatment planning.

Incorrect Answers:

Answer choice 2: Posterior heel pain and insertional Achilles tenderness suggest Achilles tendinitis, which is not typically associated with hallux valgus.
Answer choice 3: Pain or interdigital radiating pain with manual compression of the forefoot (Mulder click) is suggestive of an interdigital (Morton) neuroma, rather than metatarsalgia.
Answer choice 4: Fixed equinus contracture (indicated by a dorsiflexion limitation with a negative Silfverskiöld test) indicates Achilles contracture; this vignette points to first-ray malalignment with transfer loading instead.
Answer choice 5: Fixed hindfoot varus without correction during Coleman block testing is suggestive of rigid cavovarus deformity. Pes planus, not cavus, is a risk factor for hallux valgus.
