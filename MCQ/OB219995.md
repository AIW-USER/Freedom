## Question

A 76-year-old male presents to your clinic with increasing shoulder pain, limiting his ability to perform activities of daily living. The patient has a history of a previous ipsilateral rotator cuff repair approximately 20 years ago. On physical examination, the patient has decreased strength with internal rotation of the shoulder and a positive belly press test. The patient has failed all reasonable attempts at nonoperative management and would like to discuss the possibility of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA). What is the management of the most common postoperative complication after RTSA in this clinical setting?

## Options

1. obtain electromyography
2. irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange
3. irrigation and debridement with antibiotic spacer placement
4. immediate closed reduction and sling immobilization
5. open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**4. immediate closed reduction and sling immobilization**

## Answer explanation

Following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA), the most common complication is dislocation, which occurs in 1.5% to 31% of cases. This is most commonly managed with immediate closed reduction and sling immobilization (Answer 4).

Post-operative complications following RTSA include prosthetic instability, periprosthetic infection, component failure, post-operative hematoma formation, neurovascular compromise, periprosthetic fracture, scapular notching, and acromial or scapular spine stress fractures. The prevalence of these complications demonstrates a strong correlation with the primary surgical indication. Current literature has identified several independent risk factors associated with post-operative instability, including male gender, body mass index (BMI) >30, previous shoulder surgery, proximal humerus bone stock deficiency, compromised axillary nerve function, and insufficiency of the subscapularis musculature. Contemporary biomechanical strategies employed to optimize prosthetic stability include an increased lateral offset of the glenosphere and/or baseplate components, eccentric positioning of the baseplate, lateralization or distalization of the humeral component, implementation of large-diameter glenosphere components, and increased articular constraint.

*Chae et al. retrospectively reviewed instability in RTSA.* **They found** that dislocation is the most common complication associated with RTSA, with reported rates varying from 1.5% to 31%. **The authors emphasize** that addressing dislocations requires an understanding of the biomechanical principles governing RTSA stability to adequately increase component stability through additional constraints while maintaining the appropriate degrees of freedom of motion to achieve a satisfactory outcome. **The authors conclude** that further literature pertaining to patient selection, prosthetic design, and surgical technique is required to optimize the biomechanical stability of RTSAs.

*Ascione et al. analyzed long-term humeral complications after Grammont-style reverse shoulder arthroplasty in 1,035 RTSAs.* *At a minimum 5-year follow-up, there was a 3.3% rate of humeral complications, including fractures (1.6%), aseptic loosening (1.4%), and stem disassembly (0.3%).* These complications were more common in RTSAs performed for tumor resections, fracture sequelae, and revisions for failed arthroplasty. Risk factors for humeral complications included advanced age, superior surgical approaches, cemented stems, and proximal humeral bone loss. **The authors conclude** that in the absence of associated humeral loosening, conservative management should be employed initially for humeral-sided complications that result secondary to implant instability.

*Bacle et al. conducted a comprehensive review of long-term outcomes following RTSA with a minimum follow-up of 10 years.* **Their findings revealed** significant declines in Constant scores when compared to medium-term results, accompanied by a high prevalence of scapular notching (73%), a complication rate of 29%, and a revision surgery rate of 12%. Despite a favorable 10-year prosthetic survival rate of 93%, **the authors concluded** that long-term outcomes progressively deteriorate due to factors such as patient aging, osseous erosion, and impaired deltoid muscle strength.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Electromyography (EMG) can be considered to evaluate for possible axillary nerve dysfunction if deltoid muscle weakness was suspected to be the primary cause for limited motion, decreased strength, or instability after RTSA. However, immediate closed reduction and sling application is the most correct management of dislocation.
Answer 2: Irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange would be the treatment of choice for acute surgical site infections within the setting of RTSA. Acute infection is not the most common complication following RTSA.
Answer 3: Irrigation and debridement with antibiotic spacer placement would be the treatment of choice for chronic surgical site infections within the setting of RTSA. Chronic infection is not the most common complication following RTSA.
Answer 5: Open reduction and internal fixation is a possible treatment modality for periprosthetic fracture. However, a periprosthetic fracture is not the most common complication following RTSA.
