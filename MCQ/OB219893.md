## Answer explanation

The procedure described is a periacetabular osteotomy (PAO). A young adult patient with acetabular retroversion and a reduced and concentric hip joint with hip dysplasia as demonstrated in Figure A, is most likely to have undergone a PAO with labral repair.

Adult dysplasia of the hip is a condition characterized by a shallow or underdeveloped acetabulum, leading to insufficient coverage of the femoral head and resultant joint instability. Over time, this may lead to insidious-onset hip pain, gait abnormalities, labral thickening or tears, and an increased risk of early-onset osteoarthritis. For symptomatic cases, long-term nonoperative management has limited success, and surgery is often required to improve symptoms and hip function. Various surgical modalities play a role in operative management, including hip arthroscopy, PAO, salvage pelvic osteotomies, hip resurfacing, and total hip arthroplasty (THA). In the absence of significant arthritic changes, labral repair with or without capsular plication may alleviate symptoms in cases of mild or borderline hip dysplasia (e.g., lateral center-edge angle [LCEA] = 18-25º), although more severe deformities or cases with acetabular retroversion often necessitate acetabular reorientation (i.e., with PAO) to provide reliable long-term benefit.

*Parvizi et. al provided a retrospective review of prospectively collected data on 24 patients undergoing arthroscopic treatment in the setting of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH; 30 hips) or acetabular retroversion (6 hips), with an average follow-up of 3.5 years.* *In their series, all patients underwent labral debridement rather than repair, as well as attempted femoroplasty in cases of femoroacetabular impingement.* *With this approach,* **they found** that 24 patients continued to have pain, and 16 went on to require further surgery, either in the form of PAO, femoroacetabular osteoplasty, or THA. **They concluded** that isolated hip arthroscopy and labral debridement may accelerate the arthritic process in this population, and caution against this approach.

*Domb et. al evaluated patient-reported outcomes (PROs) of 19 patients (21 hips) with borderline hip dysplasia (defined as LCEA 18-25º) and no or minimum arthritic changes (Tonnis grade <2), who underwent hip arthroscopy with labral repair and capsular plication, without PAO.* *At a minimum 5-year follow-up,* **they noted** statistically significant improvements in multiple PROs, with no patients requiring conversion to total hip arthroplasty. They did note that 4/21 hips (19%) went on to requiring secondary arthroscopic procedures, although ultimately these patients showed improvement in PRO scores at the latest follow-up as well. **They concluded** that cases of borderline hip dysplasia may benefit from arthroscopic surgery alone with labral repair and capsular plication. Deferring the need for PAO, which although has well-supported long-term data, is a considerably more invasive procedure.

*Yoon et. al provided outcomes of 45 adult patients (47 hips) with borderline dysplasia (LCEA 20-25º), who underwent hip arthroscopy with a combination of labral debridement versus repair, ligamentous teres (LT) debridement, and/or capsular plication, with the majority undergoing labral repair and capsular plication, with or without LT debridement.* *Although they recorded statistically significant improvements in PROs and visual analog scores (VAS),* **they noted** that approximately 40% of patients reported that the operation was unsatisfactory at the final follow-up, due to the eventual return of pain. Four hips went on to require second-look hip arthroscopy and nine were referred for pelvic osteotomy or hip arthroplasty. **They concluded** that although arthroscopic management may be successful in a subset of patients with hip dysplasia, a significant number may ultimately remain dissatisfied with this approach alone.

Figure 1 is an anteroposterior (AP) pelvis radiograph in a patient with mild hip dysplasia, with concentric hip joints and minimal arthritic changes. Figures 2 and 4 are AP pelvis radiographs of cases of severe hip dysplasia, with hip dislocation and significant proximal migration of the femoral head. Figure 3 is an AP pelvis radiograph of a 22-month-old child with normal-appearing hip joints. Figure 5 is an AP pelvis radiograph of a patient with hip dysplasia and end-stage osteoarthritis, with obliteration of their hip joint spaces.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choices 2 & 4: In severe cases of hip dysplasia, often in the setting of underlying neuromuscular disorders, eventual hip dislocation, and proximal femur migration necessitate femoral-sided procedures, such as shortening osteotomies, to safely reduce the femoral head back into the acetabulum without producing excessive stress on neurovascular structures.

Answer choice 3: This is a normal radiograph of a young child. Furthermore, PAOs are typically reserved for older children, adolescents, or adults, as less invasive pelvic osteotomies and concomitant femoral-sided osteotomies may provide adequate correction in pediatric hip dysplasia cases.

Answer choice 5: In hip dysplasia cases where severe arthritis has developed, arthroplasty is ultimately recommended as PAO alone does not address the permanent cartilage loss and resultant bony changes that contribute to the symptomatology in these cases.
