## Question

A 42-year-old male presents to your office after injuring himself weightlifting several weeks prior. He states he was doing deadlifts when he felt a sharp pain in his back and subsequently noticed shooting and burning pain traveling down his right anterolateral leg. On examination he is neurovascularly intact grossly, but does have mild weakness with great toe extension against resistance. Which pathology is most likely causing his symptoms?

## Options

1. Paracentral disc herniation at L3-L4
2. Far lateral disc herniation at L5-S1
3. Right sided foraminal stenosis at L4-L5
4. Degenerative spondylolithesis at L5-S1
5. Isthmic spondylolithesis at L4-L5

## Correct option

**2. Far lateral disc herniation at L5-S1**

## Answer explanation

The right L5 nerve root has been affected in this patient, likely due to a far lateral disc herniation at L5-S1, which affects the exiting L5 nerve root.

Lumbar disc herniation is one of the more common causes of low back pain with radiculopathy. It occurs with repeated strain that leads to tearing of the outer annulus and herniation of the nucleus pulposus, usually through the weakest portion of the posterior longitudinal ligament. It is important to understand the lumbar nerve root anatomy in order to clinically correlate imaging and physical examination findings. In the lumbar spine, the spinal cord ends at the conus medullaris around L1 and the remainder of the nerve roots traverse vertically to their respective foramen, exiting below the associated vertebrae (i.e. L5 nerve root exits between L5 and S1). When a paracentral disc herniation occurs, the traversing root (i.e S1 at L5-S1) is affected, while a far lateral herniation causes compression of the nerve root after it has already exited through its foramen (i.e. L5 affected at L5-S1).

*Khan et al. discussed the presentation and outcomes of patients with far lateral disc herniations compared to those with central/paracentral herniations.* *In a cohort of 100 patients,* **they noted** that preoperative SF-12 and VR-12 scores were lower in the far lateral group, however, they still improved postoperatively. Interestingly, they commented that postoperative ODI scores for patients with central/paracentral herniations were significantly lower compared to the far lateral group.

*Berra et al. reviewed far lateral lumbar disc herniations, which* **they note** included intraforaminal and extraforaminal herniations. **They note** that the diagnosis can be more challenging for a far lateral disc than a paracentral disc herniation and stated that MRI is the best imaging tool for assessment. However, they do mention that many cases of far lateral disc herniation can be overlooked by these radiology exams, therefore clinical examination is emphasized.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A paracentral disc herniation would cause impingement of the L4 nerve root, leading to dermatomal changes of the anteromedial leg and loss of the patellar reflex.
Answer 3: Right-sided foraminal stenosis at L4-L5 would affect the exiting nerve root (L4) and would lead to dermatomal changes of the anteromedial leg and loss of the patellar reflex.
Answer 4: Degenerative spondylolisthesis usually causes irritation of the descending nerve root, in this case S1, which would lead to dermatomal changes on the plantar aspect of the foot and loss of the Achilles reflex.
Answer 5: Isthmic spondylolisthesis involves pars insufficiency and the pars pathology causes impingement of the exiting nerve root as it is exiting through the foramen. At L4-L5, this would affect the L4 nerve root.
