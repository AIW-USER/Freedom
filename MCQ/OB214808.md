## Answer explanation

Among the above radiographs, Neurofibromatosis Type I is most commonly associated with congenital pseudoarthrosis of the forearm (Figure E).

Neurofibromatosis Type I (NF1) is an autosomal dominant (AD) disorder. NF is commonly associated with the following orthopedic manifestations: anterolateral tibial bowing, pseudoarthrosis of the tibia, pseudoarthrosis of the forearm, hemihypertrophy, scoliosis, and atlantoaxial instability. Characteristics of forearm pseudoarthrosis consist of outward deformity, little outward signs of trauma, the disappearance of the medullary canal in the distal third of the forearm, and bowing deformity. Congenital forearm pseudoarthrosis is associated with NF in approximately 50% of cases.

*Bae et al., discussed four cases of congenital ulnar pseudoarthrosis treated with free vascularized fibular graft.* Treatment goals are three-fold for bony healing, DRUJ stability, and continued skeletal growth. **They found** that at an avagere of 60 months all patients had bony union with full wrist motion compared to the contralateral side, the DRUJ was stable, and two patients had continued skeletal growth. **They concluded** the free vascularized fibular graft is a good treatment option.

*Nagaraju et al., reported on a single case of congenital pseudoarthrosis of the ulna that was reconstructed into a single bone forearm.* **They found** that it prevents the loss of hand function by effective shortening of the flexor tendons and preserves humeroulnar movement and radiocarpal movement.

Bayne discussed that early treatment of congenital pseudoarthrosis is necessary to prevent permanent angulation and shortening of the forearm with loss of function. Treatment involves the complete replacement of defective bone and excision of the abnormal fibrous tissue.

Figure A is a radiograph of a Radial club hand. Figure B is a radiograph of congenital radial head dislocation. Figure C is a radiograph of Madelung's Deformity. Figure D is a radiograph of congenital radioulnar synostosis. Figure E is a radiograph of Congenital Pseudoarthrosis of the Ulna.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Figure A is a radiograph of Radial club hand, which is more commonly associated with VATER, VACTERL, Fanconi's Anemia, and Holt-Oram Syndrome.
Answer 2: Figure B is a radiograph of Congenital Radial Head dislocation, which is more commonly associated with longitudinal deficiencies of the radius, proximal radioulnar synostosis, and malformations within the radiocapitellar joint.
Answer 3: Figure C is a radiograph of Madelung's Deformity, which is more commonly associated with Leri-Weill Dyschondrosteosis.
Answer 4: Figure D is a radiograph of congenital radioulnar synostosis, which is more commonly associated with Apert Syndrome, Carpenter's Syndrome, Arthrogryposis, Mandibulofacial Dysostosis, and Klinefelter's Syndrome.
