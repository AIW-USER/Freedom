## Question

A 24-year-old female presents with persistent pain secondary to a symptomatic cartilaginous calcaneonavicular coalition failed by conservative measures. Preoperative MRI shows no arthritic degeneration in adjacent joints, and the coalition involves less than 1/3rd of the joint surface. Which surgical management protocol offers the most predictable long-term outcome for this patient?

## Options

1. Subtalar arthroereisis utilizing a synthetic intra-sinus tarsi implant
2. Isolated resection of the coalition bridge with interposition of the extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) muscle belly or autologous fat graft
3. Medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy (MDCO) combined with flexor digitorum longus (FDL) transfer
4. Double arthrodesis involving the talocalcaneal and calcaneocuboid joints

## Correct option

**2. Isolated resection of the coalition bridge with interposition of the extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) muscle belly or autologous fat graft**

## Answer explanation

The management of rigid pes planus secondary to tarsal coalition in adults represents a distinct clinical challenge compared to the pediatric or adolescent population. In adults, the underlying coalition has typically fully ossified (synostosis), and the hindfoot has been locked in a fixed, non-reducible valgus posture for years.

By the time an adult presents with symptoms, the primary driver of pain is rarely the coalition itself. Instead, it is secondary degenerative arthritis in adjacent joints (talonavicular, calcaneocuboid, or ankle) caused by decades of altered kinematics, or severe lateral hindfoot impingement (subfibular impingement).

Management is tailored hierarchically based on the degree of secondary joint degeneration and structural deformity.

1. Clinical & Diagnostic Evaluation Prior to Treatment

Before initiating any treatment plan, a precise mapping of the foot is mandatory:

 - Weight-Bearing Radiographs: To assess the overall alignment, calcaneal pitch, and look for secondary signs (e.g., talonavicular beaking, subfibular impingement).

 - Computed Tomography (CT): The gold standard for adult management. It accurately quantifies the exact size and percentage of joint involvement of the coalition, and detects subclinical osteoarthritis in the surrounding joints.

 - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):
 Useful if a rare fibrous or cartilaginous coalition is suspected, or to assess for bone marrow edema indicative of active mechanical stress at the subfibular space or spring ligament complex.

2. Non-Operative Management (First-Line)

While conservative management cannot correct the rigid structural deformity, it aims to eliminate mechanical motion at the symptomatic interfaces and reduce inflammatory pain. It is indicated for patients with mild-to-moderate symptoms or those who are poor surgical candidates.

 - Rigid Custom Orthoses: Unlike flexible flatfoot where orthotics attempt to recreate an arch, an orthotic for a rigid flatfoot must be accommodative. A rigid University of California Biomechanics Laboratory (UCBL) shoe insert or a custom-molded orthotic is used to support the foot in its deformed position, preventing compensatory midfoot motion and reducing shearing forces.

 - Ankle-Foot Orthoses (AFO): For severe pain, a baseline Arizona brace or a solid-ankle AFO completely restricts subtalar and midtarsal motion, transferring the ground reaction forces directly to the leg.

 - Diagnostic/Therapeutic Injections:
 Targeted fluoroscopically or ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injections into the subtalar or talonavicular joint can provide temporary pain relief and confirm which joint is the primary pain generator.

 - Activity Modification & NSAIDs:
 Restricting high-impact, pivoting activities on uneven ground helps manage acute flare-ups.

3. Surgical Management

When conservative measures fail to provide functional relief, surgical intervention is required. The choice of procedure depends strictly on the size of the coalition and the presence of adjacent joint arthritis.

A. Coalition Resection (Excision)

Excision of the coalition is the gold standard in children, but its role in adults is highly controversial and strictly selective.

 - Indications:

 - Calcaneonavicular (CN) coalition involving of the joint surface.

 - Talocalcaneal (TC) coalition involving of the middle facet.

 - Absolute requirement: Completely normal, non-arthritic adjacent joints (talonavicular and subtalar posterior facet).

 - Technique: Complete excision of the bony bridge down to normal margins. To prevent recurrence, an interposition spacer must be placed in the void. For CN coalitions, the extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) muscle belly is interpositioned. For TC coalitions, an autologous fat graft or bone wax is utilized.

 - Adult Outcomes: Results are less predictable than in adolescents. Adults often have long-standing adaptive shortening of the lateral structures (peroneal tendons) and contracture of the Achilles tendon, which must be addressed concurrently via a gastrocnemius recession or Achilles tendon lengthening.

B. Hindfoot Arthrodesis (The Standard Adult Protocol)

The majority of symptomatic adults present with advanced joint destruction or a massive coalition ( of the joint space). In these cases, joint-preserving resection will fail, and structural realignment via selective fusion is required.

1. Isolated Subtalar Arthrodesis

 - Indications: Isolated, painful talocalcaneal middle facet coalition where the midtarsal joints (talonavicular and calcaneocuboid) are completely spared from degeneration, and the forefoot-to-hindfoot relationship is acceptable.

 - Technique: The remaining middle facet and the intact posterior facet of the subtalar joint are denuded of cartilage, realigned out of valgus, and fused using large fragment compressive screws.

2. Double or Triple Arthrodesis

 - Indications: The classic presentation of severe adult rigid pes planus. Indicated when there is widespread secondary arthritis across the hindfoot complex, severe fixed valgus deformity, or significant subfibular impingement.

 - Technique:

 - Double Arthrodesis: Fuses the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints (the transverse tarsal joint) along with the subtalar joint if the coalition is structural.

 - Triple Arthrodesis: The historical benchmark. Involves the surgical fusion of the talocalcaneal (subtalar), talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid joints.

 - Realignment Goal: The primary goal is not just fusion, but structural correction. The surgeon must re-align the hindfoot back to neutral ( of valgus), correct the forefoot varus/abduction to restore a plantigrade foot, and decompress the lateral column to eliminate subfibular bony rubbing.

C. Concomitant Soft-Tissue Procedures

Rigid fusions or resections in adults must frequently be paired with soft-tissue balancing because the foot has lived in a deformed state for decades:

 - Strayer Procedure / Gastrocnemius Recession: To correct the chronic, fixed equinus contracture that invariably co-exists with long-standing rigid flatfoot.

 - Peroneal Tenolysis / Lengthening:
 To relieve the chronic peroneal spasticity and adaptive shortening of the lateral tendons.
