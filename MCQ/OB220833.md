## Question

An obese 42-year-old man presents with a 2-year history of joint pain and swelling involving the wrists, knees, and several small joints of the hands. He also has extensive psoriatic plaques on his elbows and scalp. He reports morning stiffness lasting over an hour and significant impairment in daily activities. Examination reveals pain with range of motion in multiple joints and moderate-to-severe skin involvement. He has trialed NSAIDs with limited relief of his joint pain. Radiographs show end-stage degenerative changes of the right knee and he elects to undergo total knee arthroplasty. Which of the following interventions would have most likely reduced his odds of requiring a surgical intervention?

## Options

1. Continued NSAIDs and activity modification
2. A formalized weight loss program with bariatric surgery
3. Administration of a TNF inhibitor
4. Initiation of hydroxychloroquine therapy
5. Use of daily oral prednisone

## Correct option

**3. Administration of a TNF inhibitor**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with both skin and joint manifestations of psoriatic arthritis. Beginning therapy with a TNF inhibitor best reduces the likelihood of progressive joint destruction, deformity, and future orthopedic surgical intervention in a patient with severe psoriatic arthritis (Answer 3).

Psoriatic arthritis is an inflammatory disease affecting both joints and skin, and in patients with active disease who have failed nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, the risk of long-term disability is significant. The American College of Rheumatology recommends escalation to biologic DMARDs, particularly TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, or IL-12/23 inhibitors, as first-line agents for these patients. Conventional synthetic DMARDs (such as methotrexate, leflunomide, and sulfasalazine) have limited efficacy in preventing joint damage and controlling skin disease in this context.

*Ogdie et al. compare major international treatment guidelines for psoriatic arthritis,* **highlighting** a systems-based approach to therapy selection and the importance of tailoring treatment to individual patient manifestations. They discuss the recommendations from the American College of Rheumatology/National Psoriasis Foundation, EULAR, and GRAPPA, **concluding** that the use of biologic DMARDs (such as TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, and IL-12/23 inhibitors) for patients with active disease are more effective than conventional synthetic DMARDs.

*Ruta et al. evaluated the frequency of clinical features in psoriatic arthritis (PsA) patients presenting with polyarthralgia, comparing them to those with other diagnoses, including rheumatoid arthritis.* **The findings demonstrated** that PsA was present in 8.3% of this cohort and was strongly associated with a personal or family history of psoriasis, radiographic erosions, and inflammatory findings on power Doppler ultrasound, while the presence of rheumatoid factor or anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies was negatively associated with PsA diagnosis. **They conclude** that targeted clinical, family history, and imaging assessments are crucial in differentiating PsA from other causes of polyarthralgia at initial presentation.

*Ackar et al. completed a large database study assessing adults with axial spondyloarthritis and psoriatic arthritis.* *They stratified patients usage of non-TNF inhibitor biologics, TNF inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, NSAIDs, and DMARDs.* Only non-TNF biologics, TNF inhibitors, and traditional DMARDs were protective against future requirement for lower extremity arthroplasty. **The authors concluded** that biologic and conventional DMARDs may reduce the risk of end-stage arthritis requiring joint replacement in these populations, but longer-term data is needed to clarify the role of JAK inhibitors.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While effective for symptomatic relief of joint pain, NSAIDs do not alter disease progression or prevent long-term structural damage.
Answer 2: Weight reduction can improve symptoms and reduce biomechanical stress on affected joints, but it does not directly address the underlying inflammatory process driving psoriatic arthritis or reliably prevent structural progression.
Answer 4: While hydroxychloroquine may be used in rheumatoid arthritis, it is not approved for psoriatic arthritis as the medication may precipitate or exacerbate psoriasis.
Answer 5: Chronic systemic glucocorticoids are not recommended in psoriatic arthritis due to limited disease-modifying potential and the risk of inducing severe psoriasis flares, particularly pustular variants, upon withdrawal.
