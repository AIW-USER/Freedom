## Question

An 8-year-old girl with spastic diplegia presents with a persistent dynamic equinus deformity. Clinical examination reveals the following:

Silfverskiöld Test: With the knee extended, the ankle is fixed in 10° of equinus. With the knee flexed to 90°, the ankle can be passively dorsiflexed to 15° above neutral.

Gait Analysis: Persistent toe-walking with excessive knee extension (rectus femoris overactivity) in early stance, but no evidence of hamstring contracture or knee flexion deformity.

Which surgical procedure is most appropriate to address her equinus while minimizing the risk of postoperative "crouch gait"?

## Options

1. Percutaneous Tendo-Achilles Lengthening (TAL)
2. Vulpius-type gastrocnemius-soleus recession
3. Strayer procedure (isolated gastrocnemius recession)
4. Split Anterior Tibialis Tendon Transfer (SPLATT)

## Correct option

**3. Strayer procedure (isolated gastrocnemius recession)**

## Answer explanation

The Silfverskiöld Test: This is the "gold standard" for differentiating gastrocnemius tightness from soleus/Achilles contracture. Because the deformity corrects to 15° of dorsiflexion when the knee is flexed (neutralizing the gastrocnemius), the contracture is isolated to the gastrocnemius.

Avoiding Crouch Gait: Over-lengthening the Achilles tendon (Choice A) in a patient with spastic diplegia is a classic surgical error. It weakens the plantarflexion-knee extension couple, leading to excessive dorsiflexion and subsequent knee flexion (crouch gait).

 The Strayer Procedure: This involves recession of the gastrocnemius fascia only. Since the soleus is left intact, the power of the triceps surae is better preserved, and the "tension" required to prevent the tibia from tilting too far forward (which causes the knee to buckle into crouch) is maintained.

 Choice B (Vulpius): This lengthens both the gastrocnemius and the soleus. While less aggressive than a TAL, it still risks more weakness than a Strayer in a patient where only the gastroc is tight.

Choice D (SPLATT): This is indicated for dynamic equinovarus (hindfoot varus), not simple equinus.

The Vulpius vs. Strayer Distinction

While both are used when the Silfverskiöld test is positive, they differ in the anatomy of the cut:

 - Vulpius Procedure: Involves an inverted V-shaped incision through the gastrocnemius aponeurosis. Because the aponeurosis of the gastrocnemius often lies directly over (and is partially fused with) the soleus fascia at that level, a Vulpius frequently results in a lengthening of both the gastrocnemius and the underlying soleus fascia.

 - Strayer Procedure: Involves a transverse incision higher up, specifically designed to separate the gastrocnemius tendon from the underlying soleus muscle and fascia. This is considered a "purer" isolated gastrocnemius recession.

If the question specifies "minimizing the risk of postoperative crouch gait," It is about preserving the soleus at all costs.

In strict anatomical terms, the Strayer is the most "proximal" and "isolated" of the two (Vulpius and Strayer). In diplegic CP (as opposed to hemiplegic CP), the risk of crouch is so high that the more conservative/isolated the procedure, the "safer" the answer is considered.
