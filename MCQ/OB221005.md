## Question

A 22-year-old special forces soldier is increasing his training regimen in anticipation of an upcoming deployment. He presents to the clinic with continued left lower leg pain, which has made his training difficult. Radiographs are shown in Figure A. He has been so focused on his upcoming deployment in four months that he has not sought care for his leg pain until this visit. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment to provide the patient the best chance to deploy with his unit?

![[q221005-a-lat.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Activity modification with vitamin D supplementation
2. Continuation of activity with pulsed low-intensity ultrasound therapy
3. Pneumatic leg bracing with non-weight-bearing
4. Observation with repeat imaging in 3 months
5. Tibial intramedullary nail

## Correct option

**5. Tibial intramedullary nail**

## Answer explanation

This soldier has a tibial stress fracture with a time-sensitive functional demand. Surgical treatment with a tibial intramedullary nail provides the most reliable option for union in a faster timeline (answer choice 5).

Tibial stress fractures are overuse injuries from repetitive loading that exceeds bone repair. Patients will often present with anterior tibial pain that worsens with activity and improves with rest. Risk factors include a sudden increase in training intensity (military recruits, runners), low bone mineral density, menstrual irregularities, and low energy availability. Diagnosis can be made on plain radiographs, with MRI reserved for fractures not visible on plain radiographs. On plain radiographs, the presence of a transverse lucency in the anterior cortex is often termed the "dreaded black line" due to the indication of a more severe fracture pattern. Conservative care is the first line of treatment and includes activity modification/rest, addressing nutritional deficiencies, or pneumatic braces. Indications for surgical management include high-risk fractures (anterior tibial cortex), failure of nonoperative management, complete fractures, or the need to return to activity on a specific timeline (athletes, military, etc.). Fractures of the anterior cortex (tension side) are considered high-risk due to the potential for delayed union, nonunion, or progression to complete fracture.

*Feldman et al. provide a review of tibial stress fractures in athletes.* **The authors note** that careful clinical evaluation and appropriate imaging are essential for accurate diagnosis and to distinguish these injuries from other causes of shin pain. **They report** that tibial stress fractures are managed conservatively with activity modification, rest, and rehabilitation, but fractures at certain locations (anterior tibial cortex) or those that fail to heal with nonoperative care may require surgical intervention, with various fixation techniques available and different risk-benefit profiles depending on fracture characteristics and the athlete’s needs. Physicians treating athletic populations should understand the underlying fracture biology, risk factors, and healing potential to optimize treatment and facilitate a safe return to sport.

*Rue et al. published a prospective, randomized study evaluating the efficacy of daily pulsed ultrasound therapy in treating tibial stress fractures.* *The study included 26 military servicemembers with 43 tibial stress fractures that were randomly assigned to receive either pulsed ultrasound therapy or a placebo.* **The authors found** no difference in time to return to duty or healing time between the two groups. **The authors concluded** that pulsed ultrasound does not improve fracture healing in this cohort.

*Batt et al. published a case series of three patients with tibial stress fractures treated with pneumatic bracing and activity modifications.* All three patients (four stress fractures) had diagnosed tibial stress fractures on radiographs, consistent with their history and examination, for an average of 9 months before initiation of treatment. The patients underwent conservative treatment with a pneumatic lower leg brace combined with modified rest. All patients successfully returned to unrestricted activity at an average of 12 months. **The authors concluded** that pneumatic bracing and activity modification can be an effective nonoperative strategy for tibial stress fractures.

The lateral view demonstrates anterior cortical thickening and a transverse lucency of the anterior cortex known as the "dreaded black line."

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: This fracture is at a high-risk area (anterior tibia) with a time-sensitive return to activity requirement. Activity modification is not appropriate given the high risk of either fracture completion or nonunion. Furthermore, there is no indication in the stem that the patient has a nutritional deficiency that warrants addressing.
Answer choice 2: Ultrasound therapy has not been shown to reduce time to return to activity or improve healing rates and is therefore not recommended as a treatment for tibial stress fractures.
Answer choice 3: Pneumatic bracing has been shown to effectively treat similar fractures in similar patient populations. However, healing can require up to 12 months before return to full activity. Due to this patient's requirements for an earlier return, this treatment modality would not be ideal.
Answer choice 4: Observation would not be appropriate due to the risk of progression to complete fracture. Anterior tibial stress fractures with a clear fracture line on radiographs are high-risk fractures that warrant active management.
