## Question

A 3-year-old male presents to the clinic because his mother reports that the bowing of his legs has progressively worsened since he began walking two years ago. She also notes that he has been tripping more recently. A radiograph taken in the office today is shown in Figure A. The patient was delivered vaginally at 38 weeks and has met all developmental milestones. Which physical exam finding is most likely to be abnormal in this patient?

![[q221008-a-img-9184.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral thrust while walking
2. Internal thigh-foot-angle of 5°
3. Mild flexible forefoot adduction
4. Tibia recurvatum deformity
5. Distal femur varus deformity

## Correct option

**1. Lateral thrust while walking**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with infantile Blount's disease. A common abnormal physical exam finding in these patients includes a lateral thrust with gait (Answer 1).

Infantile Blount's disease consists of progressive pathologic proximal tibia vara in children two to five years of age. This disease occurs more commonly in males, with bilateral involvement in 50% of patients. Risk factors include overweight children, Hispanic and African American children, and early walkers (<1 year of age). The pathophysiology is multifactorial and related to mechanical overload in genetically susceptible children. Diagnosis can be made on physical exam and with plain radiographs. Physical exam typically demonstrates genu varum, tibia procurvatum, and internal tibial torsion, with a potential positive cover-up test. They also present with a lateral thrust while walking. Plain radiographs demonstrate proximal tibia metaphyseal beaking with a varus deformity. Treatment consists of bracing to surgical correction and depends on the child's age, the severity of medial metaphyseal beaking, and the presence of a physeal bar.

*Lincoln and Suen published a review on common rotational variation in children.* **They state** that in-toeing, out-toeing, and torticollis are typically benign and resolve spontaneously in children. Considering a rotational profile for each of these children is important to identify the culprit. Metatarsus adductus is typically found in infants, internal tibial torsion in toddlers, and femoral anteversion in children younger than 10 years of age. **They conclude** that although congenital torticollis is the most common reason for atypical head posture in children, serious conditions such as osseous malformations, inflammation, and neurogenic disorders should be excluded.

*Brooks and Gross reviewed the diagnosis and treatment of genu varum in children.* **They state** that genu varum is a relatively common finding in children. Physiologic bowing typically resolves by three years of age, and idiopathic tibia vara is the most common pathologic cause of genu varum. Conditions such as achondroplasia, vitamin D-resistant rickets, renal osteodystrophy, and osteogenesis imperfecta may also be associated with genu varum. They add that a standing radiograph of the entire lower extremity is essential for surgical planning, and restoration of the mechanical axis of the limb is the principal goal of treatment.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the bilateral lower extremities in a skeletally immature patient demonstrating medial metaphyseal beaking of bilateral proximal tibias.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 2: An internal thigh-foot angle of 5° is a normal physical exam finding in an infant.
Answer 3: Mild flexible metatarsus adductus is not associated with Blount's disease; internal tibial torsion is.
Answer 4: Tibia procurvatum is the deformity seen in patients with Blount's disease, not recurvatum.
Answer 5: An associated varus deformity in the distal femur is more commonly seen in adolescent (>10 years of age) Blount's disease.
