## Question

A 14-year-old male presents to the emergency department via ambulance. The EMTs report that he was thrown from the back of an all-terrain vehicle and has been complaining of profound right hip pain. It is currently 10:25 PM; the EMTs report that the accident happened at approximately 6:30 PM, but due to the injury location in the desert, the transfer was delayed. There are no open wounds, and the patient's right lower extremity is shortened and externally rotated. Radiographs are obtained, and a select image is available for review in Figure A. Which of the following factors is most important in minimizing the patient's chances of developing femoral head avascular necrosis?

## Options

1. Avoiding open reduction techniques during operative fixation
2. Definitive operative fixation within two hours of arrival in your emergency department
3. Placement of skeletal traction in the emergency department, prior to operative fixation
4. Obtaining an anatomic reduction during operative fixation
5. Performance of a capsulotomy during operative fixation

## Correct option

**4. Obtaining an anatomic reduction during operative fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a Type III (cervicotrochanteric) proximal femur fracture. The most important factor in minimizing the risk of developing avascular necrosis (AVN) following this injury is the quality of reduction obtained during operative fixation (Answer 4).

Pediatric proximal femur fractures are rare injuries, often caused by high-energy trauma, and typically occur in the setting of polytrauma. These injuries account for <1% of all pediatric fractures, and occur in males 2.5x more frequently than females. In patients <2-3 years old, they often occur secondary to non-accidental trauma (NAT). In older adolescents, they often occur during motor or recreational vehicle accidents. Anywhere from 30-85% of patients will have associated traumatic injuries. Patients will often report severe pain in the affected hip and will be unable to bear weight through the affected extremity. In addition, the affected extremity is often shortened and externally rotated.

Pediatric proximal femur fractures are classified according to the Delbet classification (Illustration A). This classification system is useful as it is not only diagnostic but prognostic, as there are good data that correlate Delbet classification with both avascular necrosis and nonunion rates. In almost all cases, these injuries represent fractures of necessity and are treated through a variety of fixation techniques. Historically, these injuries were considered an orthopaedic emergency, and were brought to the operating room within 6 hours of injury. Newer data, however, have demonstrated that quality of reduction is the single most important variable in predicting both patient outcomes and future complications. As a result, the current recommendation is to perform definitive fixation with a well-rested and experienced team, to ensure that all possible steps to obtain an anatomic reduction are taken.

*Shrader et al. performed a retrospective review of all pediatric femoral neck fractures at a level 1 trauma center.* *Their study included 20 patients treated over 30 years.* The mean patient age was 11, and their cohort was predominantly male (14; 70%). The authors investigated the timing of surgery, type of fixation, utilization of capsular decompression, and quality of reduction and how these factors influenced the rate of the future development of femoral head osteonecrosis. Five patients were treated more than 48 hours after injury, and two of these patients developed osteonecrosis. 18 patients healed without complication; all had good or excellent reduction and were treated, on average, 12 hours after injury. Overall, **the authors found** that poor reduction and time to definitive fixation >24-48 hours were associated with the subsequent development of osteonecrosis. In addition, **they found** no relationship between capsular decompression and the future development of osteonecrosis.

*Stone et al. investigated the influence of either open or closed reduction on the development of future osteonecrosis following femoral neck fractures in pediatric patients.* *The study included 22 patients; 6 were treated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) and 16 were treated with closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF).* **The authors found** that patients treated with CRIF develop osteonecrosis at a far higher rate (50%) when compared to patients treated with ORIF (0%). In addition, patients treated with ORIF had a higher quality of reduction, a higher rate of union, and fewer complications than patients who underwent CRIF. Overall, **the authors conclude** that ORIF allows for better reduction, which they identified as a major predictor of patient outcomes and complications.

*Stockton et al. investigated the failure patterns of femoral neck fracture fixation in young patients.* *The authors defined failure as screw cutout, implant breakage, varus collapse, or severe fracture shortening.* **The authors found** that fracture shortening >1cm was the most common failure mode observed in their cohort. **The authors found** that each fixation construct (multiple cancellous screws or sliding hip screw with a derotation screw) was associated with a unique mode of failure. Overall, **the authors conclude** that each construct has unique modes of failure and that critical evaluation of the fracture pattern and knowledge of the most likely mode of failure can be used by surgeons to minimize the rate of future hardware-related complications.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: studies have demonstrated that open reduction techniques allow for a more anatomic reduction, which should decrease the rate of future complications.
Answer 2: historically, these injuries were treated as orthopaedic emergencies. More recent data, however, have shown that delaying definitive fixation up to 24 hours does not increase the complication rates for patients.
Answer 3: skeletal traction can potentially put the capsule on tension and increase intracapsular pressure, which may increase the risk of the development of AVN
Answer 5: the role of capsulotomy is still under investigation. While the role is backed by basic science, the performance of a capsulotomy has not been demonstrated to ameliorate the risk of the development of AVN.
