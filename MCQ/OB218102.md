## Question

A 47-year-old attorney was skiing on a winter vacation in the Alps and fell directly onto his shoulder while attempting moguls. He has since had significant anterior shoulder pain, particularly at night, as well as notable weakness with attempted shoulder range of motion. His MRI is shown in Figure A. Given these findings, which of the following physical exam maneuvers is most important to perform during the office visit?

![[q218102-a-screenshot-2023-07-23-at-8-17-53-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Drop arm test
2. External rotation lag
3. Hornblower's test
4. Kim test
5. Lift-off test

## Correct option

**5. Lift-off test**

## Answer explanation

The patient's MRI demonstrates a dislocated biceps tendon (Illustration A), which is most closely associated with a tear of the subscapularis tendon. In the setting of biceps tendon subluxation or dislocation, a positive lift-off sign (Answer 5) would suggest a clinically significant tear of the subscapularis tendon, which may not be obvious on MRI.

The biceps tendon originates at the supraglenoid tubercle/superior labrum and is stabilized by the biceps sling or pulley, which is comprised of fibers from the subscapularis tendon, the coracohumeral ligament, and the superior glenohumeral ligaments. MRI does not reliably predict the presence of a tear of the subscapularis tendon unless the tear extends at least half the cranio-caudal distance of the tendon. Therefore, smaller tears can be initially missed. Given the intimate association of the tendon with the biceps pulley, subluxation or dislocation of the tendon would warrant subscapularis muscle strength testing, especially if a tear is not grossly evident on MRI. The Lift-off test or sign, as originally described by Gerber (Illustration B), involves placing the back of the affected arm away from the mid-lumbar spine area with the arm in internal rotation. If the hand falls and hits the back or the patient compensates by extending the elbow and shoulder, indicating the patient is unable to maintain the internally rotated position, there is likely a subscapularis tendon tear.

*Shi et al. performed a study to assess the accuracy of biceps tendon (LHBT) subluxation as a predictor for subscapularis tears.* *The authors reviewed the MRIs of 94 patients and found that LHBT subluxation on MRI as a predictor for full-thickness subscapularis tears had a sensitivity of 82%, specificity of 80%, positive predictive value of 35%, a negative predictive value of 97%, and was directly correlated with the severity of the subscapularis tendon tear.* **They concluded** that the diagnostic value of subluxation of the LHBT lies primarily in its negative predictive value, meaning that if the LHBT is not subluxated, it is unlikely that a full-thickness tear of the subscapularis tendon is present.

*Koh et al. retrospectively reviewed the MRIs and arthroscopic images of 270 patients to evaluate the relationship of biceps tendon subluxation with the presence of subscapularis tears by evaluating the angle of the biceps tendon with the glenoid.* **The authors concluded** that shoulders with subluxation of the biceps tendon on the preoperative MRI revealed more pathologies in the subscapularis tendon and biceps tendon during arthroscopy. However, the arthroscopically measured biceps-glenoid angle did not have clinical relevance to the determination of subluxation of the LHBT from the bicipital groove.

Figure A is an axial T1-weighted MRI demonstrating a grossly dislocated biceps tendon sitting medial to the groove at the level of the glenoid. Illustration A is an annotated version of Figure A, with the red arrow indicating the dislocated biceps tendon and the star indicating the bicipital groove where the tendon would normally be located if the pulley/sling tissues were intact. Illustration B depicts Dr. Gerber performing the lift-off test, with inset A showing proper passive positioning of the arm by the examiner, and inset B showing a positive test with the patient unable to hold the hand off of the lower back.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The drop arm test reflects weakness of the supraspinatus, not the subscapularis.
Answer 2: An external rotation lag sign indicates weakness of the infraspinatus.
Answer 3: A positive Hornblower's test indicates weakness of the teres minor, which is noted to be intact on this patient's MRI.
Answer 4: The Kim test is most sensitive for detecting posteroinferior labral tears, not subscapularis tears.
