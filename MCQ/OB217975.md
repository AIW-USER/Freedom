## Question

A 27-year-old female presents with posterolateral shoulder pain of 4 months duration. She states the pain is deep within the shoulder and causes her to feel weak with overhead movements. On examination, active range of motion is full. Strength is 5/5 in abduction, forward elevation and internal rotation. External rotation strength is 4/5 with the arm at the side. Jobe's and Hornblowers tests are negative and O'Brien's tests is equivocal. She has been going to physical therapy for about 6 weeks without appreciable benefit in strength or pain. MRI is obtained which demonstrates postero-superior labral tear, associated paralabral cyst and mild atrophy of the infraspinatus muscle. What is the next most appropriate step in management?

## Options

1. Obtain cervical MRI & NCS/EMG
2. Continue physical therapy for another 6 weeks
3. Arthroscopic biceps tenodesis
4. Arthroscopic labral repair only
5. Arthroscopic labral repair with cyst decompression

## Correct option

**5. Arthroscopic labral repair with cyst decompression**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a posterosuperior labral tear and paralabral cyst which is causing compression of the suprascapular nerve at the spinoglenoid notch and should be treated surgically by addressing the labral tear and decompressing the cyst.

The suprascapular nerve innervates the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles. It crosses under the transverse scapular ligament and impingement at this location leads to denervation of both the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. As the nerve courses further, it traverses the spinoglenoid notch to innervate the infraspinatus muscle. Therefore, compression at the spinoglenoid notch only affects the infraspinatus. The most common etiology of spinoglenoid notch compression is a paralabral cyst associated with a labral tear. In cases where mechanical compression is identified at the spinoglenoid notch, operative intervention should be undertaken to decompress the obstruction. At the time of decompression, the labral tear should be addressed as this is usually the reason for the cyst forming initially.

*Kostretzis et al. reviewed suprascapular nerve pathology,* **noting** that the diagnosis is becoming increasingly recognized in conditions causing shoulder pain. **They note** it is more common in overhead athletes and those with large rotator cuff tears. **They conclude** by noting that the location and cause of the nerve compression are often the factors that dictate treatment.

*Momaya et al. reviewed the collective clinical outcomes after decompression of the suprascapular nerve in 21 studies.* **They noted** that the most common pre-operative symptom was deep, posterior shoulder pain and that, on average, a diagnosis took 19 months after symptoms began. This said, outcome scores improved significantly after decompression, leading to high rates of return to play for athletes and low rates of complication.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While shoulder weakness out of proportion may be related to a cervical radiculopathy, usually in the C5 distribution, this patient has a specific pattern of denervation related to suprascapular neuropathy.
Answer 2: With the paralabral cyst causing compression on the suprascapular nerve at the spinoglenoid notch, the cyst should be decompressed, rather than proceeding with continued observation and therapy.
Answers 3 & 4: Arthroscopic biceps tenodesis or labral repair only would address the biceps/labral complex pathology, but would not address the resulting cyst which is compressing the suprascapular nerve and would not prevent worsening of infraspinatus atrophy and external rotation weakness.
