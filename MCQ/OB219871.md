## Question

A 62-year-old female presents to the emergency department for evaluation of right shoulder pain following anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty ten weeks prior. The patient states she tripped over her dog, falling onto her right shoulder and hearing a pop. On examination, pain is noted in the anterior shoulder. Lift-off and bear hug tests are positive, indicating a suspected subscapularis rupture. When reviewing the previous operative note, the subscapularis was found to have been repaired following tenotomy. Which of the following is true concerning the differences between the subscapularis peel, tenotomy, and lesser tuberosity osteotomy techniques?

## Options

1. Lesser tuberosity osteotomy has lower failure rates
2. Peel technique facilitates increased range of motion
3. Tenotomy exhibits higher failure rates
4. Peel technique exhibits the highest biomechanical strength
5. No differences are observed between techniques

## Correct option

**5. No differences are observed between techniques**

## Answer explanation

This 62-year-old female experienced subscapularis rupture following a fall after undergoing total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), which included subscapularis repair following tenotomy. Current literature suggests no difference between methods of subscapularis management (Answer 5).

Total shoulder arthroplasty is becoming an increasingly common procedure secondary to an amalgamation of an aging population, technical improvements, and optimization of clinical outcomes. The vast majority of shoulder replacements are performed utilizing the deltopectoral approach, which necessitates the release of subscapularis in some capacity, either by peel, tenotomy, or lesser tubercle osteotomy, in order to gain access to the glenohumeral joint. Regardless of the method chosen, a feared complication is subscapularis failure/insufficiency following repair, which can result in instability and/or early component loosening. Because of this, a vast amount of research has been performed, with the majority reporting no difference in biomechanical strength, range of motion/function, or failure rates.

Piper and Horneff review intraoperative subscapularis management, including tenotomy, peel, osteotomy, and subscapularis-sparing techniques, as well as the management of subscapularis insufficiency following TSA. The authors report recent data suggests no tangible differences between techniques concerning both objective and subjective measures and that patients often go on to experience good outcomes regardless of the technique chosen. Of those experiencing subscapularis failure, the authors recommend repair if less than six weeks from surgery, while viable alternatives exist after six weeks, including anterior capsular reconstruction, tendon transfers, or conversion to reverse TSA, with the latter being the most reliable procedure for chronic insufficiency.

*Cox et al. performed a retrospective review of 261 patients to examine the effect of the humeral component stem length on the restoration of native humeral head anatomy.* *Of the 261 patients, 31 were stemless, 41 were short-stemmed, and 187 were standard-stemmed.* After radiographic examination, there were no differences observed in restoration of the center of rotation or humeral head height. However, stemless stems were more likely to be placed in varus. **The authors concluded** the inclusion of standard stems would likely aid in accurately restoring patient anatomy.

*Shields et al. reviewed the subscapularis management during TSAs, which included a synopsis of the anatomy, techniques (peel, tenotomy, osteotomy, or subscapular-sparing approaches), and outcomes.* **The authors note** no apparent differences between techniques concerning biomechanical strength, range of motion, and failure rates, and conclude no recommendation can be made as no technique exhibits superiority over the others.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: No differences have been observed between techniques including the subscapularis peel, tenotomy, and lesser tuberosity osteotomy.
