## Question

You are seeing a 47-year-old male who you previously diagnosed with lateral epicondylitis but has failed conservative treatment with physical therapy and injections. He states the proximal forearm strap you prescribed made his symptoms worse. You are concerned there may be concomitant nerve compression at the Arcade of Frohse. Which of the following clinical symptoms would result from compression at this site?

## Options

1. Numbness over dorsoradial hand/wrist
2. Numbness in thumb, index, and middle fingers worsened with active forearm pronosupination
3. Pain in dorsoradial proximal forearm worsened with passive forearm pronation
4. Pain and crepitus in dorsoradial forearm with repetitive wrist extension
5. Weakness with thumb IP flexion

## Correct option

**3. Pain in dorsoradial proximal forearm worsened with passive forearm pronation**

## Answer explanation

Compression at the Arcade of Frohse (proximal supinator muscle) results in compression of the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) which can cause radial tunnel syndrome.

Radial tunnel syndrome is relatively uncommon, but is caused by compression of the PIN at the Arcade of Frohse. Compression of the PIN at this level can cause PIN compression syndrome or radial tunnel syndrome. While both have similar sites of compression, the symptoms help to distinguish the diagnosis. In radial tunnel syndrome, the primary symptom is dorsoradial forearm pain approximately 4cm distal to the lateral epicondyle without any motor or sensory deficits. This said, radial tunnel syndrome is closely associated with the symptoms of lateral epicondylitis and can occur concomitantly in about 5% of cases. In radial tunnel syndrome, pain can be caused with passive forearm pronation, active supination or active 3rd finger extension. In PIN compression syndrome, weakness of finger and wrist extension is typically present and is the distinguishing factor.

*Lubahn et al. published a review of uncommon upper extremity nerve compression syndromes.* **They emphasized** that surgeons must understand the anatomy of the peripheral nerves with respect to their major sources of compression. They also discuss the varying degrees of treatment for these compression syndromes, **noting** that AIN syndrome is better treated nonoperatively, compared to PIN syndrome which may be better treated surgically.

*Lawrence et al. reviewed 30 cases of radial tunnel syndrome that were decompressed, more commonly in those with occupations performing repetitive manual tasks.* **They note** that radial tunnel syndrome is often under recognized and the symptoms can mimic those of lateral epicondylitis. They also discussed their results, **noting** 70% excellent or good results, with poor results in 17% of cases.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Numbness over the dorsoradial hand represents Wartenberg's syndrome, which is compression of the superficial radial nerve.
Answer 2: Numbness to the radial three digits and pain with active pronosupination is representative of Pronator syndrome, which is compression of the median nerve at the level of the elbow.
Answer 4: Pain and crepitus in the dorsoradial forearm with wrist extension represents Intersection syndrome, which is friction between the 1st and 2nd dorsal compartments.
Answer 5: Weakness with thumb IP flexion represents AIN compression syndrome which is a motor neuropathy without sensory issues.
