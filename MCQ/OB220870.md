## Options

1. The majority of these injuries require operative management
2. Casting is unsafe in managing these injuries due to a high risk of compartment syndrome
3. Delayed follow up of >1 month is recommended to minimize unnecessary healthcare costs
4. Prompt follow-up within 1-2 weeks is recommended due to high risk of secondary displacement
5. A rigid intramedullary nail is the recommended method of operative fixation

## Correct option

**4. Prompt follow-up within 1-2 weeks is recommended due to high risk of secondary displacement**

## Answer explanation

This skeletally immature patient sustained a distal tibia fracture which is amenable to initial intervention with closed reduction and immobilization. Although this is a successful method of treatment for the majority of these injuries, close follow-up is recommended as these injuries demonstrate a high rate of secondary displacement, which may necessitate cast modification (e.g., “wedging”) or operative intervention (answer choice 4).

Pediatric tibial shaft fractures are common long bone injuries in children, typically involving the mid-diaphysis, and can result from high-energy mechanisms in older children or low-energy twisting falls in toddlers. Clinical presentation includes pain, deformity, ecchymosis, or refusal to bear weight, with diagnosis typically confirmed by plain radiographs. Careful clinical assessment is critical to evaluate for potential complications like compartment syndrome or open fracture.

In regard to treatment, distal tibial shaft fractures warrant particular attention, as these may have a high rate of secondary displacement, as well as complications arising from physeal involvement. Nonoperative management with long-leg casting is a common and effective treatment method, particularly for toddler's fractures and stable fracture patterns amenable to closed reduction, often allowing for early weight-bearing as tolerated and avoiding surgical risks. If opting for nonoperative treatment, early serial radiographs are critical to evaluate for secondary displacement, which may be improved with repeat closed reduction or cast modification. For persistently displaced or unstable fractures, operative fixation may be required, either in the form of closed versus open reduction and percutaneous pinning, flexible or rigid intramedullary nails, depending on age, plate osteosynthesis, or external fixator application. Overall outcomes are reasonably good, although complications like leg-length discrepancy, angular deformity, and nonunion or malunion can occur.

*Ramalingam et al. reviewed factors predictive of treatment failure for adolescent distal third tibia fractures, comparing outcomes of casting, closed reduction and casting (CRC), and open treatment.* **They found** that increased fracture severity was significantly linked to treatment failure, with displaced fractures treated by CRC having a 36.7% failure rate and 3.77 times higher odds of persistent malalignment compared to open treatment. Of these failures, 27% healed with malunion and 18% failed initial casting and required operative intervention. **They concluded** that while minimally displaced fractures can be successfully managed with casting, displaced fractures carry a higher risk of short-term failure with CRC, necessitating close monitoring during conservative management.

*Newman et al. reviewed the optimal management of extra-articular distal tibial metadiaphyseal fractures, acknowledging the ongoing controversy surrounding treatment choices.* **They found** that while nonoperative cast bracing may be successful for closed fractures with minimal displacement or angulation, the literature extensively discusses operative options, including intramedullary nailing, plate fixation, and external fixation, as appropriate management. **They concluded** that despite advancements in minimally invasive plating and nail design, further comparative studies are required to establish the superiority of one surgical technique over others.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer Choice 1: The majority of these injuries can be successfully treated nonoperatively.
Answer Choice 2: Although vigilant monitoring of compartment syndrome symptoms is important in treatment of these injuries, acute casting can be safely employed in most instances.
Answer Choice 3: Given the high rate of secondary displacement, delayed follow-up is not recommended.
Answer Choice 5: Although a rigid intramedullary nail may be utilized in cases of skeletally mature patients, this patient has visibly open physes, thus a more physeal-friendly fixation construct would be more appropriate.
