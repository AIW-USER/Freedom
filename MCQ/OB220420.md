## Options

1. Allow to continue training camp in a club cast with dynamic splinting between practices
2. Buddy taping and immediate range of motion to prevent stiffness
3. Extension block splinting for 6 weeks to allow volar rim fracture to heal
4. Fragment excision with volar plate advancement to allow quicker return to play
5. Open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**5. Open reduction and internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

The patient has an acute proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) fracture-dislocation with a volar rim fracture involving more than 50% of the articular surface. This results in an unstable joint and dorsal subluxation of the middle phalanx that creates a "dorsal V sign" (Figure C and Illustration A). The most appropriate treatment for this injury is ORIF (Answer 5).

Phalanx dislocations are common traumatic injuries of the hand in athletes, with PIPJ dislocations representing the most common joint injury in the hand. Dorsal dislocations are more common than volar dislocations and result from PIPJ hyperextension with longitudinal compression (i.e. ball striking fingertip) that tears the collateral ligaments and shears the volar plate off of the base of the middle phalanx. Because the base of the middle phalanx is not in contact with the condyle of the proximal phalanx, the volar plate can act as a block to reduction with longitudinal traction, resulting in incarceration and an irreducible dislocation. When a volar rim fracture of the middle phalanx occurs, on the other hand, it is no longer able to provide adequate volar buttressing, causing instability and dorsal subluxation of the middle phalanx despite adequate splinting in 30° of flexion. This can be seen on radiographs as a "dorsal V sign" (Illustration A), whereby one can visualize a small wedge (or “V”) between the nonparallel articular surfaces at the dorsal aspect of the PIP joint. In this setting, the treatment is always operative, as a failure to restore stability and joint congruency can lead to a rapid deterioration of the joint. Surgical options vary, but most commonly include ORIF when feasible, dynamic external fixation when significant comminution exists, or hemi-hamate arthroplasty when younger patients with significant (~50%) middle phalanx joint involvement have fractures that are not amenable to ORIF.

*Caggiano et al. review the management of proximal interphalangeal joint fracture dislocations.* **The authors note** that dorsal fracture dislocations are generally stable in some degree of flexion along the arc of motion, while in grossly unstable cases, instability is often noted at 30° or more of PIP joint flexion. In this setting, the goals of treatment include restoring a concentric stable joint while allowing for early range of motion, with treatment options ranging from CRPP to ORIF with interfragmentary screws to hemi-hamate arthroplasty. **They conclude** that fracture-dislocations of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint can have dramatic consequences for hand range of motion when left untreated.

*Kolovich et al. reviewed proximal interphalangeal joint dislocations and fracture dislocations.* **The authors note** that although proximal interphalangeal joint dislocations are generally straightforward to treat, fracture dislocations are among the most difficult hand injuries to manage. In general, non-comminuted fractures involving only a minor portion of the middle phalanx articulation (typically less than 30-40%) usually reduce easily and can be treated with splinting or buddy taping with consistently good clinical outcomes. Highly comminuted, displaced, and unstable PIP fracture-dislocations, however, have variable clinical outcomes with high rates of extensor lag and loss of motion. **They conclude** that early diagnosis of these complex fracture patterns coupled with treatment by an experienced surgeon and appropriate patient counseling are important factors in achieving better outcomes.

*Tyser et al. reviewed the biomechanical characteristics of hemi-hamate reconstruction versus volar plate arthroplasty in the treatment of dorsal fracture-dislocations of the proximal interphalangeal joint.* *The authors used 18 digits from 6 cadaver hands to create defects of 40%, 60%, and 80% in the palmar base of each digit's middle phalanx, simulating an acute PIP joint fracture-dislocation.* **They found** that surgeons can use both hemi-hamate and volar plate arthroplasty to restore PIP joint stability following a fracture dislocation with a large middle phalanx palmar base defect; however, they concluded that the use of volar plate arthroplasty led to an increasing flexion contracture as the middle phalanx palmar base defect increased.

Figures A and B are clinical photographs and lateral radiographs, respectively, showing a PIPJ dislocation. Figure C is a lateral radiograph showing a reduced PIPJ dislocation with a large volar rim fracture involving > 50% of the articular surface, resulting in dorsal subluxation of the middle phalanx and incongruency of the joint known as a "dorsal V sign." Illustration A denotes the "dorsal V sign" on the same radiograph shown in Figure C.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Though allowing continued play in a club cast may be reasonable, dynamic splinting is not indicated in this type of unstable fracture dislocation.
Answer 2: Buddy taping with early mobilization is indicated for dorsal PIP dislocations with or without small volar plate avulsion fractures to minimize stiffness, not for unstable dislocations with fracture fragments exceeding 40-50% of the articular surface.
Answer 3: Dorsal extension block splinting with progressive extension would be appropriate if the PIP was well reduced (i.e., no V sign) after splinting. This patient, however, becomes grossly unstable in 30° of flexion.
Answer 4: While volar plate arthroplasty may be an option for patients with fracture fragments not amenable to ORIF or in chronic deformities, young athletic patients with acute injuries are likely better served with ORIF or hemi-hamate arthroplasty.
