## Question

You are the orthopaedic surgeon performing a complex thoracic pedicle screw instrumentation and fusion for a rigid scoliosis in a 16-year-old patient. Intraoperative neuromonitoring is being utilized. During screw placement at T7, the neurophysiologist alerts you to a 60% decrease in the amplitude of the lower extremity motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and a 30% decrease in the amplitude of the lower extremity somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), with a slight increase in SSEP latency. There are no significant changes in upper extremity MEPs or SSEPs. The anesthesiologist confirms that no changes have been made to intravenous anesthetic agents. Based on these neuromonitoring findings and the established protocol for managing such events, what is the MOST appropriate immediate next step?

## Options

1. Administer a bolus of an intravenous anesthetic agent to deepen sedation and re-check signals.
2. Immediately remove the T7 pedicle screw and reverse any deformity correction made.
3. Order a wake-up test to confirm the neurological status of the patient.
4. Reposition the patient to assess for possible spinal cord compression or ischemia related to positioning.
5. Terminate the procedure immediately due to significant neurological compromise.

## Correct option

**4. Reposition the patient to assess for possible spinal cord compression or ischemia related to positioning.**

## Answer explanation

Repositioning the patient is the immediate next step to rule out a reversible cause of neuromonitoring changes, such as positional spinal cord compression or ischemia, before more drastic interventions (answer choice 4).

Intraoperative neuromonitoring changes during spinal surgery require a systematic, checklist-driven approach to identify and address the cause. While the MEP and SSEP changes in this case are significant, they have not yet met the critical thresholds (MEP 80% decrease, SSEP 50% decrease/10% latency increase) that would typically mandate immediate surgical reversal or a wake-up test. Furthermore, MEP changes manifest much faster than SSEP changes. The first line of action, after ruling out anesthetic causes (which the anesthesiologist confirms are stable regarding IV agents), is to investigate reversible mechanical causes. Patient positioning is a common culprit for neuromonitoring alterations due to potential pressure on the spinal cord or nerve roots, or compromise of blood flow. Therefore, carefully repositioning the patient is the safest and most appropriate immediate step to determine if the changes resolve, indicating a positional cause.

*Lenke et al. led the development of consensus-based best practice guidelines for responding to intraoperative neuromonitoring events in high-risk spinal deformity surgery.* *They outlined a systematic approach that includes checking anesthesia, blood pressure, and then assessing for mechanical issues like patient positioning as initial steps before considering more invasive interventions.* **They concluded** that a standardized algorithm is crucial for effective and timely management of neuromonitoring changes.

*Fehlings et al. evaluated a clinical practice guideline developed to standardize the approach to preventing, diagnosing, and managing intraoperative spinal cord injury during spine surgery.* *They provided specific recommendations for the effective use of intraoperative neuromonitoring and detailed the implementation of comprehensive preoperative and intraoperative protocols.* **They concluded** that adherence to these evidence-based guidelines can significantly improve patient safety and outcomes by minimizing the risk and impact of spinal cord complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Increased anesthetic agents can suppress MEP signals, making it harder to interpret true spinal cord function and is not the correct response.
Answer 2: This is a premature and aggressive step before ruling out more common and reversible causes like patient positioning.
Answer 3: A wake-up test is performed if repositioning and other initial measures are unsuccessful in resolving the changes, as it is more disruptive and time-consuming.
Answer 5: This is an overly drastic measure without first attempting to identify and correct the cause of the changes and ruling out reversible factors.
