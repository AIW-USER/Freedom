## Question

While working at a busy ambulatory surgical center, you often encounter patients who are not proficient in English. While discussing informed consent with a patient for ACL reconstruction, the in-house interpreter is at lunch and the telephone interpreter service predicts a 20-minute wait. The bedside nurse remarks that they speak the required language and offers to provide interpretation. As you go through the consent process, you realize that your lengthy explanations invoke only short phrases from the interpreter, and the patient offers to sign the consent form. What is the best course of action at this stage?

## Options

1. Repeat the consent process when a qualified interpreter is available
2. Sign your portion of the consent and proceed with surgery
3. Clarify if the patient has any additional questions, answer these, and proceed with surgery
4. Use the patient’s teenage child as an interpreter
5. Locate another registered nurse who may be able to interpret

## Correct option

**1. Repeat the consent process when a qualified interpreter is available**

## Answer explanation

The above interaction suggests that informed consent has not been appropriately obtained, and a trained medical interpretation professional should participate in the subsequent consent process (Answer 1).

Interpreters play a critical role in healthcare by ensuring clear and accurate communication between patients and providers. For each interaction, the interpreter should interpret in a conduit fashion, meaning they provide literal interpretation in the first person without omissions, editing, or outside conversations. Every patient should be evaluated for language proficiency, and in-person or virtual interpreters should be offered during each interaction. Many patients prefer using family members for interpretation, and this is often an acceptable practice if the family member is sufficiently fluent in both languages. Except in cases of emergency, a minor should not be used as an interpreter, even if they are a family member. Although every effort should be made to abide by these guidelines and use professional interpretation services when available, emergencies arise that require exceptions, and the patient’s best interest should be pursued.

*Tongue et al. reviewed surveys of American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons*
members and patients to characterize patient-physician communication. Orthopedic surgeons are frequently rated as having poor listening and communication skills. The authors emphasize that clear communication affects patient satisfaction, adherence to treatment, and physician satisfaction. This is especially important when treating patients who are not proficient in English, as linguistic minorities report worse care than ethnic or racial minorities. Additionally, the authors advocate for the use of professional services but note that the Office of Civil Rights strongly suggests that family members should not be used as interpreters.

*Klifto et al. reviewed current concepts of shared decision-making in orthopedic surgery.* *The authors describe shared decision-making as a two-way exchange of information that attempts to correct the inequality of power between the patient and physician.* **They emphasize** the importance of a good patient-physician relationship to reduce decisional conflict and improve patient outcomes. Of note, **the authors emphasize** that shared decision-making can be applied without significant increases in visit times.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 2: The interpreter most likely provided summaries or omitted information during this scenario, and thus informed consent was not obtained.
Answer 3: Asking the patient if they have any questions is insufficient as a significant portion of the information was likely withheld.
Answer 4: Use of minors for medical interpretation should be limited to emergency situations.
Answer 5: While another staff member may be proficient in both languages, a trained medical interpreter is the preferred option, especially in the setting of elective surgery.
