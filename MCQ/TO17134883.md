## Question

A 38 years old female presents with back ache and left side radiculopathy for the last 3 weeks. Her pain aggravates on standing and back extension. Lumbar flexion eases her pain. MRI shows a large extra foraminal disc at L4-5 level. What will be the examination finding in this case? (FNB Pattern Paper 2015)

## Options

1. Peripheral vascular disease
2. Weak EHL
3. Heel walk not possible
4. Toe walks not possible

## Correct option

**3. Heel walk not possible**

## Answer explanation

clinical presentation of lumbar canal stenosis

Spinal canal stenosis is a disabling disorder that generally occurs in the elderly in the 6th or 7th decade of life. In this disorder the diameter of the spinal canal is compromised thus causing neural compression and producing symptoms. Lumbar canal that is normally round, oval or trefoil-shaped in cross section, is most commonly involved followed by cervical canal.

Degenerative lumbar canal stenosis (LCS) is the most common form of spinal canal stenosis. Degenerative LCS anatomically can involve the central canal, lateral recess, intervertebral foramina (root canal stenosis) or any combination of these locations.

- Central stenosis: mostly it is caused by ligamentum hypertrophy posteriorly and the bulging disc anteriorly. It is characterized by cross sectional area of thecal sac <100mm2 or <10mm A-P diameter on axial CT. Symptoms of traversing nerve root appears (at the L4/5 level, the root of L5 is affected)
- Lateral recess stenosis (subarticular recess): It is caused by facet joint arthropathy (superior articular facet is mainly involved) and osteophyte formation. It results in traversing nerve root compression (at the L4/5 level, the root of L5 is affected)
- Foraminal stenosis: It is caused by a substantial loss of disk height, foraminal disk protrusions or osteophytes, or angulation in the setting of degenerative scoliosis. Here the exiting nerve root is compressed. (at the L4/5 level, the root of L4 is affected)
- Extraforaminal stenosis: It is caused by far lateral disc herniations. The exiting nerve root is compressed here. (at the L4/5 level, the root of L4 is affected)

Clinical presentation:

Clinical presentation depends on the site of involvement. In the most common LCS neurogenic claudication pain is the hallmark (i.e. pain arising in the lower back radiating down the buttock, thigh, leg to the foot on prolonged physical activity like walking a fixed distance). Pain worsens on standing or walking but is generally relieved by either bending forward (trunk flexion) or sitting. Posture-related pain in LCS is due to the fact that the available space in the central canal decreases in axial loading and extension and increases in axial distraction and flexion. This also explains why patients get more relief in lying on the side (allowing flexion posture) than lying flat. For the same reason patients find walking uphill easier and pushing a shopping cart gives them relief (positive shopping cart sign). Since they mostly remain stooped forward to relieve pressure on their neural structures, their posture is said to be ape-like. Symptoms may be unilateral in root canal stenosis.

Extra foraminal disc at L4-L5 level will compress the L4 nerve root so dorsiflexion will be weak and walking on heels will be difficult.

Answer: 3
