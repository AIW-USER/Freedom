## Question

A 61-year-old operating engineer construction worker presents with a full-thickness rotator cuff tear (Figure A). He works out 4-5 times per week in the gym including heavy resistance training and multiple compound overhead exercises. He has a history of hypertension and denies any tobacco use. Due to a failed trial of physical therapy and increasing pain, he is scheduled to undergo an arthroscopic double-row rotator cuff repair. He inquires about when and if he will be able to return to work, as he must request time off through his union. Which of the following statements regarding return to work is true?

![[q218849-a-figure-a-full-thickness-rct.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. The patient will only require taking off work the week after surgery
2. The patient can expect to return to full duty 2-4 months after surgery
3. The patient should expect to return to full duty between 6-10 months
4. The patient should expect earlier and higher work outcomes compared to desk-job patients
5. The patient should consider a career change as he has a low chance of returning to full duty

## Correct option

**3. The patient should expect to return to full duty between 6-10 months**

## Answer explanation

Patients should expect to return to full work duties approximately 6-10 months after rotator cuff repair surgery. Less physically demanding jobs can consider an earlier return to work with light duty; however, returning to work should be reserved until they have begun progressive strengthening exercises with physical therapy.

Rotator cuff injuries can be debilitating and limit patients’ ability to work and receive a stable income in order to maintain their quality of life. Particularly in labor-intensive occupations, rotator cuff tears are directly related to significant disability and time away from work. While each patient has different preoperative function and various occupational demands, it is generally considered prudent to reserve return to work until patients begin progressive strengthening exercises at the earliest 8-10 weeks after surgery. Importantly, the majority of these patients returning to work will require light duty with considerable restrictions. In a patient with a physically demanding job (i.e. construction work), prolonged restrictions are required to prevent early repair failure. While the majority of patients will return to their full previous level of work, they will require approximately 6-10 months for a full recovery.

*Jayasekara and colleagues performed a retrospective analysis of their institution’s prospectively collected data regarding return to work following different shoulder surgeries.* *They evaluated the rates of return to work at 6 months for different shoulder procedures and found that rotator cuff repair had the higher return to work rates at 6 months postoperatively (90%), however, at least half of those patients were only performing light duties.* **They concluded** that younger age, less stiffness, and preoperative working status were the best independent predictors for return to work 6 months after surgery.

*Haunschild and colleagues performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of return-to-work investigations after rotator cuff repair.* **They found** that 63% of heavy/strenuous work patients returned to their previous level of work at ~8 months after surgery, with lighter work intensity jobs having an overall higher rate of return to their previous level of work (94% for light work, 75% for moderate work). **They found** no difference in return to work rates or level of return work compared between operative technique (open versus arthroscopic repair) or worker’s compensation status. **They concluded** that a large number of patients (>35%) are unable to return to their previous work level, however, the majority return to previous work at approximately 8 months after surgery.

Figure A shows coronal imaging from this patient’s MRI demonstrating a full-thickness rotator cuff tear of the supraspinatus with minimal retraction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient should request far more than one week off work. Many patients do not start physical therapy until 2-6 weeks after surgery with the initiation of passive range of motion exercises. Patients should not begin strengthening exercises until at least 2-3 months after surgery. This patient has a labor-intensive job and should not expect to perform vigorous physical activity until at least 6 months after surgery.
Answer 2: The patient should not expect to return to full duty 2-4 months after surgery. After initiation of strengthening exercises (~2-3 months after surgery) patients can be considered to return to light duty. However, unrestricted full duty is not expected until 6-10 months post operatively.
Answer 4: The patient should expect a LATER return to work with LOWER work outcomes compared to less physically demanding desk-job patients.
Answer 5: While patients in physically demanding jobs should expect a LATER return to work with LOWER work outcomes, the majority do return to a full previous pre-operative work function.
