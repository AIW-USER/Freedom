## Answer explanation

Calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD), or pseudogout, most commonly affects the wrist and knee joints.

Pseudogout is a rheumatologic disease characterized by the deposition of calcium pyrophosphate in joints. The knee is most commonly affected, followed by the wrist, then second and third metacarpophalangeal joints. CPPD causes inflammatory arthritis, which can result in rapidly progressive joint destruction. It is can present very similar to gout and septic arthritis with acute joint pain and associated warmth and erythema of the joint. Inflammatory markers are often normal or minimally elevated. Synovial aspiration will demonstrate mild leukocytosis, negative culture, and weakly positive birefringent rhomboid-shaped crystals. Treatment is typically conservative with rest, NSAIDs, and possibly intra-articular corticosteroids or oral colchicine. Chondrocalcinosis can be identified on X-ray by calcification of soft tissue (specifically meniscus and TFCC) by CPPD crystal deposition.

*Andres et al. present a comprehensive discussion of therapies for CPPD.* **They note** that most CPPD treatment is based on gout therapies without specific research for CPPD. Once CPPD crystals form, they cannot dissolve, so therapies are directed at controlling the associated inflammation. Colchicine, glucocorticoids, NSAIDs, and interleukin-1 antagonists are currently the most common effective therapies. DMARDs are now considered controversial.

*Rosenthal and Ryan reviewed CPPD from a historical and clinical perspective.* *They discuss the common clinical presentation of mono- or oligoarthritis with pain, warmth, erythema, and swelling.* Further, they discuss the pathophysiology, genetics (familial variants), diagnostic tools, and management principles. Their management strategy starts with intra-articular glucocorticoids, and step-wise recommends other treatments based on patient-specific feasibility.
