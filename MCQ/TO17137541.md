Chapter: Trauma MCQs — Femoral Head Fractures

## Question

Which of the following is an indication for non-operative treatment of femoral head fracture?

## Options

1. A Pipkin type 1 fracture with 3mm of displacement
2. A Pipkin type 3 fracture
3. Pipkin type 2 fracture with 1mm of displacement
4. A Pipkin type 1 fracture with nonconcentric reduction

## Correct option

**3. Pipkin type 2 fracture with 1mm of displacement**

## Answer explanation

Femoral head fractures

Femoral head fractures are relatively uncommon injuries; however, appropriate treatment of these fractures is of prime importance to help prevent the development of post-traumatic osteoarthritis. Approximately 15 % of posterior hip dislocations have been noted to be associated with a femoral head fracture. 95% femoral head fractures have hip dislocations.

Mechanism of injury

The classic mechanism of injury for femoral head fracture is traumatic posterior dislocation of the hip. Shear forces against the femoral head as it exits the contained acetabulum are thought to cause the femoral head fracture during hip dislocation.

A common position of the lower extremity during dislocation of the hip is akin to the position during a dashboard injury to the knee, in which the hip is positioned in flexion, adduction, and internal rotation.

Classification

Pipkin classified fractures by their location in the femoral head and the presence of any associated fractures as one of four types:

- type I: fracture inferior to the fovea capitis, a small fracture not involving the weightbearing surface
- type II: fracture extending superior to the fovea capitis, a large fracture involving the weightbearing surface
- type III: type I or II fracture with a fracture of the femoral neck, has an increased risk of avascular necrosis
- type IV: type I or II fractures with a fracture of the acetabular wall, usually the posterior wall

Pipkin used the fovea capitis as a landmark between type I and type II fractures. The ligamentum capitis femoris remains attached to the inferior fragment in type II injuries, which often results in substantial rotation of this fragment. As a result, the rotated caudal segment with its attached ligamentum may prevent a reduction of the cranial head segment.

Chiron Classification

Newest classification, used by Pelvi-acetabular surgeons

Type 1 – Osteochondral injury

Type 2 – Involving 1/4th femoral head

Type 3 – Involving 1/3rd femoral head

Type 4 – Involving ½ femoral head

Type 5 – Cartilage impaction at weight bearing dome (Superior collapse of femoral head)

Each type is divided into subtypes –

Subtype A – Pure femoral head fracture

Subtype B – Associated with Acetabular fracture

Subtype C – Associated with Femoral neck fracture

Treatment

For femoral head fractures following hip dislocations, emergency closed reduction of hip joint under sedation is performed. Post reduction, stability of the joint is tested. Post reduction repeat X-Ray and CT scan is performed.

Definitive management –

Following closed reduction of the hip, non-operative treatment can be considered for Pipkin type 1 and type 2 fractures if the following criteria are met

- Anatomic or near anatomic reduction <2 mm displacement
- A stable hip
- No interposed fragment preventing a congruent hip reduction

Surgical management

Approach

![[TO17137541-3.jpg]]

The anterior approach avoids additional compromise to the femoral head blood supply, and allows easier access to the femoral head fracture fragment, which is usually anteromedial. Only disadvantage of anterior approach is significantly higher incidence of heterotopic ossification.
The use of the Kocher-Langenbeck posterior approach is reported to have a 3.2 times higher incidence of subsequent avascular necrosis then the Smith-Petersen approach.
The rate of traumatic sciatic nerve palsy has been reported from 10% to 23%. Iatrogenic sciatic nerve palsy can occur due to traction during a posterior approach, but it would be uncommon to occur following an anterior approach.
