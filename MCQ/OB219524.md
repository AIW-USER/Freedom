## Question

A 10-year-old female presents to your pediatric orthopedic clinic for evaluation of bilateral cavovarus foot deformity. She was recently diagnosed with Charcot-Marie-Tooth by her pediatrician and neurologist. On evaluation, she has varus alignment of her hindfoot and a cavus arch which appears to be hindfoot driven based on her Coleman block test. She has tightness in her posterior heel cord. She denies having any pain currently. You recommend close follow-up and heel cord stretching but no immediate intervention. However, you do warn her that she is at a higher risk of having which of the following concomitant injuries?

## Options

1. Distal fibula stress fracture
2. Medial malleolus stress fracture
3. 2nd metatarsal shaft stress fracture
4. Posterior tibial tendon insufficiency
5. Flexor hallucis longus tendonitis

## Correct option

**2. Medial malleolus stress fracture**

## Answer explanation

Medial malleolus stress fractures are more common in patients with cavovarus foot deformities due to the increased compression stress placed on the medial side of the ankle.

Cavovarus deformities of the feet are common in both pediatric and adult populations and are characterized by hindfoot varus and cavus deformity of the arch. Compensatory plantarflexion of the 1st ray can occur in order for the foot to remain plantigrade. Almost 2/3 of cases are attributable to an underlying neurologic disorder. Conservative treatment usually involves orthotics or special orthoses and surgical management usually involves a combination of osteotomies and soft tissue reconstructions. It is important to educate this group of patients that they are at a higher risk for other foot/ankle pathologies such as stress fractures of the 4th/5th metatarsals, navicular and medial malleolus. They are also at higher risk for peroneal tendonitis and peroneal tendon tears. Most stress fractures can be treated nonoperatively but medial malleolus stress fractures are at a higher risk for nonunion and should be followed closely with a period of casting and non-weightbearing.

*Mayer et al. published a review article on stress fractures of the foot/ankle in athletes.* **They note** there is a wide spectrum of presentation for these injuries and many can be treated nonoperatively with immobilization and activity modification. High-risk stress fractures, such as tension-sided femoral neck fractures, are more appropriately treated with internal fixation. They specifically talk about medial malleolus stress fractures, **noting** that incomplete fractures seen only on MRI can be treated with non-weightbearing in a cast but internal fixation can be considered to allow for a quicker return to sports.

*Welck et al. also reviewed stress fractures of the foot and ankle,* **noting** that they occur when the bone is subjected to submaximal stresses repeatedly. **They note** that stress fractures of the medial malleolus are rare and have a high risk for nonunion. **They recommend** initial treatment with a cast and non-weightbearing but note that screw fixation may be required and can lead to more rapid healing and return to sport.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 3-5: Patients with cavovarus foot deformities are at higher risk for stress fractures of the navicular, 4th/5th metatarsals, and the medial malleolus, as well as peroneal tendon pathology.
