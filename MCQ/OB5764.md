## Question

A 68-year-old man presents with a 60-pack-year history of smoking presents to the emergency room with a sudden onset of medial groin pain without associated trauma. Radiographs are shown in Figure A. He is discharged with NSAIDS. He returns for follow-up to your office 2 weeks later. He now walks with a limp and uses 2 canes. There is generalized limitation in range of hip motion secondary to pain. What is the next best step?

![[q5764-a-fig-a1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Repair with suture anchors
2. Repair with suture button
3. Laboratory evaluation (complete blood count [CBC], chemistry panel, serum and urine protein electrophoresis [SPEP, UPEP]) and intramedullary fixation
4. Laboratory evaluation (CBC, chemistry panel, SPEP, UPEP), CT, MRI
5. Biopsy

## Correct option

**4. Laboratory evaluation (CBC, chemistry panel, SPEP, UPEP), CT, MRI**

## Answer explanation

An atraumatic lesser trochanteric fracture in an adult is highly suspicious for malignancy. A metastatic workup is necessary with laboratory evaluation (CBC, chemistry panel, SPEP/UPEP). Imaging for a primary should include CT chest, abdomen and pelvis. MRI of the entire femur should be performed to look for soft tissue masses. Bone scan is also recommended.

Unlike in adolescents, lesser trochanteric fractures are rare in adults. Approximately 5% of proximal femoral malignancy involves fractures of the lesser trochanter. Of this, 60%-70% are metastatic. The most common diagnosis of a destructive bone lesion in adults >40 years is metastases. Management depends on the stage and location of tumor, and the presence of other metastases. An algorithm is presented in the Review Topic.

*Weber reviewed management of patients with destructive bone lesions in patients aged >40 years.* **They recommend** staging workup and biopsy for solitary destructive lesions ± history of cancer. **They recommend** surgery without biopsy if there is an obvious primary and multiple metastases, but intraoperative specimens should be sent for pathology. **They recommend** preop biopsy if the appearance of the primary resembles sarcoma. For possible pathological femur fractures, femur radiographs, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and bone site (or MRI), and biopsy should be performed. Perform locked intramedullary stabilization if the diagnosis is mets, lymphoma or myeloma. If the diagnosis is sarcoma, start neoadjuvant treatment and defer surgery.

*Rougraff reviewed metastases to bone.* *He advised that investigations should NOT start with a biopsy before other tests because: (1) An ill-planned biopsy of a sarcoma may compromise limb salvage, (2) another lesion may be identified that may be easier/safer to sample, (3) renal cell metastases are vascular and should be embolized prior to open biopsy (or alternatively, perform needle biopsy), (4) biopsy is unnecessary in multiple myeloma, (5) histology alone will identify the primary site in only a small number of patients.*

*Cheung* **recommended** NOT using reamings for pathology specimen because the tissue quality is compromised by shearing forces of the reamer, reducing its accuracy. Furthermore, this practice contaminates the entire femur with tumor, which may complicate reconstructive options if the tumor was not a carcinoma. **They recommend** a separate open biopsy and waiting for the results before taking the steps for fixation (incision extension, reaming). If surgical fixation is already planned, a separate open biopsy with frozen section (FS) immediately before the fixation should be performed and the case should not proceed until the pathology report confirms metastasis. If FS is inconclusive, the case should be aborted until the final pathology report is returned.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Fixation should not proceed without exclusion of tumoral involvement.
Answer 3: Imaging workup to identify the source of/exclude metastases should be performed prior to surgical treatment. Intramedullary fixation of a primary sarcoma leads to a poor prognosis.
Answer 5: A biopsy may or may not be necessary depending on findings of the metastatic workup. One should not jump to a biopsy prior to laboratory tests and imaging.
