Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Hallux Valgus Bunion

## Question

patient presents with a symptomatic hallux valgus and an Intermetatarsal Angle (IMA) of 18 degrees. Which surgical procedure is most appropriate given the severity of the IMA?

## Options

1. Akin osteotomy
2. Proximal (basal) metatarsal osteotomy
3. Isolated Silver procedure
4. Distal Chevron osteotomy

## Correct option

**2. Proximal (basal) metatarsal osteotomy**

## Answer explanation

Non-Operative
and Operative Management of Adult Hallux Valgus (6 Marks)
Non-Operative Management
Non-operative
management is the first-line treatment for all patients with hallux valgus and
is appropriate as definitive treatment for patients with mild symptoms, those
unwilling or unfit for surgery, and those with early deformity. It is important
to counsel patients that non-operative measures do not correct the deformity
— they aim to alleviate symptoms and slow progression.
Footwear
Modification: This
is the single most important non-operative intervention. Patients are advised
to wear wide toe-box shoes with adequate depth to accommodate the bunion
without compression. Low-heeled shoes (heel height <2.5 cm/1 inch) reduce
forefoot loading by reducing the transfer of body weight onto the metatarsal
heads. Avoidance of narrow, pointed-toe, and high-heeled shoes is emphasised.
Soft, stretchable upper materials (leather, mesh) accommodate the medial
prominence. Custom shoes or extra-depth shoes may be necessary for severe
deformities.
Protective
Padding: Bunion
pads (silicone, felt, or gel) placed over the medial eminence reduce friction
and pressure from footwear, alleviating bursitis pain. Interdigital spacers
(silicone wedges placed between the first and second toes) may provide
temporary symptom relief and reduce overlapping.
Orthotic
Management: Custom
or over-the-counter arch supports and orthotic insoles may be beneficial in
patients with associated pes planus or metatarsalgia. Orthoses correct hindfoot
valgus, support the medial longitudinal arch, and redistribute forefoot
pressure. A Morton's extension (a rigid extension beneath the first
metatarsal and hallux) can be incorporated to limit first MTP joint motion if
there is an associated hallux rigidus component. However, orthotics do not
correct the hallux valgus deformity itself.
Night
Splints and Toe Spacers: Bunion night splints hold the hallux in a corrected position during sleep.
While they may provide temporary symptomatic relief, there is no evidence
that they alter the natural history or prevent progression of the deformity.
They are most commonly used in early, flexible deformities in motivated
patients.
Anti-Inflammatory
Measures: NSAIDs
(oral or topical), ice application, and activity modification for acute bunion
bursitis flares. Corticosteroid injection into the inflamed bunion bursa can
provide short-term relief of acute bursitis, though repeated injections are not
recommended due to risk of skin atrophy and MTP joint capsule weakening.
Physiotherapy: Hallux mobilisation exercises, intrinsic
foot muscle strengthening (towel curls, marble picks), and stretching of the
gastrocnemius-soleus complex (tight gastrosoleus increases forefoot loading).
These measures maintain joint mobility and may slow progression but do not
correct the structural deformity.
Operative Management
Surgical
intervention is indicated when symptomatic hallux valgus fails to
respond to adequate non-operative management. Surgery is performed for symptoms
(pain, functional limitation, difficulty with footwear, second toe deformity) —
never for cosmetic correction alone. The choice of procedure is determined by
the severity of deformity (HVA, IMA, DMAA), joint congruency, presence of
arthritis, metatarsocuneiform joint stability, and patient factors (age,
activity level, expectations).
Over 150
surgical procedures have been described for hallux valgus — the following
represents the current evidence-based approach organised by deformity severity.
Mild Deformity (HVA 15–20°, IMA <13°)
Distal
Chevron Osteotomy (Austin Osteotomy): The most commonly performed procedure for
mild-to-moderate hallux valgus. A V-shaped osteotomy is made through the
first metatarsal head (apex dorsal, arms directed proximally and plantarly),
and the capital (distal) fragment is translated laterally by 5–10 mm,
correcting the increased IMA at the distal level. The osteotomy is inherently
stable due to the interlocking V-shape and is fixed with one or two compression
screws. The medial eminence is resected, and a medial capsular plication
(capsulorrhaphy) is performed to tighten the attenuated medial capsule.
The chevron
osteotomy can correct up to 5–6° of IMA and is therefore appropriate for mild
deformity only. For moderate-to-severe deformities, the degree of translation
required would exceed the width of the metatarsal shaft, destabilising the
osteotomy.
Modified
McBride Procedure (Distal Soft Tissue Procedure): This involves lateral soft tissue release
(release of the contracted lateral capsule, adductor hallucis tendon from the
lateral sesamoid, and transverse intermetatarsal ligament) combined with medial
capsulorrhaphy. As an isolated procedure, it has a high recurrence rate and is
now rarely performed alone — it is typically combined with a metatarsal
osteotomy as part of a combined procedure.
Akin
Osteotomy: A
medial closing wedge osteotomy of the proximal phalanx of the hallux,
correcting hallux valgus interphalangeus. Often performed as an adjunct to
metatarsal osteotomies to fine-tune hallux alignment, rather than as a
standalone procedure.
Moderate Deformity (HVA 20–40°, IMA 13–16°)
Scarf
Osteotomy: A Z-shaped
(step-cut) diaphyseal osteotomy of the first metatarsal, consisting of a
long horizontal longitudinal cut through the metatarsal shaft with short
transverse limbs at each end. The plantar fragment is translated laterally
relative to the dorsal fragment, correcting the IMA. The long contact area
provides excellent inherent stability and is fixed with two compression screws.
The scarf osteotomy is versatile — it allows lateral translation, rotation (to
correct pronation), shortening or lengthening, and plantar or dorsal
displacement of the metatarsal head. It can correct IMA up to 8–10° and is
typically combined with lateral soft tissue release and medial capsulorrhaphy.
A potential complication specific to the scarf osteotomy is "troughing"
— dorsal displacement of the plantar fragment creating a step in the metatarsal
shaft, which can cause transfer metatarsalgia.
Proximal
Crescentic (Ludloff/Mau) Osteotomy: A curved or oblique osteotomy at the proximal
first metatarsal allows angular correction at the base, producing greater IMA
correction than distal osteotomies. Combined with distal soft tissue procedure
(modified McBride). Provides correction of larger IMAs but requires careful
fixation (screws or plate) and has a longer healing period than distal
osteotomies.
Combined
Distal Soft Tissue Procedure with Proximal Metatarsal Osteotomy: The combination of lateral release,
medial capsulorrhaphy, and a proximal osteotomy provides powerful correction
for moderate deformities with elevated IMA. Various proximal osteotomy
configurations (opening wedge, closing wedge, crescentic, chevron) are
described.
Severe Deformity (HVA >40°, IMA >16°)
Lapidus
Procedure (First Tarsometatarsal Arthrodesis): Arthrodesis of the first metatarsocuneiform
joint, originally described by Lapidus in 1934. This procedure addresses the
deformity at its most proximal origin — the medial deviation of the first
metatarsal originates from the metatarsocuneiform joint in many severe cases.
By fusing this joint in a corrected position (the first metatarsal is laterally
translated and derotated at the fusion site), large IMAs (>16°) can be
corrected. It is specifically indicated when metatarsocuneiform joint
hypermobility is identified as a contributing factor.
The Lapidus is
combined with lateral soft tissue release and medial capsulorrhaphy at the MTP
level. Fixation is achieved with compression screws, locking plates, or
dedicated Lapidus plating systems. Modern modifications include plantar plating
for enhanced stability and early weight-bearing protocols. The main
disadvantage is the requirement for bony fusion (non-union rate 3–10%) and
historically prolonged non-weight-bearing (6–8 weeks), though modern fixation
has allowed earlier weight-bearing.
Proximal
Opening Wedge or Closing Wedge Osteotomy with Distal Soft Tissue Procedure: An alternative to the Lapidus for severe
IMA without metatarsocuneiform instability. A medially based opening wedge
(with interposition bone graft) or laterally based closing wedge at the
proximal first metatarsal corrects the severe metatarsus primus varus.
Salvage Procedures
First MTP
Arthrodesis (Fusion): The gold standard for hallux valgus with significant first MTP joint
arthritis (hallux valgus with hallux rigidus), failed prior bunion
surgery, neuromuscular hallux valgus (e.g., cerebral palsy,
poliomyelitis — where muscle imbalance makes recurrence after osteotomy
likely), rheumatoid hallux valgus with severe joint destruction, and very
severe deformity in elderly patients. The first MTP joint is fused in the
functional position — 10–15° of valgus, 10–15° of dorsiflexion relative to
the first metatarsal (ensuring the hallux clears the ground during swing
phase and provides a stable push-off platform during toe-off). Fixation is with
a dorsal plate and compression screw combination, or crossed lag screws. Fusion
eliminates pain, corrects the deformity permanently, and prevents recurrence.
The main functional limitation is the loss of MTP joint motion — high-heeled
shoes cannot be worn, and some patients find the stiff hallux noticeable during
gait, though the majority adapt well and report high satisfaction.
Keller's
Excision Arthroplasty (Resection Arthroplasty): Excision of the base of the proximal phalanx of
the hallux, originally described as a salvage for elderly, low-demand patients.
It relieves pain and corrects the deformity by removing the deforming
articulation, but produces a shortened, floppy, weak hallux with loss of
push-off power and risk of transfer metatarsalgia. Rarely performed in current
practice as primary surgery for hallux valgus — largely replaced by first MTP
arthrodesis in the salvage setting. May still have a role in very elderly,
low-demand, sedentary patients where a simple, low-morbidity procedure is
preferred.
First MTP
Arthroplasty (Joint Replacement): Total or hemi-arthroplasty of the first MTP joint
has been attempted with various implant designs (silicone interpositional —
Swanson, metallic hemi-arthroplasty, total joint replacement). Results have
been historically inconsistent with concerns about implant wear, loosening,
osteolysis (particularly with silicone implants), and limited longevity. Modern
anatomic total MTP replacements are showing improved short-to-medium-term
results in selected patients, but long-term data remain limited. Currently
reserved for patients who desire motion preservation and are unsuitable for or
decline arthrodesis.
General Principles of Surgical Management
Lateral
Soft Tissue Release: An essential adjunct in most hallux valgus procedures (except congruent
joint deformities where release would create incongruency). Performed through a
separate dorsal first web space incision or through the medial approach with
specialised instruments. Includes release of the adductor hallucis tendon from
the lateral sesamoid, lateral capsulotomy of the first MTP joint, and release
of the lateral metatarsosesamoid ligament. This releases the lateral
contracture that bowstrings the hallux into valgus.
Medial
Eminence Resection: The prominent medial aspect of the metatarsal head is resected with an
oscillating saw, flush with the medial metatarsal shaft. Over-resection must be
avoided as it weakens the metatarsal head and can lead to hallux varus
(overcorrection).
Medial
Capsulorrhaphy:
Tightening of the attenuated medial capsule through plication sutures,
restoring the medial restraint to valgus angulation.
Sesamoid
Reduction:
Correction of the metatarsal position (through osteotomy and lateral release)
should result in the sesamoids sitting beneath the metatarsal head in their
anatomical grooves (crista). Persistent sesamoid subluxation after correction
indicates inadequate lateral release or insufficient metatarsal correction.
