Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Carpal Instability

## Question

Which ligamentous structure is the primary volar stabilizer preventing ulnar translocation of the carpus, often implicated in CIND-radiocarpal translocation?

## Options

1. Ulnolunate ligament
2. Radioscaphocapitate ligament
3. Short radiolunate ligament
4. Long radiolunate ligament

## Correct option

**4. Long radiolunate ligament**

## Answer explanation

The long radiolunate ligament is the strongest volar constraint preventing the carpus from sliding down the ulnar slope of the radius.

The extrinsic volar ligaments are the primary stabilizers of the wrist, far thicker and stronger than their dorsal counterparts. They originate from the forearm bones (radius and ulna) and insert into the carpal bones.

Biomechanically, these ligaments form an inverted "V" shape (the proximal and distal arcs), which creates a stable "sling" for the carpus.

1. The Radiocarpal (Radial) Group

These ligaments originate from the volar margin of the distal radius and are the primary resistors of ulnar translocation and carpal collapse.

A. Radioscaphocapitate (RSC) Ligament

 - Anatomy: Originates from the lateral aspect of the radial styloid and inserts onto the waist of the scaphoid and the center of the capitate.

 - Function: It acts as a constant fulcrum
 or "sling" for the scaphoid to rotate around.

 - Stability Role: It prevents excessive scaphoid extension and provides a stable bridge between the radius and the distal carpal row.

B. Long Radiolunate (LRL) Ligament

 - Anatomy: Located just ulnar to the RSC, originating from the radial styloid and inserting onto the volar-radial tubercle of the lunate and the triquetrum.

 - Function: This is the primary stabilizer against ulnar translocation.

 - Stability Role: It tethers the carpus to the radius. When it fails (as often seen in Rheumatoid Arthritis), the entire carpus slides ulnarly down the slope of the radius.

C. Short Radiolunate (SRL) Ligament

 - Anatomy: Originates from the ulnar notch of the radius (the "brink") and inserts into the volar-ulnar aspect of the lunate.

 - Function: It stabilizes the lunate specifically against the distal radius.

 - Stability Role: It acts as a critical "anchor" for the lunate, preventing its dissociation during the wide arcs of wrist motion.

2. The Ulnocarpal (Ulnar) Group

These ligaments originate from the ulnar styloid and the Triangular Fibrocartilage Complex (TFCC). They stabilize the ulnar side of the wrist.

A. Ulnolunate (UL) Ligament

 - Anatomy: Arises from the volar aspect of the TFCC (specifically the disc and ulnar styloid) and inserts into the lunate.

 - Function/Role: Prevents dorsal displacement of the lunate and stabilizes the ulnar-proximal carpus.

B. Ulnotriquetral (UT) Ligament

 - Anatomy: Arises from the TFCC and inserts onto the volar triquetrum.

 - Function/Role: Works in tandem with the UL ligament to stabilize the ulnar column. Failure of the UL/UT complex is a major contributor to VISI (Volar Intercalated Segment Instability).

3. The "Space of Poirier"

While not a ligament itself, this is a critical clinical landmark within the extrinsic system.

 - Location: It is a central area of relative weakness between the Radiocapitate and the Long Radiolunate ligaments.

 - Clinical Significance: It lies directly over the capitate-lunate joint. During a perilunate dislocation (Mayfield Stage II), the carpus dissociates through this space, allowing the capitate to displace dorsally while the lunate stays attached to the radius via the Short Radiolunate ligament.
