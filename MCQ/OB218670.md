## Question

An 11-year-old basketball player comes with his parents to an urgent care after sustaining an inversion injury to his ankle while posted up against a defender. When comparing the findings on the radiographs shown in Figures A and B, which of the following statements is most true?

*Figure A*

![[q218670-b-screenshot-2023-09-16-at-2-41-27-pm.jpg]]
*Figure B*

## Options

1. The injury shown in Figure A should receive an MRI at time of diagnosis, whereas an MRI is not indicated for the injury in Figure B
2. The injury shown in Figure B will likely require re-attachment of the anterior talofibular ligament
3. The injury shown in Figure A typically presents with symptomatic ankle instability and clinical laxity
4. The injury shown in Figure B should be treated surgically, while that shown in Figure A should be treated in a CAM walker boot
5. The injuries in both Figure A and Figure B can be treated initially with protected weightbearing

## Correct option

**5. The injuries in both Figure A and Figure B can be treated initially with protected weightbearing**

## Answer explanation

Both transepiphyseal (Type VII) ankle fractures (Figure B) and symptomatic os subfibulare (Figure A) in the pediatric population can initially be treated with protected weightbearing in a CAM walker boot.

Pediatric ankle injuries are common, giving rise to ∼17% of all physeal injuries. An os subfibulare in a child with an ankle sprain may be confused with a type VII transepiphyseal fracture, the latter of which most commonly represents a sleeve avulsion fracture as the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), the calcaneofibular ligament, and the posterior talofibular ligament attach to the epiphysis and are stronger than the physis/bone in this age group. This fracture subtype does not involve the primary physis but rather is a fracture involving the secondary center of ossification extending into the articular surface. Failure to distinguish a type VII ankle fracture from an ankle sprain with associated os subfibulare can lead to further injury as inappropriately treated ankle sprains may lead to subsequent ankle instability. Though there is still debate in the literature regarding the etiology of os subfibulare, with some authors regarding it as a mere anatomic variant and others believing that it is a result of an avulsion injury of the ATFL from the lateral malleolus, similar to that of a type VII ankle fracture with the caveat that it does not involve a fracture through the secondary ossification center. Despite the debate on the etiology of the os subfibulare, it is important to recognize that most patients with a traumatic type VII ankle fracture or a symptomatic os subfibulare will improve with immobilization, rest, and other conservative treatment modalities, with the first step in treatment being protected weightbearing. For those who have persistent symptoms, further advanced imaging, such as an MRI may be warranted to assess for chondral avulsion injuries or isolated pathology of the os subfibulare that would benefit from os excision and/or ligament re-attachment.

Sugi, et al. provide a review of transepiphyseal (type VII) ankle fractures versus os subfibulare in pediatric ankle injuries. The authors reviewed injuries in 23 pediatric patients, 11 of which had traumatic type VII injuries and 12 of which had trauma with an associated os subfibulare on presenting radiographs. They concluded that radiographs, not clinical presentation, can differentiate an os subfibulare from a type VII transepiphyseal fracture, with type VII fractures having a long, irregular fracture line within the middle third of the distal fibular epiphysis and ankle sprains with os subfibulare having a smooth-edged ossicle of relatively short length located within the inferior pole of the epiphysis.

Moukoko, et al. retrospectively reviewed the surgical treatment of subfibular ossicles in 36 children with functional instability but without overt laxity one year after ankle inversion trauma. Seventeen underwent ossicle resection and 19 received only rehabilitative care. The authors found that at a mean of 4 years and 4 months followup, a significant improvement of the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society score was observed and was significantly higher in the resection group. They concluded that in the absence of objective laxity, excision of the os subfibulare appears as a simple and effective technique in the treatment of posttraumatic functional instability and ankle pain.

Figure A represents an AP radiograph of the ankle with an os subfibulare, while Figure B is an AP radiograph of the ankle demonstrating a type VII transepiphyseal fracture of the lateral malleolus.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: MRI is not indicated in the initial management of either injury but can be considered in the setting of chronic pain and/or instability.
Answer 2: A type VII transepiphyseal ankle fracture (as shown in Figure B) can be successfully treated non-operatively and does not necessarily require ligamentous repair or reconstruction.
Answer 3: An os subfibulare can present with symptomatic ankle instability, but most commonly is an asymptomatic finding and, even when symptomatic, may not exhibit distinct, objective clinical laxity on exam.
Answer 4: There is no indication that a type VII transepiphyseal ankle fracture should be treated surgically in the acute phase when compared to a symptomatic or incidental os subfibulare.
