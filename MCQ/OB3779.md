## Question

A 46-year-old male patient presents with worsening axial back pain that has been present for several months without an inciting event. His neurologic exam is otherwise unremarkable. Current MRI imaging is shown in Figure A. Which statement is true regarding this condition?

![[q3779-a-thoracic-disc-herniation-t2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Scheurmann's kyphosis does not predispose patients to this condition
2. Almost all radiographic lesions demonstrate clinical findings
3. Lesions most commonly occur in the upper thoracic spine
4. Transthoracic discectomies can be safely performed with a forced vital capacity of 20%
5. This condition most commonly resolves with conservative treatment and time

## Correct option

**5. This condition most commonly resolves with conservative treatment and time**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a thoracic disc herniation, which may resolve with conservative treatment and observation, much like lumbar disc herniations.

Thoracic disc herniations can occur in the 4th to 6th decades of life and are rarely symptomatic. If they are symptomatic, they can present as either axial back pain, radiculopathy or myelopathy. The vast majority of symptomatic cases resolve with conservative treatment and observation. Patients with persistent symptoms despite conservative treatment or those with progressive neurologic deficits may require surgical decompression. The optimal surgical approach depends on the location of the disc herniation, which is detailed in illustration A.

*Vanichkachorn and Vaccaro reviewed the diagnosis and treatment of thoracic disc herniations.* *The authors described three different types of clinical presentations with either predominantly axial back pain, radiculopathy, or myelopathy.* Acutely herniated discs tend to have mostly a soft tissue component, whereas chronic herniations may have calcifications present in the herniated material.

*Wood et al. performed a retrospective natural history study of thoracic disc herniations in 20 patients with 48 asymptomatic disc herniations.* **They reported** at 26 months follow-up all patients remained asymptomatic with most herniations showing no significant change in size during the study period. However, smaller herniations did have a mild propensity to increase in size without causing symptoms. **The authors concluded** that asymptomatic thoracic disc herniations most likely will remain asymptomatic and are unlikely to change in size.

Figure A is the sagittal T2 MRI of the thoracic spine demonstrating a T6-7 disc herniation with cord effacement. Illustration A is a table that depicts the surgical approaches depending on the location of the thoracic disc herniation.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Patients with Scheurmann's kyphosis are predisposed to the development of thoracic disk herniations. Also, these patients tend to develop neurologic deficits as a result of the disc herniation.
Answer 2: Most radiographically identified thoracic disc herniations never cause symptoms. Smaller herniations may stay the same size or slightly increase in size and large herniations tend to decrease in size over time.
Answer 3: Thoracic disc herniations rarely occur in the upper thoracic spine, with only 1% of cases occurring from T1-5. Approximately 50% to 75% of disc herniations occur from T8 to L1.
Answer 4: A vital capacity of less than 35% can be indicative of postoperative pulmonary complications and should prompt the surgeon to avoid a thoracotomy.
