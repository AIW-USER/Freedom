## Question

A 40-year-old recreational bicyclist presents to the emergency department with severe lower back and bilateral gluteal pain as well as difficulty ambulating after flipping over his handlebars when a motor vehicle cut him off. Physical examination is significant for midline tenderness at the thoracolumbar junction, no palpable step-offs, marked paraspinal muscle spasm, and otherwise intact neurologic function and rectal tone. A CT is obtained and is shown in Figures A and B. An MRI confirms intact posterior elements. What is the patient’s Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity (TLICS) Score and recommended management?

## Options

1. TLICS score of 1, supporting nonsurgical management
2. TLICS score of 2, supporting nonsurgical management
3. TLICS score of 3, supporting nonsurgical management
4. TLICS score of 3, supporting surgical management
5. TLICS score of 4, supporting either surgical or nonsurgical management

## Correct option

**2. TLICS score of 2, supporting nonsurgical management**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with an uncomplicated L2 burst fracture. By the TLICS scoring system, the injury morphology is burst (+2 points), neurologic status is intact (0 points), and the posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) is intact based on the MRI (0 points). A TLICS score <4 supports nonsurgical management.

The Thoracolumbar Injury Classification System (TLICS) score was established in order to provide a reliable classification system and to guide management of thoracolumbar injuries. The score is based on the sum of the points attributable to each of the three composite elements: fracture morphology, PLC integrity, and neurologic status. A combined score of >4 supports surgical management while a score of <4 may supports conservative treatment of the respective injury. In multi-level contiguous injuries, the management is dictated by the injury with the greatest individual TLICS score. For noncontiguous injuries, each fracture is evaluated independently.

*Wood et al. prospectively compared operative with nonoperative management of thoracolumbar burst fractures without neurologic deficits.* **The authors found** no significant difference in the ability or timing of return to work or pain scores at final follow-up. However patients who underwent surgical fixation, whether performed through an anterior or posterior approach, has a significantly increased risk of complications and experienced greater long-term disability. **They concluded** that operative treatment of stable thoracolumbar burst fractures without neurologic findings provided no significant advantages over nonoperative management.

*Wood et al. continued to follow these patients prospectively, obtaining an average 18-year follow-up.* **The authors found** that there was no significant difference in alignment between the operative and nonoperative groups but that those patients treated nonoperatively had significantly less pain and greater functional outcome scores. They affirmed that stable burst fractures without neurologic deficits were best managed nonoperatively with supportive therapy and bracing as tolerated.

*Agus et al. evaluated the clinical and radiographic outcomes following nonoperative treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures, comparing outcomes in patients with two- or three-column involvement.* **The authors found** slight progression of deformity only in patients with three-column injuries. There was additionally significant remodeling and reduction of canal encroachment, and the functional outcomes were decisively independent of radiographic parameters. None of the patients went on to develop later neurologic compromise and functional results were satisfactory whether two or all three columns were involved.

Patel and Vaccaro review the TLICS system and its application to thoracolumbar trauma. The authors highlight the high degree of both high inter- and intra-observer reliability of the system and discuss its clinical application. They concluded that the TLICS system accounted for many of the limitations in the existing classification systems and held promise as a tool by which to continue to improve communication and evidence-based management of thoracolumbar trauma.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: A TLICS score of 1 would be consistent with an uncomplicated compression fracture, which would be managed nonoperatively as supported by the TLICS score <4.
Answer 3: An uncomplicated burst fracture conveys a TLICS score of 2, which supports conservative management. A TLICS score of 3, such as in the instance of a compression fracture with radiculopathy, would also support nonoperative management.
Answer 4: A TLICS score <4 supports nonoperative management.
Answer 5: A TLICS score of 4 is indeterminate, and supports either conservative or surgical management at the surgeon’s discretion. One such injury would be a burst fracture with radiculopathy from nerve root compression. The patient in this vignette however has no evidence of neurologic compromise..
