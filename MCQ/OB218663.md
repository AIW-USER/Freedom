## Question

Figure A is a radiograph of a 64-year-old male who presents with complaints of foot swelling and redness only when his leg is in a dependent position. Examination shows no ulcers and sensation to light touch is absent throughout the foot. His ESR and CRP are slightly elevated and his most recent HbA1c was 8.5%. Of the answers listed, which of the following would be the most appropriate initial treatment for this patient?

![[q218663-a-8.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Arthrodesis
2. Exostectomy and Achilles tendon lengthening
3. Irrigation and debridement
4. Total contact casting
5. Amputation

## Correct option

**4. Total contact casting**

## Answer explanation

This patient has diabetic Charcot neuropathy and initial management would be best addressed with nonoperative management with total contact casting, shoe wear modifications, and/or medications. Additionally, this patient has a hemoglobin A1c >8.0% which signifies poor glycemic control and is associated with poor outcomes with surgical treatment.

Diabetic Charcot neuropathy is a chronic and progressive disease that occurs as a result of loss of protective sensation which leads to the destruction of foot and ankle joints and surrounding bony structures. Diagnosis can be made clinically with a warm and erythematous foot with erythema that decreases with foot elevation. Radiographs often reveal obliteration of joint space and fragmentation of both articular surfaces of a joint leading to subluxation or dislocation. Treatment is a trial of total contact casting for acute Charcot deformities without skin breakdown. Operative management is indicated for recurrent infections, deformities, and severe skin breakdown. A hemoglobin A1c >8.0% signifies poor glycemic control and is associated with poor outcomes with surgical treatment.

*Jones et al. reviewed the management of Charcot foot arthropathy.* **They reported** that historical management has consisted of immobilization until the active phase of the disease resolves, followed by longitudinal accommodative bracing of the acquired deformity. **They noted** that unfortunately, this historic management of Charcot foot arthropathy has not resulted in improved quality of life and has fostered interest in the surgical correction of the acquired deformity. **They concluded** that surgical management to correct acquired deformities in patients with Charcot foot and ankle disorders could be beneficial.

*Strotman et al. reviewed Charcot foot arthropathy.* **They reported** that total contact casting is an effective treatment to prevent collapse and deformity of the foot in early-stage arthropathy; the patient may be weight-bearing or non-weight-bearing. **They concluded** that operative treatment with ostectomy without reconstruction has been more successful in treating nonhealing ulcers of the medial column than the lateral column.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Arthrodesis is considered in severe deformity that is not "braceable."
Answer 2: Resection of bony prominences (exostectomy) and TAL is indicated in the "braceable" foot with equinus deformity and focal bony prominences causing skin breakdown.
Answer 3: This patient has Charcot arthropathy, NOT an infection as suggested with erythema that improves with elevation of the leg.
Answer 5: Amputation is indicated in failed previous surgery (unstable arthrodesis) and/or recurrent infection.
