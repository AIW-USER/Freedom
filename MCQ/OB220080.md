## Question

A 39-year-old auditor and recreational tennis player presents with nine months of shoulder pain that has not improved with physical therapy or anti-inflammatory medications. His chief complaint is that he has had to transition to serving underhanded given the amount of pain he has with overhead motion. On exam in the office today he has exquisite tenderness over the bicipital groove and a positive O'Brien's test. Representative images from his MRI are shown in Figures 1 and 2. Which of the following findings on arthroscopic assessment of the shoulder would most influence your decision to perform a biceps tenodesis instead of a superior labral repair?

## Options

1. Type II superior labrum anterior-posterior (SLAP) tear with anteroinferior labral extension
2. Type II SLAP tear with posterior extension
3. Type III bucket-handle SLAP tear
4. Type IV SLAP tear with extension involving 40% of the biceps tendon
5. Type V SLAP tear

## Correct option

**4. Type IV SLAP tear with extension involving 40% of the biceps tendon**

## Answer explanation

Patients with a type 4 superior labrum anterior-posterior (SLAP) tear extending into and involving more than 30% of the long head of the biceps tendon are most frequently treated with a biceps tenodesis.

A SLAP lesion (Superior Labrum from Anterior to Posterior tear) generally occurs as a result of overuse injury to the shoulder in overhead athletes or traumatic falls in older patients and can result in deep shoulder pain and biceps tendonitis. These tears have traditionally been classified into four subtypes by the Snyder system, which was subsequently modified into subclassifications V-X by Maffet (Illustration A). First-line treatment remains rest from sports and physical therapy with rehab focusing on scapular dyskinesia and rotator cuff strengthening for all patients. Patients with Types I, III, and IV tears involving <1/3rd of the biceps tendon, causing severe symptoms that have failed non-operative management may be appropriately treated with arthroscopic debridement alone, while those with Type II tears and an unstable biceps anchor will benefit from either arthroscopic debridement with repair of the labrum versus debridement with biceps tenotomy or tenodesis depending on the patient's age and activity level. While historically reserved for older patients (> 35) or those in which SLAP repair has failed, recent studies demonstrate biceps tenodesis as a viable treatment for athletes under the age of 25. Patients over 35 who under SLAP repair are at risk for postoperative stiffness. Importantly, in the presence of a Type IV tear with involvement of more than 30% of the long head of the biceps tendon, tenodesis procedure is recommended, though controversy remains on what to do with the remaining unstable superior labrum.

*Griffin et al. evaluated outcomes after biceps tenodesis performed in patients younger than 25 years to evaluate reoperations and complications in this population and to critically appraise return to pre-injury level of play for this population.* *The authors reviewed 45 consecutive patients younger than 25 years who underwent subpectoral biceps tenodesis for biceps tendinopathy or biceps-labral complex injuries including SLAP tears.* **They found** that of these 36 patients, 34 (94%) were athletes, with 20 patients playing at the collegiate level, of which 73% returned to sports, with 19 returning at the same level and 6 returning at a lower level of play. **They concluded** that, when indicated, biceps tenodesis offers an alternative to SLAP repair in patients younger than 25 years and yields satisfactory outcomes, with two-thirds of patients returning to sport and a low revision rate.

*McDonald et al. reviewed disorders of the proximal and distal aspects of the biceps muscle.* **The authors note** that for proximal biceps tendon pathology, tenodesis of the long head of the biceps may offer improved cosmesis, improved strength, and diminished activity-related pain compared with tenotomy, although comparative studies have shown similar outcomes in some patient populations.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: These tears would most likely be treated with SLAP and Bankart repairs rather than biceps tenodesis in this active patient.
Answer 3: This tear type would most likely be treated with debridement and repair.
Answer 5: A type V tear is the same tear described in Answer 1, a Type II SLAP with anterior extension and a resultant Bankart lesion.
