## Question

A 20-year-old male presents after sustaining a gunshot wound to his right proximal forearm. There are small (<1 cm) entry and exit wounds, and he cannot pronate or supinate due to pain. He also is experiencing partial posterior interosseous nerve palsy. His presenting radiographs are shown in Figure A and selected CT images in Figure B. ## Options

1. Kocher approach to perform ORIF in the anatomic region between Lister’s tubercle and the radial styloid
2. Kaplan approach to perform ORIF in the anatomic region between Lister’s tubercle and the radial styloid
3. Kocher approach to perform radial head arthroplasty
4. Kaplan approach to perform radial head arthroplasty
5. Perform radial head excision

## Correct option

**2. Kaplan approach to perform ORIF in the anatomic region between Lister’s tubercle and the radial styloid**

## Answer explanation

This is a young patient with a minimally displaced two-part radial head fracture that extends distally through the radial neck and past the bicipital tuberosity. The Kaplan approach would allow for direct visualization to perform open reduction internal fixation with longer plate fixation and also allow for PIN neurolysis if needed (Answer 2).

Radial head and neck fractures are relatively common in the adult population. Minimally displaced fractures with no mechanical blocks to motion can be reliably treated with a short period (~1 week) of immobilization followed by early range of motion exercises. Grossly displaced fractures and fractures with mechanical blocks to motion should undergo operative intervention. Highly comminuted fractures often require radial head arthroplasty reconstruction. However, in young patients (<40) with ≤ 3 radial head fragments, open reduction internal fixation remains the standard of care. Most of these radial head fractures can be fixed through the Kocher approach with headless compression screws or a short mini-fragment plate. However, when the fracture extends distally through the bicipital tuberosity, this requires a more extensive dissection than can be reasonably done through the typical Kocher approach. The Kaplan approach is an alternative option that provides excellent exposure of the radial head/neck/proximal shaft while avoiding potential disruption of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament. Of note, the Kaplan approach does place the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) at risk, however, also allows for exploration and neurolysis if needed. Plates should be placed in the “safe interval” between the proximal interval between the radial styloid and Lister’s Tubercle. This interval does not articulate with the proximal radioulnar joint through range of motion and therefore the plate will NOT block pronosupination.

Cheung and Steinmann provided a comprehensive review of the surgical approaches to the elbow. They discuss posterior approaches for distal humerus fractures, lateral approaches for radial head and capitellar fractures, and medial approaches for coronoid fractures. They also discuss ligamentous injuries and tips/tricks for each approach. They conclude that the surgeon must understand relevant neurovascular and ligamentous structures to safely perform surgical approaches to the elbow.

*Catalano and colleagues provided a comprehensive review regarding the surgical approaches to the elbow.* *They discuss both volar and dorsal approaches to the radius and the subcutaneous approach to the ulna.* **The authors highlighted** the simplicity of ulnar approaches, whereas the radius incurs a much more complex dissection. **They concluded** by emphasizing the importance of anatomical knowledge and staying vigilant regarding the potential neurovascular dangers during each approach.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The Kocher approach utilizes the interval between the extensor carpi ulnaris and anconeus musculature. This allows for exposure of the radial head, lateral ulnar collateral ligament, and intraarticular space for loose body removal. However, it would not provide adequate exposure for this patient’s injury.
Answer 3&4: In younger patients, open reduction internal fixation should be attempted in all reasonably indicated fractures. As discussed, ≥ 3 fracture fragments portend a worse outcome and therefore should be considered for radial head arthroplasty.
Answer 5: Radial head excision is a relatively antiquated procedure due to the poor functional outcomes, elbow instability, and rapid progression of ulnohumeral arthritis. Therefore, it should be reserved for older, low-functional demand patients or end-stage rheumatic elbows.
