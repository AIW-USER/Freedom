## Question

A 61-year-old female is being seen in your clinic for her preoperative visit for a planned anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA). You explain to the patient that due to her glenoid deformity, you will need to take certain surgical steps in order to obtain the appropriate implant position. Outside the examination room, you and the medical student reviewed her computed tomography (CT) scan, and the student correctly identified that the patient has a B2 glenoid according to the Walch classification system. Which of the following best describes a Walch B2 glenoid?

## Options

1. Biconcave glenoid with posterior humeral head subluxation and posterior glenoid erosion with glenoid retroversion of 10°
2. Biconcave glenoid with posterior humeral head subluxation and posterior glenoid erosion with glenoid retroversion of 25°
3. Glenoid anteversion with anterior humeral head subluxation
4. Concentric glenoid wear with no humeral head subluxation and deep central erosion
5. Monoconcave glenoid with posterior wear and 70% posterior humeral head subluxation

## Correct option

**1. Biconcave glenoid with posterior humeral head subluxation and posterior glenoid erosion with glenoid retroversion of 10°**

## Answer explanation

The Walch classification system is commonly used as a means through which to describe the glenoid deformity present in the setting of glenohumeral osteoarthritis. A B2 glenoid is best described as one with a biconcave glenoid with asymmetric glenoid wear, posterior humeral head subluxation with posterior rim erosion, and glenoid retroversion <25° (Answer 1).

Glenohumeral arthritis is a degenerative joint disease of the shoulder characterized by damage to the articular surfaces of the humeral head and/or glenoid. The incidence of the pathology increases with age, and it is most commonly encountered in female patients over the age of 60. Glenohumeral osteoarthritis can broken down into primary and secondary; common causes of secondary osteoarthritis include post-traumatic changes, arthritis of dislocation, inflammatory arthropathy, osteonecrosis, neuropathic arthropathy, and rotator cuff arthropathy. While the exact etiology of primary osteoarthritis is unknown, it is characterized by the irreversible progressive loss of articular cartilage, flattening of the humeral head, glenoid wear, and (often) posterior humeral head subluxation.

The initial treatment of glenohumeral osteoarthritis is similar to that of hip and knee arthritis and includes oral NSAIDs, intraarticular corticosteroid injections, activity modification, and physical therapy. Patients with symptoms recalcitrant to nonoperative management are often treated with either an anatomic or reverse shoulder arthroplasty, with the exact implant dependent on a variety of patient- and surgeon-centric factors. Much attention has been given to the pattern and degree of glenoid deformity and wear present, and certain wear patterns can greatly impact the amount of glenoid bone stock present for component fixation and also may complicate or prevent appropriate component position. The Walch classification system (Illustration A) is commonly utilized to describe glenoid wear patterns and can be broadly broken down into four categories. Type A glenoids demonstrate concentric wear with no humeral head subluxation. Type B glenoids demonstrate asymmetric wear along a biconcave glenoid with posterior humeral head subluxation. Type C glenoids are those that are retroverted more than 25°. Finally, Type D glenoids are those with glenoid anteversion or anterior humeral head subluxation.

*Collin et al. provide the mid-term results of reverse shoulder arthroplasty for glenohumeral osteoarthritis in patients with posterior glenoid deficiency and humeral subluxation.* *Their study included 49 patients with a B1, B2, B3 or C glenoid from 7 high-volume centers.* *Overall, the mean total constant score at a minimum follow-up of 5 years increased from 30 to 68 (p < .001) and all 16 patients who received a glenoid bone graft had graft incorporation.* Overall, **the authors conclude** that reverse shoulder arthroplasty is an effective treatment for patients with primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis, posterior glenoid deficiency, and humeral subluxation.

Denard and Walch present the current concepts in the surgical management of primary glenohumeral arthritis in patients with a biconcave glenoid. The authors note that historically, patients with B2 glenoids have experienced poorer outcomes following arthroplasty when compared to other patterns of glenoid wear. The authors explain that patients who receive an anatomic shoulder arthroplasty often experience postoperative instability or early glenoid loosening, especially with glenoid retroversion > 27° or with 80% posterior humeral head subluxation. The authors conclude that many strategies exist to correct the underlying glenoid deformity, and suggest that in older patients or in patients with substantial glenoid deformity, a reverse-type prosthesis may be superior to an anatomic prosthesis.

*Ho et al. investigated the clinical and radiographic outcomes of a posteriorly augmented glenoid component in anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty for patients with primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis with posterior glenoid bone loss.* *Their study included 71 shoulders with B2 or B3 glenoids that underwent anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty with an augmented glenoid component with a minimum 2-year follow-up.* **The authors conclude** that posteriorly augmented components can improve pathologic retroversion and posterior humeral head subluxation in B2 and B3 glenoids, and provide most patients with significant improvements in clinical outcome scores.

*Simovitch et al. investigated the rate of improvement in clinical outcomes with both anatomic and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty.* *Their study population consisted of 1,183 patients, 505 who underwent an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty and 678 who underwent a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty.* While both procedures improved patient outcomes, anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty more reliably improved range of motion (particularly external rotation). The majority of improvement occurred within the first 6 months of surgery, but **the authors note** that improvements can be seen for up to two years following surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Secondary to the high amount of glenoid retroversion and biconcave wear, this best describes a C2 glenoid.
Answer 3: Secondary to the glenoid anteversion and anterior humeral head subluxation, this best describes a D glenoid.
Answer 4: Secondary to the deep, concentric central wear, this best describes an A2 glenoid.
Answer 5: Secondary to the monoconcave glenoid with posterior wear and 70% posterior humeral head subluxation, this best describes a B3 glenoid.
