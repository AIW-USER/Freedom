## Question

A 12-year-old patient presents with sudden onset knee pain when she planted to kick a ball while playing soccer. She is unable to bear weight and her knee demonstrates a large effusion that occurred immediately. You obtain the X-rays shown in Figure A. Under sedation you attempt a reduction in the ER, however, you were unsuccessful. What is most likely blocking your reduction?

![[q217523-a-type-2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Anterior cruciate ligament
2. Posterior cruciate ligament
3. Lateral meniscus
4. Medial meniscus
5. Intermeniscal ligament

## Correct option

**4. Medial meniscus**

## Answer explanation

Tibial spine (or eminence) fractures are associated with anterior horn medial meniscus entrapment. This can occur in up to 65% of type III tibial eminence fractures.

Tibial eminence fractures are the pediatric equivalent to ACL ruptures in adults and occur by similar mechanisms and present similarly. Patients often report rapid acceleration or a hyper-extension/rotation mechanism with immediate pain, effusion, and inability to bear weight. These occur most often in children 8-14 years old and occur because the intercondylar eminence is not yet completely ossified, making it a point of weakness compared to the ACL. Usually, the fracture occurs through the deep cancellous bone and remains confined to the tibial eminence without propagation into either plateau. Treatment is often with attempted closed reduction, and if successful, casting in extension for 3-4 weeks. If closed reduction is not successful it is often due to the anterior horn of the medial meniscus, or less often the intermeniscal ligament or anterior horn lateral meniscus. Open or arthroscopic reduction is needed in these cases with internal fixation of the fracture.

*Kocher et al. performed a case series of tibial eminence fractures to determine the prevalence of meniscal entrapment in 80 type II and type III fractures.* *All patients underwent arthroscopic, open, or combined reduction and fixation.* **They found** that the medial meniscus was entrapped in 36 knees, intermeniscal ligament in 6, and lateral meniscus in 1. In total, 26% of type II fractures and 65% of type III fractures had a structure entrapped within them.

Chandler and Miller present a case report of an 11-year-old boy who sustained a type II tibial eminence fracture. The patient underwent apparent successful closed reduction as determined by Xray with subsequent arthroscopic evaluation demonstrating entrapment of the medial meniscus. The meniscus was removed and the tibial eminence was secured with suture fixation. In their discussion, the authors argue that all displaced tibial eminence fractures should undergo arthroscopic reduction to ensure that there are no entrapped structures that may not be identified on Xray.

Figure A demonstrates an AP and lateral knee x-ray with an anterior elevation of the tibial eminences consistent with a type II tibial eminence fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The ACL attaches to the tibial eminence, however, it does not prevent reduction.
Answer 2: The PCL does not interact with the tibial eminences nor prevents reduction.
Answer 3 & 5: The intermeniscal ligament and lateral meniscus can become entrapped in the fracture, however, they are much less common reduction blocks compared to the medial meniscus.
