## Question

A 9-year-old girl sustains the injury shown in Figures A and B while playing on the playground. Postreduction images are shown in Figure C. Where did the fracture fragment entrapped in the joint most likely originate from, and what is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. medial epicondyle; attempt at re-reduction
2. lateral epicondyle; open reduction and internal fixation
3. coronoid process; open reduction and internal fixation
4. medial condyle; attempt at re-reduction
5. medial epicondyle; open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**5. medial epicondyle; open reduction and internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

Medial epicondyle fractures are the most common associated fractures in pediatric elbow dislocations; an entrapped fracture fragment in the joint following a pediatric elbow dislocation is an absolute indication for open reduction and internal fixation (Answer #5).

Pediatric elbow dislocations often occur due to a fall onto an outstretched hand. The most common associated fracture is a medial epicondyle fracture, which may become entrapped in the joint and hinder anatomic reduction. An incarcerated bone fragment in the joint after a pediatric elbow dislocation is one of the few absolute indications for open reduction and internal fixation of the medial epicondyle fracture (the other absolute indication being an open fracture). Orthopaedic surgeons must also be vigilant for neurovascular injuries, particularly to the brachial artery, median nerve and ulnar nerve, which can occur in association with pediatric elbow dislocations.

*Edmonds performed a retrospective review of 11 patients with medial epicondyle fractures of the humerus that were initially diagnosed as nondisplaced or minimally displaced.* **The author found** that plain radiographs underestimated the degree of fracture displacement compared to a CT scan. *Edmonds* **concluded** that standard radiographs (AP and lateral) are not sufficient to measure anterior or medial displacement and that 3D-CT scans are the most accurate way to measure true displacement.

*Gottschalk et al. reviewed medial epicondyle fractures in the pediatric population.* **They note** that management of these injuries is controversial, but that documented absolute indications for surgical intervention are open fractures and incarcerated fracture fragments in the joint. **They further note** that no consensus exists in the literature regarding the amount of fracture displacement that warrants surgical fixation.

*Pathy et al. also reviewed medial epicondyle fractures in children.* **They noted** that good outcomes have been reported with nonoperative management for minimally displaced fractures, but that recent studies have demonstrated the underestimation of fracture displacement using standard radiographs. **The authors also note** that there is evidence that supports open reduction and internal fixation of medial epicondyle fractures in adolescent athletes to permit the return to prior level of play.

Figures A and B show the AP and lateral radiographs, respectively, of a pediatric elbow dislocation with an associated medial epicondyle fracture. Figure C shows the post-reduction lateral radiograph in a splint with an incarcerated fracture fragment from the medial epicondyle in the elbow joint.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: While the most likely origin of the incarcerated fracture fragment is the medial epicondyle, the most appropriate next step in management is open reduction and internal fixation of the fracture fragment.
Answer 2: The medial epicondyle is the most likely origin of the incarcerated fracture fragment in the joint.
Answer 3: The coronoid process is not the most common donor site for the fracture fragment.
Answer 4: The medial condyle would involve the articular aspect of the elbow joint. With the amount of displacement of the fragement, the next step would likely be open reduction and internal fixation.
