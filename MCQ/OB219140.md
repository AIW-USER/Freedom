## Question

A 75-year-old female presents to the emergency department with pain and deformity just proximal to her right knee. She slipped and fell on the ice while trying to take her trash out. Radiographs reveal a comminuted supracondylar distal femur fracture. You elect to perform open reduction and internal fixation with a lateral distal femur locking plate for her fracture. However, you want to avoid the complication of nonunion by ensuring the construct isn't too stiff. Which one of the following would decrease the stiffness of this plate construct?

## Options

1. Use of far cortical locking screws
2. Placing screws closer to the fracture
3. Adding a supplemental medial plate
4. Placing bicortical locking screws
5. Use of stainless steel rather than titanium plate

## Correct option

**1. Use of far cortical locking screws**

## Answer explanation

Far cortical locking screws (Illustration A) are a tool used to decrease the stiffness of a plate construct.

Far cortical locking screws were created to address several problems postulated with current locked plating systems. First, it is thought that locked plates provide such stiff fixation that they may suppress most inter-fragmentary motion and minimize callus formation, which may not be advantageous in comminuted, metaphyseal fractures such as this supracondylar distal femur fracture. Additionally, locked plates are thought to create an asymmetric zone of healing, where stress-shielding directly under the plate is caused by the lack of motion at the plate/bone interface. Far cortical locking screws help address this issue by still providing rigid fixation into the far cortex, but allowing an increase in inter-fragmentary motion to create callus and preventing stress-shielding underneath the plate. Far cortical locking screws are able to achieve this through its flexible shaft with a reduced diameter without threads at the near cortex thus allowing controlled motion.

*Bottlang et al. reviewed the biomechanics of far cortical locking screws,* **noting** their development was encouraged by concerns for lack of biologic healing and callus formation that results from excessive stiffness with locked plate constructs. **They also note** that standard locking screws can cause uneven stress distributions through the fracture and lead to stress shielding underneath the plate. **They note** that far cortical locking screws decrease the stiffness of a locked plate construct by 80-88% in order to better distribute load and promote callus formation which they believe replicates the natural biomechanical behavior of bone.

*McLachlin et al. reviewed proximal screw configuration and its effect on construct stiffness in comminuted supracondylar femur fractures.* **They noted** that strain increased with increasing working length but suggested this may not be the sole solution to this problem as shear forces were also increased with greater working lengths.

*Heyland et al. looked at the use of semi-rigid screws and their effect on interfragmentary movement in locking plate use at the distal femur.* **They noted** that placing a far cortical locking screw in the first hole proximal to the fracture increased the strain at the near cortex by 8-28% but only changed the strain at the far cortex by ~1%. **They also noted** that increases in working length increased the strain and shear forces at the fracture. Their ultimate conclusion was that far cortical locking screws may be beneficial in altering the asymmetric strain and healing seen at the near cortex under the plate.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Placing screws closer to the fracture site decreases the working length of the construct, thereby increasing stiffness.
Answer 3: Use of a second plate would increase the stiffness of the construct.
Answer 4: Placing bicortical locking screws has been shown to increase the stiffness of locking plate constructs.
Answer 5: Stainless steel has a higher modulus of elasticity than titanium and is thought to create a stiffer construct.
