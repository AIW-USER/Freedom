## Question

A 72-year-old female presents to your clinic for evaluation of chronic right shoulder pain. Radiographs are obtained and demonstrate evidence of Hamada grade 3 rotator cuff arthropathy. You obtain a CT scan which demonstrates significant glenohumeral joint arthritis and superior glenoid inclination. You recommend reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) for him, and he elects to proceed with the surgery. When discussing the post-operative protocol with him, what position do you recommend he avoid to minimize his risk for anterior prosthetic dislocation?

## Options

1. Extension, abduction, external rotation
2. Extension, adduction, internal rotation
3. Flexion, abduction, external rotation
4. Flexion, abduction, internal rotation
5. Flexion, adduction, external rotation

## Correct option

**2. Extension, adduction, internal rotation**

## Answer explanation

After reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA), patients should avoid the position of extension, adduction, and internal rotation to prevent anterior prosthetic dislocation.

RTSA is advantageous in multiple different clinical situations, one of which includes rotator cuff tear arthropathy. The prosthesis moves the center of rotation medial and inferior and improves the mechanical advantage of the deltoid muscle for forward elevation. Instability of the prosthesis is not common (2-3% incidence), but it can be a devastating complication. The position of dislocation is most commonly in extension, adduction, and internal rotation, which is the position assumed while pushing off of a chair when rising. Patients should be educated about the potential for dislocation if attempting to bear their body weight in this position.

*Cheung et al. looked at complications in RTSA,* **noting** that indications have recently continued to expand, which has caused the number of cases performed to go up, with a concomitant increase in the number of total complications. Common complications include neurologic injury, periprosthetic fracture, hematoma, infection, scapular notching, dislocation, baseplate failure, and acromial fracture. They discuss the incidence and management of these complications.

*Gerber et al. wrote a JAAOS review article on RTSA, discussing the biomechanical rationale behind its development.* **They note** the advances that RTSA has allowed but also cite the complication rate being 3x higher than conventional total shoulder arthroplasty. **They emphasize** the importance of patient selection and technical detail to optimize results and minimize complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3-5: Extension, adduction, and internal rotation represent the combined upper extremity positioning that should be avoided after RTSA to minimize the risk of anterior prosthetic dislocation.
