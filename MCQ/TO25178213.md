## Question

Which of the following clinical features most accurately characterizes the "Gilliatt-Sumner Hand" in a patient with true neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (nTOS)?

## Options

1. Symmetric wasting of all interossei and the hypothenar eminence, sparing the thenar muscles.
2. Preferential atrophy of the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) with relative sparing of the hypothenar muscles.
3. Global "claw hand" deformity resulting from isolated compression of the ulnar nerve at the humerotricipital groove
4. Sensory loss strictly localized to the thumb and index finger, corresponding to the C6 dermatome.

## Correct option

**2. Preferential atrophy of the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) with relative sparing of the hypothenar muscles.**

## Answer explanation

The Gilliatt-Sumner Hand is a classic sign
of true neurogenic TOS, typically caused by a cervical rib or a congenital
fibrous band compressing the lower trunk of the brachial plexus (C8 and T1
fibers). While both the median and ulnar nerves receive fibers from the lower
trunk, the T1-innervated muscles are often more severely affected.
Specifically, there is preferential wasting of the thenar eminence,
particularly the Abductor Pollicis Brevis (APB), which often appears
more hollowed out than the hypothenar eminence. This is a crucial "red
flag" because thenar wasting is more commonly associated with Carpal
Tunnel Syndrome; however, in TOS, it is accompanied by medial forearm sensory
changes and often less severe involvement of the ulnar-innervated intrinsic
muscles compared to the APB.

Gilliatt-Sumner hand (GSH) is a rare,
severe manifestation of true neurogenic thoracic outlet
syndrome (NTOS) caused by chronic compression of the lower trunk of the
brachial plexus (C8-T1). It features prominent atrophy of hand muscles—specifically
the thenar eminence (Abductor Pollicis Brevis) and hypothenar muscles.

Key Aspects of Gilliatt-Sumner Hand:

 - Clinical Presentation: Severe
 wasting of the hand, particularly the thenar muscles, accompanied by pain
 and sensory abnormalities in the ulnar and medial forearm distribution.

 - Causes: Often associated with
 congenital anomalies like a cervical rib or fibrous bands causing
 compression, typically treated via decompression surgery.

 - Diagnosis: Confirmed through
 electrodiagnostic studies (showing characteristic neurogenic changes) and
 imaging, such as MRI or ultrasound, to visualize brachial plexus
 compression.

 - Treatment & Outcomes: Surgical
 decompression of the thoracic outlet is the primary treatment, which
 usually stops the progression of atrophy and pain. While motor recovery
 was previously considered rare, studies show that surgical intervention
 can lead to partial improvement of motor deficits in many cases.

 - Differential Diagnosis: Must
 be distinguished from conditions like carpal tunnel syndrome, motor neuron
 disease (ALS), or Hirayama disease

Answer: 2
