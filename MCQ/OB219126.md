## Question

An 85-year-old female recently fell while tending to her garden. She was seen in the emergency room two months after the fall and diagnosed with a chronic parasymphyseal pubic ramus fracture after a month of declining independence and pain with ambulation. Which of the following additional radiographic findings, if present, would most strongly demonstrate lumbopelvic instability and indicate operative fixation in this patient?

## Options

1. Bilateral inferior pubic rami fractures
2. Bilateral sacral ala fractures
3. Contralateral superior pubic ramus fracture
4. Contralateral pubic root and inferior pubic ramus fractures
5. Unilateral sacral ala fracture

## Correct option

**2. Bilateral sacral ala fractures**

## Answer explanation

Bilateral sacral ala fractures often have an associated transverse fracture line ("sacral U-type fractures") that effectively separates the lumbar spine from the remaining pelvis and lower extremities with varying degrees of kyphotic or translational deformity that necessitates operative fixation.

Pelvic fragility fractures in the elderly population represent a difficult subset of injuries to treat. Fractures of the anterior pelvic ring can often be treated non-operatively in elderly populations; however, if ambulation is limited by pain or instability is noted, fixation may be required. A very specific subset of injuries involving the posterior pelvic ring, or sacrum, deserves special consideration because of the possibility of frank lumbopelvic or spinopelvic dissociation. Given the relative rarity of this injury pattern and the sometimes subtle radiographic findings, injury severity can be under-appreciated or the diagnosis delayed or missed altogether. Thus, although bilateral sacral ala fractures may be readily apparent on axial computed tomography (CT) images, lumbopelvic instability is best identified and characterized on the sagittal reconstructions, as defined by the Roy-Camille (RC) classification system (Illustration A).

*Rommens et al. provide a comprehensive classification for fragility fractures of the pelvic ring, as well as recommendations for surgical management.* **The authors note** that most fragility fractures of the pelvis are minimally displaced and do not require surgical therapy; however, in some patients, an insidious progression of bone damage leads to increasing displacement, nonunion, and persisting instability. **The authors conclude** that Type I injuries (anterior rami fractures only) typically do not require surgery, while Type IV lesions (i.e., bilateral sacral U-type fractures) necessitate iliolumbar fixation or a combination of osteosynthesis techniques.

*Pulley et al. published a retrospective analysis of surgical fixation of geriatric sacral U-type insufficiency fractures.* *Of 16 patients treated over 36 months, delayed surgery was performed after primary nonoperative treatment had failed in 62.5% (10/16) at an average of 83 days post-injury, while acute surgery was performed in 37.5% (6/16) at an average of 5 days post-injury.* **The authors concluded** that treatment of sacral U-type insufficiency fractures by percutaneous screw fixation permits early mobilization, provides rapid pain relief, and prevents progressive deformity.

*Bishop et al. provide a retrospective radiographic case series demonstrating the strong association of bilateral sacral ala fractures with the presence of lumbopelvic instability.* *Of 443 patients with unilateral sacral ala fractures, none were associated with lumbopelvic instability, while 41/47 (87%) with bilateral sacral ala fractures had a transverse component indicating some degree of lumbopelvic instability.* **The authors concluded** that the presence of bilateral sacral fractures was 100% sensitive and 99% specific for lumbopelvic instability.

Illustration A represents the Roy-Camille (RC) classification system of lumbopelvic instability, with Type 1 being a simple flexion deformity of the sacrum, Type 2 a partial translation and hyperkyphosis, Type 3 a complete translation with no fracture overlap, and Type 4 a segmental comminution of the S1 body. Illustration B represents the CT findings of a sacral U-type fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, and 4: Though bilateral and segmental rami fractures are predictors for pelvic ring instability and pain that may fail non-operative management, anterior ring injuries alone do not lead to lumbopelvic dissociation as mentioned in the question stem.
Answer 5: Unilateral sacral ala fractures have not been shown to lead to lumbopelvic dissociation.
