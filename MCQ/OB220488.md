## Question

A 30-year-old male presents to your clinic with persistent right dorsal foot pain and numbness that radiates from the ankle to the forefoot between the first and second toes. He regularly runs marathons and loves hiking. On physical examination, he exhibits tenderness over the anterior ankle and reports radiating pain and tingling between the first and second toes when tapping on this region. Based upon this patient's diagnosis, what is the most superficial structure that defines the space causing his symptoms?

## Options

1. Extensor hallucis longus
2. Extensor digitorum longus
3. Peroneus tertius
4. Inferior extensor retinaculum
5. Extensor hallucis brevis

## Correct option

**4. Inferior extensor retinaculum**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome secondary to compression over the anterior ankle and dorsal foot, most commonly from shoe wear. The most superficial structure that defines the anterior tarsal tunnel is the inferior extensor retinaculum (answer 4).

Tarsal tunnel syndrome is a compressive neuropathy at the level of the ankle. This condition is broken up by region. Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome involves a compressive neuropathy of the deep peroneal nerve, whereas posterior tarsal tunnel syndrome is a compressive neuropathy of the tibial nerve. Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome is caused by intrinsic (e.g., ganglion cyst, tenosynovitis, and osteophytes) and extrinsic (e.g., shoe wear, trauma, and anatomic deformities) factors. The anterior tarsal tunnel is defined by the inferior extensor retinaculum (superficial), talonavicular capsule (deep), medial malleolus (medial), and lateral malleolus (lateral). The structures inside include the deep peroneal nerve, dorsalis pedis artery and vein, as well as the tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, and peroneus tertius tendons. Physical exam typically demonstrates pain over the tarsal tunnel and along the deep peroneal nerve distribution, terminating between the first and second toes. Treatment most often includes nonoperative management with activity modification or removal of the etiology (e.g., compressive shoes). If this does not improve symptoms, then surgical release can be pursued.

*Yassin et al. performed a retrospective review of 13 patients who underwent surgical release of the anterior tarsal tunnel.* **They noted** 12/13 patients had a bulge in the anterior ankle, and all of these patients had a positive Tinel’s sign on exam. **They reported** that endoscopic tunnel release is preferable for osteophytes or isolated ganglion cysts, whereas open release is preferable for synovitis or unknown causes. **They concluded** that anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome only accounts for 5% of cases with foot numbness and is commonly misdiagnosed; therefore, providers should keep this condition on their radar as it can be readily treated.

*Fortier et al. performed an updated review on peroneal nerve entrapment in the lower extremity.* Peroneal neuropathy is the most common compressive neuropathy of the lower extremity and can occur due to compression of the common peroneal nerve (CPN), superficial peroneal nerve (SPN), or deep peroneal nerve (DPN). Common sites of compression of each nerve include the bony prominence of the fibula (CPN), entrapment as it exits the lateral compartment of the leg (SPN), and entrapment as it crosses underneath the extensor retinaculum (DPN). Treatment ranges from removing external compression, providing stability to unstable joints, and reducing inflammatory processes. **They concluded** that peroneal neuropathy should be considered in patients presenting with foot drop, pain, and numbness in the lower extremity.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answers 1-3: All 3 of these tendons run through the anterior tarsal tunnel, deep to the inferior extensor retinaculum.
Answer 5: The extensor hallucis brevis tendon does not run through the anterior tarsal tunnel as it originates distal to this structure.
