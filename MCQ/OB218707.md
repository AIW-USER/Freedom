## Question

A 52-year-old male with a longstanding history of type II diabetes presents with ulceration along the left plantar forefoot (Figure A). He endorses pain in the area with some mild fevers overnight and denies ever having a foot ulceration. A cotton-tipped swab confirms the wound probes to an underlying bone. Transcutaneous oxygen pressure is measured at 48 mmHg. His labs are consistent with elevated inflammatory markers and an Hb A1C of 7.2%. A diagnostic MRI was obtained suggestive of sesamoid and first-ray osteomyelitis. He adamantly refuses any amputation procedures and undergoes an Achilles lengthening procedure, outpatient wound care, forefoot offloading bracing, and oral antibiotics. Which of the following risk factors is most predictive of treatment failure and eventual need for amputation?

![[q218707-a-figure-1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. This being the patient’s first ulceration
2. The patient’s elevated Hb A1C
3. The transcutaneous oxygen pressure results
4. Presence of osteomyelitis in his sesamoids
5. Performing an Achilles lengthening procedure

## Correct option

**4. Presence of osteomyelitis in his sesamoids**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a diabetic plantar forefoot ulceration and underlying osteomyelitis. In this case, the presence of underlying osteomyelitis serves as the greatest predictor for eventual amputation (Answer 4).

The three pillars of diabetic foot ulceration treatment include: (1) ensuring there is sufficient blood supply to the area, (2) treating any underlying infection, and (3) achieving appropriate pressure relief from the ulceration site. This patient’s ulceration is located at the metatarsal heads, suggestive of an underlying equinus contracture. Treating the equinus contracture to offload pressure at the ulceration site has been shown to significantly decrease the risk of recurrent ulcerations in this area. A Silfverskiöld test would help determine the most appropriate lengthening procedure. Isolated gastrocnemius contractures may be treated with a gastrocnemius recession (e.g. Strayer) procedure whereas Achilles tendon contracture requires formal Achilles tendon lengthening. In any case, the greatest predictor for treatment failure is the presence of underlying osteomyelitis at the time of diagnosis.

*Mauler and colleagues reviewed the outcomes of their 18-patient cohort managed with nonoperative treatment for diabetic sesamoid osteomyelitis.* *Despite wound care, immobilization, offloading devices, and oral antibiotics, 15 of 18 patients eventually required surgical excision of the sesamoids, internal resection, or ray amputation.* **The authors conclude** that they no longer consider nonsurgical therapy as a viable option when sesamoid osteomyelitis is present. **They recommend** advising patients of the high likelihood of future surgery if they desire nonoperative treatment.

*Nishimoto and colleagues provided a comprehensive review regarding the treatment of diabetic plantar forefoot ulcerations.* **They discuss** that diabetic foot ulcerations are attributed to multifactorial causes including peripheral vascular disease, peripheral neuropathy, poor-fitting shoe wear, and localized infection. The authors endorse the early release of the Achilles tendon for these patients to correct equinus contractures, promote rapid healing via offloading the ulceration site, and prevent recurrences. **They conclude** by reviewing the various techniques for performing Achilles tendon lengthening procedures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: First-occurrence diabetic foot ulcerations are more likely to heal than recurrent ulcerations.
Answer 2&3: Hb A1C levels above 8%, transcutaneous oxygen pressures <30-40 mmHg, serum albumin < 3.0 g/dL, and lymphocyte count < 1,500/mm3 are all serum labs associated with poor healing potential.
Answer 5: Achilles tendon lengthening procedures have been shown to improve plantar forefoot ulceration healing rates and decrease the risk of ulceration recurrence.
