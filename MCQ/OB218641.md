## Question

A 37-year-old recreational rock climber comes to the emergency room with the injury shown in Figures A and B after a fall at the climbing gym. He is reduced, splinted, and referred to an Orthopaedic surgeon for definitive fixation. Which of the following steps taken by the treating surgeon will most reliably restore the strongest portion of the syndesmosis?

## Options

1. Antiglide plate fixation of the fibula fracture
2. Performance of an intra-operative cotton test
3. Placement of two quadricortical syndesmotic screws
4. Reduction and fixation of the posterior malleolus
5. Use of flexible syndesmotic fixation placed 5cm above the plafond

## Correct option

**4. Reduction and fixation of the posterior malleolus**

## Answer explanation

The patient sustained a trimalleolar ankle fracture-dislocation with a large posterior malleolus fragment. Given that the posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL), the strongest component of the syndesmosis, originates on the posterior malleolus, reducing it and fixing it directly will most reliably restore the anatomy of the disrupted syndesmosis.

The syndesmosis between the fibula and tibia is formed by the anterior and posterior inferior tibiofibular ligaments (AITFL and PITFL) and the interosseous ligament, which is the lower part of the interosseous membrane (Illustration A). Co-existing syndesmotic injuries are typical in high-energy ankle fracture-dislocations. Regarding posterior malleolus fractures, which represent the origin of the PITFL, the old teaching was that those affecting < 25% of the tibial plafond could be treated non-operatively; however, this information was based on a study from the 1940s that reviewed only 8 fractures involving the posterior malleolus. Newer studies have shown that internal fixation of posterior malleolus fractures stabilizes the syndesmosis more than syndesmotic screws or suture buttons, and can make further syndesmotic fixation unnecessary. The preferred approach typically involves having the patient in the prone position, utilizing either a posterolateral or posteromedial approach depending on the location of the posterior malleolus fracture, as identified on preoperative CT imaging. The fragment can then be reduced under direct visualization and a posterior 1/3 tubular buttress plate or similar fixation can be used to facilitate and maintain the reduction, restoring the footprint of the PITFL.

Lampridis, et al. provide a review of stability in ankle fractures, including the diagnosis and preferred treatment methods. The authors note that posterior malleolus fractures, regardless of size, should be considered for surgical fixation to restore stability, reduce the need for syndesmosis fixation, and improve contact pressure distribution in the ankle mortise.

Mason, et al. published on the pathoanatomy and associated injuries of posterior malleolus fractures of the ankle, creating a modification of the Haraguchi classification system to help guide treatment. The classification system was divided into three subtypes: (1) a type 1 fracture was described as a small extra-articular posterior malleolar primary fragment, (2) Type 2 fractures consisted of a primary fragment of the posterolateral triangle of the tibia (Volkmann area), (3) a type 3 primary fragment was characterized by a coronal plane fracture line involving the whole posterior plafond. The authors concluded that posterior malleolar fractures are variable in their nature, and as such should not be grouped together for analysis.

Figures A and B are oblique and lateral radiographs of the ankle, respectively, depicting a supination-external rotation (SER) ankle fracture-dislocation. Illustration A depicts the anatomy of the syndesmosis. Illustration B depicts the Haraguchi classification of posterior malleolus fractures (from left to right): Type I (the posterolateral-oblique type), type II (the medial extension type), and type III (the small-shell type). Illustration C depicts the tap test, in which Inset A (non-stressed) and B (stressed) represent an intact syndesmosis with Inset B showing no significant widening when stress is applied across the tap, while Inset C represents an unstable syndesmosis that widens with stress placed across the tap.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Antiglide plate fixation of the fibula fracture with a 1/3 tubular plate placed along the posterior border after anatomic reduction would be appropriate in this case and would help to maintain fibular length; however, fixation of the posterior malleolus fracture would be most crucial in restoring the footprint of the syndesmosis and would best be performed prior to fixing the lateral malleolus.
Answer 2: A cotton test would be performed intra-operatively after fixation of both the posterior malleolus and the fibula via lateral pull on the fibula with a bone hook to assess for syndesmotic widening. Other methods, such as the tap test (Illustration C), may represent more controlled and reliable intra-operative tests of post-operative syndesmotic stability.
Answers 3 and 5: Screw and flexible syndesmotic fixation have been shown to malreduce the syndesmosis in up to 30% of cases in post-operative CT studies. Thus, direct visualization and reduction of the posterior malleolus fragment would be recommended in this case prior to performing any additional syndesmotic fixation.
