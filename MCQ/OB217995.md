## Question

A 67-year-old patient presents to clinic for evaluation of their right shoulder. They state that have had an insidious onset of pain and inability to move their shoulder when performing activities of daily living. On exam, the patient has full passive range of motion. Figure A is a picture of the patient attempting to forward flex. There is no change in active range of motion after a glenohumeral injection of local anesthetic. Which of the following would most likely explain the pathomechanics of this finding?

![[q217995-a-screen-shot-2023-02-13-at-11-39-19-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Posteroinferior subluxation of the humeral head
2. Atrophy of the deltoid muscle
3. Anterosuperior subluxation of the humeral head
4. Neuropraxia of the supraspinatus
5. Failure of the rotator cuff to elevate the humeral head

## Correct option

**3. Anterosuperior subluxation of the humeral head**

## Answer explanation

This patient's history and the figure indicate pseudoparalysis, which is secondary to loss of humeral head depression from a dysfunctioning rotator cuff. Ultimately, this alteration in shoulder biomechanics leads to anterosuperior subluxation of the humeral head during attempted forward elevation, commonly known as anterosuperior escape.

In a normal functioning shoulder, the forces produced by the rotator cuff cause centering compression of the humeral head against the concave glenoid surface, which then provides a stable fulcrum for the periscapular muscles and the deltoid to move the humerus relative to the glenoid. In the above example where the patient is asked to forward flex their shoulder, a dysfunctional rotator cuff is unable to provide caudal countertraction against the cranial pull of the deltoid. During this forward elevation, the deltoid muscle pulls the humerus from its insertion, which leads to anterosuperior subluxation of the humeral head if there is no depressive force acting on the humeral head (normally provided by the rotator cuff). Stability and shoulder function are therefore dependent on muscle balance, also called ‘force coupling’, which centers the resulting force vector towards the center of the glenoid surface. Loss of force couple balance is known to lead to pathological conditions such as instability, eccentric wear, and subsequent cuff tear arthropathy (CTA). The loss of force coupling in essence is why an anatomic shoulder replacement would fail to provide any sort of mechanical improvement in motion.

*Walker et al. provide a review on how reverse total shoulder arthroplasty works, with a focus on the basic biomechanical challenges associated with the rotator cuff-deficient shoulder, the biomechanical rationale for newer reverse shoulder arthroplasty designs, the current scientific evidence related to the function and performance of reverse shoulder arthroplasty, and specific technical aspects of reverse shoulder arthroplasty.*

*Drake et al. describe classification of rotator cuff tear reparability and the concept of a balanced shoulder, explore the theory behind RTSA design relative to rotator cuff arthropathy, discuss the indications and contraindications for RTSA, and review published outcomes of RTSA for rotator cuff arthropathy.* **They note** that modern RTSA designs restore deltoid tension and a functional fulcrum to the rotator cuff deficient shoulder, which allows recovery of active shoulder elevation and effectively restores function in short- and medium-term follow-up studies.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: During forward elevation, the deltoid muscle acts to pull the humerus superiorly in the anterior plane from its insertion. With rotator cuff deficiency, there is no counterforce acting on the humeral head, thereby leading to anterosuperior subluxation, NOT posteroinferior subluxation.
Answer 2: Deltoid atrophy alone as a cause to why the patient is unable to elevate beyond what is displayed in the figure is unlikely given the patient’s history (which is classic for a chronic rotator cuff tear).
Answer 4: An intact rotator cuff in the setting of a suprascapular nerve neuropraxia is still able to provide a checkrein to the humeral head during forward elevation. While strength would be expected to be diminished, complete loss of forward elevation beyond 45 degrees in the setting of this patient’s history is most likely secondary to loss of force-coupling of moment arms, as explained above.
Answer 5: Pseudoparalysis is secondary to loss of the rotator cuff’s ability to DEPRESS the humeral head during forward elevation, not elevate.
