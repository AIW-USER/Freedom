Chapter: Arthroplasty MCQs — Complications of THR

## Question

A patient is having persistent groin pain post THR. There is no foot drop, no weakness of plantar flexion either. Gait was evaluated to check for a Trendelenburg pattern but wasn’t found. The nerve likely involved is [FNB 2022]

## Options

1. Obturator
2. Femoral
3. Sciatic
4. Superior Gluteal

## Correct option

**1. Obturator**

## Answer explanation

Ans: A

Although obturator nerve is uncommonly injured post THR, its injuries often lead to groin pain and hence has been chosen as the answer. Sciatic nerve damage would have led to foot drop and Superior gluteal to Trendelenburg gait. Femoral nerve injuries mostly lead to antero-lateral thigh pain, all of which are not mentioned in the question.

NERVE INURIES POST TOTAL HIP REPLACEMENT

The reported incidence of nerve injuries associated with THA ranges from 0.6 to 3.7%, with a higher risk found in patients receiving revision THAs (7.6%).

Nerve injuries can occur during THA due to compression, traction, ischemia, or transection. They can occur perioperatively with patient positioning (CPN is particularly at risk in lateral position) and draping, improper retractor placement, manipulations during hip dislocation, or in the setting of a developing hematoma. Traction, or stretch injuries often occur with intraoperative manipulation, such as during dislocation and reduction of the hip, or with limb lengthening (> 3.8 cms lengthening places peroneal component of Sciatic nerve at risk and > 4 cm can lead to complete sciatic palsy) or lateralization as a result of THA. Lastly, bone cement may also cause neural injury as a result of compressive and/or thermal damage, and therefore, leaked bone cement should be removed.

Risk factors: -

- THR in DDH and post traumatic arthritis
- Revision THR or history of previous hip surgery
- Limb lengthening
- Uncemented THR > Cemented [Reason controversial: Farrell et al. hypothesized that due to the requirement of an inference fit with the cementless prosthesis, surgeons tend to ream and fixate the prosthesis with more force, thus increasing the likelihood of more transection nerve injuries].
- Females > Males [Could be from higher incidence of DDH in females]
- Pre-existing spinal problems [Double crush hypothesis could be contributive]

Nerves at risk and Surgical approach

- ANTERIOR: Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve > femoral
- ANTERO-LATERAL: Superior Gluteal Nerve
- POSTERIOR: Sciatic (Peroneal branch specifically)

Major Specific Nerve Injuries

SCIATIC NERVE

Sciatic nerve palsy is the most commonly encountered nerve injury associated with THA, accounting for over 90% of all reported nerve injuries. The common peroneal nerve is at an increased risk for injury compared to the tibial nerve as a result of more anatomical variations, its superficial fibre arrangement in nerve bundle and its fixity. Also, the common peroneal nerve consists of tightly packed fascicles, rather than the abundance of connective tissue that comprises the tibial nerve; thus, it is more vulnerable to transection and compression.

The posterior/posterolateral approach to the hip is most commonly used for THA, and that places the sciatic nerve at the highest risk of injury. Regarding incidence, the incidence of sciatic nerve palsy following THA has been reported to be 1.5% (so overall incidence is low), which could increase to 3% to 8% in revision THA and 5.8% in those with preexisting DDH. The etiology of injury remains unknown in atleast 50% of cases.

Prognosis is less than desirable, with even the best results in any given study demonstrating full recovery in only 50% of nerve injury cases. Management is variable (no fixed guidelines) for sciatic nerve injury with acute surgical intervention being considered in cases where a compressing structure, such as hematoma, has been identified and in cases of acute palsy with limb lengthening of concern. Most other scenarios surgeons follow wait and watch approach.

FEMORAL NERVE

Femoral nerve palsy is the second most common nerve injury associated with THA, accounting for 2.3% of all nerve injuries. Reports suggest around 0.8% of THA patients will present with a femoral nerve injury; however, unlike the sciatic nerve, patients tend to complain of medial pelvic or more commonly anterior thigh paresthesias, and may have difficulty climbing stairs secondary to quadriceps weakness.

Anterior > Antero lateral approaches place the nerve at risk. Mechanisms of femoral nerve injury include keeping limb in prolonged hyper-extension during surgery (nerve compressed by iliopsoas and in femoral triangle) or iliacus hematomas compressing nerve especially in patients on anticoagulation. Furthermore, there is a potential for compression injuries to both the femoral and obturator nerves when hip positioners are used without adequate positioning and cushioning.

OBTURATOR NERVE

Obturator nerve palsy is a very rare and unfortunate complication of THA. Obturator nerve injuries typically present as medial thigh paresthesias, groin pain, and/or adductor weakness. Diagnosis can be difficult given its low prevalence and lack of serious functional disability. The postulated mechanism for most cases is cement extrusion from a medial wall acetabular defect. Screws in anterio inferior acetabular quadrants can also injure the nerve.

SUPERIOR GLUTEAL NERVE

The superior gluteal nerve exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic notch, exits proximal to the piriformis muscle, and then branches into superior and inferior divisions before innervating the hip abductors. Injury to the superior gluteal nerve may occur if the gluteus medius is dissected 5 cm proximal to the greater trochanter, and would present with abductor weaknesses and Trendelenburg gait. Violation of this critical area is most common during a direct lateral or anterolateral approach. Prognosis is better than that seen in sciatic nerve injuries, rather many cases recover spontaneously.

LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS NERVE OF THIGH (LFCN)

After originating from the posterior divisions of the anterior rami of the L2 and L3 nerve roots, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) runs on the anterior surface of the iliacus muscle within the iliac fascia, eventually entering the thigh medial to the anterior superior iliac spine and then passing over the sartorius muscle before branching.

The LFCN is a sensory nerve, and injury does not typically lead to functional limitations. Injury is a known complication of the anterior THA approach; however, the incidence of injury has been reported to range from 15% to 81%. Another risk factor identified with LFCN palsy is hip resurfacing, and this has been attributed to a larger incision required for the procedure.

Medico legal aspect

Rather than the complication itself, medico-legal cases resulted more so from the failure to diagnose and properly manage nerve palsies, specifically in regards to the identifying the level of the lesion, which may be considered negligent.
