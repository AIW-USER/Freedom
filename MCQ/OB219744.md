## Question

An adolescent presents to the clinic with their mother for evaluation of right knee pain. The mother states that she forgot the imaging at home, and radiographs of the knee are pending. When considering the broad differential, osteochondritis dissecans is included. What is the most common presentation for this condition?

## Options

1. 13 year-old male with a lesion on the lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle
2. 13 year-old male with a lesion on medial aspect of lateral femoral condyle
3. 13 year-old female with a lesion on the lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle
4. 18 year-old male with a lesion on lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle
5. 18 year-old female with a lesion on the lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle

## Correct option

**1. 13 year-old male with a lesion on the lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle**

## Answer explanation

For patients suffering from osteochondritis dissecans, the most likely presentation would be a male between the ages of 10 and 15 (median age: 13 years) with a lesion on the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle (60-70%; Answer 1).

Osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) is a disorder characterized by a pathological lesion that disrupts the articular cartilage and subchondral bone. The entity is most often seen in males (5-6:1) between the ages of 10 and 15 (median age: 13 years), with a predilection for the medial condyle (60-70%). While radiographs are the first-line diagnostic modality, MRI is often employed to characterize the size of the lesion and integrity of the subchondral bone. In instances of instability (displacement of lesion, subchondral fluid, expanding lesions), subchondral drilling or fixation of the lesion may be required. However, most OCDs can be treated conservatively with restricted weight bearing and rest, particularly in those who possess stable lesions and have open physes.

*Kessler et al. performed a retrospective review of over 1,000,000 patients contained in an institutional database, identifying 206 OCD lesions.* **They noted** those between ages 12 and 19 represented the majority of cases. In this age group, females were found to have an incidence of 3.9 per 100,000, while males had a rate of 18.1 per 100,000. After regression analysis, patients aged 12 to 19 accrued a 3.3-fold increase in risk for an OCD lesion compared to those 6 to 11.

*Nissen et al. performed a multicenter cross-sectional study of 1,004 knees with OCD lesions.* **They noted** that most lesions were found in males (68.9%), multisport athletes (68.1%), and patients with an average age of 13.1 years. The lesion was most commonly seen on the medial femoral condyle (66.2%). Interestingly, of those with OCDs, the majority were treated with surgical intervention (55.4%), and a significant proportion of lesions were deemed stable upon intraoperative assessment (48.1%).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-5: males are far more commonly affected, with the median age of OCDs being 13, and the most common location being seen on the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle
