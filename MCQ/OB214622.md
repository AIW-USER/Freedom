## Question

An 67-year-old very active woman has chronic pain in the right shoulder 5 years after undergoing a hemiarthroplasty for a proximal humerus fracture. On examination, she is unable to perform forward elevation beyond 85 degrees and external rotation is limited to 20 degrees. Serologic studies for infection are negative. Figures A and B demonstrate her plain radiographs. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. MRI to evaluate for a rotator cuff tear
2. Rotator cuff repair with conversion to anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty
3. Conversion to a glenohumeral fusion
4. Conversion to a reverse shoulder arthroplasty
5. Revision to cemented hemiarthroplasty

## Correct option

**4. Conversion to a reverse shoulder arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient's clinical examination of pseudoparalysis and radiographs are consistent with rotator cuff dysfunction in the setting of a prior hemiarthroplasty, which has led to superior escape of her humerus. The most appropriate next step is conversion to a reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA).

Rotator cuff arthropathy after shoulder hemiarthroplasty is characterized by bony erosion, superior migration of the humeral head, and erosion of the acromion and acromioclavicular joint. The results of total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) performed for failed hemiarthroplasty in the setting of rotator cuff disease has been disappointing in the literature, with reports of poor function, continued superior migration, and glenoid component loosening due to the rocking horse phenomenon. In these scenarios, a RSA is a better treatment option. With the increased usage of modular systems, the humeral stem may sometimes be retained with the placement of a prosthetic glenoid component.

*Dines et al. investigated the outcomes after revision TSA.* **They reported** the outcome of revision shoulder arthroplasty can be predicted on the basis of the indication for the revision, and that reconstruction of the tuberosity, hemiarthroplasty for treatment of cuff tear arthropathy, and revision due to infection had consistent poor outcomes. **They highlighted** that while component revisions (excluding humeral head revision for salvage) provided the best results, soft-tissue reconstructions yielded poorer results.

*Carroll et al. investigated the outcomes after conversion of painful hemiarthroplasty to TSA.* **They reported** unsatisfactory outcomes in the majority of patients in their series. **They concluded** that revision of a failed hemiarthroplasty to an anatomical TSA is a salvage procedure whose results are inferior to those of primary TSA.

Figures A and B demonstrate a right shoulder hemiarthroplasty with superior escape suggesting rotator cuff incompetence. Illustrations A and B demonstrate conversion of the failed shoulder hemiarthroplasty to a RSA.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: With her examination findings of pseudoparalysis and significant superior migration of the previous hemiarthroplasty component, rotator cuff pathology is implied. Thus, an MRI is not necessary.
Answer 2: Pain relief and functional outcomes after rotator cuff repair and conversion to anatomic TSA are inferior to conversion to a RSA.
Answer 3: While a glenohumeral fusion is a good salvage option, this patient still has good glenoid bone stock and conversion to a RSA would maximize her functional outcomes.
Answer 5: Revision to a cemented hemiarthroplasty would not address the rotator cuff dysfunction present and would result in continued superior escape of the humerus despite revision.
