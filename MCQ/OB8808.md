## Options

1. Dislocation of peroneus longus tendon at the level of the superior peroneal retinaculum
2. Dislocation of peroneus brevis tendon at the level of the superior peroneal retinaculum
3. Dislocation of peroneus longus and peroneus brevis tendons at the level of the superior peroneal retinaculum
4. Dislocation of peroneus longus tendon at the level of the inferior peroneal retinaculum
5. Dislocation of peroneus brevis tendon at the level of the inferior peroneal retinaculum

## Correct option

**1. Dislocation of peroneus longus tendon at the level of the superior peroneal retinaculum**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a torn superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) with dislocation of the peroneus longus tendon.

The SPR functions as the primary restraint to peroneal tendon subluxation and a secondary restraint to anterolateral ankle instability. The SPR creates a single fibro-osseous tunnel superiorly. Distally at the level of the peroneal tubercle, the inferior retinaculum forms 2 separate sheaths. SPR injury occurs from forceful ankle dorsiflexion and reflex peroneal muscle contraction e.g. when ski tips become lodged in the snow and the skier’s forward momentum causes passive ankle dorsiflexion. The most common SPR injury is a Type I injury (SPR detached from the lateral malleolus together with periosteum to which it is attached, forming a false pouch that the tendon dislocates into).

*Philbin et al. reviewed peroneal tendon injuries.* **They recommend** casting the foot in a below-knee cast in an inverted, plantarflexed position initially for 6 weeks. Nonoperative management is unlikely to be successful in chronic injuries and **they recommend** operative treatment which can include primary SPR repair, or augmented SPR repair (with bone block procedures or groove deepening).

Figures A and B show a single peroneal tendon posterior to the lateral malleolus and the 2nd peroneal tendon is dislocated lateral to the fibula. The peroneus brevis myotendinous junction, which is more distal than the peroneus longus MT junction, is visible in Figure A, implying that the PB is not subluxated. The superior peroneal retinaculum is laterally displaced from its normal attachment at the lateral margin of the lateral malleolus, remaining attached to the fibular periosteum which is stripped and elevated by the dislocated tendon. The stripped periosteum forms a false pouch into which the peroneus longus is displaced. Figures C and D (with annotations in Illustrations D and E) are fat-suppressed T2-weighted sagittal images showing the peroneus longus tendon (blue arrowheads) dislocated from its normal position posterior to the lateral malleolus. Fluid is seen along its site of origin (green arrowheads). The tendon returns to a normal course distally at the level of the peroneal tubercle where it remains restrained by the inferior peroneal retinaculum. The peroneus brevis tendon (red arrowheads) is seen in its normal course, heading to its attachment on the base of the 5th metatarsal. Illustrations A and B show the arrangement of tendons. Illustration C shows the Eckert and Davis classification of SPR injuries.

Incorrect Answers
Answers 2,3,4,5: The peroneus brevis tendon is not dislocated. The imaging shown is at the level of the SPR. Dislocation occurs at the level of the superior retinaculum.
