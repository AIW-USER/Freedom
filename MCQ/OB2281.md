## Question

Figure 1 is the radiograph of a 5-year-old male who presents to the ED with right hip pain. The patient’s parents report that he was recently diagnosed with an upper respiratory infection, but began having hip pain this morning. Initially, he was unable to walk secondary to pain, but can now ambulate with a limp. His temperature is 99.5°F, ESR 20 mm/hr, CRP 9 mg/L (normal range, 0-10mg/L). On exam, he has limited internal rotation of the right hip. What is the most likely diagnosis?

![[q2281-a-screen-shot-2020-02-03-at-9-15-25-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Septic arthritis
2. Transient Synovitis
3. Osteomyelitis
4. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis
5. Legg Clave Perthes

## Correct option

**2. Transient Synovitis**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a history of recent upper respiratory infection and now has right hip pain with minimally elevated inflammatory markers consistent with transient synovitis.

Transient synovitis is a common cause of pediatric hip pain occurring most commonly in patients between 4-8 years-old. The diagnosis of transient synovitis must be differentiated with that of septic arthritis. This distinction becomes more difficult in patients who were recently diagnosed with other ailments. The history in transient synovitis is typically that of a child that woke up with significant hip pain and was unable to ambulate but improved slightly over the course of the day and at presentation can ambulate with a limp. Laboratory analysis, specifically CRP, can be very useful as CRP > 20 mg/L is an independent risk factor for septic arthritis. The treatment of suspected transient synovitis is NSAIDs and close observation, with most symptoms resolving in <1 week.

*Del Beccaro et al. review screening laboratory tests in the diagnosis of septic arthritis and transient synovitis.* **They report** that patients with septic arthritis had a significantly higher initial temperature (>38.1°C), ESR (>44 mm/hr), and mean WBC (13,200/mm2) than those patients with transient synovitis, however, there was still significant overlap. **They conclude** that patients with an irritable hip and an elevated ESR or temperature should be considered for hip aspiration.

*Nouri et al. review transient synovitis of the hip.* **They report** that this is a benign, self-limiting condition that is likely due to a viral illness. **They conclude** that transient synovitis can be distinguished from septic arthritis by its unremarkable bloodwork (WBC, CRP, and ESR).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: CRP < 20mg/L is one of the best tests for ruling out septic arthritis. Additionally, you would expect the patient to have a higher fever and ESR if septic arthritis were present.
Answer 3: Osteomyelitis, while on the differential, is less likely in this patient as he does not have point tenderness, swelling to the painful extremity, or elevated inflammatory markers.
Answer 4: Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE) commonly occurs in children slightly older, 10-16 years of age and would be visible on the AP pelvis radiograph.
Answer 5: Legg Calve Perthes is idiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral head and would be visible on a radiograph at the time of presentation.
