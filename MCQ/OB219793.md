## Question

A 59-year-old female presents to the emergency department with three months of increased pain in the right knee with associated erythema and swelling. The patient recently underwent a right total knee arthroplasty eight months ago, and at her three-month follow-up appointment was reported to be doing well. In the emergency department, the patient has a heart rate of 98 beats per minute, blood pressure of 128/82 mmHg, and temperature of 101.2°. A physical examination of the right knee shows +2 effusion, erythema surrounding the incision and extending over the anterior aspect of the knee, and a healed surgical incision without drainage from the incision. ESR 96 mm/hr, CRP 120 mg/L, and joint aspiration yields 30 cc of purulent sanguinous fluid, 65,000 WBC/microliter, with 90% PMN. What is the organism most likely found in microscopy evaluation and what is the next best step in management?

## Options

1. Catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, gram-positive cocci; two-stage replacement arthroplasty
2. Catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, gram-positive cocci; irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange
3. Catalase-positive, coagulase-negative, novobiocin-positive, gram-positive cocci; two-stage replacement arthroplasty
4. Catalase-positive, coagulase-negative, novobiocin-positive, gram-positive cocci; irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange
5. Catalase-negative, ß-hemolytic, bacitracin-positive, gram-positive cocci; two-stage replacement arthroplasty

## Correct option

**1. Catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, gram-positive cocci; two-stage replacement arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with a chronic periprosthetic joint infection, which is most likely caused by Staphylococcus aureus (Catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, gram-positive cocci in clusters), and the gold standard in treatment is a two-stage replacement arthroplasty (Answer 1).

Periprosthetic joint infection is a challenging problem in total knee arthroplasty, occurring in 1-2% of primaries and 5-6% of revisions. Infections are characterized as either acute infection, occurring within three months of surgery, or chronic infection, occurring three months or more from surgery. The treatment of these infections primarily includes DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention), one-stage revision arthroplasty, and two-stage revision arthroplasty. The indications for DAIR are an acute periprosthetic joint infection with variable success postoperatively. Two-stage replacement arthroplasty remains the gold standard for periprosthetic joint infection occurring four weeks after the index arthroplasty procedure and involves irrigation, debridement, with component explantation and antibiotic spacer placement, which is then followed by four to six weeks of antibiotics and ultimately reimplantation. The most common organisms causing periprosthetic joint infection acutely are Staphylococcus aureus (43.8%), coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (Staph epidermidis; 17.7%), and Enterococcus (6.3%). In chronic infections, Staphylococcus aureus (35.8%), coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (21.2%), and Streptococcus (11.6%) are most common.

*Parvizi et al. performed a multi-center retrospective chart review of patients undergoing revision total joint arthroplasty to evaluate and improve diagnostic criteria of periprosthetic joint infection.* **They established** criteria for diagnosing periprosthetic joint infection including major criteria (two positive cultures of the same organism and the presence of a sinus tract with communication to the joint/visualization of the prosthesis), minor criteria (CRP >10 mg/dlL, D-Dimer >860 ng/mL, ESR >30 mm/hr, synovial WBC >3000 cell/microliter, synovial PMN >80%, synovial CRP >6.9 mg/L, synovial alpha defensin 1.0). **The authors concluded** that >6 points of minor criteria were indicative of infection, score of 2-5 indicating possible infection, and 0-1 indicating no infection with a sensitivity of 97.7%.

*Garvin et al. reviewed the prevention and management of periprosthetic joint infection in the setting of total knee arthroplasty.* *The authors described the incidence of periprosthetic knee infections to be approximately 70,000 infections annually and identified Staphylococcus aureus as the most common organism.* **The authors concluded** that the most important factor determining patient outcomes was the timing of infection relating to the index surgery with the success rate of two-stage component exchange being 80-90%.

*Rakow et al. performed a retrospective review of 106 consecutive patients diagnosed with periprosthetic joint infections from 2010 to 2018 to investigate the characteristics of periprosthetic joint infection.* **The authors identified** Staphylococcus aureus as the most common organism, which was followed by Streptococci, gram-negative bacteria, and then coagulase-negative Staphylococcus. Moreover, in the setting of infection, positive blood cultures were also found in 61% of preoperative samples. **The authors concluded** Staphylococcus aureus to be the most common organism resulting in acute periprosthetic joint infection, and for hematogenous infection to be one of the primary routes of inoculation.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 2. This answer choice correctly identifies the organism as Staphylococcus aureus, but due to the patient’s chronic infection performing an irrigation and debridement with polyethylene exchange is not indicated

Answer 3: Catalase-positive, coagulase-negative, novobiocin-positive, gram-positive cocci is consistent with Staphylococcus epidermidis, which is not the most likely organism causing this patient's infection. Two-stage replacement arthroplasty is correctly identified as the appropriate treatment for this patient.

Answer 4: Catalase-positive, coagulase-negative, novobiocin-positive, gram-positive cocci; gram-positive cocci is consistent with Staphylococcus epidermidis, which is not the most likely organism causing this patient’s infection, and as this patient has a chronic infection two-stage arthroplasty is indicated.

Answer 5: Catalase negative, ß-hemolytic, bacitracin positive, gram-positive cocci is consistent with Streptococcus pyogenes, which is not the most likely organism causing this patient's infection. Two-stage replacement arthroplasty is correctly identified as the appropriate treatment for this patient.
