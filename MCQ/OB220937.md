## Question

## Options

1. Giant cell tumor of bone; total knee arthroplasty
2. Osteosarcoma; wide resection and allograft reconstruction
3. Osteoid osteoma; anti-inflammatory medication and observation
4. Chondroblastoma; curettage and bone grafting
5. Enchondroma; observation

## Correct option

**4. Chondroblastoma; curettage and bone grafting**

## Answer explanation

A lytic lesion in the epiphysis of a skeletally immature patient is most likely a chondroblastoma. The diagnosis is confirmed with the biopsy demonstrating chicken-wire pericellular calcifications with foci of chondroid matrix. The treatment of choice for chondroblastoma is curettage and bone grafting (Answer 4).

Chondroblastoma is a benign tumor of immature chondroblasts that classically arises in the epiphysis of long bones. Roughly 80% of these tumors occur in patients under the age of 25. While benign, chondroblastoma can be locally aggressive and typically presents as progressive pain at the tumor site, stiffness, and joint effusions may be present. Metastatic disease is rare with chondroblastoma (the incidence of lung metastatic disease is <1%). The treatment of chondroblastoma is typically intralesional curettage and bone grafting. Care should be taken during curettage as damage to the physis can lead to growth disturbances in skeletally immature patients, and damage to subchondral bone and articular cartilage can lead to early arthritis. Endoprosthetic reconstruction or amputation is very rarely indicated for highly aggressive or locally destructive lesions. Overall, survival is quite good with chondroblastoma, with most patients essentially being “cured” with appropriate surgical intervention, though secondary complications such as arthritis can occur later in life.

*Farfalli et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 53 patients who underwent curettage and bone grafting for chondroblastoma.* *The mean age of patients was 18 years, and the mean follow-up time was 77 months.* Overall, 42% of patients developed local complications, with arthritis being the most common (77% of patients). Tumor recurrence occurred in 4 patients. Joint survival was 90% and 74% at 5 and 10 years, respectively. Location of the tumor in the proximal femur was a significant risk factor for poor joint survivorship. **The authors concluded** that arthritis is a common complication of the treatment of chondroblastoma, and patients should be made aware that arthroplasty at a young age is a potential outcome of treatment for proximal femur chondroblastomas.

*Arkader et al. conducted a multi-center retrospective study of 130 patients with a mean age of 14.5 years who were diagnosed with chondroblastoma.* **They found** a local recurrence rate of ~11% after treatment, but there were no patients with metastatic disease in their cohort. The authors challenged the notion that patients who present with chondroblastoma should undergo routine chest staging, as **they report** the rate of lung metastases to be extremely low (<1%).

Figure A shows a lytic lesion isolated to the epiphysis in a skeletally immature individual, concerning for chondroblastoma. Figure B shows the histology characteristics of chondroblastoma. Note the chicken-wire pericellular calcifications and foci of cartilage matrix.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While giant cell tumor of bone can involve the epiphysis, it typically begins in the metaphysis and spreads to the epiphysis. Additionally, a total knee arthroplasty is inappropriate for this 14-year-old male.
Answer 2: The biopsy is not consistent with osteosarcoma.
Answer 3: Osteoid osteomas typically arise in the diaphysis.
Answer 5: Enchondromas typically present as popcorn calcifications seen on radiographs and typically occur in the metaphysis.
