## Question

A 73-year-old man slipped in the bathroom, falling, and striking his forehead on the toilet. In the emergency room he complains of neck pain and subjective weakness of his upper extremities. Examination reveals 3/5 strength to elbow flexion, elbow extension, and grip. Examination of his lower extremity shows he is able to straight leg raise against gravity bilaterally, with 4+/5 strength to knee extension bilaterally. The patient reports prior to the fall he was active, playing doubles tennis, and had no problems with dexterity in his hands. Figure A is the sagittal MRI of the cervical spine. What is the likely pathogenesis for the patient's symptoms?

![[q213087-a-ccs2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Disruption of the anterior spinal artery
2. Chronic cord compression secondary to diffuse spondylotic changes.
3. Herniation of intervertebral disc
4. Transient cord compression with central cord edema affecting longitudinal motor tracts
5. Pathologic ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament resulting in cord compression

## Correct option

**4. Transient cord compression with central cord edema affecting longitudinal motor tracts**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with central cord syndrome (CCS) which is due to cord compression resulting in central cord edema affecting the longitudinal corticospinal tracts.

CCS is the most common incomplete spinal cord injury and is usually the result of a hyperextension injury in a patient with pre-existing cervical spondylosis. The injury event leads to cord compression with edema of the central portion of the spinal cord. This manifests as disproportionately weak upper extremities compared to the lower extremities due to the adjacency of the upper extremity long tracts to the central portion of the spinal cord. Treatment remains very controversial but new literature is favoring early surgical treatment.

*Riew et al. wrote a letter to the editor regarding operative treatment for central cord syndrome.* *The authors explained that previously nonoperative treatment was the standard of care for central cord syndrome due to old literature supporting this claim.* However, there has been increasing surgical treatment of CCS, which the authors claim to be from improved imaging modalities and surgical techniques. Further, studies suggest that early surgical treatment of CCS, within 24 hours, has been associated with greater neurological recover. **The authors state** that early surgical treatment for CCS may become the standard by surgeon consensus.

*Paquet et al. performed a prospective observational study looking at demographic and outcomes in patients presenting with cervical spinal cord injuries with and without instability.* **The authors found** that patients presenting with stable spinal cord injuries were more likely to be male, older in age, sustained an injury from a fall, have less severe neurologic impairments, less likely to undergo surgery, shorter hospital stays, and higher total motor score change. **The authors concluded** that classifying cervical spinal cord injuries without instability is more relevant given that there are a unique patient population and treatment course that classical central cord syndrome definitions have not identified.

Figure A depicts a sagittal T2 MRI of the cervical spine with diffuse degenerative changes, most notably at C3-4 and C4-5.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Disruption of the anterior spinal artery results in anterior cord syndrome and presents with complete paralysis with loss of pain and temperature sensation below the lesion while preservation of proprioception and deep touch sensation.
Answer 2: Diffuse spondylosis with chronic cord compression results in cervical myelopathy and presents with loss of balance, loss of urinary control, and increased hand clumsiness which progressively worsens over time.
Answer 3: Herniation of the intervertebral disc with cord compression can lead to cervical radiculopathy which presents as arm pain with weakness in specific myotomes.
Answer 5: Pathologic bone formation in the posterior longitudinal ligament is known as ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament and can result in myelopathic symptoms that progress over time.
