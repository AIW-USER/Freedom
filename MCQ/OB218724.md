## Question

A 14-year-old male presents to your pediatric emergency department after jumping to spike a ball at the net in a high school volleyball game. There is significant pain and crepitus over the tibial tubercle with overlying swelling and ecchymosis. He is neurovascularly intact. Radiographs demonstrate a type III tibial tubercle fracture. You place him in a long leg splint for comfort overnight and schedule him for open reduction and internal fixation the following morning. However, you get a phone call from his nurse several hours later stating his pain is increasing despite increasing analgesic use and he is becoming increasingly anxious. She is concerned about compartment syndrome. Injury to what structure is most likely responsible for these symptoms?

## Options

1. Inferior lateral geniculate artery
2. Inferior medial geniculate artery
3. Middle geniculate artery
4. Anterior tibial artery
5. Recurrent anterior tibial artery

## Correct option

**5. Recurrent anterior tibial artery**

## Answer explanation

Injury to the recurrent anterior tibial artery is typically responsible for the development of compartment syndrome in the setting of pediatric tibial tubercle fractures.

Tibial tubercle fractures most commonly occur in adolescent boys towards the end of their skeletal growth between the ages of 12-15. They are classified using the Ogden classification which helps to identify the involvement of the primary and secondary ossification centers. Usually, it is caused by excessive quadriceps contraction, either concentrically or eccentrically. Compartment syndrome occurs uncommonly, in around 4-5% of cases, and is thought to be caused by injury to the recurrent anterior tibial artery. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is the preferred method of management in most cases and rates of nonunion are low. Fasciotomies must be performed in cases where compartment syndrome is suspected and may be done prophylactically.

*Pretell-Mazzini et al. evaluated outcomes and complications of tibial tubercle fractures in the pediatric population.* **They found** that 50% were type III fractures and compartment syndrome was present in 3.57% of all cases. There was a high rate of healing after ORIF and return to to pre-injury activity level was close to 100%, despite a 28% complication rate, mostly related to the need for implant removal.

*Pandya et al. evaluated the classification and complications associated with tibial tubercle fractures.* The rate of compartment syndrome in this cohort was much higher at 10%. **They noted** that a large proportion of fractures were underestimated in severity with traditional plain radiographs and encouraged the use of preoperative CT or MRI to more appropriately evaluate intra-articular congruity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: Injury to the recurrent anterior tibial artery is usually responsible for the development of compartment syndrome in pediatric tibial tubercle fractures.
