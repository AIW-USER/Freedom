## Question

A 74-year-old female is in your clinic for her preoperative appointment for right total hip arthroplasty (THA). When explaining the implants you tell her she will receive a ceramic femoral head. A friend of hers has a THA with a metal head. In explaining why many surgeons have transitioned away from metal heads to ceramic heads you bring up the concept of corrosion. Which of the following statements is most accurate with regard to trunnionosis?

## Options

1. Head size has no relationship with corrosion
2. Likely to be accompanied by elevated serum cobalt-to-chromium ratio
3. Longer trunnions predispose to corrosion
4. Thicker trunnions predispose to corrosion
5. Unlikely to have accompanying adverse local tissue reaction

## Correct option

**2. Likely to be accompanied by elevated serum cobalt-to-chromium ratio**

## Answer explanation

Trunnionosis is a complication of metal-on-polyethylene total hip arthroplasty (THA) and is caused by metal ion release from corrosion at the femoral head-neck interface. It is characterized by elevated serum cobalt and chromium, with cobalt being more elevated than chromium (Answer 2).

Trunnionosis is wear that occurs at the femoral head-neck interface and is a source of THA failure in metal-on-polyethylene THAs. Metal ions released from this interface may lead to adverse local tissue reactions (ALTRs), osteolysis, and failure. Cobalt chrome femoral heads can release ions at the trunnion junction with the titanium stem. Treatment for this problem consists of removing the metal femoral head and replacing it with a ceramic head and titanium sleeve, thereby eliminating the offending cobalt and chromium ions entirely. Metal-on-polyethylene hip replacements with ion levels >1.0 mcg/L should raise suspicion for possible trunnion issues and warrant further workup and evaluation.

*Plummer et al. retrospectively reviewed 28 patients who underwent revision for ALTR secondary to trunnionosis.* **They found** that serum cobalt (mean 11.2 ppb) and chromium (mean 2.2 ppb) levels were elevated in all cases. They performed modular bearing exchange to a ceramic head with a titanium sleeve in 23 of 27 cases. **They concluded** that serum ion levels are elevated in cases of trunnionosis with cobalt exceeding chromium, and that retention of well-fixed components with modular bearing exchange is a successful treatment strategy.

*Mistry et al. reviewed trunnionosis in THA.* **They found** that large-diameter femoral heads were associated with greater corrosion, and that shorter trunnions increase the likelihood of edge-loading (and thereby corrosion). In their review, **they additionally found** that local soft-tissue reactions (like those found in metal-on-metal THAs) have been observed in cases of trunnionosis.

*Berstock et al. performed a systematic review on trunnionosis in which* **they found** that trunnionosis accounts for 2% of revision THAs, and that thinner and shorter trunnions contribute to mechanically assisted corrosion. Moreover, they discovered femoral head components greater than 36 mm contribute to corrosion. **They conclude** that smaller femoral head components with neutral head length and stem offset may reduce the occurrence of trunnionosis.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Larger metal heads have been associated with greater corrosion.
Answer 3 and 4: Shorter, thinner trunnions predispose to corrosion.
Answer 5: ALTRs have been found to be present in cases of trunnionosis.
