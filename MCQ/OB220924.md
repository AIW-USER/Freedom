## Question

A 29-year-old male is brought to the trauma bay as a gunshot wound victim. He has multiple ballistic fractures in his upper and lower extremities. The accompanying law enforcement report a handgun as the weapon. Upon examination, the patient is neurovascularly intact to the bilateral upper and lower extremities, all entry wounds have an accompanying exit wound, and all fractures present a similar level of minimal contamination. Assuming appropriate care is performed for his open ballistic fractures, which fracture has the highest risk of developing an infectious complication?

## Options

1. Distal radius
2. Femoral diaphysis
3. Second metacarpal shaft
4. Proximal humerus
5. Tibial shaft

## Correct option

**5. Tibial shaft**

## Answer explanation

The tibial diaphysis represents the highest-risk location for infectious complications in ballistic fractures (answer choice 5).

Ballistic fractures represent a uniquely challenging fracture pattern, characterized by high-energy transfer, extensive soft tissue disruption, and wound contamination. Tibial shaft ballistic fractures are particularly vulnerable to complications due to the tibia's subcutaneous medial border with minimal soft tissue coverage, relatively limited blood supply, and weight-bearing nature. Many factors influence the risk of infection in ballistic fractures, including time to antibiotics, time to debridement, degree of contamination, injury severity, and patient comorbidities. However, recent evidence suggests that anatomic location, specifically the tibial shaft, may be one of the highest predictors of infectious complications, even in low-energy ballistic injuries. Orthopedic surgeons should understand the unique risks that ballistic tibial shaft fractures pose to enact evidence-based treatment protocols, accurately counsel patients, and vigilantly treat these complicated fractures.

*Tornetta et al., on behalf of the FLOW (Fluid Lavage of Open Wounds) Investigators, published a prospective multicenter study to evaluate risk factors for infection in open fractures.* *The study utilized data from 2,500 patients to identify risk factors associated with infection 12 months postfracture.* **The authors found** that anatomic location, specifically tibial fractures, was a significant independent risk factor for infection. This study also found that the risk of infection was higher in lower extremity fractures than in upper extremity fractures. **The authors concluded** that tibial fractures pose a significantly higher risk of developing infection from an open fracture, and patients should be counseled appropriately.

*Kortram et al. published a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating risk factors for infectious complications after open fractures.* *A total of 116 studies were included,* **finding** that male gender, diabetes mellitus, smoking, lower extremity fracture location, Gustilo-Anderson grade III fractures, contamination, and polytrauma patients were significant risk factors for infectious complications. **The authors concluded** that further studies should elicit the risk associated with individual factors; however, the identification of these factors remains critical for appropriate care.

*Lee et al. published a multicenter retrospective study examining low-energy ballistic tibia fractures and complication rates.* **The authors found** a high complication rate (49%) in the 121 patients studied. Seventeen (14%) patients developed deep infection, and 33 (27%) developed wound complications. This study reported higher rates of infection in low-energy ballistic tibia fractures than previous studies have reported. **The authors concluded** that tibial location remains a non-modifiable risk factor for infectious complications, regardless of injury mechanism or energy level.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Studies have found that upper extremity fractures have significantly lower infection rates compared to tibial fractures. Unlike the tibia, the distal radius benefits from the coverage of multiple muscle bellies, providing a robust soft tissue envelope. Furthermore, the distal radius has improved healing potential from the metaphyseal region and a robust blood supply.
Answer choice 2: The femur, particularly the diaphysis, has a much more robust soft tissue envelope with excellent muscular coverage compared to the tibia. The soft tissue coverage provides better vascularity and protection against infection, making it less likely to develop an infectious complication than the tibia.
Answer choice 3: Metacarpal fractures fall under upper extremity injuries, which the Tornetta study showed have significantly lower infection rates than tibial fractures. Despite the hand's exposure to contamination, the robust vascularity and the predominantly cancellous bone of metacarpals provide better resistance to infection compared to tibial diaphyseal fractures.
Answer choice 4: The proximal humerus similarly benefits from a robust blood supply and soft-tissue envelope. Compared to the subcutaneous tibia, the proximal humerus is at a lower risk of developing an infectious complication.
