## Question

A 12-year-old boy presents with atraumatic right foot pain. Physical examination is remarkable for pes planus and tenderness at the sinus tarsi. He has no other musculoskeletal or developmental abnormalities, but reports that his older brother had a "foot problem" when he was around the patient's age. ## Options

1. Accessory navicular; autosomal dominant
2. Calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition; autosomal dominant
3. Talonavicular tarsal coalition; autosomal dominant
4. Calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition; X-linked recessive
5. Accessory navicular; X-linked recessive

## Correct option

**2. Calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition; autosomal dominant**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition which can be inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion. Tarsal coalitions may also be present in syndromes such as Apert syndrome or FGFR-associated craniosynostosis.

Tarsal coalitions result from failure of mesenchymal segmentation and can occur in any joint in the foot but most commonly occur in the calcaneonavicular region and the talocalcaneal joint. Patients with a calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition demonstrate the "anteater sign" on lateral radiographs and may have pain in the sinus tarsi and lateral foot/ankle. The "anteater sign" represents the elongated anterior process of calcaneus. Talar beaking can be present as well. Patients with a talocalcaneal tarsal coalition demonstrate talar beaking and/or the "c-sign" on lateral radiographs and may have medial foot/ankle pain. Talar beaking is thought to result from limited motion of the subtalar joint, while the "c-sign" represents the c-shaped arc formed by the medial outline of the talar dome and posteroinferior aspect of the sustentaculum tali. Many patients with tarsal coalitions are asymptomatic.

*Dare et al. published a review on pediatric flatfoot.* **They report** that pediatric flatfeet range from the painless and flexible normal variant of growth, to stiff or painful manifestations of tarsal coalitions, collagen abnormalities, neurologic diseases, or other underlying conditions. **They state** that children with asymptomatic rigid flatfeet may not require treatment, whereas those with pain or functional deficits may benefit from orthotics, osteotomies, or fusions.

*Bouchard et al. reviewed flatfoot deformities in children and adolescents.* **They report** that flatfeet can be subdivided into 3 types: flexible flatfoot, flexible flatfoot with a short Achilles, and rigid flatfoot. **They report** that rigid flatfoot, characterized by decreased subtalar joint motion, is most commonly associated with tarsal coalitions.

Figure A is an oblique radiograph of the right foot demonstrating the absence of the typical space between the calcaneus and navicular bones. Figure B is a lateral radiograph of the right foot demonstrating the characteristic beaking of the anterior calcaneus, commonly called the “anteater sign”. Talar beaking is noted as well. Illustrations A, B, and C are lateral radiographs of the right foot demonstrating the "anteater sign" seen in calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition, and talar beaking and the "c-sign" seen in talocalcaneal tarsal coalition, respectively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, 4, and 5: The radiographs demonstrate a calcaneonavicular tarsal coalition rather than a talocalcaneal coalition or accessory navicular. Inheritance of tarsal coalitions may occur in an autosomal dominant pattern.
