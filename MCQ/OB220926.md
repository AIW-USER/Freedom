## Question

A 38-year-old man presents after a fall from a roof and is diagnosed with a displaced joint depression-type calcaneus fracture. Computed tomography of the foot demonstrates four fracture lines through the posterior facet, marked lateral wall blowout, and a small cortical fragment displaced from the tip of the distal fibula. During operative fixation via an extensile lateral approach, what intraoperative finding is most likely to correlate with these imaging findings?

## Options

1. Intact superior peroneal retinaculum with stable peroneal tendons
2. Disrupted superior peroneal retinaculum allowing anterior subluxation of the peroneal tendons over the fibula
3. Entrapment of the flexor hallucis longus tendon beneath the sustentaculum tali
4. Lateral talus process fracture with intact peroneal tendon sheath
5. Posterior heel skin necrosis requiring intraoperative debridement

## Correct option

**2. Disrupted superior peroneal retinaculum allowing anterior subluxation of the peroneal tendons over the fibula**

## Answer explanation

A small cortical avulsion fragment from the distal fibula on CT strongly suggests injury to the superior peroneal retinaculum, which can be confirmed intraoperatively when the peroneal tendons subluxate anteriorly over the fibula during manual testing. Increasing Sanders classification type (in this case, Sanders IV) and fracture-dislocation are additional risk factors for peroneal instability (answer choice 2).

Intra-articular calcaneus fractures frequently involve lateral wall blowout and associated soft-tissue injury. A small cortical fragment adjacent to the distal fibula is characteristic of retinacular avulsion and correlates with loss of restraint of the peroneal tendons. Because preoperative CT has limited sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing peroneal instability, intraoperative testing of the superior peroneal retinaculum is recommended during fixation of displaced calcaneus fractures. Manually advancing an instrument within the peroneal sheath anterior to the fibula confirms retinacular disruption and tendon instability.

*Ketz and colleagues* **found** that intraoperative peroneal tendon instability occurred in 11.6% of operatively treated intra-articular calcaneus fractures and was strongly associated with distal fibular cortical avulsion, fracture-dislocation, and increasing Sanders classification. They described an intraoperative maneuver where a Freer elevator advanced anterior to the distal fibula indicated disruption of the superior peroneal retinaculum. **They concluded** that intraoperative testing is essential because CT imaging has limited sensitivity and produces many false positives.

*Rammelt et al. reviewed calcaneal fracture-dislocations and* **reported** that these rare, high-energy injuries were frequently associated with additional fractures, including those involving the calcaneocuboid joint, lateral talar process, and tip of the distal fibula. **They emphasized** that early recognition is critical because these injuries are often missed at initial evaluation. **They concluded** that a lateral extensile approach provides adequate visualization and reduction of all components of the injury and that outcomes were favorable when these injuries were treated promptly.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Intact retinaculum is unlikely because the cortical fragment adjacent to the fibula suggests an avulsion injury.
Answer choice 3: FHL entrapment is associated with markedly displaced posterior facet fragments, not lateral fibular avulsion.
Answer choice 4: Lateral talar process fractures may accompany fracture-dislocations but do not correlate with a distal fibular cortical avulsion.
Answer choice 5: Posterior heel skin necrosis is a soft-tissue complication of tongue-type tuberosity fractures or delayed fixation, not a finding associated with a distal fibular avulsion fragment.
