## Question

A 22-year-old male is brought to the emergency department following a gunshot injury. He presents with the injury seen in Figure A. On initial examination the patient demonstrates full motor and sensory function distally; however, the hand appears paler compared to the contralateral extremity and pulses are neither palpable nor detectable with Doppler. A CTA of the affected extremity demonstrates a brachial artery injury, and the patient undergoes surgical intervention for his vascular injury as well as external fixation of the fracture. During the application of the external fixator, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. The safe zone for percutaneous distal pin insertion is the 90-degree arc between the olecranon fossa and the lateral epicondyle
2. Distal pins may be placed percutaneously in the anterolateral aspect of the distal humerus
3. There is no safe zone and distal pins should be placed through a mini-open approach
4. Percutaneous distal pins should be placed in a direct posterior fashion
5. The safe zone for percutaneous distal pin insertion is the 90-degree arc between the lateral epicondyle and the anterior surface of the distal humerus

## Correct option

**3. There is no safe zone and distal pins should be placed through a mini-open approach**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a comminuted humeral shaft fracture with an associated injury to his brachial artery. During external fixation of the humeral shaft, the distal pins must be inserted under direct visualization through a mini-open approach to minimize injury to the radial nerve.

Humeral shaft fractures may be treated with any number of nonoperative or operative modalities, depending on the energy of mechanism, open versus closed, degree of comminution, associated neurovascular injury, body habitus, failure of conservative management, and other indications. However, the injury described in this vignette begets operative stabilization, especially given the presence of a concomitant vascular injury warranting surgical intervention. Initial external fixation is useful for temporizing the fracture in a damage control setting until definitive fixation can be performed. Typically, proximal pins are placed in the anterolateral proximal humerus and anterolateral distal humerus. While percutaneous techniques for inserting the proximal pins have been described, mini-open approaches are frequently utilized to decrease injury to the axillary nerve and biceps tendon. Percutaneous approaches for inserting the distal pins should not be employed, as this approach puts the radial nerve at high risk for injury. Therefore, a mini-open approach to placing the distal pins should always be used.

*Wegmann et al. published a 2014 anatomic study to investigate the course and variability of the radial nerve along the lateral humerus in relation to the center of rotation of the elbow joint in the context of lateral pin placement for hinged external fixation.* *The authors dissected 95 cadaveric upper extremities and measured the radial nerve using three distinct anatomic landmarks, from distal to proximal: point 1 was the radial nerve crossing the anterior aspect of the humeral shaft; point 2 was the radial nerve crossing the transverse midpoint of the humeral shaft; and point 3 was the radial nerve crossing the posterior aspect of the humeral shaft (its exit from the radial groove).* **The authors found** that the course of the radial nerve was highly variable: while the location of point 3 correlated well with the overall length of the humerus, the more distal points 1 and 2 had no correlation. These findings support the viewpoint among surgeons that there is no safe zone for pin placement at the distal humerus, and a mini-open approach with direct visualization should be utilized.

*Moses and Tejwani published a recent review on the role of external fixation in the management of upper extremity fractures.* Regarding external fixation of the humeral shaft, **the authors advocate** the usage of a unilateral external fixator, where the two distal pins are placed parallel to the elbow joint between the triceps and brachialis. Their preferred technique is to make incisions of sufficient size to safely spread down to the level of bone. The distal pins may be placed in a divergent manner to minimize the size of skin incisions. In addition, by placing the most distal pin first, one can avoid the olecranon fossa and the distal neurovascular structures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, 5: There is no established safe zone around the lateral aspect of the distal humerus for external fixator pin insertion.
Answer 4: While posterior or posterolateral placement of external fixator pins has been described, this area in the distal third is usually not accessible in polytraumatized patients, who are supine. In addition, a posteriorly inserted pin may irritate the triceps tendon and may be uncomfortable to position the arm in bed.
