## Question

A 67-year-old female sustains a right-sided, standard obliquity, intertrochanteric femur fracture after a fall from a standing height. She is evaluated by the resident on duty in the emergency department and put on the surgery schedule for open reduction and internal fixation with a sliding hip screw. Which of the following combinations of radiographic measurements portends the highest risk of fixation failure for this construct when used for this type of fracture?

## Options

1. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness of 25 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance of 20.5 mm
2. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness of 18.5 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance of 18.5 mm
3. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness of 25 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance of 30.5 mm
4. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness of 18.5 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance of 28.5 mm
5. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness and post-operative tip-apex distance only apply to short cephalomedullary nails, not sliding hip screws

## Correct option

**4. Pre-operative lateral wall thickness of 18.5 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance of 28.5 mm**

## Answer explanation

A variety of pre-operative and post-operative factors have been identified as predictors for failure of fixation following sliding hip screw (SHS) fixation of intertrochanteric hip fractures. Specifically, a pre-operative lateral wall thickness < 20.5 mm and a post-operative tip-apex distance > 25 mm have been identified as predictors of fixation failure and/or post-operative fracture following sliding hip screw fixation (Answer 4).

Intertrochanteric fractures account for approximately 50% of hip fractures, and approximately 150,000 patients in the US sustain these injuries each year. Risk factors for these injuries include increasing age, a prior fragility fracture, osteoporosis, increasing medical comorbidities, and increased dependency with ADLs. These fractures can be broadly classified as "stable" or "unstable", with stable fractures resisting medial compressive loads after adequate reduction is achieved. Unstable fractures, however, lack this stability, and as a result will collapse into varus. Examples of unstable intertrochanteric femur fractures include fractures with a comminuted posteromedial cortex, a reverse obliquity/transtrochanteric pattern, or fractures with subtrochanteric extension.

Other important considerations in the evaluation of these fractures include a thorough assessment of the integrity of the lateral wall, with the measurement being made 3 cm distal to the innominate tubercle at a 135° angle to the fracture site (Illustration A). A pre-operative lateral wall thickness < 20.5mm has been identified as a risk factor for a post-operative lateral wall fracture, and patients with this fracture morphology are best treated with a cephalomedullary nail (CMN) as opposed to a sliding hip screw. In addition to careful preoperative planning, appropriate surgical technique is of paramount importance if complications are to be minimized. Specifically, SHSs implanted with a tip-apex distance > 25mm are at higher risk for post-operative fixation failure (Illustration B).

*Hsu et al. investigated the impact that lateral wall thickness had on the development of a post-operative lateral wall fracture.* *Their study included 208 patients who had an intertrochanteric femur fracture fixed with a dynamic hip screw (DHS).* *Their study included 103 men and 150 women with a mean age of 78 years and a mean follow-up of 23 months.* Overall, **the authors found** that a pre-operative lateral wall thickness < 20.5 mm represented the threshold value below which the risk of developing a post-operative lateral wall fracture becomes unacceptably high. **The authors conclude** that patients with a pre-operative lateral wall thickness <20.5 mm should not be treated with DHS alone.

*Palm et al. also investigated the contribution that lateral wall integrity had on the development of postoperative fracture displacement.* *Their study included 214 consecutive patients with an intertrochanteric femur fracture who were treated with a 135° SHS device with a four-hole side plate.* Overall, 5 (3%) of patients with an intact lateral wall postoperatively underwent a reoperation within six months versus 10 (22%) patients with a fractured lateral wall. Multivariate analysis identified preoperative/intraoperative lateral wall compromise as a predictor for reoperation. Overall, **the authors conclude** that patients with lateral wall compromise or lateral wall fracture should not be treated with a SHS device.

*Baumgaertner et al. provide a seminal paper on the value of tip-apex distance in predicting failure of fixation of peritrochanteric fractures of the hip.* *Their study included 198 fractures in 193 patients with an average follow-up of 13 months.* Overall, **the authors found** that none of the 120 screws with a tip-apex distance of 25 mm or less cutout, but that tip-apex distance values above this threshold failed at an unacceptably high rate, with 60% of fractures with a tip-apex distance of 45 mm experiencing failure of fixation.

Illustration A is a graphical representation of how to measure lateral wall thickness. Illustration B is an illustration of how to calculate tip-apex distance. It is important to note that tip-apex distance is a summative value of the measurements on both the AP and lateral.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This represents an appropriate lateral wall thickness and tip-apex distance.
Answer 2: This represents a compromised lateral wall and an appropriate tip-apex distance.
Answer 3: This represents an appropriate lateral wall thickness but a high tip-apex distance.
Answer 5: These values are well-studied in SHS devices.
