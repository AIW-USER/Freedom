## Question

An 11-year-old African-American boy presents to your clinic with complaints of a painful left knee for the past month. He denies any recent trauma and states that it is interfering with his ability to participate in physical education at school, and it has not improved with anti-inflammatory medication. On physical exam, the knee is nontender, ligamentously stable, without an effusion, and has full range of motion. The patient ambulates with a limp but is able to bear weight on the left leg. The patient’s BMI is in the 95th percentile. X-rays of the knee are unremarkable. Which of the following is the best next step?

## Options

1. Stat infection labs (CRP, ESR, CBC)
2. MRI of the left knee
3. X-ray of the left hip
4. Physical therapy
5. Steroid injection

## Correct option

**3. X-ray of the left hip**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with atraumatic knee pain and a normal knee exam and radiograph, suggesting that this may be referred pain from the hip. This constellation of findings is concerning for a slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE), and the next best step should be to obtain radiographs of the ipsilateral hip (Answer 3).

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is a condition characterized by the physeal separation between the femoral epiphysis and the metaphysis, where the metaphyseal component commonly slips anteriorly and externally rotated relative to the epiphysis. SCFEs commonly occur in preteen males with high BMIs (obesity is the single greatest risk factor), with African-Americans, Pacific Islanders, and Latinx children at particularly high risk. SCFEs are often classified based on the ability to bear weight (able = stable; unable = unstable), with unstable SCFEs having a 2-5x higher risk of AVN compared to stable SCFEs. The severity of the slip is often classified based on the Southwick angle, with slips ranging from mild (<30 degrees) to moderate (30-50 degrees) to severe (>50 degrees). Treatment for a vast majority of SCFEs is in-situ screw fixation - this stabilizes the slipped epiphysis from slipping further and effectively facilitates the development of an epiphysiodesis of the proximal femoral physis. Prophylactic fixation of the contralateral hip is indicated in young patients (<10 years old), those with open triradiate cartilages, concomitant endocrinopathies, and obese patients. Severe slips may be initially stabilized with an in-situ screw, followed by an intertrochanteric (Imhauser) osteotomy to realign the weight-bearing surface.

*Aronsson and Karol published a 1996 JAAOS article discussing the evaluation and management of SCFE.* This classic article discussed how SCFEs were classified traditionally on the basis of the duration of symptoms, but surgeons recognized that this classification system may be misleading. It has instead been recommended that slips be classified on the basis of the presence or absence of gross instability between the epiphysis and the metaphysis. The first priority of treatment of stable chronic SCFE is to avoid the complications of avascular necrosis and chondrolysis while securing the epiphysis from further slippage. The treatment of choice for stable chronic SCFE is stabilization in situ, which can be most easily achieved with single-screw fixation.

*Sucato published a 2018 perspective article discussing the approach to SCFE by reflecting on the opinions and practices of North American surgeons.* For the stable, mild SCFE, most surgeons would agree that in situ pinning with a single screw remains the treatment of choice. For the severe, stable SCFE most surgeons would plan to perform in situ pinning with either a concomitant or delayed reconstruction through an intertrochanteric approach. For the unstable SCFE, the traditional methods of fixation following an incidental reduction are being challenged by a more formal closed reduction or the open approach to achieve a reproducible reduction while monitoring the epiphyseal blood flow.

*Swarup et al. published a 2020 meta-analysis to analyze risk factors for subsequent contralateral SCFE and identify the strongest risk factors for a subsequent slip.* *Their meta-analysis showed that younger age and higher posterior sloping angle (PSA) of the unaffected hip were predictive of a subsequent contralateral SCFE.* **They concluded** that younger patients with a high PSA of the unaffected hip would most likely benefit from prophylactic fixation of the unaffected hip.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: These labs would be obtained in the setting of a suspected intra-articular infection. This patient does not present with the typical symptoms of a septic joint.
Answer 2: An MRI of the left knee would not be useful in this patient, who presents with a painless, stable knee.
Answer 4: This patient has a high likelihood of having a SCFE; therefore physical therapy is not the correct treatment.
