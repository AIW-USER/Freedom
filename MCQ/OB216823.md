## Question

A 55-year-old male sustains a left diaphyseal humerus fracture and left tibial plateau fracture following a motorcycle accident. The patient's left arm is depicted in Figure A. Due to multiple injuries, you decide to perform open reduction internal fixation of the humerus through an anterior approach as to not cause further trauma to the posterior skin. What internervous interval is utilized during the deep dissection of this approach?

![[q216823-a-screenshot-2021-11-07-7-19-49-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Median nerve and radial nerve
2. Musculocutaneous nerve and median nerve
3. Musculocutaneous nerve and radial nerve
4. Musculocutaneous nerve and ulnar nerve
5. Radial nerve and ulnar nerve

## Correct option

**3. Musculocutaneous nerve and radial nerve**

## Answer explanation

The deep dissection of the anterior approach to the humeral diaphysis requires an intramuscular split of the brachialis, which has dual innervation from the musculocutaneous and radial nerves.

There are multiple surgical approaches to the humeral diaphysis, and knowledge of these various approaches helps plan for the unexpected. This patient has a significant road rash to the posterior skin, which could lead to wound healing complications if a posterior approach is utilized. Therefore, to approach the humerus through native skin, an anterior or anterolateral approach should be considered. The difference between the anterior and anterolateral is the anterior approach is a muscle splitting technique of the brachialis and the anterolateral is an intermuscular approach between the lateral brachialis and lateral head of the triceps. It is important to be mindful of the posterior structures, notably the radial nerve, during an anterior approach so that it does not get incarcerated in the fracture during fixation or injured with lateral humeral shaft dissection.

*Capo et al. completed an extensive review of arm anatomy and surgical exposures to the humeral shaft.* *They describe the anterior, anterolateral, posterior (both triceps sparing and splitting), and extended posterolateral approaches, and the risks associated with each.* **They concluded** that the extended posterolateral approach offered the greatest exposure to the humeral shaft (94%) and cautioned surgeons to make note of the location of the radial nerve as it crosses the plate in their operative dictations in case revision surgery is required.

*Gerwin et al. performed an anatomic study to define the course of the radial nerve in the posterior aspect of the arm.* *They dissected 10 cadaver specimens, and the radial nerve was found to cross the posterior aspect of the humerus from an average of 20.7 +/- 1.2 centimeters proximal to the medial epicondyle to 14.2 +/- 0.6 centimeters proximal to the lateral epicondyle.*

*Mahakkanukrauh et al. completed an anatomic study on 76 cadaver models to evaluate the innervation patterns of the brachialis muscle.* **They found** the brachialis muscle receives dual innervation in approximately 80% of patients from both the musculocutaneous nerve (medial fibers) and radial nerve (lateral fibers). 2 patterns of radial innervation were noted, with approximately 60% having descending branches and 40% having ascending/recurrent branches.

Figure A is a picture of an extensive road rash on the posterior arm, elbow, and forearm. Operating through compromised skin may predispose to wound breakdown and the development of infection.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: The median nerve does not innervate any muscles in the upper arm and passes medial to the brachialis muscle during an anterior approach.
Answers 4 & 5: The ulnar nerve does not innervate any muscles in the upper arm and is medial to the brachialis muscle during deep dissection of the anterior approach.
