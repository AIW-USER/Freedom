## Options

1. 28-year old male with severe unilateral leg pain for 8 months that has failed nonoperative treatment. Imaging studies are shown in Figure A.
2. 18-year old female with isolated low back pain that has failed to respond to 6 weeks of physical therapy. Imaging studies are shown in Figure B.
3. 65-year old male with bilateral buttock pain, worse with walking and improves with sitting, that has failed epidural injections. Imaging studies are shown in Figure C.
4. 22-year old male with isolated low back pain, that has failed non-operative management. Imaging studies are shown in Figure D.
5. 31-year old male with bilateral buttock pain, worse with walking and improves with sitting, that has failed epidural injections. Imaging studies are shown in Figure E

## Correct option

**3. 65-year old male with bilateral buttock pain, worse with walking and improves with sitting, that has failed epidural injections. Imaging studies are shown in Figure C.**

## Answer explanation

A single level laminectomy (without instrumentation and fusion) is most appropriate for a single level spinal stenosis. This is seen in Figure C.

Persistent spinal stenosis refractory to physical therapy and epidural injections is best addressed with decompression. Segmental instability or facetectomy (>50% of facets) will require additional instrumented fusion.

*Weinstein et al. examined surgery vs nonoperative treatment (spine patient outcomes research trial, SPORT) for patients with lumbar spinal stenosis.* **They found** that patients (without spondylolisthesis) who underwent surgery had greater improvement in pain, function, satisfaction, and self-rated progress than patients who were treated nonsurgically at two-year follow up.

*Deyo et al. examined the trends in surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis.* Decompression without fusion was most commonly performed for stenosis alone. For stenosis with spondylolisthesis or scoliosis, there were more decompressions with simple or complex fusions. From 2002 to 2007, the frequency of complex fusion procedures increased while the frequency of decompression surgery and simple fusions decreased.

Illustration A shows left L4 pars repair (Buck's technique). Illustration B shows the extent of decompression during a laminectomy.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: Figure A comprises lateral neutral (left), flexion (center) and extension (right) radiographs of L4-L5 grade I spondylolisthesis. This should be treated with laminectomy WITH instrumented fusion as a laminectomy alone will not address instability and may potentially worsen it.
Answer 2: Figure B shows spondyloptosis of L5 on S1. Spondyloptosis is treated with the Gaines technique of L5 vertebrectomy and reduction of L4 on S1. In the 1st stage, the body of L5 and L4-5, L5-S1 discs are removed (anterior approach). In the 2nd stage, the posterior arch and pedicles of L5 are resected and L4 is reduce onto S1 (with or without interbody fusion) and reduction is maintained with transpedicular L4-S1 instrumentation and posterolateral fusion.
Answer 4: Figure D shows a unilateral, spondylolysis of the left L4 pars. A laminectomy is inadequate for symptomatic spondylolysis that is refractory to nonoperative management. At L4 and above, pars repair is indicated (see Illustration A). At L5-S1, in-situ fusion is indicated.
Answer 5: Figure E comprises sagittal and axial T2-weighted MRI showing a left paracentral herniated nucleus pulposus at L5-S1. Surgical treatment comprises a discectomy.
