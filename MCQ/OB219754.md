## Question

A 42-year-old female with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis presents to the clinic for continued management of her right wrist pain. She had been previously diagnosed by her primary care doctor with "wrist arthritis" identified on plain films. She has attempted immobilization, injections, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatories without improvement. When discussing the potential surgical options, the prospect of radiolunate or radioscapholunate fusion is discussed. What examination and radiographic findings would indicate the aforementioned procedures?

## Options

1. The presence of pancarpal arthritis with an 20° arc of wrist motion
2. Isolated midcarpal arthritis with an 80° arc of wrist motion
3. Isolated midcarpal arthritis with a 20° arc of wrist motion
4. Isolated radioscaphoid arthritis with a 20° arc of wrist motion
5. Isolated radioscaphoid arthritis with an 80° arc of wrist motion

## Correct option

**5. Isolated radioscaphoid arthritis with an 80° arc of wrist motion**

## Answer explanation

This 42-year-old female with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis demonstrates right wrist arthritis recalcitrant to nonoperative treatment. The patient would be indicated for radiolunate (RL) or radioscapholunate (RSL) fusion in the presence of isolated radiocarpal arthritis with a maintained mid-carpal joint and sufficient wrist motion (Answer 5).

Wrist arthritis is a common entity, with an incidence of 10-15%. The condition can stem from many conditions, including degenerative, post-traumatic (distal radius fractures, scapholunate & scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse), congenital, idiopathic (Kienböck's, Preiser's disease), and inflammatory etiologies. Following attempts at nonoperative means, treatment is largely predicated on the degree of arthritis and wrist motion, with the two main options being fusion and/or excision of some capacity. For those with radiocarpal arthritis but with well-preserved midcarpal joints and maintained motion, radiolunate (RL) or RSL fusions are indicated, with the former procedure being the most commonly utilized. Total wrist arthrodesis is reserved for those with pancarpal (radiocarpal, intracarpal) arthritis and limited range of motion.

*Raven et al. examined their long-term results (mean 11-year follow-up) following RL (n=33) and RSL (n=13) arthrodesis in rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis patients.* While recognizing a 50% attrition rate and a 13% conversion rate to total wrist arthrodesis, of the remaining patients, the average visual analog score was two out of ten. At the same time, the mean range of motion arc in flexion and extension was 60 degrees. However, all radiographs demonstrated progression of arthritis in all intracarpal joints. **The authors conclude** that, despite the progression of arthritis radiographically, the patient can sustain long-term clinical relief with the aforementioned procedures.

McGuire and Bain review the various surgical techniques (RL, RSL, and total wrist arthrodesis) and fixation methods available for all-comers with radiocarpal arthritis. With regards to RSL arthrodesis, the authors noted that incorporating distal scaphoid pole and triquetral excisions improved postoperative range of motion significantly in the flexion-extension and radial-ulnar directions, respectively, and advocate for the adoption of this technique to maintain wrist motion.

*Fakunle et al. performed a systematic review of 13 studies (n=180) employing RL or RSL arthrodesis to manage radiocarpal arthritis recalcitrant to nonoperative means.* Both procedures significantly improved pain scores and grip strength. However, nonunion rates (15 vs. 2%) and progression to total wrist arthrodesis (4 vs 0%) were significantly higher for RSL arthrodesis compared to RL arthrodesis. Ultimately, RSL facilitated a better range of motion than RL fusions, however, at the expense of increased nonunion rates and conversion to total wrist arthrodesis.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1-3: Pancarpal or isolated midcarpal arthritis would be better treated with total wrist arthrodesis or four-corner fusion, respectively.
Answer 4: Isolated radioscaphoid arthritis with a limited degree of wrist motion has demonstrated inferior outcomes with RL/RSL arthrodesis, and would be better suited for total wrist arthrodesis.
