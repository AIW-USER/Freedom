## Question

A 19-year-old male presents to the emergency room after a motor vehicle accident that occurred when driving home from a late-night party. In the hospital gurney his left leg is in a neutral but foreshortened position. Imaging demonstrates a hip dislocation. Multiple failed attempts are made at reducing the hip under adequate sedation, but he is ultimately brought to the operating room for closed versus possible open reduction under general anesthesia. Which radiographic features seen on the patient's initial presenting films should caution the provider that the patient may have an irreducible variant hip dislocation?

## Options

1. Absence of intra-articular fracture fragments
2. Cranially displaced femoral head with an externally rotated femur
3. Decreased visualization of the lesser trochanter
4. Disruption of Shenton's line
5. Femoral head that is medial or inferior to the acetabulum

## Correct option

**2. Cranially displaced femoral head with an externally rotated femur**

## Answer explanation

A cranially displaced femoral head that is closely apposed to the posterior ilium with a femur that has a degree of external rotation, as evidenced by a more prominent lesser trochanter, should caution the provider of the presence of an irreducible hip dislocation.

Hip dislocations represent high-energy injuries that have a variable presentation. In a typical posterior hip dislocation (90% of all hip dislocations) the patient presents with a flexed, adducted, and internally rotated extremity. Anterior dislocations, on the other hand, can be either anteroinferior ("obturator") or anterosuperior ("pubic") in nature, and most often occur with the hip in abduction and external rotation. Both types of dislocations can be successfully closed reduced at the bedside with adequate sedation; however, certain characteristics portend a lower likelihood of a successful closed reduction. A straight cranial rather than posterosuperior positioning of the femoral head on radiographs often indicates that the head is closely apposed to and will be blocked by the ilium. This positioning is also often associated with femoral head fractures, the loose fragments of which remain in the acetabulum and can block a closed reduction (Illustration A). Additionally, irreducible hip dislocations tend to exhibit an overly externally rotated femur, which will be evidenced by greater visualization of the lesser trochanter profile. Accordingly, the clinical presentation of this subset of dislocations can also be more benign, as there is not overt shortening, flexion, or internal rotation of the extremity, which may appear only mildly foreshortened and in a neutral position, as indicated in the question stem.

Mehta, et al. published on irreducible fracture-dislocations of the femoral head without posterior wall acetabular fractures. The authors identified 7 irreducible hip dislocations out of 72 patients over a 6-year period. They note that this subset of pathology is heralded by unique clinical and radiographic features that include neutral limb alignment instead of obvious shortening and internal rotation, as well as the close radiographic approximation of the dislocated proximal femoral component relative to the acetabulum with the lack of an acetabular fracture on imaging. The authors concluded that urgent open reduction and internal fixation for such injuries should be undertaken without closed manipulative reduction attempts in order to avoid iatrogenic femoral neck fractures.

Scolaro, et al. provide a review of the management and radiographic outcomes of femoral head fractures. One hundred and forty-seven fractures were reviewed with 53.1% receiving ORIF, 25.1% being treated with fragment excision alone, and 19% receiving non-operative treatment. Of the fractures that were fixed, 89.9% proceeded to uneventful union and 40.6% developed HO. The authors concluded that non-bridging, asymptomatic
HO is common, but that AVN and post-traumatic degenerative disease of the hip occur but are uncommon.

Illustration A and B are radiographs demonstrating the characteristic features of an irreducible hip fracture-dislocation: cranial displacement, femoral external rotation with increased visualization of the lesser trochanter profile, and presence of a large intra-articular femoral head fracture fragment with an intact acetabulum.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The presence of intra-articular fragments, not the absence, is more likely to indicate an irreducible hip dislocation.
Answer 3: Increased, not decreased, visualization of the lesser trochanter indicating increased femoral external rotation is more likely to indicate an irreducible hip fracture.
Answer 4: Disruption of Shenton's line, which represents the arc along the inferior femoral neck that is contiguous with the superior aspect of the obturator foramen, is an aspect of all hip dislocations, not just irreducible variants.
Answer 5: A femoral head that is medial or inferior to the acetabulum indicates an anterior variant hip dislocation, which does not necessarily make it irreducible.
