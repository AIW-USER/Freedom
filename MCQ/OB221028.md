## Question

A 35-year-old woman sustains a high-energy ankle injury in a motor vehicle collision, with radiographs showing a pantalar dislocation without fracture. On physical exam, the skin is closed, the ankle is grossly swollen, and distal pulses and neurologic function are intact. Closed reduction attempts are unsuccessful, and the patient is taken urgently for operative intervention. What is the recommended acute operative intervention?

## Options

1. Primary talectomy with tibiocalcaneal fusion
2. Primary talectomy with total talus replacement
3. Percutaneous or open talar reduction with primary tibiotalar arthrodesis
4. Percutaneous or open talar reduction with primary subtalar arthrodesis
5. Percutaneous or open talar reduction with soft tissue repair

## Correct option

**5. Percutaneous or open talar reduction with soft tissue repair**

## Answer explanation

In order to protect the soft-tissue envelope and best preserve talar blood supply, failed closed reduction attempts should be followed promptly by percutaneous or open reduction of the talus with temporizing fixation to maintain the reduction (answer choice 5).

A total talus—or pantalar—dislocation is defined by the talus being completely dislocated from the tibiotalar, subtalar, and talonavicular joints and is typically the result of very high-energy injuries. In closed injuries, initial management is a trial of closed reduction, but operative reduction is often required, as anatomic blocks to reduction are common. Open injuries are treated with urgent irrigation and debridement and operative reduction. After reduction, temporary transarticular pins or a spanning external fixator are typically utilized to maintain alignment while soft tissues recover. There were historical recommendations for primary talectomy in these cases due to reported high risks of complications; however, more modern series have supported the role of reduction and stabilization, with reservation of talectomy and other salvage procedures to address complications as they arise.

*Githens et al. published a narrative review regarding current concepts in the surgical management of talus fractures,* **emphasizing** the challenges posed by complex talar morphology, extensive articular cartilage coverage, soft-tissue constraints, and a tenuous blood supply. **They reported** that temporizing measures may include debridement of open fractures, emergent reduction of dislocations, and spanning external fixation. In regard to extruded talus injuries, **they noted** that these rare high-energy injuries were historically treated with acute talectomy and fusion, but that more recent literature supports sterilization, reimplantation, and stabilization of the extruded talus. **They concluded** that definitive treatment should be guided by fracture pattern and soft-tissue condition, with restoration of anatomic alignment and stable fixation as key goals.

*Boden et al. performed a retrospective review of 19 patients with pantalar dislocation without talar fracture, including 14 open and 5 closed injuries, to evaluate complications and functional outcomes.* **They reported** no deep wound infections after urgent debridement and talar reimplantation but found osteonecrosis in 88% of patients with adequate radiographic follow-up, collapse in 2 patients, and posttraumatic arthrosis in 44%, with persistent pain and functional limitations common on patient-reported outcome surveys. **They concluded** that urgent debridement and reimplantation were not associated with deep infection, that osteonecrosis was frequent but collapse was uncommon, and that long-term pain and functional impairment are common after pantalar dislocation.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choices 1 & 2: Primary talectomy with tibiocalcaneal fusion or total talus replacement represent possible salvage options, particularly in cases of talar osteonecrosis; however, reduction (or reimplantation in cases of open injury) is supported in the modern literature.
Answer choices 3 & 4: Tibiotalar or subtalar arthrodesis may be indicated in the future should this patient develop post-traumatic arthritis at these joints; however, fixation is recommended for acute stabilization following reduction.
