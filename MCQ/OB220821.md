## Question

A 45-year-old male presents to your clinic 10 years after a left knee dislocation following a skiing accident. He was managed with multiple reconstructive surgeries to his knee, but ever since the accident, he has had difficulty walking. He feels that his foot slaps every time he takes a step, and he needs to wear a brace to ambulate effectively. He has been researching tendon transfers and asks if he would be a candidate. Which of the following is a contraindication to tendon transfers if present in this patient?

## Options

1. 0/5 tibialis anterior strength
2. History of attempted common peroneal nerve reconstruction
3. A rigid foot deformity with inability to passively dorsiflex the ankle past neutral
4. Left sided L4 radiculopathy
5. History of a gastrocnemius contracture which improved with physical therapy

## Correct option

**3. A rigid foot deformity with inability to passively dorsiflex the ankle past neutral**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with a foot drop from a knee injury many years ago. Tendon transfer procedures (eg, transferring the posterior tibial tendon to the dorsum of the foot) can often help restore enough dorsiflexion of the ankle to allow patients to ambulate without braces. However, in cases of longstanding foot drop, a rigid deformity can develop, which cannot be corrected with soft tissue procedures alone (Answer 3).

A foot drop can occur due to a variety of etiologies. Trauma, such as tibial plateau fractures or knee dislocations, can lead to peroneal nerve injury, which can manifest as an inability to dorsiflex the ankle actively. Other causes include longstanding lumbar spine pathology or iatrogenic from prior surgery. Patients typically present with significant weakness in ankle dorsiflexion. Many patients can be managed with physical therapy and ankle bracing to improve gait and prevent contractures. In patients who do not tolerate bracing, surgery can be considered. Tendon transfer procedures aim to restore anti-gravity ankle dorsiflexion, enabling patients to ambulate without bracing. Tendon transfers can be considered in patients with flexible ankles who do not have significant arthritis or contractures. If a gastrocnemius contracture is present, this should be corrected with stretching before tendon transfer. Ankle inversion strength testing should also be checked, as significant weakness in the posterior tibial tendon would limit the success of tendon transfers. Patients with longstanding foot drop may also develop rigid foot deformities, and once this develops, tendon transfers alone are typically not advised. In these patients, bony reconstructive procedures such as osteotomies and arthrodesis are typically utilized.

*Dwivedi et al. reviewed the diagnosis and treatment of foot drop.* **They highlight** the mechanical considerations when planning tendon transfers for correction of foot drop, as well as review strategies to appropriately balance the muscular forces on the foot and ankle after tendon transfer.

*McCormick et al. similarly reviewed foot drop.* **The authors highlight** outcomes following tendon transfer procedures and strategies to manage rigid foot drop deformities. They also provide a treatment algorithm when managing patients with foot drop.

*Richardson & Gause authored a surgical technique article describing their strategy for performing the Bridle tendon transfer procedure for managing foot drop.* **They highlight** the advantages, disadvantages, and technical considerations when performing this procedure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Significant tibialis anterior weakness is expected with a foot drop and is an indication to proceed with tendon transfers.
Answer 2: This is not a contraindication to proceed with tendon transfers.
Answer 4: A left-sided L4 radiculopathy would likely worsen a foot drop, but is not a contraindication to proceed with posterior tibial tendon transfer. Significant weakness with plantarflexion (S1 radiculopathy) would likely be a contraindication to tendon transfer.
Answer 5: A gastrocnemius contracture that has improved with therapy is no longer a contraindication to proceed with tendon transfers.
