## Question

A 68-year-old male patient presents with back pain and inability to walk more than 1 city block distance. He underwent a previous L3-S1 posterior instrumented fusion and decompression for multilevel spinal stenosis with associated degenerative spondylolisthesis. Current standing with full hip and knee extension radiographs are shown in figure A. His lumbar lordosis is measured at 22°, pelvic incidence of 55°, and thoracic kyphosis of 35°. What would be the best approach to addressing this patient's spinal deformity?

![[q214133-a-asd-preop-edit.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Pedicle subtraction osteotomy at L1
2. Smith-Peterson osteotomies at L1-3
3. Pedicle Subtraction osteotomy at L3
4. Vertebral column resection at L2
5. Smith-Peterson osteotomies at T5-9

## Correct option

**3. Pedicle Subtraction osteotomy at L3**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sagittal plane malalignment from lumbar hypolordosis, which would be best addressed with a pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) at L3 to provide ~30° of increased lumbar lordosis.

Adult spinal deformity is commonly defined as coronal and sagittal imbalance. Studies have shown that patients with sagittal imbalance >5+ cm have significantly decreased patient-reported outcome measures compared to patients with a sagittal vertical axis <5+ cm. This can commonly occur due to secondary degeneration from lumbar spine surgery and may require osteotomies to obtain harmonious sagittal plane alignment. Pedicle subtraction osteotomies are wedge-shaped osteotomies that remove the pedicle and posterior elements and can achieve up to 30° of sagittal plane correction. Ideally, this would be performed in the lower lumbar spine, such as L3 or L4, as the apex of lumbar lordosis is at L4 and 70% of lumbar lordosis is from L4-S1.

*Cogniet et al. performed a retrospective study of 63 patients undergoing PSO for global spinal malalignment.* **The authors reported** a significant improvement in sagittal alignment with correction of the sagittal vertical axis from +9 cm preoperatively to +4.3 cm postoperatively. **The authors concluded** that PSO is very effective for patients with a pelvic incidence less than 60°, and patients with a pelvic incidence greater than 60° may require complementary osteotomies.

*Glassman et al. performed a retrospective review of 352 patients with adult spinal deformities that were evaluated for a relationship between spinopelvic parameters and adverse health outcomes.* **The authors reported** that there was a significant correlation between increasing C7 plumb line deviation and SF-12 physical composite score, SRS-29 pain domain, SRS-29 activity domain, SRS-29 total, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores. Further, **they found** that ODI scores worsened when the region of maximal kyphosis progressed from thoracic to lumbar. **The authors concluded** that patient-reported outcome scores were inversely related to the degree of C7 plumb line deviation.

Figure A is an AP and lateral standing radiograph of the spine with previous posterior instrumented fusion at L3-S1 with sagittal plane malalignment and decreased lumbar lordosis. Illustration A is a diagram depicting the cutting planes of a PSO.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The apex of lumbar lordosis is at L3-4. Performing a PSO at L1 would result in non-anatomical lumbar lordosis.
Answer 2: Smith-Peterson osteotomies at L1-3 would result in approximately 30° of lordosis correction, but would be localized above the natural apex of lumbar lordosis.
Answer 4: Vertebral column resection is a technically challenging procedure with significant complication rates, and should be reserved for patients requiring >45° of sagittal plane correction for sharp angular deformities.
Answer 5: Smith-Peterson osteotomies in the thoracic spine would decrease the patient's thoracic kyphosis, which is currently within normal parameters, and would not address the lumbar hypolordosis.
