## Question

A 32-year-old orthopaedic resident sustains a right arm injury when participating in his residency's annual arm wrestling competition. Radiographs are obtained and shown in Figure A. When performing a posterior approach to the humerus, which approach provides the greatest amount of exposure to the humerus?

![[q219758-a-distal-third-humerus.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral reflection of both the lateral and medial heads of the triceps
2. Medial reflection of both the lateral and medial heads of the triceps
3. Standard triceps-splitting approach
4. Triceps-splitting approach with mobilization of the radial nerve
5. The interval between brachialis and brachioradialis

## Correct option

**2. Medial reflection of both the lateral and medial heads of the triceps**

## Answer explanation

The medial reflection of the triceps' lateral and medial heads provides the greatest amount of exposure to the distal humerus.

When performing operative fixation of humerus shaft fractures, a surgeon has multiple approaches at his or her disposal. The deltopectoral approach with distal extension into the anterolateral approach to the humerus is used for more proximal fractures or when anterior fixation may be indicated. Posterior approaches are often used for more distal fractures as it allows better access to the condyles. Modifying the posterior approach with medial reflection (i.e., elevating the lateral aspect of the triceps and reflecting the muscle bellies medially) achieves the greatest exposure for the answer choices provided. With all posterior approaches, proximal exposure requires mobilizing and retracting the radial nerve. Of note, all posterior exposures are limited proximally by the axillary nerve.

*Gerwin et al. performed a cadaveric study to define the course of the radial nerve in the posterior aspect of the arm in relation to the humeral diaphysis.* **They found** the radial nerve crossed the posterior aspect of the humerus 20.7 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle and 14.2 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle. With approaches to the humerus, **they found** a standard posterior triceps-splitting approach exposed 15.4 cm of the humerus to the level of the radial nerve, and mobilization of the radial nerve achieved an additional 6 cm of the humerus. With a modified posterior approach with medial reflection of both the lateral and medial heads of the triceps, 26.2 cm of humeral exposure was achieved, with proximal exposure limited by the axillary nerve.

*Seigerman et al. performed a cadaveric study on 30 extremities.* **They identified** the relation of the radial nerve as being two finger breadths (or 39.0 mm) proximal to the distal aponeurosis of the long and lateral heads of the triceps. **They concluded** that this could be a visual reference point in finding the radial nerve when performing a posterior approach to the humerus.

Figure A is a plain radiograph demonstrating an extra-articular distal third humerus shaft fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: this is not a typical variant of the posterior approach to the humerus.
Answers 3 and 4: None of these posterior approach variants provides as much exposure as a medial reflection of the triceps heads.
Answer 5: This is the anterolateral approach to the humerus (not the posterior approach).
