## Question

A 65-year-old woman presents to an orthopedic adult reconstruction clinic with a 3-month history of worsening left hip pain and lower extremity weakness despite activity modification and oral NSAIDs. She reports left hip and lower back pain that has bothered her for several years; however, over the past 3 months it has become more difficult to walk. In the clinic, she walks with a wide-based gait. She has pain in her mid and lower back with palpation and has pain with flexion and internal rotation of the hip that is focused in the groin. She is hyperreflexic in her bilateral lower extremity reflexes with notable clonus. She denies any change in her upper extremity findings and has a benign cervical spine exam. An AP pelvis plain film taken in the clinic is shown in Figure A. What is the most appropriate treatment recommendation?

## Options

1. Left total hip arthroplasty
2. Left hip intraarticular corticosteroid injection
3. Selective nerve root corticosteroid injection
4. 6 weeks of physical therapy with repeat clinical exam
5. Thoracic and lumbar spine MRI with referral to a spine surgeon

## Correct option

**5. Thoracic and lumbar spine MRI with referral to a spine surgeon**

## Answer explanation

Patients often present with concomitant hip and lower back pain. Those who present with clinical signs and symptoms of thoracolumbar myelopathy should undergo spine evaluation before total hip arthroplasty.

Approximately half of patients with end-stage arthritis who undergo total hip arthroplasty report some level of lower back pain. The degenerative nature of both hip arthritis and spondylosis helps explain why these two clinical presentations frequently coexist. Appropriate management of combined lower back and hip pain requires a focused history and physical exam. If a patient has a history, exam, and radiographic findings supporting end-stage arthritis and no radicular or myelopathic symptoms, then hip arthritis should be addressed first. Patients who present with myelopathy (wide-based gait, hyperreflexia, bowel or bladder changes) should first undergo spinal treatment. The management of patients with hip arthritis and isolated radicular symptoms is slightly more controversial; however, patients with radicular symptoms would ideally undergo spine evaluation before hip arthroplasty.

*Liu et al presented the findings from asking 51 hip surgeons and 37 spine surgeons the recommended treatment course in 5 different clinical scenarios in which patients present with combined hip osteoarthritis and lumbar spondylosis.* The 5 clinical scenarios were hip arthritis and lumbar spondylosis in combination with 1) neurogenic claudication, 2) leg pain, 3) leg weakness, 4) scoliosis, and 5) myelopathy. Patients in scenario 5 (combination of myelopathy and hip arthritis) had the most consistent percentage of surgeons recommending spine surgery first (hip surgeons 86%, spine surgeons 97%).

*Parvizi et al published a cross-sectional study identifying the prevalence of lower back pain in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (n=344, 49.4%).* *They then followed these patients post-operatively for 1-year.* Preoperative lower back pain resolved in 2/3 of the patients. Pain radiating below the knee was most closely associated with preexisting spinal disease. **They suggest** that lower back pain with known spinal disorders is not an absolute contraindication to hip arthroplasty.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1.) Patients with recently progressive myelopathy should not undergo hip arthroplasty without first evaluating their spine.
Answer 2.) A hip corticosteroid injection will not address her myelopathy.
Answer 3.) Nerve root corticosteroid injection is not indicated in the management of myelopathy.
Answer 4.) The patient has recently progressed myelopathic symptoms and should undergo spinal evaluation. Therapy is not the first-line treatment for myelopathy.
