## Question

## Answer explanation

Figure A demonstrates an AP radiograph of a revision THA with an allograft cortical strut (medially) and evidence of extensive structural defect of the lateral femoral cortex, severe proximal bone loss, and a mechanically loose prosthesis. Of the Figures provided, this preoperative radiograph would be most indicated for a proximal femur replacement (PFR).

The Paprosky classification is used as an objective assessment of bone loss in order to provide reconstruction options during revision total hip arthroplasty (THA). The classification is based on the location of femoral bone loss (metaphyseal or diaphyseal), degree of remaining support of the proximal femur (i.e., degree of cancellous bone loss), and the amount of isthmus remaining for diaphyseal fixation. Patients with Type IV defects have a complete loss of the femoral isthmus wherein biologic fixation is unlikely. Type IV femoral defects are the most challenging subtype because there is no intact isthmus to provide adequate distal fixation of the component. For this reason, the treatment options include an allograft prosthetic composite (APC), reconstruction of the femoral canal with impaction grafting and insertion of a cemented stem, or using a tumor megaprosthesis to replace the defective proximal femur.

*Parvizi et al. performed a retrospective review on the outcomes of reconstructive surgery with the use of a modular PFR in 43 hips with proximal femoral bone loss due to non-neoplastic conditions.* **They reported** that there was a significant improvement in function as measured with the Harris hip score and that PFR achieved an excellent or good functional outcome in 22 patients. **They concluded** that patients with severely compromised bone stock in whom the use of a conventional prosthesis is precluded because of an inability to achieve adequate fixation may be candidates for proximal femoral replacement.

*Savvidou et al. provide a review of the technical pearls of PFR.* **They note** that despite recent advances in device manufacturing and surgical techniques, the management of proximal femoral bone loss in revision total hip arthroplasty remains challenging. **They further discuss** that the new generation of modular proximal femoral replacement megaprostheses and the increased experience obtained with these surgeries have reduced complication rates and improved outcomes.

*Viste et al. performed a retrospective review of 44 patients with bone loss classified as Paprosky IIIB or IV who underwent revision THA using a PFR.* **They reported** improvement in Harris Hip Score (42.8 to 68.5) and survivorship free of any revision or removal of an implant of 86% at five years and 66% years at ten years. **They also reported** that 12 patients (27%) had a complication including six with a dislocation. **They concluded** that PFRs provide a useful salvage option for patients, particularly the elderly with massive proximal femoral bone loss who require revision THA.

Figures B, C and E demonstrate a Paprosky type II femoral bone defect. Figure D demonstrates a Paprosky type IIIA defect.

Illustration A is the postoperative radiograph of the patient in Figure A after undergoing a proximal femur replacement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2, 3 and 5: Paprosky type II defects can be managed with an uncemented, long femoral stem (diaphyseal-engaging).
Answer 4: Paprosky type IIIA femoral defects can be managed with a diaphyseal-engaging femoral stem (porous-coated/grit blasted) or a modular tapered stem with or without splines (i.e., circumferential fluted projections around the tapered stem that confer rotational stability of the implant).
