## Question

A patient presents with a 'Swan-neck' deformity. During clinical examination, you find that PIP flexion is significantly more restricted when the MCP joint is extended compared to when the MCP is flexed. What does this 'Bunnell-Littler test' result indicate?

## Options

1. Joint capsule contracture
2. Volar plate laxity
3. Intrinsic muscle tightness
4. Extrinsic extensor tendon adhesions

## Correct option

**3. Intrinsic muscle tightness**

## Answer explanation

When MCP extension limits PIP flexion, it confirms that the intrinsic muscles (interossei and lumbricals) are too tight to allow the lateral bands to slide properly.

The Swan-Neck deformity is characterized by a "double-curve" posture: hyperextension of the Proximal Interphalangeal (PIP) joint and flexion of the Distal Interphalangeal (DIP) joint. In the rheumatoid hand, it represents a profound imbalance between the extrinsic and intrinsic muscle forces.

Pathoanatomy & Pathogenesis

The deformity is a cascade of events that can begin at the PIP joint, the MCP joint, or the DIP joint.

The Primary "Trigger"

 - Volar Plate Laxity: Chronic synovitis of the PIP joint weakens the volar plate (the primary restraint to hyperextension).

 - FDS Rupture/Elongation: The Flexor Digitorum Superficialis (FDS) normally acts as a dynamic stabilizer against hyperextension. Its failure removes this "check-rein."

The Cascade

 - Dorsal Subluxation of Lateral Bands: As the PIP joint hyperextends, the lateral bands of the extensor mechanism shift dorsally (above the axis of rotation).

 - Increased Lever Arm: This dorsal shift increases the extensor power at the PIP, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of hyperextension.

 - The Tenodesis Effect: Because the extensor mechanism is "used up" at the hyperextended PIP joint, there is relative slack at the DIP joint. Combined with the pull of the Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FDP), the DIP joint is pulled into flexion.

Classification (Nalebuff System)

Surgical management is strictly dictated by the Nalebuff stages, which assess joint flexibility and the state of the articular surface.

 Stage

 Clinical Findings

 Surgical Principle

 Type I

 PIP joint is flexible in all positions.

 Restore the "Check-rein" (Soft tissue).

 Type II

 PIP flexion is limited when MCP is extended (Positive Bunnell-Littler test).

 Address Intrinsic Tightness.

 Type III

 PIP flexion is limited regardless of MCP position, but joint space is preserved.

 Mobilize the joint and extensors.

 Type IV

 Stiff PIP joint with radiographic destruction (Arthritis).

 Joint sacrifice (Arthrodesis/Arthroplasty).

Surgical Management

Type I (Flexible)

 - Dermodesis: Removing an elliptical wedge of skin from the volar aspect of the PIP joint to create a "skin tether."

 - FDS Tenodesis: Taking one slip of the FDS tendon, leaving it attached distally, and anchoring it to the proximal phalanx (A1/A2 pulley area) to create a permanent check against hyperextension.

 - Brooks-Graner Procedure: A spiral oblique retinacular ligament (SORL) reconstruction using a tendon graft to provide dynamic stability to both PIP and DIP.

Type II (Intrinsic Tightness)

 - Intrinsic Release (Littler Procedure): Resection of the triangular aponeurosis (the oblique fibers of the intrinsics) on the radial and ulnar sides. This allows the lateral bands to relax and the PIP joint to flex even when the MCP is extended.

Type III (Stiff Joint, Preserved Surface)

 - Manipulation under Anesthesia (MUA): Gentle mobilization.

 - Lateral Band Mobilization:
 Surgically freeing the dorsally displaced lateral bands and relocating them volarly.

 - Skin Release: Often, the dorsal skin is too tight and requires a "Z-plasty" to allow flexion.

Type IV (Destroyed Joint)

 - Arthrodesis: Fusing the PIP joint in a functional position (approx. 40°–50° for the ulnar digits, less for the index). This is the most predictable treatment for pain and stability.

 - Silastic Arthroplasty: Replacing the joint with a silicone spacer. Preferred in the ring and small fingers where motion is prioritized over heavy pinch strength.

The "Thumb" Swan-Neck (Nalebuff Type III)

In the thumb, the Swan-neck deformity is unique. It starts with CMC joint subluxation/adduction. The patient compensates by hyperextending the MCP joint to achieve a pinch.

Management:the "foundation" is addressed first by performing a CMC arthroplasty (LRTI) and then stabilizing the MCP joint (either via capsulodesis or fusion).

Answer: 3
