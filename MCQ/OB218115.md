## Question

A 21-year-old collegiate baseball pitcher has developed medial elbow pain over the past several months that is worse in the layback throwing position despite conservative treatment. He presents to your office with an MRI shown in Figure A. He is indicated for surgical management. What is the management of the most likely complication following surgery?

![[q218115-a-ucl-tear.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Observation
2. Surgical excision
3. Antibiotics
4. Open reduction internal fixation
5. Revision ligament reconstruction

## Correct option

**1. Observation**

## Answer explanation

The most common complication following surgery for an ulnar collateral ligament tear is ulnar neuropraxia which frequently resolves spontaneously.

Elbow ulnar collateral ligament injuries occur due to valgus stress on the anterior band of the UCL during the late cocking (lay-back position) and early acceleration phase of throwing. The patient generally complains of decreased throwing performance, medial elbow pain, and sometimes feels a pop with acute injuries. Physical exam findings consistent with UCL injuries include medial elbow tenderness, along with provocative tests including positive milking maneuver, valgus stress test, and moving valgus stress test. Radiographs are helpful to assess for associated posterior medial osteophytes, calcifications, or loose bodies, however, MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis. Nonoperative treatment is indicated for most which includes rest, physical therapy, addressing kinetic chain deficiencies, and a return to throwing program. For patients who fail nonoperative treatment and competitive overhead athletes who want to continue playing, operative treatment is indicated. Surgical options include UCL reconstruction, repair, and hybrid repair-reconstruction techniques. The most common complication is ulnar neuropraxia which can occur in up to 26% of patients and the majority resolve within a few weeks to months with observation. Surgical modifications such as muscle splitting approaches, more superior exiting proximal humeral tunnels, and selective ulnar nerve transposition have been proposed to reduce the risk of ulnar neuropraxia. Other complications include elbow stiffness, fracture of the medial epicondyle or ulna, inability to return to performance, and infection.

Bruce and Andrews provide a comprehensive review of UCL injuries. The UCL primarily restrains valgus stress and has three main components: the oblique, posterior, and anterior bundles. The anterior bundle is crucial for valgus restraint during the pitching motion. It attaches to the anteroinferior edge of the medial humeral epicondyle, and its dimensions and characteristics have been well-documented. Diagnosing UCL injuries involves a thorough patient history and physical examination, including stability tests such as the valgus stress test and milking maneuver. Imaging includes radiographs (static and dynamic) and MRI. Prevention of UCL injuries is a challenge, with recommendations for pitch counts, rest periods, and limiting throwing at a young age. Nonsurgical approaches involve rest, therapy, and rehabilitation. Surgical techniques like UCL reconstruction have evolved over time, with various methods described, such as the American Sports Medicine Institute technique, the docking technique, and the DANE TJ procedure. Complications include transient ulnar nerve neurapraxia, avulsion fractures, stiffness, and heterotopic ossification.

*Cain et al analyzed a case series of more than 1,200 patients with the vast majority (>98%) undergoing a UCL reconstruction using a modified Jobe technique.* **They found** that 83% of players were able to return to the previous level of competition or higher. **They also noted** that on average players started throwing at 4.4 months and returned to full competition at 11.6 months. Lastly, **they found** that there was a 20% complication rate with the most being minor and ulnar neuropraxia being the most common.
