## Question

A 60-year-old female presents to your office for evaluation of her left shoulder. An MRI has been performed which shows a small full-thickness rotator cuff tear of the supraspinatus tendon. She has been treated by her local primary care sports medicine physician with repeat corticosteroid injections into the shoulder. She states that she has had approximately 6 injections in total with the most recent being one week ago. She reports that they are no longer effective and would like to discuss rotator cuff surgery. How should this patient be counseled if surgery was to proceed 3 months from now?

## Options

1. She is not an increased risk of infection nor need for revision surgery
2. She is at increased risk of infection but not revision surgery
3. She is at increased of revision surgery but not infection
4. She is at increased risk of both infection and revision surgery
5. She is not a surgical candidate and should continue with repeat injections

## Correct option

**3. She is at increased of revision surgery but not infection**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a rotator cuff tear and is treated initially with multiple corticosteroid injections. She is at elevated risk of revision due to the numerous injections she has received; however, she is not at elevated risk of infection given surgery will take place 3 months after her last injection.

Rotator cuff tears are a very common source of shoulder pain and decreased motion that can occur due to both traumatic injuries in young patients as well as degenerative disease in the elderly patient. Diagnosis can be suspected clinically with provocative tests of the supraspinatous, infraspinatous, teres minor and subscapularis, but confirmation requires an MRI of the shoulder. Treatment can be nonoperative or operative depending on the chronicity of symptoms, severity of the tear, degree of muscle fatty atrophy, patient age and patient activity demands. Nonoperative management with physical therapy, NSAIDS, subacromial corticosteroid injections is the first line of treatment for most tears. Frequent corticosteroid injections, and those performed close to surgery, have been shown to increase the risk of postoperative infection and need for revision surgery.

*Forsythe et al. investigated if there is a temporal relationship between corticosteroid injections and the risk of surgical site infection after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair.* **They reported** that injections within 1 month of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair significantly increase the risk of surgical site infection. **They concluded** that there is no increased risk of infection if the surgical procedure is delayed by 1 month following an injection.

*Puzzitiello et al. reviewed the adverse effects of preoperative corticosteroid injections in patients with rotator cuff disease, especially before rotator cuff repair.* **They reported** that several recent clinical trials have demonstrated that corticosteroid injections are correlated with increased risk of revision surgery after RCR in a temporal and dose dependent matter. **They concluded** that caution should be taken when deciding to inject a patient, and this treatment should be withheld if an RCR is to be performed within the following 6 months.

*Weber et al. reviewed whether shoulder injections prior to rotator cuff repair are associated with deleterious surgical outcomes.* **They reported** that there is a strong correlation between preoperative shoulder injections and revision RCR. **They concluded** that there is also a frequency dependence and time dependence to this finding, with more frequent injections and with administration of injections closer to the time of surgery both independently associated with higher revision RCR rates.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,2,4,5: Corticosteroid injections within 1-3 months of surgery are believed to increase the risk of postoperative infection. However, surgery performed >3 months from the last injection does not carry a significant increased risk of postoperative infection. Frequency dependence has been shown to have a strong correlation with the need for revision rotator cuff repair
