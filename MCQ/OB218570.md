## Question

Figures A & B are the radiographs of a 12-year-old female who presents to the foot and ankle clinic with insidious-onset, activity-related forefoot pain. Her pediatrician had obtained ESR and CRP, which were within normal limits. What is the underlying pathology and the next best step in treatment?

## Options

1. Osteomyelitis requiring advanced imaging
2. Osteochondrosis requiring foot immobilization in boot and observation
3. Avascular necrosis requiring osteotomy versus resection arthroplasty
4. Benign bone tumor requiring reassurance and observation
5. Normal foot radiographs requiring reassurance

## Correct option

**2. Osteochondrosis requiring foot immobilization in boot and observation**

## Answer explanation

The patient in the vignette has Freiberg Infraction (Freiberg Disease) which is a result of osteochondrosis of the metatarsal head. The best next step in treatment is foot immobilization in a CAM boot or cast and observation.

Freiberg disease most commonly affects adolescent females. The cause of Freiberg disease can be multifactorial. However, one prevailing theory is that repetitive stress to the metatarsal head leads to vascular compromise. The most commonly affected site is the second metatarsal head(68%), followed by the third metatarsal (27%) and fourth metatarsal head (3%). Disease severity can be staged radiographically, with more severe stages described as a collapse of the metatarsal head. Presenting symptoms include progressive forefoot pain that is made worse with weight bearing. Initial treatment is nonoperative, focusing on minimizing pressure on the affected metatarsal head. Spontaneous resolution with metatarsal head remodeling can occur in the early stages of the disease process.

*Wax and Leland published a review article reviewing the etiology, presentation, classification, and treatment of Frieberg's disease.* **The authors note** multifactorial etiology, including gastrocnemius contracture, hyper dorsiflexed position of metatarsals when wearing high-heel shoes, and abnormal vascularization of metatarsal heads as factors contributing to Freiberg disease. The authors reviewed four different staging classifications based on radiological findings and suggested that early stages are best treated with nonoperative management.

*Charen et al. published a review article overviewing metatarsalgia, its causes, diagnostic workup, and treatment.* **The authors conclude** that nonoperative management, including physical therapy, shoe modification, steroid injections, and orthotics as first-line management with surgical intervention as the next best step if nonoperative management fails.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient does not have osteomyelitis based on normal inflammatory labs (ESR and CRP).
Answer 3: Freiberg disease can also be described as avascular necrosis of the metatarsal head. However, the next best step is conservative management and not surgical intervention.
Answer 4: The patient does not have a benign bone tumor
Answer 5: The patient does not have normal foot radiographs. Conservative management, such as shoe modification and immobilization, is necessary to prevent the progression of the patient's current pathology.
