## Question

A Child presents to the Pediatric orthopedician with Femoral anteversion. The surgeon is deciding on whether the child needs surgery. According to Staheli, which one the following is not an indication for surgery?

## Options

1. Child older than 8 years
2. Anteversion exceeding 50 degrees
3. Medial hip rotation greater than 35 degrees
4. Lateral hip rotation lesser than 10 degrees

## Correct option

**3. Medial hip rotation greater than 35 degrees**

## Answer explanation

Answer-c

 Femoral anteversion

Anteversion of the femur is a measure of its rotation in the transverse plane. It is defined as the angle between the plane containing the femoral head/neck and that containing the femoral condyles. The degree of anteversion is greatest in infancy and gradually decreases as skeletal maturity is approached.

In infants, the mean degree of anteversion is approximately 40 degrees, but decreases to 16 degrees by adulthood. When anteversion is increased or when it fails to decrease with age, the gait is altered and the person walks with the hip internally rotated. This rotation produces a gait in which the patella is medially rotated in stance phase and the foot is also internally rotated, resulting in intoeing.

Demographics

It is seen in early childhood (3-6 years)

It is twice as frequent in girls than boys and can be hereditary.

Anatomic location-

Femoral anteversion is often bilateral

Normal progression of femoral anteversion –

3-12 months of age – average of 39 degrees

2nd year – decreased to 31 degrees

Subsequently the anteversion decreased by 1 to 2 degrees a year through age 10 years - average 24 degrees

Between 14 and 16 years – further decrease from 21 to 16 degrees

Clinical features

Children with excessive femoral anteversion come to medical attention because of an intoeing gait. This gait may be noted when the child first begins to walk or it may be noted later in childhood.
The knees are internally rotated in the stance phase of gait and the feet follow unless external tibial torsion is present. There is excessive internal rotation of the hips and decreased external rotation. This is best demonstrated in the prone position

The degree of tibial torsion, which is measured by the thigh-foot angle, is also noted in the prone position. In severe cases, there may be 90 degrees of internal rotation of the hip without any external rotation

Differential diagnosis:

- Intoeing can be caused by Internal Tibial Torsion, Metatarsus Adductus
- Residual deformity in clubfoot
- Intoeing may be present in children with mild cerebral palsy
- Increased anteversion noted in children with Clubfoot, Blount disease and flat foot
- Also seen in female soccer players

Associated conditions

-Osteoarthritis of hip

-Torsional malalignment syndrome – femoral anteversion + external tibial torsion

-Patellar malalignment

-Chondromalacia patellae

Treatment:

The first step is the careful education of parents. Observation is the mainstay of treatment, only patients with serious consequences need surgical intervention

Staheli listed 5 indications for surgical correction of Femoral anteversion –

(1) child older than 8 years

(2) deformity severe enough to create a significant cosmetic and functional disability

(3) measured anteversion exceeding 50 degrees

(4) internal hip rotation greater than 85 degrees and external rotation of less than 10 degrees

(5) family should be aware of the risks of the procedure

FEMORAL OSTEOTOMY

To correct excessive femoral anteversion can be done at inter-trochanteric, sub trochanteric or supracondylar level.

Inter-trochanteric femoral osteotomy done with blade plate or screw –side plate combination
