## Question

An 81-year-old female with a history of rheumatoid disease presents to your clinic complaining that she recently noticed she is no longer able to extend the small and ring fingers of her left hand. She denies any recent trauma or antecedent weakness. You recommend tendon transfers to improve the function of her affected fingers. Which of the following is true of the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Most commonly preceded by period of pain and catching
2. Ring finger usually affected first
3. Caused by ulnar head prominence secondary to DRUJ instability
4. If MCP joints are placed into extension, the patient could then hold them extended
5. Secondary to nerve compression at the Arcade of Fröshe

## Correct option

**3. Caused by ulnar head prominence secondary to DRUJ instability**

## Answer explanation

This patient has Vaughan-Jackson syndrome, which describes the attritional rupture of the extensor tendons from ulnar to radial secondary to ulnar head prominence.

Vaughan-Jackson syndrome occurs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and takes place secondary to synovitis and destruction in the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ), which leads to dorsal prominence of the ulna and subsequent extensor tendon rupture. Extensor tendons are usually affected from ulnar to radial starting with the extensor digiti minimi (EDM) and the process is typically painless without any obvious warning or antecedent trauma. Rupture of the extensor tendons leads to weakness in extension of the digits and given the extensive synovitis and attritional wear, repair is often not feasible. Therefore, tendon transfers and distal ulna resection are the most common treatments performed to address this pathology.

*Biehl et al. evaluated extensor tendon ruptures in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.* **They noted** a 4% rate of tendon rupture in rheumatoid arthritis wrists and found that the average time duration from diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis to extensor tendon rupture was almost 8 years. **They found** that in rheumatoid patients, the success of the operation (usually tendon transfers), relied on the severity of the underlying disease, as well as the patient's postoperative care and demands.

*Papp et al. reviewed the rheumatoid arthritis wrist.* **They note** that while soft tissue procedures provide good relief and functional improvement in the short-term, bony stabilization is often required to address the underlying pathology. They discuss treatment options including the Darrach and Sauvé-Kapandji procedures as well as variations of wrist fusions to address the bony pathology in addition to managing the tendon and soft tissue problems.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Vaughan-Jackson syndrome usually involves the painless rupture of the extensor tendons after attritional wear.
Answer 2: The EDM is affected first in Vaughan-Jackson syndrome, followed by other extensor tendons from ulnar to radial.
Answer 4: In cases of sagittal band rupture, patients are able to maintain digit extension if the fingers are passively placed in extension, however, that would not be the case for a complete tendon rupture.
Answer 5: A palsy of the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) could also cause a lack of finger extension, but would not selectively involve the small and ring fingers.
