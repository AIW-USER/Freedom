## Options

1. Volar surface of radius and adjacent interosseous membrane to the base of the distal phalanx of the thumb
2. Volar surface of radius and adjacent interosseous membrane to the base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb
3. Medial epicondyle of the humerus to the base of the distal phalanx of the thumb

5. Dorsal surface of middle 1/3 of ulna and interosseous membrane to the base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb

## Correct option

**1. Volar surface of radius and adjacent interosseous membrane to the base of the distal phalanx of the thumb**

## Answer explanation

This patient has undergone volar locked plating for a distal radius fracture, with the plate placed volar to the "critical line" and distal to the volar rim, placement which places the flexor pollicis longus (FPL) at risk for attritional rupture. The FPL origin and insertion, respectively, are the anterior surface of the radius and adjacent interosseous membrane to the base of the distal phalanx of the thumb (Answer 1).

Distal radius fractures represent the most common orthopaedic injury and generally result from a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH). These injuries account for 17.5% of all fractures in adults and occur more commonly in females in a 2-3:1 ratio. Distal radius fractures occur in a bimodal distribution, with younger patients sustaining these injuries due to high-energy mechanisms, and older patients due to low-energy mechanisms. Over 50% of these injuries are intra-articular in nature, and osteoporosis serves as a primary risk factor for these injuries, especially in post-menopausal women.

The optimal treatment of these injuries is hotly debated, with recent evidence suggesting that initial nonoperative management is the most appropriate treatment modality in older patients with most varieties of distal radius fractures. There remain, however, many types of distal radius fractures that may warrant operative fixation (Illustration A). For reconstructible fractures, the treatment of choice is often volar-locked plating, performed through a volar approach. Appropriate plate placement during ORIF is of critical importance, as malpositioned plates can place the FPL tendon at risk of attritional rupture, and dorsal screw penetration can place the extensor tendons at risk. The degree of plate malposition can be classified with the Soong classification (Illustration B). To determine the Soong classification, a line is drawn tangential from the most volar prominence of the distal radius, parallel to the volar cortex. Grade 0 is given to plates that are dorsal to the critical line, Grade 1 is given to plates volar to the critical line but proximal to the volar rim, and Grade 2 is given to plates that are directly on or distal to the volar rim.

*DeGeorge et al. provide a single-center retrospective review investigating the rate of complications following volar-locked plating of distal radius fractures.* *Their cohort consisted of 647 fractures in 636 patients with a mean follow-up of 9.1 months.* The incidence of tendon rupture was 0.5% and of tendon irritation was 2.5%. Hardware removal for painful and/or symptomatic hardware occurred in 6.2% of patients at a mean of 427.8 days after surgery. **The authors note** that patients with Soong grade 2 plate prominence were 3.3 times more likely to undergo hardware removal.

*Soong et al. provide a seminal paper on the incidence of flexor tendon rupture following volar-locked plating for distal radius fractures.* *The study population was broken into two cohorts which were fixed with two different types of locking plates.* Overall, **the authors found** that a plate that was overly prominent at the watershed line of the distal radius was prone to cause flexor tendon rupture (mostly FPL), and the authors conclude that surgeons should ensure that volar locking plates are both low-profile by design and not placed overly distal, as this greatly increases the likelihood of flexor tendon rupture.

*Kitay et al. investigated the impact of volar locking plate position on the incidence of flexor tendon rupture following ORIF of distal radius fractures.* *Their study consisted of 8 patients who experienced flexor tendon rupture and a matched control group of 17 patients without rupture.* Overall, **the authors found** that a higher Soong grade (meaning, a distally placed and/or prominent plate) was strongly associated with subsequent flexor tendon rupture. **They conclude** that elective hardware removal should be considered in symptomatic patients with plate prominence greater than 2.0 mm volar to the critical line or within 3.0 mm of the volar rim.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The FPL inserts on the base of the distal, not the proximal phalanx, of the thumb. The flexor pollicis brevis inserts on the base of the proximal phalanx.
Answer 3: While the flexor-pronator mass originates at the medial epicondyle, the FPL originates on the anterior surface of the radius and adjacent interosseous membrane.
Answers 4 and 5: The extensor tendons are at risk with dorsal screw penetration, which is not apparent in Figure B. Attritional EPL rupture has also been described as a complication of non-displaced distal radius fractures, due to the impingement of the extensor mechanism and subsequent ischemia and/or wear.
