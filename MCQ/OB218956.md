## Question

A 27-year-old male falls off his bicycle while participating in a street race. He notes an obvious deformity to his elbow and radiographs demonstrate the findings seen in Figures A & B. Reduction is performed and post-reduction radiographs do not demonstrate any apparent fractures. Given the most likely ligamentous injury pattern, how should the elbow be immobilized?

## Options

1. Sling for comfort, no immobilization
2. Splint within stable arc of motion and full supination
3. Splint within stable arc of motion and full pronation
4. Splint at 45° flexion and neutral rotation
5. Splint at 90° flexion and full supination

## Correct option

**3. Splint within stable arc of motion and full pronation**

## Answer explanation

Simple posterolateral elbow dislocations often have concurrent lateral ligament injury and should be splinted in full pronation in order to tighten the lateral-sided ligaments.

Elbow dislocations are usually the result of traumatic injury and occur with a combination of supination, axial load and valgus stress. This causes failure of the lateral ligament complex, principally the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL). In some cases, this is associated with a bony injury like a coronoid or radial head fracture. Medial ligamentous structures may also be injured, although these are much less likely. Immobilization of the arm should be based on the spectrum of these injuries. When an isolated lateral ligamentous injury (LUCL) is suspected, immobilization should be performed within a stable arc of flexion and in pronation to tighten the lateral structures. When the LUCL and medial collateral ligaments (MCL) are both disrupted, the arm is splinted in neutral. Injury to the MCL in isolation is very unlikely, therefore splinting in full supination is not recommended.

*Badhrinarayanan et al. reviewed indications, outcomes, and complications of LUCL reconstruction of the elbow for posterolateral instability.* *They discussed their postoperative protocol and noted that most of the reconstructions were performed in patients with a prior traumatic injury.* The most common complication was recurrent instability which occurred in 15% of cases.

*Robinson et al. reviewed simple elbow dislocations,* **noting** it is the 2nd most commonly dislocated joint in adults. They discuss the pathoanatomy and management of these injuries and reviewed the position of immobilization after injury/surgery. **They noted** that a small portion of these patients, around 10%, fail nonoperative management after simple elbow dislocation, but most patients can be treated non-operatively.

Figures A & B demonstrate the AP and lateral radiographs of a posterolateral elbow dislocation without obvious evidence of associated fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: After an acute elbow dislocation, the elbow should be initially immobilized within a stable arc of flexion, with the degree of extension increasing every 5-7 days until full, stable, range of motion is achieved.
Answer 2: Given the most likely pattern of posterolateral elbow instability involves injury to the LUCL, the elbow should be splinted in full pronation, as this best tightens the lateral structures.
Answers 4 & 5: There is no specific degree of flexion necessary for post-reduction splinting; rather, the elbow should be splinted in a position of stability which can be noted after the reduction is performed. Additionally, it should be splinted in full pronation if an isolated injury to the lateral elbow ligaments is suspected.
