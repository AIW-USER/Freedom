## Question

A 55-year-old male with insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus presents to the emergency department with two weeks of increasing low back pain with radiation to his bilateral lower extremities. Social history is notable for housing instability, active intravenous drug use, and heavy alcohol and tobacco use. Vital signs reveal a temperature of 101.8º F. Laboratory serum analysis is significant for an elevated white blood cell count and C-reactive protein (CRP), and a lumbar MRI is obtained and shown in Figure A. Which of the following factors most strongly supports the decision to obtain additional cervical and thoracic MRIs?

![[q220832-a-1040px-mri-of-the-lumbar-spine-with-abscess-in-the.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Active intravenous drug use
2. Duration of symptoms
3. Presence of insulin-dependant type 2 diabetes mellitus
4. Febrile on presentation
5. Elevation of CRP

## Correct option

**2. Duration of symptoms**

## Answer explanation

This patient has multiple risk factors, clinical symptoms, and objective findings consistent with the development of a spinal epidural abscess. Of the listed options, only duration of symptoms—specifically, ≥ 7 days—has been shown to be suggestive of the presence of additional noncontiguous (“skip”) spinal lesions (answer choice 2).

Spinal epidural abscess is a suppurative infection within the epidural space, most commonly from hematogenous seeding or contiguous spread, with Staphylococcus aureus the leading pathogen. Risk rises with IV drug use, diabetes or other immunosuppression, malignancy, and recent spinal procedures. Patients typically present with severe focal axial pain, with or without fever; evolving radiculopathy or myelopathy signals with or without evolving neural compromise. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are elevated in the majority of cases. MRI with gadolinium is the diagnostic study of choice and defines the abscess, any concomitant spondylodiscitis, and the degree of neural compression, if present. Imaging of the entire spine is recommended when skip lesions are a concern, and multiple factors have been shown to predict the presence of these lesions. Management is guided by the severity of infection and neurologic status: carefully monitored medical management is reasonable in cases of small abscesses without evidence of neurologic compromise; however, surgical decompression plus IV antibiotics is recommended for any cases with associated neurologic deficits or cord compression, failure or risk factors for failure with medical treatment, and spinal deformity or instability. Prognosis tracks most closely with the neurologic state at presentation, underscoring the need for early recognition and intervention.

*Ju et al. retrospectively reviewed 233 adult patients diagnosed with spinal epidural abscesses, 211 with single lesions, and 22 with multiple lesions, to identify predictors of concurrent noncontiguous (“skip”) lesions using whole-spine imaging.* **They found** three independent predictors—delay in presentation ≥7 days, a concomitant extra-spinal infection, and ESR >95 mm/h—with a simplified model yielding predicted probabilities of 73% (all three), 13% (two), 2% (one), and 0% (none) and an AUC of 0.936. **They concluded** that this three-factor rule may help determine which patients warrant imaging of the entire spine to detect skip lesions.

*Patel et al. retrospectively reviewed 128 consecutive spontaneous spinal epidural abscess cases to compare medical versus surgical management and identify predictors of failure with antibiotics alone.* **They found** that 41% of medically treated patients failed and required delayed surgery. These patients had worse motor recovery than those who underwent immediate surgery (mean ASIA motor score change −14.86 after delay vs +9.52 with early surgery). Abscess location/extent and organism did not influence recovery. **They concluded** that early surgical decompression with antibiotics should be favored, and that diabetes, CRP >115 mg/L, WBC >12.5 k/mL, and bacteremia mark high risk for failure of nonoperative care (failure risk 8.3% with 0 factors, 35.4% with 1, 40.2% with 2, and 76.9% with ≥3).

Figure A is the sagittal STIR MRI of the lumbar spine demonstrating a localized fluid collection in the posterior aspect of the spinal canal, consistent with a spinal epidural abscess.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Although active IV drug use is a risk factor for the development of spinal epidural abscesses, it has not been found to be predictive of additional skip lesions once a single SEA is diagnosed.
Answer choices 3 and 5: Although the presence of diabetes or CRP > 115 mg/L are risk factors for failure of medical treatment, they have not been found to be predictive of skip lesions.
Answer choice 4: Fever is a common finding in cases of SEA, but has not been shown to predict additional lesions once an SEA has been diagnosed.
