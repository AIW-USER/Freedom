## Question

The procedure illustrated in Figure A would be most appropriate for a patient undergoing anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty with which of the following anatomic parameters?

![[q212183-a-reaming.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Glenoid retroversion of 9 degrees
2. Glenoid retroversion of 19 degrees
3. Humeral anteversion of 11 degrees
4. Humeral retroversion of 19 degrees
5. Superior glenoid inclination of 18 degrees

## Correct option

**1. Glenoid retroversion of 9 degrees**

## Answer explanation

Figure A depicts eccentric glenoid reaming. This is most commonly performed to correct glenoid retroversion <10-15 degrees in patients undergoing anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA).

Normal glenoid version is most often cited at 0-3 degrees of retroversion. When performing a total shoulder arthroplasty, glenoid retroversion <10-15 degrees may often be corrected with eccentric reaming in order to restore near-native version. Failure to do so results in glenoid malposition. The decreased glenohumeral contact area leads to increased contact pressures, thereby contributing to accelerated glenoid component loosening. With excessive glenoid retroversion (>15 degrees), the eccentric reaming required to correct the glenoid to native version would be excessive and compromise the glenoid bone stock, risking glenoid vault penetration. In these cases, bone graft or component augmentation is instead recommended.

*Nowak et al. utilized 3-dimensional models created from computed tomography of 19 patients with advanced shoulder osteoarthritis, to simulate glenoid resurfacing for varying degrees of retroversion.* **They reported** that glenoids with preoperative retroversion of <12 degrees could always be implanted with 46-mm and 52-mm glenoid components at neutral version without vault violation. However, glenoids with >18 degrees of preoperative retroversion could not be implanted at neutral version due to vault violation from the pegs. **The authors concluded** that that glenoid retroversion is a critical factor in determining successful glenoid implantation.

*Shapiro et al. evaluated the effects of glenoid retroversion after total shoulder arthroplasty on glenohumeral joint contact pressures, contact areas, and forces in a cadaveric series.* **The authors report** that placement of the glenoid in 15 degrees of retroversion significantly decreased glenohumeral contact area, increased contact pressure, and decreased inferior and posterior glenohumeral forces. **They concluded** that glenoid component retroversion may result in eccentric loading of the glenoid component and possibly lead to accelerated wear and component loosening, highlighting the importance of correct anatomic glenoid placement during total shoulder arthroplasty.

*Sears et al. reviewed glenoid wear characteristics following total shoulder arthroplasty.* **They reported** that the degree of anatomic glenoid version correction was critical to glenoid component wear characteristics. **The authors concluded** that several methods of correction are available and include asymmetric reaming of the anterior glenoid, bone grafting of the posterior glenoid, and implanting a specialized glenoid component with posterior augmentation.

Figure A represents eccentric reaming of a glenoid defect. Illustration A depicts a glenoid defect managed with either eccentric or on-axis reaming.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Glenoid retroversion exceeds the amount which can be corrected with eccentric reaming, as this amount of correction would in all likelihood result in glenoid vault penetration.
Answers 3, 4: Humeral anteversion is not corrected with eccentric reaming of the glenoid.
Answer 5: This amount of glenoid superior inclination exceeds the amount of inclination that can be corrected with eccentric reaming.
