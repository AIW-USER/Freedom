## Question

A 17 year-old-female gymnast presents to your clinic after sustaining an injury to her knee while performing her floor routine one week prior. She states that her knee felt like it popped out then popped back into place. Which combination of history, examination and imaging findings would be the best indication for this patient to undergo an isolated medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction?

## Options

1. First patellar dislocation, 1 quadrant of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, no J sign, tibial tubercle to trochlear groove distance (TT-TG) of 16mm
2. First patellar dislocation, 2 quadrants of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, presence of a J sign, TT-TG of 13mm
3. Recurrent patellar dislocation, 3 quadrant of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, J sign, TT-TG of 24mm
4. First patellar dislocation, 1 quadrant of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, no J sign, TT-TG of 11mm
5. Recurrent patellar dislocation, 3 quadrants of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, presence of a J sign, TT-TG of 15mm

## Correct option

**5. Recurrent patellar dislocation, 3 quadrants of lateral translation of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion, presence of a J sign, TT-TG of 15mm**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained an acute patellar dislocation (APD) that spontaneously reduced. Recurrent patellar dislocations, physical exam findings consistent with an APD, and a normal TT-TG distance would be the best indication for surgical intervention with isolated medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction. Her age and sex are risk factors for repeat patellar dislocation.

The MPFL is a thickened band of retinacular tissue on the medial side of the knee that acts as the primary soft tissue restrain to lateral translation of the patella. It originates from the “saddle” area on the femur between the medial epicondyle and the adductor tubercle. The MPFL inserts on the proximal half of the medial border of the patella. It functions primarily in the first 30 degrees of knee flexion (before the patella becomes engaged in the trochlea) and becomes lax in knee flexion. Most APD spontaneously reduce. Pertinent findings for APD are the presence of effusion, tenderness to palpation along the medial border of the patella, patellar apprehension, lateral patellar translation >2 quadrants at 30 degrees of knee flexion, decreased range of motion at the knee, and the presence of a J sign. Pertinent findings on diagnostic imaging include trochlear dysplasia, osteochondral fractures of the medial facet of the patella and/or lateral femoral condyle, patella alta, sulcus angle >140 degrees, lateral patellofemoral angle <11 degrees, TT-TG >20 mm, or chondral damage within the patellofemoral joint. The different constellations of history, physical examination findings, and diagnostic imaging abnormalities will lead you to pick the appropriate surgical option: MPFL reconstruction, tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO), and/or cartilage procedure.

*Iobst and Stanitski published a review of common acute knee injuries.* **The authors reported** recurrent patellar dislocation rates in adolescents to be between 17% and 44%. **They state** that an osteochondral fracture of the medial facet of the patella and/or the lateral femoral condyle is commonly encountered with patellar dislocations.

*Fithian et al performed a prospective cohort study with 189 patients with a history of acute patellar dislocation.* **Their findings stated** that patella dislocators were most likely to be female, between the ages of 10 to 17 years of age, and to have a history of prior patellar subluxation or dislocation.

*Schepsis and Rogers wrote a review of the indications and techniques for MPFL reconstruction.* **The authors highlighted** important physical examination findings specific to patellar instability - the “J sign,” lateral patella translation >2 quadrants, and a soft endpoint to lateral patellar translation. When treating patients surgically **they emphasize** the importance of identifying the exact femoral position to place the graft both radiographically and anatomically before securing it in place.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1, 2 & 4: Recurrent instability is a better indication for surgery. While some first-time dislocators may benefit from surgery, this is more controversial.
Answer 3: Surgery is recommended for this patient; however, the elevated TT-TG is an indication that there is underlying rotational mal-alignment. This could necessitate a concomitant distal realignment procedure (TTO) in the same settings as the MPFL reconstruction. The importance of the TTO is controversial as recent studies have shown good results with the MPFL alone in these patients.
