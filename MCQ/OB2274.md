## Question

An 8-year-old patient presents to the emergency department after being involved in a motor vehicle collision. The patient was a restrained passenger. The patient is complaining of severe abdominal pain and back pain. There are no motor deficits on the physical exam. The current radiographs are shown in figure A. Computed tomography scan of the lumbar spine was negative for a bone injury at the affected level. What is the best treatment course at this time?

![[q2274-a-chance-peds-labeled.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. L3-4 posterior percutaneous screw fixation
2. Thoracolumbosacral orthosis with follow-up in 3 weeks
3. L3-4 posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation
4. L1-5 posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation with L3-4 laminectomy
5. L3-4 posterior lumbar interbody fusion

## Correct option

**3. L3-4 posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a soft-tissue chance fracture at L3-4 without neurologic deficits. The optimal treatment option for this would be L3-4 posterior instrumented fusion.

Chance fractures are the result of a flexion-distraction mechanism of the spine that can result in either purely ligamentous, purely bone, or a combination of bone and soft tissue injuries. There has been a high correlation with abdominal injuries with the use of lap seatbelts in motor vehicle collisions. These injuries are highly unstable and can lead to progressive kyphotic deformity and neurologic deficits. Treatment of purely ligamentous injuries involves posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation. Modern pedicle screw constructs limit the instrumented levels to one above and below the injury.

*Taylor et al. performed a retrospective study of 365 consecutive abdominal CT of children assessed for intraabdominal trauma following lap-belt injuries.* Five of the patients had associated lumbar spine injuries that were missed by the initial CT scan, which included four facet subluxation with the anterior translation of L3 and multiple compression fractures in the fifth patient. **They concluded** that missed lumbar trauma after CT for lap-belt injuries can be detected with lateral radiographs of the lumbar spine.

*Reid et al. presented seven cases of lumbar Chance fractures associated with intraabdominal trauma and seatbelt use.* *The authors cited literature supporting the "seatbelt sign" association with Chance fractures.* *They further describe the unique features of pediatric anatomy in relation to the design of the adult restraint and the subsequent development of intraabdominal injuries and vertebral fractures.*

Figure A is the lateral radiograph of the lumbar spine with a purely soft-tissue chance fracture present at L3-4.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Reduction with percutaneous screw fixation could be a treatment option in boney Chance fractures since there is a potential for bone healing through the injury. Soft tissue injuries are inherently unstable and require fusion to provide adequate stability.
Answer 2: Thoracolumbosacral orthosis can be a treatment option in boney Chance fractures stable injury patterns without neurologic deficits.
Answer 4: Classically, posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation involved three levels about and two levels below the injury, but modern pedicle screw fixation allows for one level above and below the injury.
Answer 5: A posterior lumbar interbody fusion at L3-4 is unnecessary for this injury pattern. Fixation posteriorly would provide the stability that this injury lacks.
