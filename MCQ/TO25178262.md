## Question

The 'Vaughan-Jackson' syndrome involves a progressive rupture of the extensor tendons. Which tendon is typically the first to rupture, and in what direction does the cascade proceed?

## Options

1. EDC to the index finger; proceeds from radial to ulnar
2. EIP; proceeds from radial to ulnar
3. EDQ; proceeds from ulnar to radial
4. EPL; proceeds from central to ulnar

## Correct option

**3. EDQ; proceeds from ulnar to radial**

## Answer explanation

Attritional rupture typically starts with the Extensor Digiti Quinti (EDQ) over the prominent ulnar head (Caput Ulna Syndrome) and moves radially.

The Vaughan-Jackson syndrome is a classic and devastating manifestation of the "Rheumatoid Hand." It is defined by the progressive, sequential rupture of the extensor tendons
at the level of the wrist, occurring from the ulnar side toward the radial side.

It is often the final stage of Caput Ulna Syndrome, where the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) undergoes complete biomechanical collapse.

Pathoanatomy: The "Bony Saw" Mechanism

The pathology can be broken down into three distinct, yet simultaneous, processes:

A. Attritional Wear (The Mechanical Factor)

Synovitis of the DRUJ destroys the stabilizing ligaments (TFCC), causing the ulnar head to subluxate dorsally. The distal ulna becomes prominent and "raw" due to the loss of its articular cartilage. As the patient moves their wrist, the extensor tendons rub against this roughened, dorsalized bony prominence, leading to mechanical wear—much like a rope fraying over a sharp rock.

B. Ischemic and Inflammatory Tenosynovitis (The Biological Factor)

Hypertrophic synovium invades the tendons directly (proliferative tenosynovitis), weakening the collagen structure. This invasive tissue also increases pressure within the dorsal compartments, compromising the microvascular blood supply to the tendons.

C. The "Ulnar-to-Radial" Cascade

The ruptures almost always follow a specific order because of the proximity to the prominent ulnar head:

 - EDQ (Extensor Digiti Quinti): The most ulnar and therefore the first to go.

 - EDC (Extensor Digitorum Communis) to the Small finger.

 - EDC to the Ring finger, then Middle, and occasionally the Index.

Clinical Presentation

 - The "Drop Finger": The patient is unable to actively extend the MCP joints of the affected fingers.

 - The Sensation of "Pop":
 Unlike acute trauma, these ruptures are often painless or associated with a minor "snap" during routine activity.

 - Clinical Test: one must distinguish this from MCP joint dislocation or radial nerve palsy. If you can passively extend the fingers and they stay extended (tenodesis), but the patient cannot actively hold them there, the tendons are ruptured.

Surgical Management

Management is a two-pronged approach: Eliminate the cause and Restore the function.

Phase 1: Addressing the "Saw" (Bone Work)

You cannot repair the tendons without first removing the bony prominence that caused the rupture.

 - Darrach Procedure: Resection of the distal 1–2 cm of the ulna. This is the gold standard for low-demand rheumatoid patients.

 - Sauvé-Kapandji Procedure:
 Arthrodesis of the DRUJ with a proximal ulnar pseudoarthrosis. Preferred in younger/higher-demand patients to maintain a platform for the carpus.

 - Dorsal Synovectomy: Thorough excision of the diseased tenosynovium.

Phase 2: Restoring Extension (Tendon Work)

Direct end-to-end repair is rarely possible because the tendon ends are usually frayed and "moth-eaten."

 Tendon Transfer: Usually the EIP (Extensor Indicis Proprius) is transferred to the small and ring fingers.

 Four Tendons (Small through Index)

 Requires more complex transfers, such as FDS (Flexor Digitorum Superficialis) of the ring finger or the Extensor Carpi Radialis Longus (ECRL).

 Post-Operative Rehabilitation

 - Splintage: A dynamic extension splint is typically used for 4–6 weeks to protect the transfers while allowing early controlled motion to prevent adhesions.

 - The "Golden Rule": If the wrist is in severe radial deviation (the "zigzag" effect), a wrist arthrodesis or stabilization should be performed concurrently with the tendon work to prevent recurrence of the bowstringing forces.

Also know:

Mannerfelt's Syndrome is the "volar" equivalent of Vaughan-Jackson, involving rupture of the FPL (Flexor Pollicis Longus) due to a bony spur on the scaphoid (the scaphoid tubercle). In any rheumatoid patient with a "drop finger," always check for both dorsal and volar attrition!

Answer: 3
