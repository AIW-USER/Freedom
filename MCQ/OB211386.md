## Question

A 37-year-old construction worker falls off a rock and lands on his right leg. He reports severe pain and inability to bear weight on the right leg. Current imaging is shown in figures A-C. On examination, the injury is closed, but there is substantial soft tissue swelling. An ankle spanning external fixator is placed on the right leg to allow for soft tissue stabilization. The treating surgeon decides to perform an open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) through combined anterolateral and medial approaches. What is true regarding the anterolateral approach for this injury?

## Options

1. The entire articular surface cannot be visualized through the anterolateral approach
2. Anterior compartment tendons are retracted laterally to protect the neurovascular structures
3. Anterolateral approach is contraindicated with central dome comminution
4. Dorsal foot numbness is the most common associated neurologic complication
5. Fibular fixation is usally performed through the same incision

## Correct option

**4. Dorsal foot numbness is the most common associated neurologic complication**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a comminuted pilon fracture with varus collapse. Neuropraxia to the superficial peroneal nerve is the most common neurologic injury associated with the anterolateral approach and results in dorsal foot numbness.

Pilon fractures, also known as tibial plafond fractures, are high-energy injuries that commonly present with extensive soft tissue swelling. Historically, these injuries were associated with high infection rates and wound complications when treated in the acute setting. Temporizing the soft tissue for approximately 10-14 days has reduced infection rates to about 3%. Definitive fixation can be accomplished through various approaches. The anterolateral approach is centered over the ankle joint between the tibia and fibula and in line with the 4th ray. Through the anterolateral approach, the entire articular surface can be visualized to aid in anatomic reduction. Injury to the superficial peroneal nerve is the most common neurologic complication, resulting in dorsal foot numbness.

*Sirkin et al. performed a retrospective study of 56 AO type C pilon fractures that were treated with a staged treatment protocol consisting of initial ORIF of the fibula and ankle spanning external fixation followed by definitive fixation of the tibia.* **They reported** an average of 12.7 days to conversion from ex-fixator to ORIF. All closed pilon fractures surgical wounds healed with no full-thickness skin necrosis. **The authors reported** a high rate of successful healing and reduced infectious complications for pilon fractures treated with staged ORIF.

*Mehta et al. reviewed reduction strategies through the anterolateral approach for type B and C pilon fractures and retrospectively reported the results of 131 patients treated.* **They noted** that the anterolateral approach allows reduction of the entire articular surface and reported a 2.2% infection rate and a 5% rate of superficial peroneal nerve palsy. **They concluded** the anterolateral approach is not suitable for fractures with medial comminution, medial crush, impaction of the medial shoulder of the joint, segmental medial malleolar injuries, or varus deformity at the time of the injury.

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of the right ankle with a comminuted pilon fracture with varus malalignment. Figure C is an axial CT of the right ankle with a comminuted fracture of the tibial plafond.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The entire articular surface can be visualized through an anterolateral approach.
Answer 2: After the extensor retinaculum is incised, the anterior compartment extensor muscles are retracted medially to expose the distal tibia and ankle joint.
Answer 3: The presence of central dome comminution does not preclude the execution of the anterolateral approach.
Answer 5: Fibular fixation is typically performed through an additional incision, typically the posterolateral approach, to allow for sufficient skin bridges.
