## Question

A 76-year-old female presents to the emergency department after a fall getting out of the shower. Her presenting imaging displays a three-part proximal humerus fracture with the glenohumeral joint remaining concentrically reduced (Figures A and B). She has minimal comorbidities, however, she was recently diagnosed with osteoporosis. She lives alone at home and walks daily for physical activity, but otherwise, she does not have any high physical demands. After a discussion of treatment options, the patient and her family elect to proceed with surgical intervention. Which of the following would be the most appropriate option for this patient?

## Options

1. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF)
2. Intramedullary nail (IMN)
3. Hemiarthroplasty (HA)
4. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA)
5. Proximal humerus replacement (PHR)

## Correct option

**4. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA)**

## Answer explanation

The majority of geriatric proximal humerus fractures can be treated non-operatively. However, if surgical intervention is sought in a geriatric patient with osteoporosis, rTSA serves as the best surgical option.

Proximal humerus fractures are complex injuries with a plethora of treatment modalities. In younger and middle-aged patients, ORIF is the standard of care with few exceptions requiring HA/TSA. The elderly population requires a separate approach, as these patients are prone to osteopenia, multiple comorbidities, and lower healing rates. Many geriatric proximal humerus fractures can, and should, be treated non-operatively. In 3- and 4- part fractures, there remains controversy over the best surgical treatment and timing of surgery. While significant debate remains, it is well established that reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has significantly improved outcomes and lower reoperation rates when compared to hemiarthroplasty (HA) in this older cohort. This is likely secondary to HA outcomes remaining dependent on tuberosity healing (i.e. rotator cuff functionality), whereas rTSA does not heavily rely on tuberosity healing for the functional outcome (dominant deltoid movement). It should be noted that rTSA outcomes are even more so improved with tuberosity healing, however not required due to the change in underlying glenohumeral joint biomechanics.

*Mease and colleagues reviewed controversies in the treatment of geriatric proximal humerus fractures.* *They provide an all-encompassing comparison of the different surgical options for these patients.* **The authors report** consistent findings of improved post-operative outcomes when utilizing rTSA over HA in this patient cohort. **They conclude** that rTSA is a consistently reliable option for 3- and 4- part proximal humerus fractures with better outcomes than ORIF or HA in this patient population.

*Sebastiá-Forcada and colleagues performed a blinded, randomized prospective study comparing rTSA to hemiarthroplasty for the treatment of geriatric proximal humerus fractures.* **They found** that rTSA resulted in better post-operative pain and functional outcomes scores with lower rates of revision. **They conclude** that rTSA should be utilized over HA in this older patient cohort.

*Torchia and colleagues performed a systematic review regarding the timing of surgery when performing rTSA for a geriatric proximal humerus fracture.* **They found** that acute vs delayed rTSA did not show significant differences in functional outcomes or reoperation rates. The authors stressed that given the risks associated with surgery in the elderly, saving rTSA for those in whom nonoperative treatment fails is safe, appropriate, and prudent.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) can be appropriate in patients with high functional demand and expected tuberosity healing. However, in general, ORIF in elderly proximal humerus fractures results in equivalent functional outcomes as nonoperative management with a higher complication rate.
Answer 2: Intramedullary nailing (IMN) has been described in the elderly population for 2- and 3- part proximal humerus fractures with similar outcomes compared to ORIF. However, they both have high failure rates in patients with osteoporosis and would not serve as the best surgical option for this patient.
Answer 3: Hemiarthroplasty (HA) used to be the standard arthroplasty option for geriatric proximal humerus fractures. However, there is increasing evidence in favor of rTSA over HA due to improved functional outcomes and lower revision rates. This is likely due to rTSA relying on deltoid function for strength/ROM rather than relying on the tuberosities healing (needed for HA functionality).
Answer 5: Proximal humerus replacement (PHR) is a last resort option when there is a lack of proximal humerus bone stock for HA or rTSA. This is most commonly utilized in the multiple-failed revision proximal humerus replacement or after tumor resection.
