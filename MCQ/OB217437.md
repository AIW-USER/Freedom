## Question

A 19-year-old male with recurrent anterior shoulder instability presents to your office for evaluation. Radiographs and CT imaging were obtained and demonstrates glenoid bone loss and an impaction fracture of the humeral head. You are considering surgical intervention with a coracoid transfer procedure. However, you first try to determine if the patient has an "on-track" or "off-tract" lesion. How should the glenoid track be calculated?

GT = Glenoid Track
D = Diameter (anterior to posterior) of the inferior glenoid
L = Length (superior to inferior) of the center glenoid
ABL = Anterior bone loss

## Options

1. GT = 0.50(D)/ABL
2. GT = 0.50(L)/(D-ABL)
3. GT = 0.84(D)-ABL
4. GT = 0.84(D-ABL)/D
5. GT = 0.84(ABL)/D

## Correct option

**3. GT = 0.84(D)-ABL**

## Answer explanation

The patient has anterior glenoid bone loss from recurrent shoulder instability. The glenoid track is a calculation that may be performed to determine if a Hill-Sachs lesion will "engage" the anterior glenoid.

Traumatic anterior shoulder instability, also referred to as TUBS (Traumatic Unilateral dislocations with a Bankart lesion requiring Surgery), are traumatic shoulder injuries that generally occur as a result of an anterior force to the shoulder while its abduced and externally rotated and may lead to recurrent anterior shoulder instability. Diagnosis is made clinically with presence of positive anterior instability provocative tests and confirmed with MRI studies that may reveal labral and/or bony injuries of the glenoid and proximal humerus (Hill Sachs). Treatment may be nonoperative or operative depending on the chronicity of symptoms, the presence of risk factors for recurrence, in the setting of recurrence of instability and the severity of labral and/or glenoid defects. The glenoid track is a CT measurement that may be used to determine if a Hill-Sachs lesion will engage the anterior glenoid and is useful in determining treatment. If the Hill-Sachs interval is greater than the glenoid track, the shoulder is off track and this has higher recurrence rate with arthroscopic Bankart procedures only. In these cases, a Bankart procedure with a Remplissage or Latarjet procedure should be considered for an off-track lesion with greater than 25% glenoid bone loss.

*Di Giacomo et al. reviewed anterior instability with glenoid bone loss comprising 25% or more of the inferior glenoid diameter (inverted-pear glenoid).* **They reported** that use of the glenoid track may determine whether a Hill-Sachs lesion will engage the anterior glenoid rim and whether or not there is concomitant anterior glenoid bone loss. **They concluded** that if the Hill-Sachs lesion engages, it is called an "off-track" Hill-Sachs lesion; if it does not engage, it is an "on-track" lesion.

*Shaha et al. reviewed the relationship of the glenoid track to the outcomes of arthroscopic Bankart reconstructions.* **They reported** that the application of the glenoid track concept was superior to using glenoid bone loss alone with regard to predicting postoperative stability. **They concluded** that this method of assessment is encouraged as a routine part of the preoperative evaluation of all patients under consideration for arthroscopic anterior stabilization.

*Momaya et al. reviewed the recent evolution in the treatment of anterior shoulder instability and the dynamic interaction between the glenoid and Hill-Sachs lesion.* **They reported** that cadaveric studies have validated the glenoid track concept and clinical studies have demonstrated a much higher rate of failure after arthroscopic Bankart repair for shoulders that were off track. **They concluded** that the glenoid track concept is a useful tool in evaluating patients with anterior shoulder instability and shoulders that are off track may require more than a simple arthroscopic Bankart, and the addition of a remplissage or bony transfer may be considered.
