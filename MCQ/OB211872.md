## Options

1. A femoral prosthesis with a full-length press-fit stem is most appropriate to protect the entire bone
2. Lesions are poorly radiation or chemotherapy-sensitive
3. Pathologic fractures have a low chance of healing
4. Prophylactic fixation was indicated prior to fracture to avoid delay systemic therapy
5. Radionuclide bone scan is ideal for detection

## Correct option

**4. Prophylactic fixation was indicated prior to fracture to avoid delay systemic therapy**

## Answer explanation

In a patient with multiple myeloma (MM) and a lesion at high-risk for pathologic fracture, aggressive timely prophylactic fixation is indicated. The patient will require chemotherapy, and if pathologic fracture were to occur during treatment, surgery would be delayed and the patient would be at risk for complications resulting from bedrest and immunocompromise.

MM is a clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow, often resulting in disseminated skeletal and visceral metastases. Diagnosis is made by a skeletal survey, bone marrow aspiration, and urine/serum electrophoresis. Radionuclide bone scan is unreliable given that MM is largely lytic. Patients with MM who are stem cell transplant candidates should undergo aggressive induction chemotherapy followed by autologous transplantation. This should not be delayed by potential prophylactic surgical management. Those who are physiologically unfit for stem cell transplant (non-candidates) still undergo chemotherapy. Radiation therapy may be implemented as a primary treatment for impending pathologic lesions given the radio-responsiveness of MM. Failure to respond necessitates prophylactic stabilization while pathologic fracture requires fixation or endoprosthetic replacement, both with the goal of protecting the whole bone. Adjuvant radiation is indicated post-operatively in patients with active disease.

*Johnson et al. review the contemporary management of appendicular skeletal metastases.* *The authors discuss and compare the most common primary tumors, including MM.* **They note** that chemotherapy is the first line of treatment, whether the patient is a stem cell transplant candidate or not, and that bisphosphonates are successful in significantly reducing skeletal related events. **The authors emphasize** that at-risk lesions should be addressed aggressively in transplant candidates to avoid any delay in treatment and to thus maximize survival. **They conclude** that due to the progressive nature of the disease that full-length cemented stems should be used to proximal femoral lesions.

*Scharschmidt et al. discuss the diagnosis and orthopaedic management of MM.* **The authors highlight** that the primary management of MM is medical, with a combination of radiation and chemotherapy. **They note** that pathologic fractures are common and that aggressive prophylactic stabilization may be necessary pre-induction in stem cell transplant candidates to avoid pathologic fracture during treatment. **They conclude** that surgical fixation or endoprosthetic replacement should be aimed at protecting the whole bone.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the right hip demonstrating multiple lytic lesions within the proximal femur with a “punched-out” appearance.
Figure B is a histologic specimen demonstrating pathology consistent with plasma cells of MM.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A full-length CEMENTED femoral stem is most appropriate to protect the entire bone. Due to the progressive nature of the disease, a long stem should be used regardless of any obvious more distal lesions. Press fit components rely on bony ingrowth, which is impaired by the post-operative radiation and chemotherapy.
Answer 2: MM is very sensitive to both radiation and directed chemotherapy. In fact surgery may be delayed in the presence of an impending pathology fracture in order to facilitate aggressive pre-transplant treatment and possibly an autologous stem cell transplant.
Answer 3: Myelomatous lesions have among the best potential for pathologic fracture healing (67%) as compared to other metastatic disease.
Answer 5: Work-up for myeloma involves a skeletal survey, because MM lesions will frequently (~30-50% of the time) be “cold”, or silent, on radionuclide bone scan.
