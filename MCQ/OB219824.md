## Question

A 27-year-old professional baseball pitcher presents to the orthopedic clinic. He recently underwent a routine preseason physical and an MRI of his throwing shoulder was completed. The pitcher performed slightly above his career average during his last season. He is left-hand dominant. He is concerned because the MRI reported a partial thickness rotator cuff tear, a posterior-superior labral tear, and increased humeral retroversion. On physical exam, he has approximately 10° less internal rotation on the left side compared to the right. His overall arc of motion is 5° less on the left. Shoulder impingement testing, rotator cuff testing, and instability testing are all negative. The patient asks you what treatment is best to allow him to continue pitching at the professional level. What is the most appropriate recommendation?

## Options

1. Rest from throwing
2. Posterior capsular release
3. Rotator cuff repair
4. Humeral rotational osteotomy
5. Continue pitching

## Correct option

**5. Continue pitching**

## Answer explanation

This professional baseball pitcher presents with common adaptive changes that are seen in an overhead athlete’s shoulder. The patient is asymptomatic and should be allowed to continue pitching (Answer 5).

Overhead throwing athletes develop adaptive changes due to the repetitive stresses associated with throwing. Many throwing athletes demonstrate asymptomatic but abnormal findings on MRI. Common adaptive changes include increased proximal humeral retroversion, alterations in the total arc of motion, thickening of the posterior capsule, and cystic changes in the posterior lateral humeral head. Asymptomatic rotator cuff tears, superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tears, and anterior or posterior labral tears are common findings on MRI in this population. Glenohumeral internal rotation deficiency (GIRD) is defined as a deficit of 15° of internal rotation and a deficit in total arc of motion of 10° or more. GIRD places a throwing athlete at an increased risk of injury. However, the clinician must be able to differentiate between adaptive and pathologic changes in the shoulder arc of motion.

*Gelber et al published a review article on “The Thrower’s Shoulder”.* *They review common adaptive changes that are seen in throwing athletes.* *They also review several studies of pitchers who had MRIs taken on asymptomatic shoulders.* *40-52% of asymptomatic pitchers had a rotator cuff tear, 48% had a SLAP tear, and 62% had anterior or posterior labral tears.* **They conclude** that treatment must be based on symptoms and examination.

*Noonan et al published a cross-sectional study of 220 professional pitchers.* **They report** that pitchers with GIRD displayed greater side-to-side differences in humeral retroversion. This greater humeral retroversion may place greater stress on the posterior shoulder structures. **The authors conclude** that their study does not result in any treatment recommendations.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Despite the MRI findings, the patient is asymptomatic and should not be restricted from pitching this season.
Answer 2: Posterior capsular release is reserved for patients who fail nonoperative management of GIRD.
Answer 3: Asymptomatic partial-thickness tears should not undergo operative repair.
Answer 4: Increased humeral retroversion is an adaptive change often seen in overhead athletes.
