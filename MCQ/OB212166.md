## Question

A 56-year-old female presents to your clinic with right foot pain and complains that she has "lost her arch". This has been progressive for several years and her pain and dysfunction are starting to affect her job where she is on her feet all day. On examination, she has pain over the medial aspect of the mid and hindfoot. She is unable to perform a single leg heel raise. Her hindfoot is supple with eversion/inversion. Ankle dorsiflexion improves 10 degrees when the knee is brought from extension to flexion. There is no evidence of instability at the tarsometatarsal joint. Her AP radiograph is seen in Figure A. Which of the following is the most appropriate surgical management to address her mid- and hindfoot deformities?

## Options

1. Posterior tibial tendon synovectomy, flexor digitorum longus transfer
2. Flexor digitorum longus transfer, lateral calcaneal slide osteotomy, gastrocnemius recession
3. Flexor digitorum longus transfer, medial calcaneal slide osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, cotton osteotomy, gastrocnemius recession
4. First tarsometatarsal arthrodesis, medial calcaneal slide osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, gastrocnemius recession
5. Triple arthrodesis

## Correct option

**3. Flexor digitorum longus transfer, medial calcaneal slide osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, cotton osteotomy, gastrocnemius recession**

## Answer explanation

This patient has stage IIB posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) with hindfoot equinus, which is best treated with a flexor digitorum longus (FDL) tendon transfer, a medial calcaneal slide osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, cotton osteotomy and gastrocnemius recession.

PTTI is the most common cause of adult flatfoot deformity and is often seen in middle-aged females. Sinus tarsi pain and difficulty performing a single-leg heel raise are common. In stage I, there is tenosynovitis of the posterior tibial tendon, but it still functions without deformity. This is often managed with bracing and therapy but occasionally requires a tenosynovectomy of the posterior tibial tendon. Stage IIa lacks the ability to perform a single-leg heel raise, but the hindfoot remains flexible. This is managed with FDL transfer and medial calcaneal slide osteotomy, because performing the tendon transfer alone would fail without bony realignment. In stage IIb, there is also forefoot abduction with talonavicular uncoverage which requires a lateral column lengthening in addition to the FDL transfer. Also often required is a cotton osteotomy (medial cuneiform opening wedge) which corrects for the compensatory forefoot supination seen with hindfoot valgus. Stage III represents rigid hindfoot deformity which requires arthrodesis. Stage IV is defined as having deltoid ligament insufficiency that alters congruity and stability of the tibiotalar joint. Often this stage is accompanied by rigid forefoot and hindfoot deformities. Treatment may involve repair/reconstruction of the deltoid ligament vs. tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis. Additionally, equinus contracture and in some cases, compensatory forefoot deformity, must be evaluated and addressed at the time of surgery. Performing the appropriate procedure is important in maximizing patient outcomes in this population.

*Deland et al. reviewed adult-acquired flatfoot deformity,* **noting** there is significant variability in its presentation. **They emphasize** that early diagnosis and treatment can help prevent progression. If surgery becomes necessary, achieving proper alignment and maintaining appropriate flexibility are the primary goals.

*Flores et al. reviewed the anatomy, staging, and imaging of adult-acquired flatfoot deformity.* *They review the progression of disease based on the anatomic structures involved,* **noting** that the posterior tibial tendon is first affected, followed by the spring ligament and talocalcaneal ligaments, and finally the deltoid ligament. They finish by reviewing the different stages of PTTI and associated surgical recommendations, which vary by the specific anatomic deformity present.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Posterior tibial tendon synovectomy and FDL transfer would not be appropriate for a stage IIB deformity as it does not address the bony deformity.
Answer 2: FDL transfer and gastrocnemius recession are appropriate, but a lateral calcaneal slide osteotomy would be used for varus deformity, not valgus hindfoot deformity, as seen here.
Answer 4: First tarsometatarsal (TMT) arthrodesis would be appropriate in cases of TMT instability associated with flatfoot deformity and would not be the best choice in this patient.
Answer 5: Triple arthrodesis would be used in stage III PTTI, where there is a rigid hindfoot deformity.
