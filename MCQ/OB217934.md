## Question

A 25-year-old professional ice hockey player sustains an anterior shoulder dislocation after being checked against the boards. This is his third total dislocation and his first since undergoing arthroscopic labral repair. You obtain an MRI which demonstrates a medium-large Hill-Sachs lesion and anterior glenoid bone loss measuring 20%. You discuss surgical management with the player and his agent and ultimately decide to perform a latarjet procedure to address his bone loss. Which of the following is true regarding possible complications after latarjet?

## Options

1. Long-term decreases in shoulder internal range of motion are frequently seen
2. Glenohumeral arthritis occurs due to placement of the coracoid graft too medial on the glenoid surface
3. Graft resorption and loosening is most likely to occur > 2 years after index procedure
4. Injury affecting branch of lateral cord of brachial plexus can occur due to aggressive intraoperative retraction
5. Sling must be worn for 6 weeks to protect against subscapularis rupture given its complete detachment during approach

## Correct option

**4. Injury affecting branch of lateral cord of brachial plexus can occur due to aggressive intraoperative retraction**

## Answer explanation

Musculocutaneous nerve palsy is possible after undergoing a latarjet procedure, and is usually caused by overaggressive medial retraction or instrumentation around the conjoint tendon.

A latarjet procedure involves open transfer of the coracoid into the antero-inferior glenoid defect. This is typically performed through a deltopectoral approach and a subscapularis splitting technique. While results can be good/excellent, there is a relatively high rate of complication. These include nonunion, graft lysis/resorption, stiffness, glenohumeral arthritis, and nerve injury. The axillary and musculocutaneous nerves are the two most commonly affected and are typically neuropraxias that resolve on their own. Traditional teaching has noted the musculocutaneous nerve is damaged more commonly during retraction and instrumentation around the conjoint tendon, while the axillary nerve is more affected during fixation of the graft itself. A musculocutaneous nerve palsy can affect elbow flexion strength and may also cause paresthesias over the volar lateral forearm, as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is the terminal branch of the musculocutaneous nerve.

*Richards et al. published a case report describing nerve injury during bony transfers around the shoulder.* *The most common nerve affected was the musculocutaneous nerve, with 6 of the 8 patients studied having complete musculocutaneous nerve palsies.* *They attributed injury to the neurovascular structures in this region to inadequate knowledge of regional anatomy, use of axillary incisions with poor exposure, and failure to properly identify the nerve during the case.*

*Young et al. reviewed the latarjet procedure for the management of bone loss in anterior shoulder instability.* *They offered several technical, "pearls," including the use of a "two-finger," screw tightening technique during coracoid fixation to avoid fracture of the coracoid graft as well as ensuring no remaining lateral overhang of the graft relative to the glenohumeral joint.* **They concluded** that, overall, the latarjet procedure is a safe and reliable method of managing anterior shoulder instability that is associated with bone loss and most of the reported complications can be avoided with good patient selection and meticulous surgical technique.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Range of motion is typically decreased in external rotation postoperatively, as the bony block and capsular repair provided by the latarjet tend to increase stiffness in this direction.
Answer 2: Glenohumeral arthritis is a risk after a latarjet procedure, but excessive lateral placement of the graft compared to the native glenoid surface is thought to contribute rather than medial placement of the graft.
Answer 3: While uncommon overall, graft reabsorption that may contribute to loosening occurs more frequently in the first 6 months postoperatively and would be an unlikely long-term complication after the graft is fully healed.
Answer 5: A sling is provided in order to allow the coracoid graft to heal to the glenoid, not necessarily to prevent subscapularis rupture as the subscapularis is typically split, not completely detached, during the approach.
