## Question

A 36-year-old male presents after a left shoulder radiograph incidentally noted a lesion in the humerus. His imaging is demonstrated in Figures A and B, and you recommend observation with repeat imaging in 6 months. At his follow-up visit, you note an increase in the overall size of the lesion, and the patient now has vague pain in the proximal humerus. At this point, what would be the most appropriate next treatment?

## Options

1. Continued observation
2. Chemotherapy
3. Radiofrequency ablation
4. Radiation therapy follow by wide resection
5. Extended intralesional curettage with bone grafting

## Correct option

**5. Extended intralesional curettage with bone grafting**

## Answer explanation

This patient presented with an incidentally found chondroid lesion. Observation is an appropriate option for asymptomatic lesions that do not demonstrate aggressive radiographic features. In this case, the lesion has demonstrated growth on serial imaging, and treatment should be considered. There is evidence to support intralesional curettage and bone grafting for enchondromas and low-grade chondrosarcomas, making this the best option of the listed answer choices (Answer 5).

Chondroid lesions are often incidentally noted, and the majority of them can be managed with observation and serial imaging. The diagnosis can be made radiographically, with lesions demonstrating ring and arc calcifications indicative of chondroid matrix. Chondroid lesions present on a spectrum from benign lesions such as enchondromas to aggressive malignant pathologies such as chondrosarcoma. Chondrosarcoma can be classified as low, intermediate, or high grade. High-grade cartilage lesions are typically radiation and chemotherapy-resistant and are treated with wide resection. Low-grade lesions like enchondromas can be treated with intralesional curettage and bone grafting, and there is now some evidence to support curettage in low-grade chondrosarcomas. Indications for curettage include symptomatic lesions, pathologic fracture, lesions with radiographic suspicion for low-grade chondrosarcoma, or lesions that have changed in size or morphology on serial imaging (as in this case).

*Levy et al. conducted a retrospective review of incidentally noted enchondromas of the proximal humerus.* *They included 57 patients who had been referred to their oncology clinic after an enchondroma had been found during a work-up for shoulder pain.* **They noted** that 82% of patients had MRI findings consistent with other shoulder pathology (rotator cuff pathology, biceps tendinopathy, etc) unrelated to the enchondroma. **The authors concluded** that incidentally noted enchondromas are commonly identified during the work-up of shoulder pain, and that the majority of patients have other pathology present, which drives symptoms rather than the enchondroma itself.

*Akoh et al conducted a cost analysis study of 55 patients who presented with enchondromas.* *They recorded the number of pre-referral imaging studies and follow-up studies each patient received, with a minimum follow-up time of 12 months.* **They noted** that 52/55 lesions were stable and did not show interval growth on imaging. The median pre-referral costs per patient were $383 for radiographs and $3,969 for MRI studies. The median follow-up imaging costs per patient were $1326.23 and $2,240.64 for radiographs and MRI, respectively. The median number of follow-up visits was 4 for stable lesions and 5 for lesions that exhibited growth. **The authors concluded** that follow-up imaging with radiographs alone is reasonable given the low risk of growth, and MRI follow-up studies should be limited as these incur additional costs to the patient without significant added value.

*Chen et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on intralesional extended curettage as a treatment option for grade 1 (low-grade) chondrosarcoma.* *They included 10 studies with 394 patients.* *A total of 214 patients underwent intralesional curettage, and 180 underwent wide resection.* Intralesional curettage had a significantly lower complication rate and better functional outcome. There were no differences in rates of local recurrence or metastatic disease between the two surgical techniques. **The authors concluded** that intralesional curettage may be a suitable option for low-grade chondrosarcoma with improved functional and surgical outcomes compared to wide resection.

Figure A shows a shoulder radiograph with a geographic lesion about the proximal humerus. Note the ring and arc calcifications suggestive of cartilage matrix. Figure B is a post-contrast T1-weighted fat-suppressed MRI image that further highlights the ring and arc calcifications within the lesion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This lesion has demonstrated growth on serial imaging. It is reasonable to proceed with treatment at this point.
Answer 2: Cartilage lesions (except de-differentiated chondrosarcoma) are typically resistant to chemotherapy
Answer 3: Radiofrequency ablation is not typically performed for cartilage lesions
Answer 4: While wide resection can be considered if there is a concern for chondrosarcoma, radiation is typically not performed, as chondrosarcoma is typically resistant to radiation therapy.
