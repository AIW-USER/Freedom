## Question

A professional minor league baseball catcher is hoping to be called up to a major league team in the spring, but he has been having a nagging, constant pain in the dorsomedial aspect of his foot for the last five months, particularly when jumping from a crouched position. Radiographs were negative for fracture, but physical therapy, anti-inflammatories, and conservative measures have not helped his pain. An MRI is obtained and is shown in Figure A. Which of the following is he currently most at risk for with this injury?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Arch collapse and acquired flatfoot deformity
2. Cavovarus foot deformity
3. Nonunion
4. Posterior tibialis tendon insufficiency (PTTI)
5. Transfer metatarsalgia

## Correct option

**3. Nonunion**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a navicular stress fracture, as shown in Figure A, and is at risk for delayed union with possible nonunion given the duration of his symptoms and activities as a professional athlete.

Tarsal navicular stress fractures are relatively rare fractures of the midfoot that, outside of frank trauma, occur due to repetitive microstress. They are more frequently seen in athletes that run on hard surfaces and have an apparent predilection for baseball players. Symptoms are sometimes vague with an insidious onset, so a high index of suspicion is required to appropriately diagnose and care for these injuries. If radiographs show no evidence of fracture and the index of suspicion is high or the patient has failed attempts at conservative management and restricted weight-bearing, an MRI is the best test to confirm the diagnosis, given that CT scans may give false-negative results in the absence of a frank cortical disruption. Initial treatment for navicular stress fractures is typically immobilization with non-weightbearing for 6-8 weeks, but in high-level or professional athletes who are unlikely to tolerate a prolonged recovery course, ORIF is indicated to prevent progression to a painful nonunion.

*Shakked et al. reviewed tarsal navicular stress fractures.* **The authors note** that this entity is more common in athletes and that management is debated. **They conclude** that because these fractures tend to have poor healing potential related to the unique anatomy and vascular supply of the tarsal navicular, there is a trend towards operative management to allow definitive healing shown on cross-sectional imaging and earlier return to play with a low risk of re-injury.

*Gross et al. provide a sports topical review of navicular stress fractures.* **The authors note** that a delay in diagnosis and improper management can cause long-term functional deficits and pain due to nonunion. **They conclude** that though navicular stress fractures can be treated effectively both surgically and conservatively with good clinical outcomes if one has a high index of suspicion, the patient is best served when the athletic level and fracture pattern are taken into account, with surgery being proposed for athletes who fail conservative management or who cannot tolerate a prolonged recovery course.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Acute midfoot collapse with flatfoot deformity is unlikely in the setting of a tarsal navicular stress fracture. This would be expected as the chronic sequela of PTTI.
Answer 2: A cavovarus foot with an elevated longitudinal arch would not be the expected outcome of a navicular stress fracture.
Answer 4: Though the posterior tibialis tendon does insert onto the navicular, a stress fracture would not likely lead to acute PTTI, which represents a chronic condition.
Answer 5: Transfer metatarsalgia typically occurs after procedures that effectively shorten the first ray, and would not necessarily directly be caused by nonunion of a tarsal navicular fracture.
