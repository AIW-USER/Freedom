Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Hallux Valgus Bunion

## Question

During a lateral release in hallux valgus surgery, which structure is typically released from its insertion on the lateral sesamoid and proximal phalanx?

## Options

1. Flexor hallucis longus
2. Extensor hallucis brevis
3. Adductor hallucis tendon
4. Abductor hallucis tendon

## Correct option

**3. Adductor hallucis tendon**

## Answer explanation

The adductor hallucis pulls the hallux into valgus and must be released to allow the toe to return to a neutral position.

Pathoanatomy of hallux valgus— The Vicious Cycle of Progressive Deformity

Once initiated, hallux valgus follows a self-perpetuating biomechanical cascade that progressively worsens the deformity:

First Metatarsal Deviation: The first metatarsal drifts medially (metatarsus primus varus), increasing the first-second intermetatarsal angle (IMA). As the metatarsal head moves medially, the sesamoid complex (the two sesamoids embedded within the flexor hallucis brevis tendons and connected to the proximal phalanx by the plantar plate) remains in its anatomical position, anchored to the second metatarsal by the deep transverse intermetatarsal ligament. The metatarsal head effectively subluxates off the sesamoid complex — the sesamoids are not displaced laterally; rather, the metatarsal head migrates medially away from them. This creates the appearance of "sesamoid subluxation" on radiographs, though the sesamoids have not moved.

Medial Capsular Attenuation: As the metatarsal head moves medially, the medial capsule and the medial collateral ligament of the first MTP joint are progressively stretched and attenuated, losing their restraining force against further valgus angulation.

Lateral Soft Tissue Contracture: Simultaneously, the lateral capsule, the adductor hallucis tendon (which inserts into the lateral sesamoid and the base of the proximal phalanx via the conjoined tendon), and the lateral head of the flexor hallucis brevis become contracted, creating a laterally directed bowstring force that actively pulls the hallux further into valgus.

Extensor and Flexor Tendon Bowstringing: As the hallux deviates laterally, the extensor hallucis longus (EHL) and flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendons shift lateral to the mechanical axis of the first MTP joint. These tendons, now lateral to the joint axis, become dynamic deforming forces — instead of acting as pure flexors and extensors, they act as lateral deviators, perpetuating and worsening the valgus angulation with each step. This bowstringing effect is a critical component of the self-perpetuating nature of the deformity.

Medial Eminence (Bunion) Formation: The uncovered medial aspect of the metatarsal head becomes prominent subcutaneously — this is the "bunion." It is not an exostosis or bony overgrowth but rather the normally covered medial metatarsal head that has become exposed by medial migration of the metatarsal away from the sesamoids. Reactive bursitis (adventitious bursa) develops over the prominence due to pressure from footwear, producing the painful, inflamed, erythematous swelling that is the most common presenting complaint. Over time, osteophyte formation on the medial eminence may contribute to the prominence.

Pronation of the Hallux: As the deformity progresses, the hallux pronates (rotates in the axial plane) due to the asymmetric pull of the adductor hallucis and the unconstrained sesamoid mechanism. The nail of the great toe faces laterally rather than dorsally — this pronation component is increasingly recognised as an important element of the deformity that must be addressed during surgical correction.

Hallux Valgus Interphalangeus: In some patients, an additional valgus angulation occurs at the hallux interphalangeal joint (due to a laterally angled distal articular surface of the proximal phalanx — the distal phalanx deviates laterally relative to the proximal phalanx). This component must be identified preoperatively as it will persist after MTP-level correction if not addressed.

Second Toe Consequences:
Progressive hallux valgus crowds the second toe, which may develop a crossover toe deformity (the hallux underlies or overlies the second toe), second MTP joint instability (with plantar plate attenuation and dorsal subluxation), hammer toe or claw toe deformity of the second digit, and transfer metatarsalgia (increased loading beneath the lesser metatarsal heads as the first ray loses its weight-bearing function due to the deformity).

Answer: 3
