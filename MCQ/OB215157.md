## Question

A 63-year-old diabetic female presents with progressively worsening right knee pain over the last 10 months. She underwent a right total knee arthroplasty 3 years ago and her immediate post-op course was uncomplicated. She denies any fevers, chills, or wound drainage. Examination reveals a well-epithelialized incision without surrounding erythema, drainage, or fluctuance. Pain is elicited with range of motion. Her current radiographs are shown in figures A and B. Recent laboratory studies include WBC count of 7.2k cells/mm^3 (reference 4.5k - 11k cells/mm^3), ESR 41 mm/hr (reference 0-20 mm/hr), and C-reactive protein 6.3 mg/L (reference <3.0 mg/L). What is the most appropriate next step in treatment?

## Options

1. Arthrotomy, debridement, and polyethylene exchange
2. One-stage exchange arthroplasty
3. Two-stage exchange arthroplasty
4. Parenteral antibiotics
5. Knee aspiration with fluid analysis

## Correct option

**5. Knee aspiration with fluid analysis**

## Answer explanation

The patients presention is highly concerning for a chronic prosthetic joint infection (PJI) given her duration of symptoms, radiographic findings, and laboratory data. The next best step would be a synovial fluid aspiration for culture, gram stain, cell count with differential, and possible alpha-defensin.

PJIs are highly morbid complications of adult reconstructive surgery and are associated with tremendous increased health care expenditures. Chronic infections, by definition, are present for >4-6 weeks and can result in the formation of glycocalyx biofilm that further complicates treatment. Confirmation of the diagnosis is dependent on a battery of tests, clinical findings, and reported history. An initial assessment with serum laboratory markers includes ESR and CRP. Patients with elevated inflammatory markers or high suspicion for PJI should undergo a synovial fluid aspiration. A leukocyte count >~3,000 cells/μL and >80% neutrophils are highly suggestive of prosthetic joint infections. Cutoffs vary by study and sensitivity/specificity desired (some as low as 1500) but the key being an order of magnitude lower counts than native knees. Further confirmation can be made with alpha-defensin analysis, but this comes at an increased cost and may not be readily available in all institutions.

*Trampuz et al. performed a retrospective review of the synovial fluid analysis of 133 patients undergoing revision total knee arthroplasty.* **The authors reported** that synovial fluid leukocyte counts were significantly higher in prosthetic joint infections than aseptic failures and reported a cutoff of 1700 leukocytes/μL having a 94% sensitivity and 88% specificity for the diagnosis of prosthetic joint infection. **They concluded** that a synovial fluid leukocyte count >1700 cells/μL and >65% neutrophils are a sensitive and specific test for the diagnosis of prosthetic joint infections.

*Mason et al. performed a retrospective study of the synovial fluid analysis of 86 patients undergoing revision total knee arthroplasty.* **They reported** the aseptic failure cases to have a mean leukocyte count of 645 cells/mm^3 and 27.3% neutrophils compared to 25951 cells/mm^3 and 72.8% neutrophils for septic failures. **They concluded** that synovial fluid aspirate with WBC count >2500 cells/mm^3 and >60% neutrophils are highly suggestive of prosthetic joint infection.

*Kersey et al. performed a retrospective study of the synovial fluid analysis of 79 patients undergoing revision total knee arthroplasty.* **They reported** the mean leukocyte count to be 782 cells/μL and 13% neutrophils. **They concluded** patients with primary knee arthroplasty, a leukocyte count <2000 cells/μL and <50% neutrophils to have a 98% negative predictive value for prosthetic joint infection.
