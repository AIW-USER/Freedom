## Question

A 35-year-old man presents with a history of right–leg compartment syndrome following a motorcycle crash, complicated by delayed diagnosis and treatment. He has persistent functional deficits in the right lower extremity and currently relies on an ankle-foot orthosis (AFO) for ambulation. He reports pain and discomfort from the brace and is interested in potential surgical options to eliminate the need for it. On examination, his ankle is supple, demonstrating 40° of passive plantarflexion and 20° of passive dorsiflexion, unchanged with knee flexion or extension. Motor testing reveals 5/5 plantarflexion strength, 0/5 dorsiflexion strength, and 4/5 eversion strength. What is the most appropriate intervention to restore active ankle dorsiflexion?

## Options

1. Peroneal longus to brevis tendon transfer
2. Achilles lengthening, continued AFO use with physical therapy
3. Flexor hallucis longus transfer to the dorsal navicular
4. Tibialis anterior tendon transfer to the dorsum of the talus
5. Posterior tibialis transfer through the interosseous membrane to the dorsum of the foot

## Correct option

**5. Posterior tibialis transfer through the interosseous membrane to the dorsum of the foot**

## Answer explanation

In chronic foot drop secondary to weak/deficient dorsiflexors (devitalized anterior compartment, in this scenario), the most reliable functional restoration of ankle dorsiflexion is a posterior tibialis (PT) tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot through the interosseous membrane (answer choice 5).

Foot drop, or the inability to dorsiflex at the ankle, can be a result of many etiologies. Most commonly, it results from peroneal nerve palsy. However, compartment syndrome involving the anterior compartment can cause devitalized anterior musculature, leading to foot drop. For flexible foot drop, nonoperative modalities such as therapy and AFOs play a role. However, the downsides of bracing are decreased ankle range of motion and patient discomfort with prolonged bracing. Surgical goals for flexible foot drop include improving gait and eliminating the need for a brace. Most commonly, this is performed via tendon transfer. Transfer of the PT to the dorsum of the foot assists with dorsiflexion. Many techniques exist for this transfer, including transfer through the interosseous membrane vs the circumtibial approach. Despite the different techniques, the overall goal remains the same: restoration of ankle dorsiflexion by transfer of the PT to the dorsum of the foot.

*Klima et al. published a study surveying Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) members on the management of compartment syndrome.* *A total of 224 respondents provided their experience in the management of compartment syndrome at either academic or community trauma centres.* Differences were found in the monitoring of potential compartment syndromes and the rates of prophylactic fasciotomies between the two different environments. **The authors concluded** that variability in practice between institutions exists and recommend a written protocol to reduce adverse events.

*Stevoska et al. published a systematic review of tendon transfers used in foot drop.* *A total of 37 studies were analyzed to evaluate various tendon transfer techniques used to treat foot drop and assess functional outcomes.* The most common technique involved an interosseous transfer of the PT tendon to the dorsum of the foot. Overall, patients reported improvements in mobility, decreased need for ankle-foot orthoses, and increased functional scores. In conclusion, **the authors found** that a PT transfer to the dorsum of the foot can provide improved patient outcomes and decrease the need for bracing.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Peroneus longus (PL) to peroneus brevis (PB) tendon transfer is used to improve eversion strength through the foot when PB is weak or deficient. This transfer is most often indicated in the setting of a cavovarus foot deformity or chronic PB tears, but it would not restore dorsiflexion in this patient.
Answer choice 2: This patient does not have a fixed equinus contracture, as demonstrated by his Silverskiold exam; therefore, an Achilles lengthening is not warranted. As the patient has already trialled one year of AFO use and is searching for surgical fixation of his foot drop, this answer choice is not appropriate.
Answer choice 3: A flexor hallucis longus (FHL) transfer to the dorsal navicular and would theoretically help with regaining dorsiflexion of the foot. However, the FHL muscle belly does not allow adequate excursion to reasonably restore ankle dorsiflexion, nor does it have the strength that the PT tendon does. For that reason, FHL transfers are typically reserved for chronic Achilles ruptures to augment plantarflexion.
Answer choice 4: This patient has a deficient anterior compartment, rendering the tibialis anterior (TA) nonfunctional. Transferring the TA to the dorsum of the talus would not restore dorsiflexion of the ankle. In patients undergoing a midfoot amputation, with a firing TA, this transfer can be useful to prevent equinus contractures.
