## Question

A 62-year-old male who underwent a right uncomplicated primary total knee arthroplasty 8 months ago is scheduled for a routine dental procedure. The procedure will involve manipulation of the gingival tissue. Additionally, he has a history of diabetes mellitus with his most recent A1c being 9.0. He is otherwise healthy. What would the most appropriate recommendation for antibiotic prophylaxis for this patient?

## Options

1. Daptomycin 600 milligrams intravenous 2 hours prior to procedure
2. No prophylaxis is needed
3. Prophylaxis with cephalexin 2 g oral within 1 hour of the procedure is appropriate
4. Delay dental treatment and refer to his PCP for diabetes management
5. Order Hb A1c and blood glucose levels

## Correct option

**3. Prophylaxis with cephalexin 2 g oral within 1 hour of the procedure is appropriate**

## Answer explanation

Antibiotic prophylaxis for a dental procedure may be appropriate for this patient given there is an elevated risk for a prosthetic joint infection in the setting of diabetes with an elevated A1c. The optimal oral antibiotic would be 2g of either cephalexin, amoxicilin, or cephradine within 1 hour of the dental procedure if there are no penicillin allergies.

Patients with hip and knee arthroplasty implants have a potentially increased risk of prosthetic joint infections following dental procedures. However, the current evidence of routine use of antibiotics for dental procedures is lacking as there has not been a strong correlation made between dental procedures and prosthetic joint infections. Current recommendations from multidisciplinary societies recommend the use of antibiotic prophylaxis in high-risk cases. Such cases include immunocompromised patients, previous prosthetic joint infections, and uncontrolled diabetes.

Rees provided three case studies of patients with prosthetic joints undergoing dental procedures. The author reported using antibiotic prophylaxis in patients that are immunocompromised (organ transplant, AIDS, inflammatory arthritis, and chemotherapy patients), diabetic, and a history of previous prosthetic joint infection.

*Quinn et al. reported on the American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons (AAOS) appropriate use guidelines of patients with orthopedic implants undergoing dental procedures based on a multidisciplinary panel.* *The panel was presented 64 cases, of which only 10% of the scenarios were deemed appropriate for antibiotic treatment.* The basis for antibiotic prophylaxis was made on the dental procedure manipulating the gingival or periapical tissue, patient immunocompromised state, diabetes diagnosis, glycemic control (A1c and blood glucose), timing since index procedure, and history of prosthetic joint infection.

*Rethman et al. summarized the recommendations made by the AAOS and the American Dental Association (ADA) regarding the prevention of orthopedic implant infections in patients undergoing dental procedures.* *The authors were able to make a limited recommendation to discontinue the routine use of oral antibiotics for dental procedures, an inconclusive recommendation for the use of topical antibiotics, and a consensus recommendation of maintaining good oral hygiene.*

Incorrect answers
Answers 1: The use of IV antibiotics routinely for dental procedures is no longer recommended. Patients that may require oral antibiotic prophylaxis can be given oral cephalexin/amoxicillin/cephradine.
Answer 2: The patient has an elevated risk of infection given there is a history of diabetes that is not well controlled. A prophylactic antibiotic should be given.
Answer 4 and 5: In the setting of unknown A1c and a diagnosis of diabetes it is appropriate to delay dental care until further diabetes management is performed by the patient's primary care provider. The patient already has an A1c that is relatively high. Repeating blood tests would not add to management.
