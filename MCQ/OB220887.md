## Question

A 19-year-old college quarterback presents for evaluation of right foot pain after taking a sack during a game. He states that he was trying to run away from a defender, but his leg got caught underneath another player, which caused a twisting motion to his foot. He has midfoot swelling and plantar ecchymosis. Which of the following is most likely to be present on weight-bearing radiographs?

## Options

1. A fleck of bone present just proximal and lateral to the base of the fifth metatarsal
2. A sclerotic line through the calcaneus
3. Dorsal displacement of the 1st metatarsal proximal base on a lateral radiograph
4. Proximal displacement of the medial sesamoid of the great toe
5. Positioning of the talar head directly superior to the navicular on a lateral radiograph

## Correct option

**3. Dorsal displacement of the 1st metatarsal proximal base on a lateral radiograph**

## Answer explanation

The patient has physical exam findings and a mechanism of injury consistent with a Lisfranc injury. Characteristic findings on plain radiographs include dorsal displacement of the proximal base of the 1st metatarsal on a lateral radiograph due to the presence of ligamentous instability (Answer 2).

Lisfranc injuries are uncommon injuries of the midfoot and frequently occur due to a rotational force about a hyper-plantarflexed foot. On exam, patients may have plantar ecchymosis as well as midfoot swelling and associated tenderness. Radiographic signs suggestive of the presence of such an injury include widening between the first and second rays, dorsal displacement of the proximal base of the first metatarsal, disruption of the medial column line, or discontinuity in the lines drawn from the medial base of the second metatarsal to the medial middle cuneiform or from the medial 4th metatarsal base to the cuboid. Weight-bearing films are warranted if non-weight-bearing films are inconclusive and the patient can tolerate them. Contralateral radiographs may also aid in detecting subtle irregularities present in lower-energy injuries.

*Shakked published a review of Lisfranc injuries sustained in the athlete population.* *She reviewed the anatomy of the Lisfranc ligament, physical exam findings, predisposing risk factors, radiographic findings, non-operative and operative strategies, outcomes, and rehabilitation protocols.* **The author concluded** that Lisfranc injuries in athletes tend to be best treated with operative treatment to minimize treatment failure, though the ideal choice of fixation remains to be determined.

*Seybold and Coetzee reviewed Lisfranc injuries specifically in the athletic population.* *They reviewed the lower-energy mechanism of injury seen in athletes and the unique symptomology that patients may report in more subtle injuries.* **They highlighted** the importance of weight-bearing radiographs and the role that advanced imaging has in diagnosing more subtle injuries. They also described the various surgical implants that can be utilized, which include Kirschner wire fixation, fully threaded screws, one-third tubular plates, and suture buttons. **They concluded** that the optimal method to address these injuries is yet to be determined and requires additional research.

Mulcahy provided an update on the current literature regarding Lisfranc injuries and gave an overview of radiologic findings consistent with this diagnosis. On the AP radiograph view, they recommended careful assessment of the relationship between the 2nd metatarsal and middle cuneiform, the third metatarsal and lateral cuneiform, the first metatarsal and medial cuneiform, and the medial cuneiform and second metatarsal. On the oblique view, they emphasized the assessment of the relationship between the middle cuneiform and the second metatarsal, as well as the lateral cuneiform and the third metatarsal. Finally, on the lateral radiograph, they recommend assessing the relationship between the long axis of the talus and second metatarsal, the medial cuneiform and fifth metatarsal, and the dorsal aspects of the tarsometatarsal joints. They concluded that these uncommon injuries can have detrimental sequelae if missed and require careful interrogation of imaging to make a diagnosis systematically.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: A fleck of bone off the base of the fifth metatarsal represents an avulsion fracture of that bone. A fleck of bone between the first and second metatarsals is called a “fleck sign” and is more suggestive of a Lisfranc injury.
Answer 2: A sclerotic line through the calcaneus is more consistent with a stress fracture and would present with more chronic rather than acute pain.
Answer 4: Proximal displacement of the medial sesamoid of the great toe is suggestive of turf toe, not a Lisfranc injury.
Answer 5: A talar head located directly superior to the navicular is suggestive of a medial subtalar dislocation, not a Lisfranc injury.
