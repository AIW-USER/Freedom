## Question

A 62-year-old male with poorly controlled diabetes presents to the clinic with concerns about a diabetic foot ulcer. On exam, he has a two-centimeter plantar foot ulcer; he lacks plantar sensation and this ulcer probes to bone. His laboratory examination demonstrates elevated ESR and CRP. Which of the following is the most specific for diagnosing osteomyelitis?

## Options

1. HbA1c over 15%
2. Bone biopsy with cultures and pathology
3. Elevated ESR and CRP
4. Positive probe-to-bone test
5. Radiographs demonstrating cortical erosions

## Correct option

**2. Bone biopsy with cultures and pathology**

## Answer explanation

The gold standard for diagnosing osteomyelitis in diabetic foot ulcers is a bone biopsy (answer choice 2).

Osteomyelitis (OM) is a common complication of diabetic foot ulcers that is often missed or underdiagnosed. The diagnosis is critical in the setting of diabetic foot ulcers as osteomyelitis has been reported to increase the risk of amputation up to four times. Imaging, laboratory tests, and clinical tests are useful and often readily available. However, a bone biopsy with definitive microbiological and histopathological diagnoses remains the gold standard of diagnosis. Ultimately, the surgical treatment of OM in the setting of diabetic foot ulcers is surgical removal of the infected and necrotic bone.

*Pinzur et al. provided a guideline for diabetic foot care according to the Diabetes Committee of the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society.* Along with adequate screening examinations, the article highlights patient education as a cornerstone of prevention. The authors include many treatment recommendations from nonoperative to operative management. This article provides an overall guideline on diabetic foot care to decrease the morbidity associated with diabetic foot wounds.

*Grayson et al. published a prospective study to evaluate the effectiveness of the probe-to-bone (PTB) test when diagnosing osteomyelitis in diabetic foot ulcers.* *This study included 76 diabetic foot ulcers in 75 patients.* The PTB test demonstrated a sensitivity of 66%, a specificity of 85%, a positive predictive value of 89%, and a negative predictive value of 56% when detecting osteomyelitis. This study suggests a positive PTB test is strongly correlated with the presence of underlying osteomyelitis and further imaging is often recommended. **The authors concluded** that a PTB test should be included in the initial assessment of diabetic patients with foot ulcers to aid in the detection of osteomyelitis.

*Malhotra et al. published a review article on the diagnosis and management of osteomyelitis in diabetic foot ulcers.* *The authors make note of the high risk of limb amputation in the setting of osteomyelitis and therefore emphasize the early diagnosis to help alleviate morbid complications.* Imaging, including MRI, is a powerful tool to evaluate the extent of infection. Bone biopsy with histopathology and culture remains the gold standard for definite diagnosis. Treatment for osteomyelitis in diabetic foot ulcers involves surgical debridement and targeted antibiotic therapy. **The authors conclude** that timely diagnosis and intervention are paramount to optimize outcomes with these wounds.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: HbA1c reflects the long-term blood glucose in diabetic patients. Elevated levels can increase the risk of developing osteomyelitis, but are not specific for osteomyelitis.
Answer choice 3: Elevated ESR and CRP are nonspecific findings of systemic or localized infection. These lab markers lack specificity for diagnosing osteomyelitis.
Answer choice 4: While a positive probe-to-bone (PTB) test is a useful bedside tool for diagnosing osteomyelitis, it is not as specific as a bone biopsy with histopathology and cultures.
Answer choice 5: Cortical erosions on plain radiographs can be suggestive of osteomyelitis and are often seen in late-stage osteomyelitis. However, these findings are not highly specific as other conditions can demonstrate similar findings.
