## Question

A 65-year-old male underwent a left total hip arthroplasty approximately six months ago via a posterior approach. At his two-week postoperative visit, he was found to have a foot drop on the right side and was subsequently taken back to the operating room for common peroneal nerve decompression. Since then, he has had a persistent foot drop without any improvement over the past six months. He has been ambulating with a brace but finds it uncomfortable and usually does not wear it. On examination, his ankle motion is supple, but he cannot actively dorsiflex his ankle past neutral. What is the next best treatment option to discuss with the patient?

## Options

1. Continued observation and use of his brace
2. Repeat common peroneal nerve decompression
3. Ankle arthrodesis
4. Posterior tibial tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot
5. Below knee amputation

## Correct option

**4. Posterior tibial tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot**

## Answer explanation

This patient has suffered a common peroneal nerve injury, most likely due to positioning during his total hip arthroplasty. Despite a prior nerve decompression and adequate time to allow for nerve recovery, he continues to have a persistent foot drop. Of the available options, a tendon transfer procedure would be the next best option for him (Answer 4).

Common peroneal nerve (CPN) injuries are the most common compressive neuropathy of the lower extremity. Patients typically present with foot drop and paresthesias involving the dorsum of the foot. Common peroneal nerve palsies can occur due to a variety of reasons, including trauma, direct lacerations, compressive masses (such as a ganglion cyst), iatrogenic sources, or prolonged compression/stretch from patient positioning during surgery (as in this case). As with all nerve palsies, prompt recognition and treatment are critical to optimize recovery. For acute lacerations, repair or nerve grafting can be considered. For cases in which the nerve has been compressed (either by a mass or by surgical positioning), decompression is performed. The timing of decompression is controversial and varies based on patient-specific factors, but many believe a trial of nonoperative management and observation for at least three months is appropriate to see if the palsy will recover. Tendon transfers are considered when there has been no significant improvement in function over a prolonged period of time, and the patient no longer tolerates bracing. Ankle arthrodesis can be considered as well, but it is typically reserved for patients who have fixed deformities or arthritis of the ankle joint.

*Poage et al. authored a review article summarizing the evaluation and management of CPN palsies.* *They review anatomic considerations for performing CPN decompression, as well as their preferred method for tendon transfers.*

*Fortier et al. similarly wrote an updated review of CPN palsy secondary to nerve entrapment.* *They review the most common sites of compression and briefly discuss surgical considerations and outcomes following decompression.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has had >6 months of observation and no longer tolerates his brace, making this option not ideal.
Answer 2: A repeat decompression would be unlikely to provide significant benefit since he has already failed to improve despite a prior decompression.
Answer 3: Ankle arthrodesis is typically reserved for patients with a fixed ankle deformity or with significant arthritis. This patient’s ankle motion is supple.
Answer 5: This option is extremely morbid and would not be indicated in a patient who is a good candidate for tendon transfers.
