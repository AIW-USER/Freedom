## Question

A 23-year-old ballet dancer returns to your office continuing to complain of posterior ankle pain, worsened in maximum plantarflexion. She has been taking NSAIDs in conjunction with physical therapy for 3 months, and also took a 1-month break from performing while wearing a CAM boot. Despite all of these modalities she has not shown improvement. Her MRI is shown in Figure A. You are planning to address this surgically, using the approach shown on the lateral aspect of the ankle in Figure B. What is her diagnosis and which structure is most at risk with this approach?

## Options

1. Flexor hallucis longus tendinitis, sural nerve
2. Posterior tibial tendonitis, sural nerve
3. Symptomatic os trigonum, sural nerve
4. Symptomatic os trigonum, superficial peroneal nerve
5. Posterolateral tubercle fracture, sural nerve

## Correct option

**3. Symptomatic os trigonum, sural nerve**

## Answer explanation

The patient above has a symptomatic os trigonum and structure most at risk with this posterolateral approach is the sural nerve.

Os trigonum syndrome is a result of bony or soft tissue compression impinging in the posterior ankle. This impingement can be from an elongated process from the lateral tubercle on the talus (Stieda process) fracturing from repetitive trauma, or as an accessory ossicle from a failure to fuse. Either of which can be a source of pain and may be seen with FHL tenosynovitis. Treatment centers on anti-inflammatory modalities with physical therapy and activity modification. If these fail after at least 3 months, open or arthroscopic surgical excision should be considered.

*Nault et al. reviewed management of Os trigonum syndrome.* Reviewing methods for surgical excision, **they showed** comparable satisfactory results with both procedures, but with sooner return to work with arthroscopy. Both methods were associated with neuropraxias, **noting** the sural nerve passing on average 14mm posterior and 14mm inferior to the lateral malleolus.

*Schon and Baxter reviewed common foot and ankle neuropathies among athletes.* These are usually caused by underlying bony anomalies, hence the most common neuropathies involve the interdigital nerves, with sural neuropathies among the least common. Nerve conduction tests are often negative as the compression is a dynamic phenomenon. These usually do very well with conservative treatment, while the rare recalcitrant cases resolve with surgical decompression.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1- FHL tendonitis is seen with os trigonum, as they are adjacent at this level. However the MRI does not show the FHL
Answer 2- FHL tendonitis has been seen with os trigonum but posterior tibial tendonitis/tendonopathy has not. Additionally, the posterior tibial tendon does not abut the os trigonum.
Answer 4- The superficial peroneal nerve is located more anteriorly than the incision shown.
Answer 5- Posterolateral tubercle fractures from the talus, or Shepard's fractures, can be confused for an os trigonum but is not seen on the MRI
