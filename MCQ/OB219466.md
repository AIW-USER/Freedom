## Question

A 23-year-old male presents to the emergency department with a low-velocity gunshot wound to his right knee (Figure A). While running from his assailant, he fell and sustained a puncture wound to the contralateral knee. The Emergency Department is concerned that he has traumatic arthrotomies to both knees and started intravenous antibiotics. The resident on call discusses the patient with her attending. In general, which of the following is true regarding the management of potential traumatic arthrotomies?

## Options

1. CT scans demonstrating intra-articular air would not be sensitive or specific for the diagnosis
2. A saline load test using methylene blue-saline is required to establish the diagnosis in all cases
3. Traumatic arthrotomies with small wounds and minimal contamination do not require operative management
4. Arthrotomies with retained low-velocity lead bullets in the joint do not require surgical intervention
5. There is no significant difference in the total cost of care comparing operative to nonoperative management

## Correct option

**3. Traumatic arthrotomies with small wounds and minimal contamination do not require operative management**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with potential bilateral traumatic arthrotomies. Barring a retained bullet in his right knee, both may be managed with bedside irrigation, wound care, and antibiotics with a low risk for subsequent deep infection (Answer 3).

Traumatic arthrotomies are relatively common injuries that most often occur in the knee. They may occur via a variety of mechanisms including gunshot wounds and penetrating injuries. Diagnostic workup often involves simple radiographs and a saline-load test for smaller penetrating wounds. However, CT scans may also be obtained with intra-articular air being considered diagnostic. Larger wounds with direct visualization of the intra-articular space do not require any formal rule-out test. In the past, nearly all traumatic arthrotomies were managed with operative debridement and irrigation to negate the risk of potential septic arthritis. However, there is growing evidence that traumatic arthrotomies with smaller wounds (<5cm) and minimal/no gross contamination can be appropriately managed in the emergency department with local wound care, tetanus prophylaxis, and antibiotics alone. This negates the need to undergo an operative procedure, results in minimal risk for subsequent deep infection, and is both time- and cost-effective.

*Shultz and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institutional results comparing low-velocity gunshot wound traumatic arthrotomies treated with nonoperative versus operative management.* *At 3 months follow-up, one (17.4%) of the eight nonoperative patients developed a superficial wound infection that resolved with oral antibiotics.* In the operative group, one (2.6%) patient developed a wound infection requiring operative debridement. There was no statistically significant difference in the risk of infection between the two groups. **The authors concluded** that in select patients, wound care and antibiotics are sufficient for preventing infection after gunshot wound-related traumatic arthrotomies.

*McKnight and colleagues performed a prospective observational multicenter study comparing patients with isolated open arthrotomies (i.e. no underlying fractures) treated with operative vs nonoperative management.* The 64 non-operative patients generally had smaller (<5cm) wounds with minimal/no gross contamination compared to the 125 operative patients with larger or grossly contaminated wounds. At 3 months post-injury, one nonoperative patient developed septic arthritis (1.5%) compared to 7 in the operative group (5.6%). While there was no significant difference in the risk for infection between both groups, the nonoperative treatment group incurred lower overall costs. **The authors concluded** that minimally contaminated traumatic arthrotomies with small overlying wounds can be safely treated nonoperatively and at a significantly lower cost.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: CT scans obtained to rule out traumatic arthrotomy, and found to have intra-articular air, are nearly 100% sensitive and specific to establish the diagnosis. While CT scans have notably improved sensitivity and specificity compared to saline-load tests, they are less cost-effective.
Answer 2: A saline load test using methylene blue-saline is NOT required to establish the diagnosis in all cases, particularly in cases where (1) the intra-articular space is visualized through an open wound or (2) if there is confirmation of intra-articular air on CT scan.
Answer 4: Arthrotomies with retained low-velocity lead bullets in the joint do require surgical intervention to remove the bullet. Otherwise, the retained lead results in localized plumbism (i.e. lead arthritis; accelerated joint degeneration from intra-articular metal poisoning).
Answer 5: There is a significant difference in the total cost of care between both treatment modalities, with operative management being more expensive. In the McKnight study, operative management incurred approximately $10,000 in additional costs owed to operative procedures.
