## Question

Figure A is the radiograph of a 79-year-old female who slipped and fell while walking her schnauzer. She was unable to ambulate after the fall. History reveals that she underwent a right hip hemiarthroplasty 5 years ago after a similar incident. Which of the following is the best treatment for this patient?

![[q214669-a-screen-shot-2021-03-22-at-5-49-43-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Revision to a long stem cemented hemiarthroplasty prosthesis
2. Revision to a long stem uncemented hemiarthroplasty prosthesis
3. Open reduction internal fixation plus revision to an uncemented long stem hemiarthroplasty prosthesis
4. Conversion to a proximal femoral replacement alone
5. Open reduction internal fixation alone

## Correct option

**5. Open reduction internal fixation alone**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a fracture around the distal tip of a well-fixed femoral component, which would be classified as a Vancouver B1 and should be treated with ORIF.

The incidence of periprosthetic hip fractures is increasing the population continues to age and our elderly remain active. The Vancouver classification (Illustration A) is descriptive, but also guides treatment. Fractures that occur around or just below a well-fixed prosthesis are classified as Vancouver B1 injuries. The treatment of choice for these fractures is ORIF. Intraoperative testing of stem stability should be performed in each case. Here, the fracture extends through the cement mantle distal to the stem, but the proximal cement mantle is undisturbed with no evidence of subsidence, indicating that the implant is stable. Fractures around loose stems are classified as either Vancouver B2 or B3, with the latter occurring in the setting of poor bone stock. Vancouver C injuries are those that occur distal to the prosthesis and are best treated with ORIF.

*Masri et al. review the evaluation and treatment of periprosthetic fractures.* **They report** that treatment of femoral periprosthetic fractures is based upon location, implant stability, and bone stock. **They conclude** that based on these factors, the Vancouver classification allows for reproducible description between surgeons and the formation of an effective treatment plan.

*Ricci et al. review an indirect reduction and plate fixation technique for treating fractures around stable intramedullary implants.* *They report on 50 such patients who demonstrated fracture healing at an average of 12 weeks with 73% returning to their baseline ambulatory status.* **They conclude** that this technique leads to satisfactory outcomes following fractures around stable implants.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of a right periprosthetic femur fracture around the distal tip of a well-fixed cemented prosthesis. Illustration A demonstrates the Vancouver classification. Illustration B is the postoperative radiograph of the same patient shown in Figure A after ORIF.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: Cemented stems are not the implant of choice in standard revision THA, even in the setting of a primary cemented implant.
Answers 2 & 3: Revision to a long uncemented prosthesis is the treatment of choice, in conjunction with ORIF, in the setting of a loose stem (Vancouver B2).
Answer 4: Proximal femoral replacement is a salvage operation, and not indicated in this patient given the presence of adequate bone stock.
