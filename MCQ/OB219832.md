## Question

A college football running back is tackled during the opening game of the season and sustains a hyperextension injury to his neck. His concussion screening exam on the sideline is negative, but he complains of an electric shock-like pain radiating down the front of his left arm and mild weakness with resisted elbow flexion and supination. His symptoms persist beyond 10 minutes, and on further questioning, he notes that he had two similar episodes last season that resolved prior to him being put back into the game and no further workup. Which of the following is the best next step in management?

## Options

1. Agility testing on the sideline prior to return to play
2. Allow immediate return to play given that "stingers" are benign
3. Hold from the remainder of the game and allow to return to practice in 24 hours if asymptomatic
4. Hold from play until off-field workup including cervical spine radiographs is performed
5. Initiate graduated return to play protocol within 72 hours if symptoms resolve

## Correct option

**4. Hold from play until off-field workup including cervical spine radiographs is performed**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained a "stinger," or transient brachial plexus neuropraxia involving the C6 nerve root with symptoms involving the biceps. Given a history of recurrent stingers with persistent symptoms, return to play should only be allowed after a complete off-field workup has been performed.

Burners and stingers, also referred to as "dead arm syndrome," are common in contact athletes and can occur by several mechanisms, which include: traction injuries caused by downward displacement of the arm and bending of the neck away from the side of injury, compression injuries caused by lateral head turning toward affected side, and direct contact caused by a blow at Erb's point superior to the clavicle. Diagnosis is made clinically with a neurological examination most commonly consisting of unilateral tingling in the arm with transient weakness in the C5 and C6 innervated muscles (i.e., deltoid and biceps). Having one stinger increases the risk of sustaining another by 3 times, with a prior history of having sustained a stinger in the previous season being the strongest risk factor for sustaining and developing lasting symptoms. Given this, players are not allowed to return to play in the setting of persistent or bilateral symptoms, with cervical spine radiographs indicated for persistent symptoms to rule out cervical stenosis and/or fracture, while bilateral symptoms are inconsistent with a typical stinger and require an MRI to rule out more severe acute pathology.

*Kawasaki et al. reviewed the incidence of stingers in young rugby players and* **found** among 569 players, a history of stingers was present in 33.9%. A logistic regression analysis indicated that a history of stingers in the previous season and an injury with more than 3 symptoms, especially motor weakness, were correlated with the severity of the injury. **They concluded** that a history of stingers was identified to be the strongest risk factor for injuries and lasting symptoms.

*Safran et al. reviewed nerve injury about the shoulder in athletes.* **The authors note** that the key to the management of the athlete with a burner or stinger is the prevention of recurrence. **They conclude** that returning to full contact depends on pain-free, full passive and active range of motion of the neck and shoulder and normal neurologic examination results, including full and symmetric strength of the shoulders and neck.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Agility testing is not indicated in the setting of a stinger.
Answer 2: Immediate return to play after resolution of symptoms is typical of stingers if there has not been a prior history of multiple stingers and/or a prior workup has been negative. This patient has a history of multiple stingers with persistent and unresolved symptoms on the sidelines.
Answers 3 and 5: The patient does not have a concussion and a graduated return to play protocol is not indicated.
