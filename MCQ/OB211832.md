## Options

1. A 30-year old man with chronic atraumatic knee pain, a 4.2 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure A
2. A 67-year old man with chronic knee pain, a 4.3 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure B
3. A 55-year-old woman with chronic knee pain, a 3.5 cm2 lateral femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure C
4. A 30-year old man with chronic atraumatic knee pain, a 4.5 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure B
5. A 25-year-old woman with chronic atraumatic knee pain, a 1.5 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure A

## Correct option

**1. A 30-year old man with chronic atraumatic knee pain, a 4.2 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and the radiograph depicted in Figure A**

## Answer explanation

Of the above patient scenarios, isolated osteochondral allograft transplantation would be best indicated in a 30-year-old with chronic knee pain due to a 4.2 cm2 medial femoral condyle osteochondral lesion and no mechanical axis malalignment (Answer 1).

The initial treatment for small articular cartilage defects about the femoral condyle is rest, NSAIDS and bracing, with the goal of reducing symptoms but not restoration of anatomy. For larger defects (>2-4cm2), options include arthroscopic debridement, marrow stimulation procedures (microfracture, osteochondral drilling, abrasion chondroplasty), osteochondral autograft transplantation and osteochondral allograft transplantation. There is some debate over the use of an osteochondral autograft for lesions >2cm2 due to donor site morbidity. Of recent, more surgeons are electing to use fresh osteochondral allograft transplantation for defects >2cm2 or when previous surgical attempts have failed. Coronal plane deformity should be corrected prior to or at time of transplantation. In patients >55 years, knee arthroplasty should be considered.

*Browne et al. reviewed the surgical alternatives for the treatment of articular cartilage lesions.* **They reported** that operative techniques are either reparative or restorative in nature, and while reparative techniques do not fully reestablish articular cartilage, they can be functional for varying periods of time. On the other hand, restorative techniques attempt to reestablish the native articular surface. **They concluded** that adjunctive therapies such as growth factors, adhesives, artificial bioabsorbable scaffolding matrices, and gene therapy manipulation are being investigated.

*Sherman et al. reviewed fresh osteochondral allograft transplantation for the knee.* **They reported** that the indications for osteochondral allograft transplantation range from primary management of large osteochondral defects, salvage of previously failed cartilage repair, and complex biologic knee reconstruction in the setting of osteonecrosis, fracture malunion, or posttraumatic arthritis. **They highlighted** that the challenges for the procedure include allograft storage, size matching, tissue availability, chondrocyte viability, the possibility of immunologic graft response, and a demanding surgical technique.

Figures A, B, and C are three-joint-standing radiographs demonstrating neutral, varus, and valgus lower extremity mechanical alignment, respectively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2 and 3: Older patients with chronic knee pain and an isolated large femoral condyle osteochondral lesions may be better server with a knee arthroplasty.
Answer 4: An isolated osteochondral allograft transplantation would be problematic in this scenario given his varus lower extremity mechanical alignment. Correction of this alignment prior to or at the time of transplantation is warranted.
Answer 5: This patient with a small articular cartilage defect should first be managed with rest, NSAIDs, and bracing. If her symptoms continue, consideration for arthroscopic debridement and marrow stimulation procedures may be warranted.
