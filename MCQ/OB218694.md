## Question

A 14-year-old pitcher is brought in to the pediatrician because of persistent anterolateral shoulder pain and a notable decrease in his pitch velocity. He went straight from a pitching camp into spring training and has played every game of the season thus far. His radiographs show no abnormalities and he continues to be symptomatic, so an MRI is ordered, as shown in Figure A. What is the most likely diagnosis?

![[q218694-a-screenshot-2023-09-18-at-6-46-37-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Acromial stress fracture
2. Deltoid muscle strain
3. Partial articular-sided rotator cuff tear
4. Proximal humeral epiphysiolysis
5. Superior labral anterior and posterior (SLAP) tear

## Correct option

**4. Proximal humeral epiphysiolysis**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting classically with Little Leaguer's Shoulder (LLS), or proximal humeral epiphysiolysis, with an MRI showing metaphyseal edema adjacent to the proximal humeral physis (Answer 4)

Little Leaguer’s shoulder (LLS), is an overuse condition resulting from chronic repetitive microtraumatic forces imposed on the unossified cartilage of the proximal humeral physis. Classic radiographic findings include widening of the proximal humeral physis with increased sclerosis and/or mineralization/lucency; however, it is important to note that radiographs may be normal, prompting some physicians to order MRI to rule out additional pathology such as articular-sided rotator cuff tears or labral tears. Many factors have been studied as possible contributors to the development of LSS, but no clear relationship between body weight/height or throwing mechanics appears to exist. It has also been claimed that the use of breaking pitches (e.g. curve balls, sliders) at an early age are a contributing factor, but this has been refuted by a number of recent studies. By far, the most important factors are the numbers of pitches thrown and the frequency of pitching. This is reflected in a number of indicators, including numbers of innings pitched, number of throwing days, numbers of pitches or pitched innings per week, month and year, and playing for multiple teams.

*Holt et al. published a single-season prospective shoulder MRI study of little league baseball players aged 10-12 years.* *The authors included 23 patients and found that year-round play, single sports participation, and all-star team selection were associated with post-season MRI abnormalities.* **The authors concluded** that the increased demands required for all-star selection comes at a price to the young athlete as the majority of players selected for this honor had abnormal MRI findings in their throwing shoulder, while few non-all-stars demonstrated such pathology.

*Waltham et al. report on 23 cases of little leaguer's shoulder in AJSM.* *All patients were treated with rest from baseball throwing for an average of 3 months.* *Twenty-one of the 23 patients (91%) returned to playing baseball and were asymptomatic.* **The authors recommended** rest from throwing for at least 3 months, followed by a gradual return to throwing in an asymptomatic shoulder.

Figure A is a sagittal MRI of the shoulder showing metaphyseal edema about the proximal humeral physis in a skeletally immature individual.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is no evidence of acromial fracture on this MRI.
Answer 2: The patient's MRI shows the classic features of bony metaphyseal edema about the epiphysis, not within the deltoid muscle belly.
Answer 3: There is no evidence of articular-sided rotator cuff tear on this MRI, though it can rarely occur concomitantly with LLS.
Answer 5: There is no evidence of SLAP tear on this MRI.
