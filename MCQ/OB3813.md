## Question

A 25-year-old male has a foot-drop deformity of his right foot due to a chromosomal 17 duplication which continues to progress despite stretching, strengthening, and orthotic use. A clinical picture of his foot while attempting to dorsiflex his ankle is shown in Figure A. On examination, he has supple ankle and subtalar motion, an equinus contracture, and 5/5 plantar flexion and inversion strength. Which of the following is the most appropriate surgical intervention for this patient?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Tibiotalar arthrodesis
2. Combined tibiotalar and subtalar arthrodesis
3. Tibialis posterior transfer to dorsum of foot with gastrocnemius lengthening
4. Flexor digitorum longus transfer to dorsum of foot with gastrocnemius lengthening
5. Tibialis anterior transfer to peroneus brevis

## Correct option

**3. Tibialis posterior transfer to dorsum of foot with gastrocnemius lengthening**

## Answer explanation

This young patient has developed a foot drop secondary to CMT but maintains supple joints. Because his foot is flexible, tibialis posterior tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot is the most appropriate treatment.

Patients with Charcot-Marie-Tooth develop progressive muscle imbalance resulting in cavovarus deformity. The agonist-antagonist relationship of tibialis posterior overpowering a weak peroneus brevis and peroneus longus overpowering the weak tibialis anterior leads to the cavovarus deformity. This deformity is initially flexible but may become rigid over time. Determining whether a deformity is flexible requires a thorough physical exam evaluating the patients standing alignment, coleman's block test, passive and active range of motion. In a supple foot transferring the tibialis posterior tendon to the dorsum of the foot removes a deforming force (causing plantarflexion and inversion) and allow for active dorsiflexion. A gastrocnemius lengthening procedure should be performed to correct any equinus deformity.

*Gallardo et al. describe the MRI features in the leg and foot muscles of patients with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease.* In six patients with pes cavus but with normal muscle power, MRI showed muscle fatty infiltration of intrinsic foot muscles mainly involving the lumbricals. In five patients with more severe disease, MRI showed accentuated fatty infiltration of the lateral, anterior, and superficial posterior muscle compartments, in addition to intrinsic atrophy. **They conclude** that selective involvement of intrinsic foot muscles is the characteristic pattern of CMT-1A cases with minimal disease signs.

*Guyton et al. review the surgical management of foot deformities in Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease.* **They note** that deformities are variable between patients and care should be individualized. **They recommend** addressing the plantar fascia first then proceeding from the hindfoot to the forefoot during the correction, as a hindfoot procedure may alter the resting tension of the digital flexors and extensors.

*Beals et al. review the genetics and exam findings seen in Charcot-Marie-Tooth patients.* The most common genetic abnormality that has been identified as causative of CMT is a gene defect on chromosome 17, known as CMT 1A. Classically, the clinical findings of CMT include weakness of the intrinsic muscles of the hands and feet, and peroneal muscle atrophy and weakness.

Figure A is a clinical photograph in a patient with a high arch, clawing of the toes, and no anterior compartment activity when attempting to dorsiflex his ankle. Illustration A is a depiction of the Bridle procedure, which is commonly used as a treatment for foot drop. It should be noted that there are multiple techniques used for tibialis posterior tendon transfer.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Arthrodesis would not be indicated in a young patient with supple joints/flexible deformity.
Answer 4: Given that the tibialis posterior is the main deforming force in this patient with CMT, its transfer is superior to that of the FDL for correction of this deformity
Answer 5: The tibialis anterior tendon is typically involved and weak in the setting of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease and therefore would not be an acceptable tendon to transfer.
