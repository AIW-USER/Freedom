## Question

A 62-year-old female presents to your office complaining of lower back pain and symptoms suggestive of neurogenic claudication 6 years following a lumbar fusion. Her most recent lumbar spine film is shown in Figure A. She undergoes surgery to restore her saggital alignment and immediately following surgery reports new weakness in her leg. Knee extension strength of the left is signficantly less than that of the contralateral side. A post-operative radiograph is shown in Figure B. Which of the following is the LEAST likely etiology of her post-operative quadriceps weakness?

## Options

1. Screw malposition
2. Correction of PI-LL mismatch to 10 degrees
3. Prone positioning
4. Apical derotation during deformity correction
5. Perioperative stroke

## Correct option

**4. Apical derotation during deformity correction**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with sagittal malalignment following a prior lumbar fusion, and undergoes a 3-column corrective osteotomy with cephalad extension of her fusion construct. Of the choices listed, apical derotation during deformity correction is the least likely reason for post-operative quadriceps weakness.

Three-column osteotomies such as a pedicle subtraction osteotomy are powerful tools for sagittal plane deformity correction. These operations are, however, fraught with complications. Neurologic injuries following PSO have been cited around 20%, and can often occur during osteotomy closure. Screw malposition with nerve root impingement should also be considered, and a post-operative CT should be performed to rule this out. Improper prone positioning and inadequate padding can lead to undue pressure on the femoral nerve, which could then contribute to quadriceps weakness. Finally, cerebral ischemic events can lead to focal neurologic deficits and have been reported to occur at a rate of 0.5% following 3-column osteotomies. Apical derotation is a deformity correction maneuver specific to coronal plane deformities and is not used for the correction of pure sagittal plane malalignment.

*Iorio et al. reviewed the neurological complications following adult spinal deformity surgery.* **They report** a neurologic complication rate between 0.5-17%, and as many as 30% of deficits were secondary to malpositioned screws. Among 3-column osteotomies, the rate of injury has been reported at 18.6%.

*Nasser et al. reviewed the complication rates of 105 studies (79,471 patients) and* **determined** a rate of 16.4%, with a rate of 17.8% for lumbar procedures and 8.9% for cervical procedures. **The authors noted** that defining complications varied widely across studies. **They advocated** for further efforts to be made toward standardized complication reporting.

*Ghobrial et al. reviewed the iatrogenic neurologic complications of lumbar spine surgery.* **They report** the average rate of neurologic injury following lumbar surgery is 9%, with the most common injuries resulting in post-operative radiculopathy, weakness, and spinal cord compression.

*Norton et al. evaluated the complication rates following 3-column osteotomies and* **found** an overall complication rate of 58%. The reported rate of nerve root injury was 1.4%. The rate of cerebral ischemic event was 0.5%.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Screw malposition is responsible for 30% of post-operative neurologic complications and CT should be performed to rule this out.
Answer 2: The large angular corrections achieved with PSO are associated with an increased risk of neurologic complication.
Answer 3: Improper prone positioning can cause neurapraxia to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves.
Answer 5: Perioperative stroke is an uncommon but devastating neurologic injury following 3-column osteotomy. Its prevalence is on par with post-operative cauda equina syndrome, myocardial infarction, and visual deficit.
