## Question

A 28-year-old male lacrosse player presents with increasing lower back pain, especially with repetitive overhead throwing after long practices and games. He states that he has intermittent tingling sensations along the dorsal aspect of his foot, but pain remains his primary complaint. He brings a copy of his CT scan from a recent urgent care visit (Figure A) which also confirmed a bilateral pars defect. Which of the following is true regarding this patient’s underlying condition?

## Options

1. Underlying central stenosis results in compression of the traversing nerve root
2. His pathology affects the thoracolumbar junction more often than the lumbosacral region
3. Foraminal stenosis is commonly encountered secondary to fibrocartilage accumulation
4. Adults are more likely to undergo further slip progression compared to adolescent patients
5. The first step in management involves circumferential spinal fusion to provide long-term stability

## Correct option

**3. Foraminal stenosis is commonly encountered secondary to fibrocartilage accumulation**

## Answer explanation

This patient demonstrates classic findings of adult isthmic spondylolisthesis. While most patients are asymptomatic, some patients progress to pain and radiculopathy which occurs secondary to foraminal stenosis (Answer 3).

Spondylolisthesis, referring to the anterior translation of a vertebral body relative to the adjacent lower segment, is most often caused by a pars interarticularis defect (i.e. isthmic spondylolisthesis). However, it can occur secondary to other causes as well (Illustration A). In isthmic spondylolisthesis, a bilateral pars defect results in abnormal stress distribution with the intervertebral disc space bearing disproportionally higher stress than of a normal, pars-intact spine. The vertebral disc subsequently degenerates at a higher rate due to abnormally high shearing forces and is purported to result in the onset of pain symptoms. Eventually, the lack of pars support and disc instability results in characteristic vertebral body anterior translation. This anterior “slipping” increases stress on the remaining posterior elements (i.e. facet joints), resulting in hypertrophic joint changes and fibrocartilage accumulation in the neuroforamina. Collectively, these cause foraminal stenosis with compression of the correlating exiting nerve. Central stenosis is infrequently encountered because the posterior elements remain in normal anatomic positional alignment despite the cephalad vertebral body sliding forward (which increases space available in the central spinal canal, Illustration B).

Jones and Rao provided a comprehensive review article regarding adult isthmic spondylolisthesis. The authors discuss that this pathology is present in approximately 6% of adults, most of whom are asymptomatic. They discuss that symptomatic cases should initially be managed with nonsurgical treatments including NSAIDs, physical therapy, and bracing. If the patient’s symptoms are recalcitrant to conservative measures, they may be treated with decompression and fusion procedures as indicated (i.e. dependent on clinical and imaging findings). They conclude that circumferential fusion has shown improved rates and, in some cases, better outcomes.

Bhalla and Bono provided a similar comprehensive review article on the management of lumbar isthmic spondylolisthesis. The authors discuss that the patient’s pelvic incidence is correlated with the disease severity whereas the slip angle correlates with potential disease progression. They highlight that foraminal stenosis and exiting nerve root compression result secondary to facet hypertrophy, posteriorly bulging discs, fibrocartilage accumulation within the neural foramina, and uncinate spur formation of the posterior/inferior aspect of the superior vertebral body. They conclude by emphasizing utilizing patient-specific spinopelvic radiographic parameters, along with slip grade and angle, to determine the most appropriate operative approach in patients requiring surgical treatment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: As described above, underlying central stenosis is uncommon secondary to the posterior spinal column remaining in normal anatomic position despite the cephalad vertebra slipping anteriorly. This results in a somewhat counterintuitive increase in the central spinal canal space in most cases. For this patient, that translates to compression of the exiting L5 nerve root secondary to foraminal stenosis whereas the traversing S1 nerve root remains unaffected in the central spinal canal. Central stenosis is only seen in severe cases (i.e. spondyloptosis) which are exceedingly rare.
Answer 2: Adult isthmic spondylolisthesis occurs most commonly at the L5/S1 junction (90% of cases). Degenerative spondylolisthesis, seen in older patients with spondylotic degenerative changes, more often occurs in the L4/L5 region but can occur in upper lumbar levels.
Answer 4: Generally, the risk for slip progression halts after skeletal maturity. Adolescent patients are known to be at far greater risk of disease progression compared to adult patients.
Answer 5: The first step in management should include a trial of nonoperative management. Back pain is often multifactorial and other causes of pain and disfunction should be ruled out. Further, proceeding straight to fusion is less than ideal in a young patient who is at risk for long-term adjacent segment disease.
