## Question

A 32-year-old male was skiing in the Swiss Alps two months ago and sustained an injury to his ankle. He states that he tried to stop quickly and the ankle was rapidly forced into dorsiflexion. Since the injury, he has had tenderness and pain over the posterolateral ankle. On exam, with circumduction of the ankle, you identify peroneal tendon instability. Squeeze and anterior drawer tests are negative. There is no pain at the 5th metatarsal base. Before seeing you in the office he had done six weeks of physical therapy without much improvement in his symptoms. You discuss surgical management with the patient. In order to improve his peroneal instability, which of the structures in Figure A should be repaired?

## Correct option

**2. Structure B**

## Answer explanation

This patient likely has an injury to the superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) given the mechanism of his injury and subsequent peroneal instability.

Peroneal tendon instability is usually secondary to a traumatic ankle injury in the setting of a forced dorsiflexion/eversion event. Some patients will complain of having felt a pop at the time of the injury. Findings after the injury include posterolateral ankle pain, swelling, and subluxation/dislocation of the tendons at the distal fibula with circumduction of the ankle. Injury to the SPR is usually the cause of the peroneal instability as the SPR is responsible for holding the peroneal tendons in the retromalleolar groove of the fibula. A shallow retromalleolar groove may be a predisposing factor for peroneal instability. Immobilization for 6 weeks is the first line treatment, but ultimately repair of the SPR and possible deepening of the retromalleolar groove are indicated with failed conservative treatment or in high-level athletes.

Kollias and Ferkel review the deepening of the fibular groove in the setting of recurrent peroneal tendon subluxation. They completed this procedure in 11 patients with no recurrence of instability postoperatively and 10/11 being able to return to previous sporting activities. They conclude that this procedure may be a good adjunct in helping to prevent the recurrence of peroneal tendon instability.

*Ferran et al. reviewed recurrent subluxation of the peroneal tendons.* *They discuss the anatomic risk factors for instability including the depth of the retromalleolar groove and the size of the SPR.* **They note** that conservative treatment is only successful in about half of cases with peroneal instability and that recurrent dislocations should be managed with surgical management. They discuss a variety of different procedures that can be used to manage this instability but note that none have been proven superior.

Figure A is a diagram of the lateral ankle with labeled structures as follows: (A) anterior talofibular ligament, (B) superior peroneal retinaculum, (C) calcaneofibular ligament, (D) inferior peroneal retinaculum, (E) peroneus brevis insertion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This structure is the anterior talofibular ligament, the most commonly injured ankle ligament. However, drawer testing is negative and his symptoms align primarily with peroneal instability due to SPR rupture.
Answer 3: This structure is the calaneofibular ligament, also important for lateral ankle instability but is not injured in this patient.
Answer 4: This structure is the inferior peroneal retinaculum which is less commonly injured and usually does not require repair in cases of peroneal instability.
Answer 5: This structure is the peroneus brevis at its insertion on the 5th metatarsal base. While the peroneus brevis can be injured with an inversion injury, it wouldn't directly result in peroneal instability and would usually cause pain at the 5th metatarsal base.
