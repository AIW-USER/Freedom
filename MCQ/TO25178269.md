Chapter: General Orthopedics MCQs — Bone Cement

## Question

What is the primary reason liquid antibiotics are contraindicated for mixing with Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement?

## Options

1. They accelerate the setting time too rapidly for surgical application.
2. They cause the exothermic reaction to reach temperatures high enough to melt adjacent bone.
3. They significantly decrease the porosity of the cement, preventing drug elution.
4. They interfere with the chemical cross-linking during polymerization, resulting in a soft, non-solid mass

## Correct option

**4. They interfere with the chemical cross-linking during polymerization, resulting in a soft, non-solid mass**

## Answer explanation

The use of liquid antibiotics in Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement is strictly contraindicated in orthopedic surgery. While it might seem intuitive to mix a liquid drug into a paste, the chemical and mechanical consequences are disastrous for the structural integrity of the cement and the success of the procedure.

Interference with Polymerization (The "Toothpaste" Effect)

PMMA bone cement is formed by a chemical reaction between a powdered polymer and a liquid monomer (methyl methacrylate). This is a precise exothermic polymerization process.

 - Chemical Dilution: Adding a liquid antibiotic introduces a third phase (aqueous or oily) into this two-part chemical reaction. The liquid interferes with the cross-linking of the polymer chains.

 - Failure to Harden: In many cases, adding liquid antibiotics results in a mixture that never fully polymerizes. Instead of becoming a rock-hard solid, the cement remains a soft, "toothpaste-like" or "rubbery" mass that lacks any structural stability.

Drastic Reduction in Mechanical Strength

Even if the cement managed to set, the presence of liquid creates massive voids and structural defects
within the cement mantle.

 - Stress Risers: Liquid creates large, irregular pores that act as "stress risers." When the patient bears weight, the cement is prone to catastrophic fragmentation and cracking.

 - Loss of Compression Strength:
 Studies show that adding even small amounts of liquid can reduce the compressive strength of the cement by more than 50%, making it useless for either permanent fixation or as a weight-bearing spacer.

Altered Elution Kinetics (The "Washout" Problem)

The goal of antibiotic cement is a controlled, sustained release of the drug.

 - Rapid Washout: Because liquid antibiotics are not integrated into the solid matrix of the cement, they tend to "wash out" almost immediately. This creates a massive initial spike that can reach systemic toxic levels (potentially causing Acute Kidney Injury), followed by a rapid drop-off.

 - Lack of Longevity: Powdered antibiotics create micro-pores that allow for a slow, steady elution over days or weeks. Liquids do not provide this sustained antimicrobial environment.

Thermal Lability and Concentration

 - Heat Sensitivity: Many liquid antibiotic formulations contain stabilizers and diluents that are highly sensitive to the high temperatures (80 degree to 100 degree ) generated during cement curing. This can degrade the antibiotic before it even reaches the patient's tissue.

 - Concentration Limits: To get a therapeutic dose of an antibiotic like Vancomycin from a liquid form, one would have to add a significant volume of fluid, further exacerbating the structural issues mentioned above. Powdered forms allow for high-dose "loading" (e.g., 4g to 8g) without significantly increasing the volume of the mixture.

Answer: 4
