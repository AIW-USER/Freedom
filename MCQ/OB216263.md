## Question

A 23-year-old male presents to your office after sustaining a hand injury two days prior while competing in a ribbon twirling competition. Radiographs reveal a transverse, midshaft, proximal phalanx fracture of the middle finger. Which structures contribute to the likely angulation with this injury?

## Options

1. Distal fragment flexion (due to interossei) & proximal fragment in extension (due to central slip)
2. Distal fragment flexion (due to central slip) & proximal fragment in extension (due to interossei)
3. Proximal fragment flexion (due to interossei) & distal fragment in extension (due to central slip)
4. Proximal fragment flexion (due to central slip) & distal fragment in extension (due to interossei)
5. Proximal fragment extension (due to central slip) & distal fragment in extension (due to interossei)

## Correct option

**3. Proximal fragment flexion (due to interossei) & distal fragment in extension (due to central slip)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a transverse midshaft proximal phalanx fracture. The deforming forces would result in proximal fragment flexion (due to interossei) & distal fragment extension (due to central slip).

Phalanx Fractures are common hand injuries that involve the proximal, middle, or distal phalanx. Diagnosis can be confirmed with orthogonal radiographs of the involved digit. The proximal metaphyseal location is a problematic fracture to get reduced with closed measures, and due to the forces of the extensor apparatus, is prone to collapse into the original deformity. Treatment involves immobilization or surgical fixation depending on location, severity, and alignment of the fracture. Indications for buddy taping or splinting include extraarticular fractures with <10° angulation, <2mm shortening, no rotational deformity, and non-displaced intraarticular fractures. Indications for CRPP vs ORIF include extraarticular fractures with >10° angulation, >2mm shortening, rotational deformity, displaced intraarticular fractures, and unstable or irreducible fracture pattern.

*Henry et al. reviewed the treatment of fractures of the proximal phalanx and metacarpals.* **They reported** that inherently stable fractures do not require surgical treatment; all other fractures should be considered for additional stabilization. **They concluded** that transverse and short oblique proximal phalanx fractures generally are treated with Kirschner wires, although a stable short oblique or transverse shaft fracture can be managed with an intrinsic plus splint.

*Vahey et al. reviewed extensor lag as a common sequela of proximal phalangeal fractures.* **They reported** that proximal phalangeal fractures frequently lead to extensor tendon dysfunction via adhesions and phalanx malunion with angulation and/or shortening. **They concluded** that it is important to re-establish the bone-tendon relationship for proximal phalangeal fractures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,2,4,5: A transverse midshaft proximal phalanx fracture would results in a apex volar angulation deformity due to the proximal fragment in flexion (due to interossei) and the distal fragment in extension (due to central slip)
