## Question

A 72-year-old female presents with sudden onset pain in her right femur after stepping off a curb outside her home. She endorses some prodromal pain that worsened over the past three weeks, however, thought it was normal musculoskeletal pain. Other than anti-resorptive medication for osteoporosis, she denies any other contributing medical history. Her presenting injury is shown in Figure A. The surgeon plans on utilizing an intramedullary implant for fracture fixation. What is the primary complication associated with this patient’s injury?

![[q219185-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Delayed healing and nonunion
2. Symptomatic limb length discrepancy
3. Underlying metastatic disease
4. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head
5. Occult femoral neck fracture

## Correct option

**1. Delayed healing and nonunion**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s femur fracture is transversely oriented along the lateral cortex with a medial oblique spike. These findings are consistent with a bisphosphonate-associated atypical femur fracture (AFF), which has an increased risk for delayed healing and nonunion compared to standard femoral shaft fractures (Answer 1).

Bisphosphonates are commonly prescribed medications for the treatment of osteoporosis. While these medications help diminish bone resorption and thus improve bone mineral density, they can also inhibit the necessary remodeling of osseous injuries. These findings are most notorious in the proximal femur, where small fractures along the tension side fail to remodel and can eventually lead to an atypical femur fracture (AFF) if left untreated. These patients often endorse insidious thigh pain that slowly worsens (i.e. “prodromal symptoms”) until the fracture occurs from a low-energy mechanism (i.e. stepping off a curb). There are characteristic radiographic findings diagnostic for pending and completed AFFs (Illustration A), and it is imperative to obtain contralateral femur radiographs to rule out bilateral involvement. Prophylactic fixation prior to fracture completion has demonstrated superior clinical results compared with conservative management. In both pending and completed AFF, the discontinuation of bisphosphonates is imperative for osseous healing.

*Githens and colleagues provided a review article on the surgical management of AFFs.* *They stress the importance of carefully and sequentially reaming the lateral cortex sclerotic lesion (i.e. “pedestal”) when utilizing an intramedullary implant.* Failure to adequately ream the lesion may result in either mal-reduction or fracture comminution (and prolonged fracture healing) when passing the nail (Illustrations B & C). **They also suggest** utilizing a cephalomedullary implant for prophylactic fixation of the femoral neck in this classically older population (Level V evidence). **The authors conclude** by acknowledging the expected prolonged healing times for these fractures and stressing the discontinuation of bisphosphonates.

*Lim and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institutional outcomes in the treatment of complete AFF and factors associated with increasing healing time.* *Of the 109 fractures included, only 70% showed osseous union within 6 months.* The other 30% were considered the problematic healing group and more often involved cases where iatrogenic cortical breakage occurred around the fracture site during fixation or if there was fracture diastasis after fixation. **The authors conclude** with their recommendation to utilize meticulous operative techniques to avoid these controllable factors associated with prolonged healing.

*Cho and colleagues performed a retrospective review to determine factors that predict union in the setting of AFFs.* *Utilizing their cohort of 48 patients,* **the authors reported** a healing rate of 68.7% with a mean time to union of 10.7 months. **They found** that differences in the neck-shaft angle (comparing injured to the contralateral, uninjured side) and sagittal angulation were predictors of nonunion. **The authors concluded** that the healing rate of AFF treated with cephalomedullary nailing is lower than previously reported and quality of fracture reduction is the most important factor in bony union and time to union.

Figure A demonstrates this patient’s right complete AFF with the characteristic lateral transverse and medial oblique spike fracture orientation. Illustration A demonstrates the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research’s diagnostic criteria for the diagnosis of an AFF. Illustrations B and C demonstrate this patient’s surgery which was complicated by iatrogenic fracture comminution intraoperatively. Illustration D demonstrates this patient’s radiographic union which occurred approximately 16 months later (i.e. prolonged healing).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Clinically apparent limb length discrepancies are rare in the setting of simple femur fracture patterns as primary cortical apposition is easily achieved.
Answer 3: Bisphosphonate anti-resorptive therapy is used in many cancer patients because it has been shown to decrease the number of skeletally-related events (i.e. pathologic fractures) in metastatic disease. However, the atypical femur fractures are secondary to prolonged bisphosphonate use (5+ years) rather than the cancer itself.
Answer 4: Avascular necrosis of the femoral head is not a commonly reported complication of prolonged bisphosphonate use. Avascular necrosis of the mandible, however, is a known potential complication.
Answer 5: There is a high incidence of missed ipsilateral femoral neck fractures in the setting of typical high-energy femoral shaft fractures in the younger population. However, AFFs do not have a similar association as they occur secondary to low energy mechanisms.
