## Question

A 29-year-old female presents after a motor vehicle rollover with multiple partial fingertip amputations. She undergoes multiple revision amputations as shown in Figure A. While performing primary closure of all fingers, however, the resident performs an additional tenodesis of the flexor and extensor tendons of the ring finger prior to closure. Which of the following may be seen on physical examination at the patient’s three-month follow-up appointment?

![[q217961-a-picture-quadrigia.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Paradoxical interphalangeal joint extension when attempting to make a fist
2. While PIPJ is held in 90 degrees of flexion, DIPJ hyperextends when attempting finger extension
3. Inability to make a fully closed fist secondary to decreased range of motion of adjacent fingers
4. Unable to extend MCPJ from a flexed position, but can maintain extension once passively performed
5. Significant deviation from normal hand cascade

## Correct option

**3. Inability to make a fully closed fist secondary to decreased range of motion of adjacent fingers**

## Answer explanation

Advancement of the flexor tendon with tenodesis to the extensor tendon in the setting of a distal interphalangeal joint disarticulation can result in quadriga.

The FDP tendons of the long, ring and small digits share a common muscle belly. The quadriga effect occurs with over-advancement of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon. In this case, the functionally shortened FDP tendon hinders the full tendon excursion of the adjacent digits sharing a common muscle belly with the resultant inability to perform a full closed fist of those digits. Treatment involves releasing the shortened FDP tendon. The tendon of the index finger may be shortened without negative sequelae to adjacent digits as it has its own independent muscle belly.

*Panattoni and colleagues reviewed commonly utilized reconstruction techniques in the setting of fingertip trauma.* *They discuss numerous local flaps as well as the potential complications of each flap.* *They stress the importance of adhering to proper indications for each flap in order to achieve the best outcomes.*

*Lee and colleagues reviewed the overall management of fingertip injuries.* Their goals for treatment include restoration of painless, functional digits with protected sensation. They discuss common complications including nail deformities, insensate digits, and painful neuromas as well as how to manage these problems.

*Wells and colleagues reviewed fingertip trauma in the United States military with best practice guidelines based on injury patterns and anatomic location.* *They provide key clinical care points and illustrated pictures to assist with treatment in austere environments.*

*Schreuders reviewed the quadriga effect and the underlying anatomic causes.* **He discusses** that the FDP tendons to the middle, ring, and small finger share more than just a common muscle belly with various intertendinous connections throughout the forearm, carpal tunnel, and hand. They reviewed the clinical examination findings and treatment options of quadriga, including simple releases of these intertendinous connections rather than the flexor tendon itself.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: When finger amputation occurs in zone 2 of the hand, this can lead to retraction of the residual FDP tendon and muscle belly. The lumbricals originating from the FDP muscle belly can excessively tighten as flexion is attempted through the residual FDP, resulting in paradoxical PIPJ extension. This is referred to as a lumbrical plus deformity.
Answer 2: This describes a positive Elson test and is indicative of an underlying central slip injury. When a central slip disruption occurs, the intact lateral bands’ insertion on the dorsal distal phalanx results in abnormal DIPJ hyperextension when attempting to extend the finger while the PIPJ is held in 90 degrees of flexion. Additionally, this patient no longer has a distal interphalangeal joint and therefore would not be able to perform an Elson test.
Answer 4: A sagittal band rupture, particularly when radial-sided, results in the loss of active MCPJ extension from a closed fist position due to the extensor tendon subluxation without the appropriate sagittal band check-reign. However, if the patient passively extends the involved finger using the contralateral hand, they are able to maintain extension once the tendon is placed on stretch in the appropriate terminal extended position.
Answer 5: Without a metacarpal or phalangeal fracture with rotational deformity, the hand cascade should remain unaltered.
