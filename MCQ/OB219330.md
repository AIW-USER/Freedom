## Question

A 28-year-old elite NFL quarterback sustains a Zone II fifth metatarsal base fracture and is treated with an intramedullary screw. He is kept non-weightbearing for ten days and then is allowed to start weightbearing as tolerated on the extremity at two weeks post-operatively. By six weeks, he is pain-free and is allowed to return to running and full-impact training activities. At eight weeks post-operatively, he returns with pain and the radiograph shown in Figure B. Which of the following most likely contributed to the failure of his construct?

![[q219330-a-screenshot-2023-12-19-at-4-54-48-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Choosing too long of a partially-threaded screw to accommodate the fracture
2. Early return to weightbearing at 2 weeks post-operatively
3. Failure to supplement the construct with percutaneous bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC)
4. Return to impact activities at 6 weeks post-operatively
5. Use of the largest diameter screw that would accommodate the intramedullary canal

## Correct option

**1. Choosing too long of a partially-threaded screw to accommodate the fracture**

## Answer explanation

Using too long of a screw will iatrogenically straighten the curved metatarsal shaft, causing fracture gapping and distal cortical perforation (Illustration A), leading to the ultimate failure of the construct and nonunion.

Fifth metatarsal base fractures are among the most common fractures of the foot and are predisposed to poor healing due to the limited blood supply to the proximal meta-dipahyseal junction. Nearly 90% of fractures occur at the most proximal portion of the base (termed Zone I or "pseudo-Jones" fractures) with the mechanism of plantarflexion and hindfoot inversion. When the forefoot is also adducted, the fractures more commonly occur at the critical watershed area of Zone II creating the eponymous "Jones" fracture (Illustration B). Less commonly, repetitive microtrauma can lead to stress fractures of the more distal diaphyseal base, termed Zone III fractures (Illustration C). Treatment recommendations vary based on the zone of injury, with Zone I injuries typically healing well with non-operative treatment and protected weightbearing in a hard-soled shoe, boot, or cast with union typically achieved by 8 weeks and nonunion occurring infrequently. Zone II fractures, on the other hand, experience rates of non-union or re-fracture in up to 1/3 of cases when treated non-operatively, thus, intramedullary screw fixation is recommended in elite or competitive athletes to minimize the possibility of nonunion and prolonged weightbearing restriction. When performed appropriately, the athlete can be returned to weightbearing activity at two weeks post-operatively and can often return to running and impact activities at 6 weeks if the athlete is pain-free at the surgical site and there is radiographic evidence of adequate callous formation.

*Lareau et al. published on return-to-play (RTP) in National Football League players following operative Jones fracture treatment.* *The authors reviewed 25 NFL players from 2004-2014, all of which received Jones-specific intramedullary screw and iliac crest bone marrow aspirate with demineralized bone matrix injected at the fracture site.* **They noted** a 100% RTP with a re-fracture rate of 3 patients (12%) and concluded that with an appropriately placed intramedullary screw and an aggressive rehabilitation protocol, early RTP was achievable with a low re-fracture rate in professional athletes.

*Watson et al. reviewed early return-to-play (RTP) after intramedullary screw fixation of acute Jones fractures in collegiate athletes.* *The authors reviewed 26 fractures in 25 athletes and found an average time of RTP of 3.6 weeks postoperatively, with follow-up radiographs showing no evidence of nonunion, malunion, or additional hardware complications at 1 year.* **They concluded** that athletes with acute Jones fractures can safely be allowed to return to play after intramedullary screw fixation as soon as their symptoms allow, without significant complications, usually within 4 weeks from injury.

Illustration B graphically demonstrates the vascular watershed area of Zone II. Illustration C demonstrates the different anatomic zones of the fifth metatarsal base.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 4: Studies have indicated that weightbearing activity can commence as soon as 1-2 weeks post-operatively, with return-to-play occurring on average 4-6 weeks after surgery but reasonably as soon as the patient is pain-free with adequate radiographic evidence of callous.
Answer 3: Adjunct treatment with percutaneous bone grafting has been used in elite athletes, but is not always indicated in primary surgical fixation of Jones fractures; though it is commonly performed in the setting of revision surgery or nonunions, it is not likely the cause of failure in this patient.
Answer 5: Use of the largest diameter screw that will accommodate the intramedullary canal is encouraged to allow for maximum canal fill and compression at the fracture site.
