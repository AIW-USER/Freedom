## Question

A 24-year-old active duty female service member presents with longstanding left sided hip pain. She states the pain radiates into her groin in which 6 months of physical therapy failed to provide relief. An intra articular injection did help the pain, but only for a few weeks. Figure A displays her AP pelvis imaging with mild coxa valga with a lateral center edge angle of 15.2°. A separate false profile view displays an anterior center edge angle of 14°. A recent MRI shows a hypertrophied labrum with intrasubstance tearing. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment to provide lasting symptom resolution?

![[q217992-a-ddh-hip.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Physical therapy and corticosteroid injection
2. Arthroscopic debridement of her labrum and cam resection
3. Arthroscopic cam resection and acetabuloplasty
4. Redirection Salter osteotomy
5. Bernese pelvic acetabular osteotomy

## Correct option

**5. Bernese pelvic acetabular osteotomy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has adult hip dysplasia which has failed conservative management. As a skeletally mature adult, she should be considered for a Bernese pelvic acetabular osteotomy (PAO) as it has been shown to prevent the need for early total hip arthroplasty in this patient population.

Hip dysplasia, if left untreated early in life, results in a shallow acetabulum with increased mechanical stress on the cartilage matrix beyond the physiologic level of tolerance. Importantly, dysplastic acetabuli are globally deficient both in shape and multidirectional orientation. Classically these patients will have anterior insufficiency with poor coverage of the femoral head, relative lateralization of the hip center of rotation, and a relatively small contact area between the femoral head and dysplastic acetabulum. The asymmetric concentration of force across the hip joint results in articular cartilage and labral damage. These dysplastic acetabuli are often accompanied by proximal femur abnormalities including smaller aspherical femoral heads and an increased neck-shaft angle (coxa valga). Collectively, these deformities predispose patients to early-onset arthritis with the need for total hip replacement (THA) at a young age (typically in their 30s-40s).

*Sohatee and colleagues performed a systematic review regarding different hip preservation surgeries and the rates of conversion to total hip arthroplasty.* *As part of their findings,* **they reported** 46 eligible papers including 4862 patients who underwent PAO with a subsequent conversation to THA in only 8.31% of patients. **They concluded** that PAO for joint preservation surgery appears to defer or at least delay the need for THA.

*Gala and colleagues performed an educational review of adult hip dysplasia and the different treatment options.* *The authors discuss the varying types of pelvic osteotomies dependent on the age of the patient.* *They reviewed the need for Bernese PAO for skeletally mature patients with hip dysplasia and cite up to 75% 20-year survival rate (i.e. 25% of patients went onto THA).* **They conclude** that acetabular osteotomies remain the most common procedures performed for treating adult dysplastic hips with notable success rates.

*Sanchez-Sotelo and colleagues performed a two-part, comprehensive review regarding the different treatment options for adult hip dysplasia.* *They discuss the unfortunate need for THA in some patients despite attempted hip preservation surgeries.* *They reviewed the Crowe classification and its implications in surgical planning.* While THA in this setting can be technically difficult, the results of THA demonstrate a high rate of pain relief and functional improvement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While physical therapy and a corticosteroid injection would likely provide some symptomatic relief, these conservative treatments would fail to provide definitive long-term symptom relief in patients in their 20s.
Answer 2: Poor short-term outcomes, including persistent pain and iatrogenic instability after labral debridement, has been shown in various studies in which adult DDH was treated with arthroscopy alone (without an acetabular reorienting surgery). It would not be prudent to perform arthroscopic surgery on this patient alone.
Answer 3: This patient has adult hip dysplasia, which is a separate pathology from femoroacetabular impingement. Further, she does not display the cam nor pincer type deformities classically seen on radiographic imaging. Therefore a cam resection and acetabuloplasty would not be indicated.
Answer 4: A Salter redirection osteotomy involves an opening wedge osteotomy through the ilium superior to the acetabulum in order to obtain lateral coverage of the femoral head. It relies on a hinge through malleable triradiate cartilage in a skeletally immature individual and therefore is not indicated in this patient.
