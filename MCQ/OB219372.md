## Question

A previously healthy 2-year-old male presents to your pediatric emergency department with his parents who claim he has been walking with a limp for the last week and a half. They deny any knowledge of trauma and state that he has been ambulating since he was 11 months old. On examination, he has a mild degree of warmth about his right knee and a moderate-sized effusion. Radiographs show no fracture. Lab studies demonstrate WBC of 11,500/mm3, ESR of 60 mm/hr, and CRP of 12 mg/dL. Aspiration of the knee shows 30,000 nucleated cells with 72% PMNs. Initial gram stain and culture are negative but PCR testing is positive. You inform the parents you are concerned he may have septic arthritis of the knee. Which of the following best describes the most likely causative bacteria?

## Options

1. Gram negative, aerobic bacilli
2. Gram negative, anaerobic coccobacilli
3. Gram negative, aerobic diplococci
4. Gram positive, catalase positive, anaerobic coccus
5. Gram positive, catalase negative, anaerobic coccus

## Correct option

**2. Gram negative, anaerobic coccobacilli**

## Answer explanation

Kingella kingae is the most likely causative bacteria in this 2-year-old male with subacute onset of septic arthritis in his knee and positive PCR testing.

Septic arthritis is defined as an intra-articular infection that can occur in any joint. In pediatric patients, it most commonly occurs in the hip and knee. Enzymatic destruction of the cartilage takes place within 8 hours, therefore, prompt treatment is crucial to ensuring good outcomes. In contrast, transient synovitis occurs where there is irritation of the joint lining without infection, usually after a systemic infection but occurs much most commonly in the hip than knee. Aspiration of the joint in question can help in the diagnosis of septic arthritis based on cell count as well as identification of the causative organism which can direct antibiotic treatment after incision and drainage. Kingella kingae has been increasingly recognized recently as a common cause of septic arthritis in younger pediatric patients <4 years old who have a more subacute presentation, as it is less virulent than bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus. Kingella should be cultured on blood agar media to be best identified. Kingella is a particularly common cause of infection in cases where cultures and gram stain are negative, with PCR testing helpful in identification.

*Montgomery and Rosenfeld reviewed pediatric osteomyelitis and septic arthritis.* **They note** that in all comers, Staphylococcus aureus continues to be the most commonly identified pathogen, but new techniques have increased diagnostic accuracy and identification of additional organisms such as Kingella. **They conclude** that more advanced imaging studies and laboratory testing in addition to patient examination should be combined for the best understanding of the disease process.

*Rosenfeld et al. reviewed key concepts of musculoskeletal (MSK) infections for an instructional course lecture in 2017.* **They note** that MSK infections have increased in incidence and severity over time, which they attribute to the increase in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and its virulence factors. **They note** that bacterial genome sequencing may provide insight into helping identify virulence factors which could be targets for future therapies but currently, management relies on organism identification, advanced imaging, and physical examination.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This represents Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which would be a rare cause of septic arthritis in an otherwise healthy 2-year-old.
Answer 3: This represents Neisseria gonorrhoeae which is a more common cause of septic arthritis in adolescents and young adults who are sexually active.
Answer 4: This represents Staphylococcus aureus which is the most common cause of septic arthritis in children >2 years old and is typically more virulent than Kingella, with a rapid onset of symptoms.
Answer 5: This represents Group B streptococcus (agalactiae), which may cause septic arthritis in neonates, usually from a vaginal infection at the time of delivery.
