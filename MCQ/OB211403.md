## Question

A 17-year-old basketball player went up for a rebound and inverted his left foot upon landing. He experienced immediate pain and swelling to the ankle. He was taken to the local urgent care where radiographs were taken (Figures A and B). The patient is given crutches and referred to you for further evaluation. On exam, you notice significant swelling and ecchymosis of the left ankle. With the ankle in 10 degrees of plantar flexion, the talus is noted to translate 10mm anterior to the tibia. Which of the following structures in Figure C is most likely injured in this patient?

## Correct option

**4. D**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained an inversion lateral ankle ligament injury and has a positive anterior drawer test on examination. The structure most likely injured is the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), which is labeled D in Figure C.

Lateral ankle sprains are the most common reason for missed games in the athlete population. The 3 lateral ankle ligaments are the ATFL, the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) and the posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL). The ATFL and CFL are the most commonly injured ligaments, respectively. The ATFL can be tested by performing a drawer test in 10 degrees of plantarflexion and noting 8-10 mm of anterior subluxation of the talus with respect to the tibia. The CFL is similarly tested, however, in 10 degrees of dorsiflexion. The PTFL is the strongest and least likely lateral ligament to be injured.

*Tropp et al. performed a review of 2 different methods (coordination training vs. wearing an orthosis) for the prevention of ankle joint injuries in soccer players.* **The authors noted** that the orthosis is an alternative to taping, and can be used during the rehabilitation period after injury or when playing on uneven ground. **They also noted** that coordination training on an ankle disk should be included in the rehabilitation of ankle injuries to prevent functional instability. **The authors concluded** that these 2 methods may also be done prophylactically to players with previous ankle problems in order to break the vicious circle of recurrent sprains.

*Stiell et al. performed a validation study to determine if the Ottawa ankle rules can help reduce unnecessary radiation in ankle sprains.* **The authors noted** that the original rules were found to have sensitivities of 1.0 for detecting 121 malleolar fractures, and 0.98 for detecting 49 midfoot zone fractures. **They noted** that when applied, the Ottawa ankle rules had sensitivities of 1.0 for 50 malleolar zone fractures, and 1.0 for 19 midfoot zone fractures. **The authors concluded** that the Ottawa ankle rules allow for the potential reduction in radiography of 34% for the ankle series and 30% for the foot series.

*Hertel reviewed a randomized study by Lamb et al. to determine the outcomes of 4 different types of immobilization following ankle sprains.* *The authors looked at 10 days of post-injury use of the Tubigrip compression bandage, the Bledsoe boot, the Aircast brace, and below-knee cast with patient-reported outcomes compared between the groups at 3 months and 9 months.* **The authors concluded** that a below-knee cast for 10 days resulted in more rapid resolution of symptoms and the greatest recovery of self-reported ankle function at 3 months’ follow-up compared with the other three treatments. No difference was noted at 9 months.

*Osborne and Rizzo looked at the prophylactic use of ankle braces and functional ankle rehabilitation for the prevention of ankle sprains.* *They cite that certain components of ankle rehabilitation, such as proprioceptive exercises, have been found to protect the joint from re-injury.* Multifaceted ankle sprain prevention programs that incorporate a variety of strategies for injury reduction are also effective in sprain prevention. **The authors conclude** that surgery for an ankle sprain is principally only reserved for patients who fail a comprehensive non-operative treatment program and those with chronic functional instability.

Figures A and B are normal lateral and mortise ankle radiographs. Figure C is a schematic of the lateral ankle ligaments and the syndesmosis ligaments. Illustration A is a labeled version of Figure C with the 3 primary lateral ankle ligaments being bolded (PTFL, CFL, ATFL).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Ligaments A and B are the posterior and anterior inferior tibiofibular ligaments that make up the syndesmosis. The syndesmosis can be tested in the office by the squeeze test and external rotation stress
Answer 3: Ligament C is the PTFL, which is the strongest of the 3 lateral ankle ligaments and the least likely to be torn by an inversion ankle sprain
Answer 5: Ligament E is the CFL, which is the 2nd most commonly injured lateral ankle ligament and is tested by the talar tilt test as well as anterior talar laxity with the ankle in DORSIFLEXION.
