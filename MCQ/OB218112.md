## Question

A patient is undergoing a stage 2 revision total knee arthroplasty after clearing a periprosthetic infection. The surgeon begins their medial parapatellar approach with a longitudinal skin incision. A medial parapatellar arthrotomy is made and a standard medial release is performed to elevate the proximal-medial tibial periosteum and associated soft tissues. There is significant fibrous scar tissue throughout the intra-articular space of the knee. The surgeon performs a limited debridement to visualize the all-polyethylene tibial component of the articulating spacer. The surgeon finds the knee is incredibly stiff and cannot flex past 80 degrees. Further, they are unable to subluxate/evert the patella. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in exposure?

## Options

1. Scar debridement to recreate the medial and lateral gutters and remove the polyethylene
2. Utilize a limited lateral release being careful to preserve the superolateral genicular artery
3. Perform a quadricep snip, utilize a lateral release, and connect both proximally
4. Perform a tibial tubercle osteotomy maintaining a lateral boney hinge
5. Release the collateral ligaments and plan for hinged rotating platform implant

## Correct option

**1. Scar debridement to recreate the medial and lateral gutters and remove the polyethylene**

## Answer explanation

The vast majority of revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) cases can be performed without the need for extensile approach alterations. However, these extensile exposure options may be required after appropriate scar debridement in which the medial and lateral gutters are recreated and the polyethylene has been removed (Answer 1).

Obtaining adequate exposure can be a challenge in revision TKA cases. Prior skin incisions, dense scarring, and the presence of patella baja all may significantly complicate the ability to safely explant underlying components without injuring the extensor mechanism (i.e. quadriceps tendon, patella, and/or patella tendon). During a revision medial parapatellar approach, it is often necessary to extend the medial release further posteriorly than during a standard primary approach. This is to facilitate tibial external rotation and thus lateralization of the extensor mechanism. After this is completed, generous scar debridement must be performed. A good indicator for an adequate debridement involves restoring the medial and lateral gutters around the femoral and tibial components (while being careful to not injure the collateral ligaments). The scar tissue beneath the extensor mechanism must also be removed, as this area of scar buildup is the most likely cause for the inability to subluxate or evert the patella (Illustration A). Removing the polyethylene will also facilitate exposure and further scar debridement in the posterolateral corner. If the surgeon continues to have difficulty obtaining 110 degrees of flexion (which is necessary for safe implant removal), or if they cannot subluxate/evert the patella safely without disruption of the extensor mechanism, then extensile approaches may be pursued. These should include a stepwise progression starting with a quadriceps snip followed by a tibial tubercle osteotomy (preferred) or quadricep turn-down as necessary.

*Vasarhelyi and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of the four key principles to revision TKA: (1) getting adequate exposure, (2) addressing bone loss, (3) obtaining stability and (4) restoring the joint line.* **The authors state** that exposure is the most important step as it facilitates the safe removal of TKA components, protects the extensor mechanism, and allows for easy reimplantation without placing undue stress on the underlying bone. When discussing the exposure, **they emphasize** a generous medial release to the posteromedial tibia, removing all fibrous scar from beneath the extensor mechanism, and restoring the medial and lateral gutters prior to explanting the polyethylene component (Illustration A).

*Meek and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institution’s outcomes after performing a TKA with and without a quadricep snip.* *They utilized the Oxford Hip Score, the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), Short-Form-12, Hospital for Special Surgery scores, range of motion, and patient satisfaction scores to compare both groups.* **They found** no significant difference in any variable at a mean follow-up of 3 years. **The authors concluded** that the use of a quadricep snip had no effect on post-operative outcomes.

*DeHaan and colleagues provided a surgical technique guide and overview for performing a tibial tubercle osteotomy.* *They also stressed performing an aggressive synovectomy with scar tissue debridement and removal of any modular polyethylene component to gain exposure prior to utilizing adjunct extensile approaches.* **The authors highlight** the tibial tubercle osteotomy’s ability to assist with the removal of long tibial stems, however, is predicated on the patient having good bone quality. **They further emphasize** that an appropriately performed tibial tubercle osteotomy that is anatomically reduced and fixed afterward can allow for immediate post-operative weight bearing and range of motion to 90 degrees of flexion in a hinged knee brace. **The authors conclude** that the tibial tubercle osteotomy is superior to a quadricep turn-down (decreased incidence of extensor lag) and should be utilized if a quadricep snip does not suffice to gain adequate exposure.

Illustration A is a labeled image from the Vasarhelyi article demonstrating the before and after clinical images of performing appropriate intra-articular scar tissue removal. One can appreciate the amount of scar tissue removed from the posterior aspect of the extensor mechanism. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: A lateral release was originally intended to improve patellar tracking if lateral patella subluxation was appreciated after the final TKA implants were placed. It does not readily improve exposure to the intra-articular space when performed in isolation.
Answer 3: The quadriceps snip is a quick and simple extensile approach that can improve visualization and remove tension from the patellar tendon insertion (Illustration B). While it has been correlated with initial post-operative quadricep weakness, the quadricep snip in isolation allows for the same post-operative protocol and functional outcomes with no increased risk for extensor lag. When a lateral release is extended proximally and connected to a quadricep snip, it is referred to as a quadricep turndown. While this extensile approach can dramatically improve surgical exposure, it is well established to result in a significant post-operative extension lag, disrupted patella blood supply, and worse functional outcomes.
Answer 4: An appropriately performed TTO can drastically improve surgical exposure, however, the TTO is not without caveats. It requires post-operative restrictions in range of motion until the osteotomy site heals roughly 4-6 weeks after surgery. Despite this, TTOs display a surprisingly low complication rate with union rates consistently reported at or near 100% in various studies.
Answer 5: Releasing the collateral ligaments and converting to a hinged implant serves as a final bail-out option. Less aggressive exposure techniques should be sought to allow for less constrained implants as they have better functional outcomes and lower rates of aseptic loosening compared to hinged rotating implants.
