## Question

A 56-year-old male presents with a red, warm, and swollen foot that began roughly one week ago. His medical history is significant for diabetes and a stroke several years ago, with residual sensory deficits in the ipsilateral foot. He denies fevers or chills, and he lacks protective sensation. There are no ulcers or breaks in the skin. His CRP and white count are mildly elevated, and he is hemodynamically stable. A radiograph is shown in Figure A. When you evaluate him in the emergency department, he states the erythema has significantly improved since he began elevating the leg upon arrival. What is the best initial treatment plan for this patient?

![[q221046-a-img-4545.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Emergent debridement and irrigation
2. Total contact casting
3. Midfoot arthrodesis
4. Plantar exostectomy
5. Below knee amputation

## Correct option

**2. Total contact casting**

## Answer explanation

This patient is most likely presenting with Charcot neuropathy of his foot. The acute phase of Charcot neuropathy can often be confused with infection, but improvement in erythema upon elevation of the extremity is a useful sign to distinguish the two. The best initial treatment of acute-phase Charcot foot is a trial of total contact casting (Answer 2).

Charcot neuropathy is a chronic inflammatory condition that most commonly occurs in patients with loss of protective sensation in the foot. Sensory deficits are most often due to longstanding uncontrolled diabetes but may also result from trauma or other neurologic conditions. The patient described has both diabetes and a prior stroke with residual deficits, placing him at increased risk for developing Charcot arthropathy. Diminished sensation permits repetitive microtrauma that goes unrecognized and accumulates over time. In addition, autonomic neuropathy contributes to vascular dysregulation and impaired blood flow, further compromising tissue healing. Charcot neuropathy progresses through distinct stages. The acute phase typically presents with a warm, erythematous, and swollen foot that can closely mimic infection. A helpful clinical distinction is that erythema associated with Charcot often improves with limb elevation, whereas infectious erythema typically does not. Advanced imaging, including MRI, may not reliably differentiate acute Charcot from osteomyelitis. Initial management of acute Charcot is nonoperative, consisting of strict immobilization and offloading, most commonly with total contact casting, until the inflammatory process subsides. After the acute phase resolves, protective bracing is used to reduce the risk of ulceration. Corrective osteotomy or arthrodesis may be considered for residual deformity but should be avoided during the acute inflammatory stage.

*Pinzur et al. authored a review of Charcot neuropathy.* **They highlight** the key diagnostic factors to consider and outline treatment strategies in both the acute and chronic setting. The authors include sections on surgical principles to address chronic valgus or varus deformities, and joints that have been chronically dislocated. **They emphasize** that each case should be treated uniquely based on the patient and their deformity.

*Bariteau et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 59 patients with idiopathic neuropathy and Charcot foot.* **They found** that the midfoot was most commonly affected, and roughly a third of patients presented with bilateral disease. Roughly 43% of feet were treated nonoperatively with a success rate of 92%. The remainder underwent surgery, though 8 cases had treatment failure resulting in either a transtibial amputation or persistent ulcerations. **The authors concluded** that Charcot presents more widely in nondiabetic patients with idiopathic neuropathy than previously thought, and heightened awareness of this diagnosis should be considered to appropriately take care of these patients.

Figure A shows a lateral radiograph of a Charcot foot. Note the midfoot deformity and joint collapse characteristic of Charcot foot.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While infection and acute Charcot arthropathy can present similarly, the lack of ulceration and improvement with elevation point more towards Charcot arthropathy. The initial treatment for Charcot arthropathy is conservative.
Answers 3 & 4: While all of these surgical options can be considered at some point to correct deformity, they should not be performed during the acute phase of Charcot arthropathy
Answer 5: Below-knee amputation is a salvage operation in the event of persistent ulceration or infection. All other treatment options should be exhausted first.
