## Question

A 14-year-old boy presents to the office for his two-week follow-up of the injury observed in Figures A and B. He subsequently underwent attempted percutaneous pinning, which was unsuccessful and ultimately necessitated open reduction. On examination, he is found to have a peripheral nerve palsy. Which of the following motor deficits would be most likely found?

## Options

1. Finger adduction
2. Thumb adduction
3. Thumb interphalangeal joint flexion
4. Thumb opposition
5. Small finger extension

## Correct option

**5. Small finger extension**

## Answer explanation

This 14-year-old sustained a radial neck fracture, which required open reduction. The most common nerve palsy following fixation is of the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN; 15%), which would result in the inability to extend the small finger (extensor digiti minimi; Answer 5).

Radial neck fractures are common injuries in the pediatric population, being the third most common elbow injury after supracondylar and lateral condylar fractures. They are often seen with other fractures (30-50%), most commonly occurring at the proximal ulna/olecranon or medial/lateral condyles. The treatment of radial neck fractures is largely dependent upon the degree of displacement. For fractures displaced less than 30 degrees, nonoperative treatment is the mainstay. However, for fractures with angulation greater than 30 degrees, at a minimum, closed reduction is warranted with possible percutaneous pinning or open reduction depending upon the treating physician's ability to attain reasonable reduction. When surgical intervention is performed, the most common nerve injury is to the PIN, which innervates all the muscles of the posterior compartment of the forearm, excluding the anconeus muscle, while also innervating the extensor carpi radialis brevis of the mobile wad.

Nicholson and Skaggs provide a review of pediatric radial head and neck fractures, detailing their incidence, treatment, and outcomes. They note the injury is commonly encountered in pediatric practices and can often be treated nonoperatively as long as the patient's range of motion remains full. They do note, however, that the existing literature is variable, and those with greater than 50 degrees of angulation often require fixation, which often results in good outcomes. They recommend placing pins posterolaterally with the arm in maximal pronation to put the PIN as far away from the pins as possible.

Radomisli and Rosen discuss the controversies surrounding the treatment of pediatric radial neck fractures. They recommend treating angulations less than 30 degrees nonoperatively, while fractures greater than 30 degrees should undergo closed reduction with percutaneous pinning. If reduction fails, only then should they progress to open reduction if percutaneous pinning fails. Despite this, the authors note that the tolerance for treating the injury nonoperatively varies widely, with some tolerating up to 60 degrees. Ultimately, they recommend focusing on pain and range of motion when assessing postoperative outcomes following treatment.

*Basmajian et al. performed a retrospective review of pediatric radial neck fractures at two level-1 centers, totaling 78 patients.* **They found** 37% of the cohort sustained other injuries. The majority of radial neck fractures were able to be treated without intervention, although 33% required either percutaneous pinning or open reduction. Of those that underwent open reduction, the majority (65%) experienced poor functional outcomes according to the Tibone elbow outcome scoring system. Moreover, there was a high rate of complications (38%), with stiffness being the most common. **The authors noted** that age did play a role, with those younger than ten having better functional outcomes compared to those over ten.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Finger adduction, which is achieved by the palmar interossei, are innervated by the ulnar nerve.
Answer 2: Thumb adduction, which is performed by the adductor pollicis, is innervated by the ulnar nerve.
Answer 3: Thumb interphalangeal joint flexion is achieved with firing of the flexor pollicis longus, which is innervated by the anterior interosseous nerve.
Answer 4: Thumb opposition, which is achieved with the opponens pollicis, is innervated by the median nerve.
