## Question

A 10 years old boy fell down and sustained trivial trauma. This was followed by quadriparesis, though he recovered. What likely pathology could have it been [FNB 2023]

## Options

1. Congenital Atlanto-axial instability
2. Ossified ligamentum flavum
3. Cervical cord Syrinx
4. A Bifid atlas

## Correct option

**1. Congenital Atlanto-axial instability**

## Answer explanation

Ans: A
EXPLANATION: -
A bidif atlas would not cause quadriparesis. Age doesn’t support Ossified ligamentum flavum and a cord syrinx deficit would not recover so fast. So best pick is choice A. This could well be a child with Congenital rotatory atlanto axial instability/ subluxation.

To understand the concept of atlanto-axial stability we need to know about the support provided by various ligaments around the Cranio-Vertebral Junction (CVJ). Stability is primarily due to ligaments, with little contribution from bony articulations and joint capsules.

Ligaments of the CV junction

![[TO19356254-1.jpg]]

1. Ligaments that connect the atlas to the occiput:
a) anterior atlantooccipital membrane: cephalad extension of the anterior longitudinal ligament. Extends from anterior margin of foramen magnum (FM) to anterior arch of C1.
b) posterior atlantooccipital membrane: connects the posterior margin of the FM to posterior arch of C1.
c) the ascending band of the cruciate ligament.

2. Ligaments that connect the axis (viz. the odontoid) to the occiput:
a) Tectorial membrane: 2 components
- Superficial component: cephalad continuation of the posterior longitudinal ligament. A strong band connecting the dorsal surface of the dens to the ventral surface of the FM above, and dorsal surface of C2 & C3 bodies below.
- Accessory (deep) portion: located laterally, connects C2 to occipital condyles.
b) Alar (“check”) ligaments [Paired parasagittal sturctures]
Occipito-alar portion: connects side of the dens to occipital condyle.
Atlanto-alar portion: connects side of the dens to the lateral mass of C1.
c) Apical odontoid ligament (single midline structure): connects tip of dens to the FM. Little mechanical strength.

3. Ligaments that connect the axis to the atlas:
A) Transverse atlantal ligament (TAL) or (usually) just transverse ligament: the horizontal component of the cruciate ligament. Attaches at the medial tubercles of C1. Traps the dens against the anterior atlas via a strap-like mechanism. Provides the majority of the strength (“the strongest ligament of the spine”).
B) Atlanto-alar portion of the alar ligaments
C) descending band of the cruciate ligament

The atlanto-axial joint is one of the most mobile joints of the body and is estimated to be moving at approximately 600 times every hour.
The facet joints of C1-C2 are almost horizontal which allow 60% of the entire normal rotation of the cervical spine.

ATLANTO AXIAL [C1-C2] INSTABILITY
The C1-C2 is largely stabilized by two sets of ligaments – transverse atlantal ligament (TAL) and alar ligament, of which the latter is primarily responsible for limiting the rotation of C1 over C2. Atlantoaxial instability (AAI) also called as Atlantoaxial Subluxation is characterized by excessive movement at the junction between the atlas (C1) and axis (C2) as a result of either a bony or ligamentous abnormality.
AAI could be from diverse causes as listed below: -
Trauma: The disruption or insufficiency of transverse atlantal ligament (TAL) usually occurs following a violent hyperflexion force to the neck
Metabolic: Errors of metabolism (e.g. Morquio syndrome)
Congenital: Down syndrome (most notable; these patients are prone to AAI due to lax ligaments and osseous abnormalities), Skeletal dysplasias, Os odontoideum (it is the separation of the odontoid process from the body of C2)
Infections: Grisel syndrome
Inflammatory: Rheumatoid arthritis
Tumors: Neurofibromatosis

 Criteria to label a case as AAI (Atlanto-axial instability): -
A widened anterior atlanto-dental interval (AADI) of greater than 3 mm in adults and 5 mm in children, on flexion lateral cervical Xrays suggests ruptured TAL.
Posterior atlanto-dental interval (PADI) or Space available for cord (SAC)– Distance between posterior surface of odontoid and anterior margin of posterior ring of C1. Normal is between 17-29 mm at C1. PADI < 14mm predicts cord compression.
TAL disruption is also confirmed on open mouth X rays with increased spread of C1 lateral masses over C2, of more than 6.9mm (called as Rule of Spence).

Types of atlantoaxial subluxation/ dislocation (Fielding and Hawkins)
1. Rotatory: usually seen in children after a fall or minor trauma
2. Anterior
3. Posterior: rare. Usually from erosion of odontoid. Is unstable. Requires fusion.

AARS (atlanto-axial rotatory subluxation) is an anterior displacement of one C1-C2 joint with a concomitant posterior migration of contralateral articulation and presents with the ‘cock robin position’ of the neck, with head tilted to the contralateral side with slight flexion of the neck.
Usually seen in children.
May occur spontaneously (with rheumatoid arthritis or with congenital dens anomalies), following major or minor trauma (including neck manipulation or even with neck rotation while yawning), or with an infection of the head or neck including upper respiratory tract (known as Grisel syndrome: inflammation may cause mechanical and chemical injury to the facet capsules and/or transverse atlantal ligament (TAL)).
Rotational deformity at the atlanto-axial junction is usually of short duration and easily corrected with conservative methods.

Clinical findings
Findings may include: neck pain, headache, torticollis—characteristic “cock robin” head position with ≈ 20° lateral tilt to one side, 20° rotation to the other, and slight (≈ 10°) flexion, reduced range of motion, and facial flattening.
Brainstem and cerebellar infarction and even death may occur with compromise of circulation through the Vertebral artery.

Evaluation
Diagnostic modalities – X-rays, CT & MRI are recommended to evaluate fractures, TAL & its bony attachments.
Open mouth X-rays may demonstrate an asymmetric C1 lateral masses with respect to midline.
CT is essential in diagnosing these injuries and reveals the rotated position of C1 on C2.

Fielding and Hawkins classification for Atlanto-axial subluxation/dislocation: -
Type I – Is simple rotator displacement with an intact transverse ligament.
Type II – Injuries involve anterior displacement of C1 on C2 of 3-5mm with one lateral mass serving as a pivot and a deficiency of the transverse ligament.
Type III – injuries involve greater than 5 mm of anterior displacement.
Type IV- injuries involve the posterior displacement of C1 on C2.
Both type III and IV are highly unstable injuries.

Treatment
Type I injuries (stable subluxations) – Collar for 6-8 weeks
Type II injuries may be potentially unstable – Collar/ Surgery
Type III and IV rotator displacements that are unstable are treated surgically with reduction and C1-C2 fusion. Techniques of fusion vary from anterior or posterior.
For simplicity, indications for Surgery can be categorized as follows: -

For Adults

ADI of more than 5 mm
Moderate displacement or instability in dynamic films

For Children (when one or more of the following is present)

Neurological deficits
Persistent ADI of more than 4 mm
Persistent deformity of more than three months
Recurrent deformity despite six weeks of immobilization
