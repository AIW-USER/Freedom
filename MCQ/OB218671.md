## Answer explanation

Talar neck fractures are high-energy injuries to the hindfoot that are associated with a high incidence of avascular necrosis (AVN). These injuries are classified by the Hawkins classification, which provides a reliable predictor of the risk for the development of AVN. Hawkins II talar neck fractures, described as displaced talar neck fractures with concomitant subtalar dislocation, are associated with an AVN risk of 20-50% (Answer 4).

Talar neck fractures are high-energy injuries that typically result from forced dorsiflexion during an axial load. Ipsilateral lower extremity fractures are common, and these injuries typically present with profound soft-tissue swelling. These injuries are classified by the Hawkins Classification (Illustration A), which uses the pattern of injury to predict the risk of the development of AVN.

The talus is supplied by three arterial sources: the posterior tibial artery, which gives off the artery of the tarsal canal (dominant blood supply to the body), and the deltoid branch, which supplies the medial portion of the body. In displaced fractures, the deltoid branch may be the only remaining blood supply. The anterior tibial artery and the perforating peroneal artery via the artery of the tarsal sinus both supply the head and neck as well. The development of AVN most likely occurs secondary to arterial disruption at the time of injury, as studies have shown that delayed internal fixation is not associated with the development of AVN. Although AVN is a common complication following these injuries, it is not the most common complication, as subtalar arthritis develops following 50% of all talar neck fractures.

Ahmad and Raikin provide a review of talar fractures. The authors note that talar neck fractures represent over 50% of talar injuries and that these injuries typically occur secondary to hyper-dorsiflexion and axial loading of the foot against a stationary tibia. Regarding complications, the authors note that Hawkins II fractures, defined as a displaced talar neck fracture with a concomitant subtalar dislocation, have a reported rate of AVN of 20-50%. The most common complication following talar neck fractures, however, is posttraumatic arthritis, which develops 54% of the time.

*Jordan et al. provide a systematic review of complications following talar neck fractures.* *Among the 16 studies included in their review, Hawkins type II injuries were the most common and accounted for 50.88% of all talar neck fractures.* Avascular necrosis occurred in 26.47% of all fractures and subtalar arthritis occurred in 44.97% of all fractures, with 3.97% of fractures going on to non-union following appropriate treatment. Overall, **the authors conclude** that these fractures are debilitating injuries fraught with a litany of complications, despite appropriate treatment.

*Boden et al. investigated complications and functional outcomes following pan-talar dislocation without associated talar fracture.* *Their study included 19 patients with a mean age of 39.6 years.* Overall, 88% of patients developed osteonecrosis, rates which far exceeded those following talar neck fractures. Promisingly, only 2 patients developed collapsed of the talar dome. **The authors conclude** that persistent pain and functional limitations are common after these injuries, but note that urgent surgical debridement and acute talar reimplantation were not associated with the development of deep infection.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Hawkins IV fractures have an AVN risk of 70-100%.
Answer 2: Hawkins III fractures have an AVN risk of 20-100%
Answer 3: Hawkins I fractures have an AVN risk of 0-13%.
Answer 5: Posterior process fractures are not commonly associated with the development of AVN.
