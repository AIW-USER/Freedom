## Question

A 24-year-old male presents to the emergency department complaining of left shoulder pain after being involved in an MVC as an unrestrained passenger. Radiographs are obtained of the left shoulder, which demonstrates a glenoid neck fracture. Concomitant injury to which of the following structures would create a "double disruption" to the superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC)?

## Options

1. Glenoid labrum
2. Supraspinatus tendon
3. Superior transverse scapular ligament
4. Coracohumeral ligament
5. Acromioclavicular ligaments

## Correct option

**5. Acromioclavicular ligaments**

## Answer explanation

This 24-year-old male sustained a glenoid neck fracture following an MVC. The presence of an acromioclavicular joint injury would create a "double disruption" of the superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC; Answer 5).

The SSSC is a bone and soft tissue ring in the shoulder that allows for the upper extremity to be suspended. The complex comprises several anatomic structures, including the glenoid process, coracoid process, coracoclavicular ligaments, distal clavicle, acromioclavicular joint, and the acromion process, with some reporting the coracoacromial ligament should also be included. When an isolated injury occurs to one of these structures, the complex remains stable. However, when two or more structures are compromised, which is subsequently referred to as a "double disruption" or "floating shoulder," the complex becomes destabilized, rendering the shoulder's ability to effectively resist gravitational forces and the weight of the arm, which serves as an indication for surgical intervention. While this condition most commonly manifests as ipsilateral scapular neck and clavicle fractures, combined ligamentous and bony injuries have been described in the literature, namely ipsilateral acromioclavicular dislocations and coracoid process fractures. On an even rarer occasion, "triple disruptions" have similarly been reported in the literature. Injuries to the aforementioned structures in isolation do not destabilize the complex, and they are commonly treated non-operatively. However, when double lesions occur, the complex becomes destabilized, necessitating surgical intervention.

DeFranco and Patterson provide a review of floating shoulders, detailing pathoanatomy, examination, work-up, and treatment of the entity. The authors note that classically, a floating shoulder is most widely known as an ipsilateral glenoid neck and clavicle fracture, but a glenoid neck fracture with ligamentous pathology, namely acromioclavicular and/or coracoclavicular ligament disruptions also constitutes a floating shoulder. The authors note while one disruption in the SSSC can be tolerated, double disruptions preclude the shoulder's ability to resist gravity, the weight of the arm, and other muscle forces to maintain stability, and as such, require surgical intervention.

*Cole et al. provide a review of the management of scapula fractures, expounding on the examination, work-up, and treatment of the condition.* **The authors note** high rates of concomitant ipsilateral extremity injury, at 50%, and as such, a meticulous physical exam should be performed. While they acknowledge that the indications of operative fixation of scapula fractures remain controversial, the presence of additional bony or soft tissue injury to the SSSC necessitates surgical repair, secondary altered shoulder biomechanics and its resultant instability.
