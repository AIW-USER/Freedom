## Question

A 50-year-old man presents to the clinic with 3 weeks of severe left-sided neck pain radiating into the arm. The pain began after lifting a heavy box at work. He reports worsening pain with neck extension and rotation to the left, along with intermittent numbness and weakness in his arm. Axial and sagittal MRI images are shown in Figures A and B, respectively. Which of the following combinations on physical examination is most consistent with the affected cervical nerve root?

## Options

1. Weak shoulder abduction, diminished biceps reflex, sensory loss over the lateral shoulder
2. Weak wrist extension, diminished brachioradialis reflex, sensory loss over the thumb and radial forearm
3. Weak elbow extension, diminished triceps reflex, sensory loss over the dorsal middle finger
4. Weak finger abduction, diminished triceps reflex, sensory loss over the small finger
5. Weak elbow flexion, diminished brachioradialis reflex, sensory loss in the middle finger

## Correct option

**3. Weak elbow extension, diminished triceps reflex, sensory loss over the dorsal middle finger**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a C6-7 paracentral disc herniation resulting in left-sided C7 radiculopathy. Weakness in elbow extension, diminished triceps reflex, and sensory loss over the dorsal middle finger are most consistent with a C7 lesion (Answer 3).

Cervical radiculopathy typically presents with radiating arm pain, often with axial neck pain, and may include sensory changes, motor weakness, or diminished reflexes in a dermatomal pattern. Provocative maneuvers, such as the Spurling and shoulder abduction tests, can aid diagnosis. The workup should prioritize a thorough history and physical exam, reserving MRI for cases with persistent symptoms beyond 4–6 weeks, progressive neurologic deficits, or red flag features. Electrodiagnostic studies are mainly indicated when a peripheral lesion is suspected. Management is typically conservative, emphasizing physical therapy (including neurodynamic techniques, cervical traction, and strengthening exercises), NSAIDs, and short-term immobilization, with epidural steroid injections considered for refractory cases and surgery reserved for those with disabling pain or progressive deficits.

*Rhee et al. performed a topical review on cervical radiculopathy, which involves cervical nerve root dysfunction presenting as radiating neck and arm pain, with up to 75% of acute cases improving spontaneously.* Nonsurgical management is the appropriate initial treatment for most patients, given the self-limited nature of the condition. Surgical decompression is reserved for cases with significant neurologic deficits or persistent symptoms despite conservative treatment, achieving 80-90% success rates for arm pain relief with either anterior or posterior approaches.

*Ellenberg et al. reviewed cervical radiculopathy,* **noting** that the underlying etiology is compression and inflammation of cervical nerve roots, most commonly C6 or C7, typically caused by disc herniation or spondylotic changes. The condition has an annual age-adjusted incidence of approximately 83 per 100,000 persons. Differential diagnosis must exclude peripheral neuropathies, myelopathy, and red flag conditions, including malignancy, spinal abscess, and cancer metastases. **The authors conclude** that most cases improve without surgery, and surgical intervention is indicated only for unremitting pain, progressive weakness, or new/progressive myelopathy despite conservative management.

Figure A is an axial T2-weighted MRI demonstrating a left paracentral C6–7 disc herniation consistent with C7 radiculopathy. Figure B is a sagittal T2-weighted MRI showing a C6–7 disc herniation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Weakness of shoulder abduction with a diminished biceps reflex and lateral-shoulder sensory loss localizes to a C5 radiculopathy.
Answer 2: Weak wrist extension with a diminished brachioradialis reflex and radial-forearm/thumb sensory loss is characteristic of a C6 radiculopathy, not C7.
Answer 4: Finger abduction weakness with small-finger sensory loss reflects C8/T1 involvement, which does not match the C7-level pathology.
Answer 5: Elbow flexion weakness and a reduced brachioradialis reflex point to C6, not C7, even though the middle-finger sensory change overlaps with C7.
