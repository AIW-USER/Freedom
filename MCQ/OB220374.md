## Options

1. Curettage, bone grafting, and prophylactic internal fixation
2. Intralesional doxycycline injection followed by serial radiographs
3. Intralesional triamcinolone acetonide injection and prophylactic internal fixation
4. Observation and protected weightbearing until the pain resolves
5. Percutaneous radiofrequency ablation (RFA) with a short period of protected weightbearing

## Correct option

**1. Curettage, bone grafting, and prophylactic internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a symptomatic non-ossifying fibroma (NOF) affecting her daily ability to ambulate that occupies more than 50% of the cross-sectional diameter of the distal femur, making curettage, bone grafting, and prophylactic internal fixation the most appropriate next step in her treatment.

Non-ossifying fibromas (NOF) are benign fibrous bone lesions commonly found in the metaphyseal region of the long bones of skeletally immature patients. Plain radiographs show an eccentric metaphyseal lesion with geographic margins that may have internal septations, making them appear "bubbly," though they frequently become more sclerotic as the lesions mature. They are usually found incidentally and can be observed with serial radiographs unless they are large, symptomatic, or result in pathologic fracture. In this case, they should be treated with curettage, grafting, and appropriate internal fixation.

*Moretti et al. published on whether intralesional curettage and defect grafting of NOF lesions would alleviate pain in young athletes and permit their return to unrestricted athletic activities.* *The authors retrospectively identified 29 patients who underwent curettage and grafting for solitary bone cysts (12 patients), NOF (nine), or fibrous dysplasia (eight) and found that 24 patients had no pain, five had occasional mild pain at last follow-up, and all patients resumed full activity at a mean of 3.3 months.* **They concluded** that curettage and packing with bone graft/substitute can provide pain relief and allow full athletic recovery for young athletes with benign lytic bone lesions.

*Cherix et al. published a case presentation of multiple non-ossifying fibromas as a cause of pathological femoral fracture in a patient with Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome.* **The authors note** that a 17-year-old female patient with known type 1 neurofibromatosis presented with a low-energy distal femoral fracture due to disseminated large non-ossifying fibromas that was subsequently treated with open reduction and internal plate fixation that healed uneventfully. **They concluded** that Jaffe-Campanacci is a rare syndrome associated with a high risk of pathological fracture due to the presence of multiple large non-ossifying fibromas of the long bones, though they can expect to be treated with a normal healing time.

Bovée et al. reviewed the role of activating mutations in the MAP-kinase pathway as drivers for the formation of non-ossifying fibromas of bone. Their data suggests that only a subset of cells in the lesion contain the mutation, with a second cell population consisting of histiocytes and osteoclast-like giant cells that appear to be reactive. They conclude that the finding of RAS-MAPK activation by somatic mutations in NOF indicates it should be considered a neoplasm and part of the broad RASopathy family of tumors, but that future studies should focus on unraveling the exact mechanism of spontaneous regression often seen in NOF that may provide clues to improve treatment of tumors in which the activated RAS-MAPK pathway has a more detrimental effect.

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of a skeletally immature distal femur with an expansile lucent lesion with a thin zone of transition and no internal mineralization or periosteal reaction consistent with a NOF. Figure C is an axial CT scan of the bilateral distal femurs showing the lesion occupying > 50% of the cross-sectional area of the bone compared to the normal side. Figures D and E are T1-weighted sagittal and T2-weighted coronal MRI images of the distal femur demonstrating an eccentric lesion composed of soft tissue with low T1 and T2 signal, that enhances following contrast administration. There are no fluid-fluid levels, and there is no surrounding edema, soft-tissue enhancement, or extra-osseous abnormality to suggest malignancy.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Intralesional doxycycline injections are not a common treatment for symptomatic NOFs.
Answer 3: Though intralesional cortisone injections may be used in the setting of a unicameral bone cyst (UBC), they are not used for the treatment of symptomatic NOFs.
Answer 4: Though most NOFs may be incidentalomas that can observed with serial radiography, an NOF in a weight-bearing bone that is causing daily pain with ambulation is concerning for impending fracture and should be treated accordingly.
Answer 5: Percutaneous RFA alone is not an appropriate treatment for a large symptomatic NOF.
