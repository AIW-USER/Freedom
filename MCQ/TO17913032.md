## Question

A patient for first time has experience a patellar dislocation episode. A surgical indication for the case would be

## Options

1. MRI shows complete rupture of MPFL
2. MFPL laxity is there on clinical examination
3. There is grade B trochlear dysplasia on CT
4. X ray shows an osteochondral fracture

## Correct option

**4. X ray shows an osteochondral fracture**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

It is estimated that 2%–3% of orthopedic presentations involving the knee joint will involve patellar dislocations. It is evident that there is a 17–49% risk of redislocation following first-time acute patellar dislocation. It is particularly higher in patients below 20 years of age. The risk further increases up to 70% following a second-time dislocation.

Etiology: Acute patellar dislocations typically occur as a result of trauma, usually a non-contact twisting injury to the knee, or from a direct blow to the medial aspect of the knee. A common mechanism is external tibial rotation with the foot fixed on the ground. Patellar dislocations can also occur in patients with generalized ligamentous laxity. However, those patients mostly tend to have recurrent subluxations of their patella as opposed to a frank dislocation.

Risk factors:

- Young patients (10-17 yrs old)

-Anatomic variations such as trochlear dysplasia, patella alta (Caton-Deschamps) index ≥ 1.2, patellar tilt ≥ 20°, and tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance ≥ 20 mm

- femoral anteversion, genu valgum, and external tibial torsion / pronated feet. The three bony malalignments combined are termed "Miserable Malalignment Syndrome" and lead to an increased Q angle.

- Generalised ligamentous laxity,

- Connective tissue disorders such as Marfan Syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos

-Muscular imbalance, particularly weakness of the vastus medialis oblique

Clinical evaluation should begin with a detailed history. This should include the patients age, skeletal maturity, sex, and activity level of the patient and any relevant family history. The clinician should determine if this was a first-time dislocation or a recurrent dislocation.

1. Patient standing alignment for any valgus or rotational deformities should be noted. Less than 11° is considered normal. Angles of 20° and greater are considered a risk factor and require correction.

Assess the Q-angle

The angle formed by a line from the ASIS to the center of the patella and from the center of the patella to the tibial tubercle

The Q-angle in full extension can be falsely normal because the patella is not engaged in the trochlea and not on tension. Therefore, it is recommended to assess the Q-angle in slight flexion, which is more reliable and accurate.

2. Beighton scale is assessed (to see generalised ligament laxity), thumb to forearm, MCP hyperextension, elbow hyperextension, knee recurvatum, and palms to floor and a score out of 9 possible points is documented. All tests are scored bilaterally with the exception of palms to floor.

3. “J sign” is assessed while the patient flexes and extends their knee. The J-sign refers to the inverted ‘J’ track the patella takes from extension to early flexion. This is done on both sides to compare the injured knee to the non-injured side.

4. In the seated position, crepitus is assessed for potential underlying cartilage injuries. The patient is then asked to lie supine, and with the knee in extension, ballottement of the patella is performed to assess for effusion. Knee range of motion, including hyperextension, is assessed in the supine position and compared to the other knee. In the same position, hip range of motion including internal and external rotation is assessed.

5. With the leg in full extension, the passive position of the patella is noted. In patients with passive patellar tilt, the clinician must assess whether or not the lateral structures are tight. This is done by maintaining the patella reduced in the trochlea and manually trying to evert the patella to neutral. If the clinician is unable to bring the patella parallel to the table then a tight lateral retinaculum is documented. An accurate assessment may not be possible as this maneuver may be too painful or uncomfortable for the patient, especially in those with a recent dislocation. For this reason, the ability to evert the patella to neutral and the tightness of the lateral retinaculum should also be performed prior to the start of each case as part of the exam under anesthesia in order to fully assess whether or not a lateral lengthening is a necessary part of the patellar stabilization surgery.

6. Patella apprehension test: The patella is translated both medially and laterally and quadrants of translation are assessed as well as the presence of any apprehension. Lateral apprehension is usually seen in lateral patellar instability. Medial apprehension can be seen after an over-aggressive lateral release, over-tensioned MPFL, or overcorrection of a tibial tubercle has been performed.

7.Moving patellar apprehension test: In some cases, patients with chronic instability or significant ligamentous laxity do not demonstrate apprehension in full extension. In these instances, the moving patellar apprehension test can be useful and is most sensitive with the knee ranged from 0 to 45 degrees of flexion while lateral stress is placed on the patella.

8. Patellar facet tenderness: It is done by translating the patella laterally to unroof the lateral facet and then the lateral facet can be directly palpated to elicit tenderness. This is done for the inferior pole by applying a downward posteriorly directed pressure to the superior pole of the patella thus bringing the inferior pole anterior where it can be more readily palpated. The medial facet is also palpated for tenderness by translating the patella medially. In cases of acute dislocation, the area along the medial facet is an expected area of tenderness and can help to locate the site of injury or tear of the MPFL.

9. Femoral anteversion: The patient is then asked to lie prone and hip range of motion can be assessed again in this position. Hip anteversion should also be checked in the prone position. This is done by flexing the knee to ninety degrees. The hip is then rotated internally and externally while palpating the greater trochanter. When the greater trochanter is brought parallel to the floor the position of the hip is assessed and documented. In addition, the thigh-foot axis is documented as this will help further guide the clinician to any rotational deformities that may be present.

Diagnostic imaging:

Radiographs: AP scannogram views are required for assessment of angular deformities (valgus/ varus) and lower limb alignment.

lateral views : trochlear dysplasia and patellar height are assessed on lateral views.

Signs of trochlear dysplasia:

Crossing sign: On conventional radiographs, the line which represents the deepest part of the trochlear groove never crosses the anterior border of the two condyles in a normal knee. This line will join the anterior part of the condyles in a dysplastic trochlea; this means that the groove is flat at this precise point.

Double contour sign: It represents convex trochlear groove/hypoplastic medial condyle. It is seen when anterior border of lateral condyle lies anterior to anterior border of medial condyle.

Supratrochlear spur: It arises in proximal aspect of trochlea. It indicates more severe trochlear dysplasia.

Evaluation for patellar height (patella alta vs. baja)

 Normal position of patella in lateral X ray- Blumensaat's line should extend to inferior pole of the patella at 30 degrees of knee flexion

Insall-Salvati ratio: It is the ratio of patellar tendon length(length of the posterior surface of the tendon from the lower pole of the patella to its insertion on the tibia) to patellar length. Normal value is between 0.8 and 1.2.

Blackburne-Peel method : A lateral radiograph of the knee with 30° of flexion is obtained, and a horizontal line at the level of the tibial plateau is drawn. Perpendicular to this line (i.e. vertically) two measurements are made:

A: along the patellar articular surface

B: distance between the horizontal line and the inferior aspect of the patellar articular surface

B/A is a Blackburne Peel ratio. Its normal value is between 0.5 and 1.0

![[TO17913032-2.png]]

Caton Deschamps method: The CDI is the distance from the anterior aspect of the tibial plateau to the inferior portion of the patella articular cartilage divided by the superior-inferior distance of the patellar articular cartilage.

normal between 0.6 and 1.3

![[TO17913032-3.png]]

 Lateral patellar tilt and lateral patellofemoral angle: Sunrise/Merchant views is best to assess for lateral patellar tilt. The lateral patellofemoral angle is formed by a line drawn along the lateral facet of the patella and a line connecting the anterior margins of the medial and lateral femoral condyles on an axial radiograph. This angle opens laterally in 97% of normal knees. With patellar subluxation, the lines lay parallel in 60%, and the angle opened medially in the remaining 40%

lateral patellofemoral angle (normal is >0°, an angle that opens laterally)

![[TO17913032-4.png]]

Sulcus angle: It is measured by lines connecting the anterior tips of the femoral condyles with the deepest point of the intercondylar sulcus, can be used to assess for trochlear dysplasia. The average sulcus angle in normal knees is 138° to 142°, with a greater angle suggesting dysplasia and risk factor for patellar instability.

![[TO17913032-5.png]]

 The congruence angle It is formed by a line bisecting the sulcus angle (line BA) and a line drawn from the median ridge of the patella to the deepest point of the intercondylar sulcus (line CA). The congruence angle measures an average of −6° (Medial angles are considered normal or minus angles) in normal subjects and is abnormal if it is greater than 16° (lateral angles are positive angles), indicating patellar subluxation.

![[TO17913032-6.png]]

TT –TG distance: CT scan is required to calculate TT-TG distance. It is measured as the distance between 2 perpendicular lines from the posterior cortex to the tibial tubercle and the trochlear groove. Values >20mm usually considered abnormal

TT-posterior cruciate ligament (TT-PCL): It is measured on the MRI as the mediolateral distance between the tibial tubercle midpoint and the medial border of the posterior cruciate ligament. The normal range of values for TT-PCL has been reported as 11.9 ± 4.67 mm, with a value more than 20 mm considered pathological.

TT-PCL distance negates the rotational influence of the femur in the measurement as it is solely measured from tibial reference points. The TT-TG is still more commonly utilized in assessing any gross axial malalignment and the measurements are highly specific for predicting recurrent patellar instability.

MRI is required to rule out/characterize suspected loose bodies, osteochondral lesion, bone bruising, tear of MPFL ligament and injury to other knee structures.

Trochlear dysplasia classification: On axial MRI, the trochlear depth can be determined to further assess the presence of a shallow trochlear floor as evidence of dysplasia. It has been reportedthat a trochlear depth <3 mm is highly sensitive and specific to this abnormal morphology. The mere flatness of the trochlea is not reason itself for a trochleoplasty, but rather the presence of a supratrochlear spur that can be removed as part of the deepening trochleoplasty.

The Dejour classification system is divided into Types A, B, C, and D dysplasia using specific radiographic parameters. In type A dysplasia, there is a crossing sign on the lateral radiographs and the trochlear groove is symmetric but shallower than normal, with a sulcus angle greater than 145° on axial images.

In type B, there is a crossing sign as well as a supratrochlear spur on lateral radiographs, with a flat trochlea on axial images.

In Type C, there is a crossing sign and double contour on lateral radiographs, with lateral facet convexity and medial facet hypoplasia on axial images.

In Type D, there is a crossing sign, supratrochlear spur, and double contour on lateral radiographs, and a “cliff” on axial images because of asymmetry of the lateral and medial femoral trochlear facets.

Management: Table below presents in brief a decision making algorithm for patients with Patellar instability: -

![[TO17913032-8.png]]

Non operative: it is the mainstay of treatment after first dislocation provided MRI does not show any intraarticular damage or loose body. It is also recommended in habitual dislocater. Patients are managed with ROM brace and physical therapy. After a short period (2 weeks) of immobilization in brace locked at 0 degree extension range of knee gradually increased in ROM brace. Quadriceps, core and hip strengthening exercises make the vital part of the rehabilitation.

Operative: There are a myriad of potential surgical procedures to aid in correcting the underlying pathology (Table given below). MPFL reconstruction, trochleoplasty and TTO are the more common procedures performed to address patellar instability.

In the setting of severe bony morphologic variances of genu valgum or femoral anteversion, distal femoral osteotomies, and derotational osteotomies can be indicated and effective procedures. Hemiepiphysiodesis has been successful at reducing patellar instability in the setting of severe genu valgum in the skeletally immature patient. When indicated in patients with >25° of femoral anteversion and recurrent patellar dislocations, combined supracondylar distal femoral derotation osteotomies and an MPFL reconstruction have produced encouraging results.

Medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction: Today, the main stay of proximal soft tissue stabilization procedures is the MPFL reconstruction, it dramatically reduces subjective instability and frank dislocations. To maximize outcome other bony abnormalities must be addressed simultaneously. The indications for an isolated MPFL reconstruction are a normal TTTG (20 mm or less); a normal or Dejour Type A trochlea, a CDI measurement of <1.2 indicating no patella alta, and a patellar tilt measurement of <20°.

Hamstring tendons, either gracilis or semitendinosus, are the most commonly used in a two-tailed configuration. In young patients with symptomatic patellar instability and wide-open physes, a medial quadriceps turndown as local autograft fixed to suture anchors can be used to avoid femoral tunnel.

Knee stiffness, patella fracture and continued patellar instability are reported complications. Critical to the success of the procedure is the proper positioning of the graft on the femur to restore the anatomy.

Trochleoplasty:

For patients with Type A and C trochlea variants, there is no gross supratrochlear spur, and so a deepening trochleoplasty is problematic and generally not indicated. Type B and D variants routinely can be considered indications for this sulcus deepening trochleoplasty given the presence of a spur projecting from the anterior femoral cortex.

Open physes or patellofemoral arthrosis are contraindications to performing the procedure. The most frequently reported complication associated with trochleoplasty procedures is arthrofibrosis and resultant stiffness, and trochlear shingle fracture line propagation, most commonly in the setting of dense/brittle subchondral bone associated with existing patellofemoral arthritis.

Tibial tubercle osteotomy: it is routinely performed in the setting of patellar instability when there are findings of an elevated TT-TG >20 mm, elevated TT-PCL, and patella alta with CDI > 1.2. While direct medial (Elmslie–Trillat) and direct anteriorization (Maquet) osteotomies can be utilized, a more common TTO is the anteromedialization* (AMZ) TTO, or Fulkerson osteotomy. Amount of medialization is calculated by subtracting 20 mm from TT-TG ratio of case. Basically, goal of TTO is to normalize the TT-TG to roughly 10 mm. CDI of >1.2 criterion is used to take decision about distalization of tibial tubercle. For distalization, target is to have a CDI of 1 (checked on C arm intra op). Mostly one would end up removing average 10 mm of bone on most occasions for that. Reported complications of the procedure include nonunion of the tibial tuberosity, painful hardware and tibial shaft fracture.
