## Question

He undergoes a debridement and intramedullary nailing the following day. ## Options

1. Continued pain without signs of radiographic union 1 year postoperatively
2. Purulent drainage from incision 3 months postoperatively
3. Pain out of proportion with tight thigh compartments 1 day after surgery
4. Broken proximal interlocking screw noted on radiographs 6 months from surgery
5. Varus malalignment of fracture 7 months following surgery

## Correct option

**3. Pain out of proportion with tight thigh compartments 1 day after surgery**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a gunshot wound (GSW) to the left femur and now has an open and comminuted left femoral shaft fracture. When comparing ballistic injuries and closed, blunt injuries involving the femur, ballistic injuries have a higher risk of compartment syndrome (answer choice 3).

Femoral shaft fractures are high-energy injuries that are associated with life-threatening conditions. These are common injuries occurring at a rate of 37.1 per 100,000 people each year. Injury to the femur can be classified by high-energy or low-energy mechanisms and even further subdivided by ballistic versus blunt injuries. Ballistic injuries typically involve GSWs and projectile objects that penetrate the human body. Blunt injuries include motor vehicle collision (MVC), falls, and injuries with non-penetrating impact. The femur is the most common long bone affected in ballistic injuries and can be complicated by nonunion, infection, compartment syndrome, and wound complications. Diagnosis is typically made with radiographs and CT. Treatment in the acute setting involves stabilizing the fracture and most commonly includes open reduction and internal fixation, intramedullary fixation, or external fixation. Notably, low-energy GSWs may be treated with local wound care, but high-energy ballistic injuries require formal debridement of devitalized tissue.

*Patch et al. evaluated 528 femoral shaft fractures (140 ballistic injuries vs 317 blunt closed injuries vs 71 open blunt injuries) and compared postoperative outcomes between each group.* **They reported** that ballistic injuries demonstrated a 3-fold increase in overall complications (30%) compared to blunt closed injuries (p<0.001). **They also found** that thigh compartment syndrome and soft tissue reconstruction had a significantly higher occurrence in ballistic injuries compared to both blunt injury groups (p<0.001). **They conclude** that clinicians should have a high index of suspicion for thigh compartment syndrome in ballistic femoral shaft fractures.

*Donahue et al. performed a retrospective study of 345 patients (148 GSWs vs 197 closed, blunt traumas) with femoral shaft fractures at a Level 1 trauma center.* *The average age was 33.5 years (80% male) and all patients were treated with intramedullary fixation.* **They found** zero nonunions in the GSW group compared to 8 (4.4%) nonunions in the closed, blunt trauma group (p=0.02). The rate of compartment syndrome was significantly higher in the GSW group (6.1% vs 0%, p<0.001) and arterial injury was significantly more common in the GSW group (9% vs 0%, p<0.001). **They conclude** that close clinical examination is important in patients with ballistic injuries since arterial injury and compartment syndrome are more common.

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of a left femur demonstrating a comminuted mid-shaft femur fracture secondary to a GSW. Figure C is an AP radiograph of a left femur demonstrating a closed, mid-shaft femur fracture secondary to blunt trauma.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Femoral shaft fractures secondary to closed, blunt trauma has been shown to have a higher nonunion rate than ballistic open fractures at 1-year follow-up.
Answer 2: Ballistic femoral shaft fractures demonstrate a similar rate of infection compared to closed, blunt femoral shaft fractures.
Answer 4: Rates of implant failure are similar between ballistic and blunt energy mechanisms in femoral shaft fractures at 1-year follow-up.
Answer 5: Rates of malunion are similar between ballistic and blunt energy mechanisms in femoral shaft fractures at 1-year follow-up.
