## Question

A 9-year-old female is evaluated in your clinic with complaints of multiple bony prominences in her legs that cause her pain. Her father, who accompanies her, states he had a similar complaint at her age. He says he required surgery for removal of these bony prominences when he was younger. He also required a hemiepiphysiodesis when he was a teenager to correct an angular deformity of his lower extremity. This patients radiograph is shown in Figure A. Given this patient's presentation and family history, you initially recommend molecular genetic testing. Which of the following genetic findings would most likely be seen?

![[q215526-a-l.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. GNAS mutation
2. IDH1 mutation
3. (11;22) chromosomal translocation
4. Retinoblastoma gene mutation
5. EXT1 mutation

## Correct option

**5. EXT1 mutation**

## Answer explanation

This clinical scenario describes a young female who has multiple osteochondromas, consistent with multiple hereditary exostosis (MHE), which is caused by a mutation in the EXT gene.

MHE is typically diagnosed early in life as multiple osteochondromas develop, more commonly in long bones at sites of tendon insertions. These bony tumors are almost always benign, but have a small rate of malignant transformation (5-10%). They can cause angular deformity or leg length discrepancy in affected patients. Most patients with MHE have a strong family history, as the disease is autosomal dominant with >95% penetrance, such that having a parent with MHE places all children at least 50% risk of inheritance. MHE is caused by mutations in the EXT gene, which affects chondrocytes at the growth plate.

*Wuyts et al published an overview on MHE including symptoms, diagnosis and management.* *They review the relationship with EXT gene mutations and discuss formal molecular genetic testing to aid in diagnosis.* *They also review the autosomal dominant nature of MHE and its high level of penetrance as it relates to genetic counseling.*

*Faiyaz-Ul-Haque et al review familial EXT1 mutations in consanguineous MHE patients and discuss specific genetic variations that may affect disease presentation and transmission.* **They note** that in two different families studied, males were more commonly affected. Additionally, **they noted** incomplete penetrance and variability with regards to the observed phenotype.

*Legeai-Mallet et al review the familial transmission of MHE of a cohort over 40 years and* **note** that most patients are diagnosed before age 12 and had a very low (0.5%) risk for malignancy. There was some evidence for incomplete penetrance of MHE, although this was low. They also suspect that penetrance may be higher for males than females.

Figure A is an AP radiograph demonstrating MHE with multiple osteochondromas present in the lower extremities.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: GNAS mutation is seen in fibrous dysplasia
Answer 2: IDH1 mutation is associated with Ollier's disease and Maffucci's syndromes which involve enchondromas
Answer 3: (11;22) translocation is seen in Ewing's syndrome
Answer 4: Retinoblastoma tumor suppressor gene mutation can be seen in osteosarcoma, however, it is not associated with osteochondromas or multiple hereditary exostoses
