## Question

Figures A and B demonstrate two different fracture patterns. What is the percent change in strain at fracture site Y relative to fracture site X when the fractures are unloaded?

*Figure A*

![[q215534-b-screen-shot-2021-08-19-at-2-53-00-pm.jpg]]
*Figure B*

## Options

1. 50% increase
2. 80% decrease
3. 100% increase
4. 20% decrease
5. No change

## Correct option

**2. 80% decrease**

## Answer explanation

The overall displacement during unloading is the same (5mm) between Figures A and B. The full amount of displacement of the end fragments in Figure A is active in within the single fracture gap, which increases from 5mm to 10mm, or 100%. In figure B, the total displacement is shared equally among the fracture gaps, so each fracture gap displaces from 5mm to 6mm, which is a 20% increase. Given that strain within a fracture is mathematically calculated as the change in length (delta L) divided by original length (L), there is an 80% decrease in strain at fracture site Y compared to X.

During operative fixation of fractures, strain is an important concept to consider when determining the amount of inherent stability a fixation construct renders. Bones have an optimal setting in order to heal, which can either be through direct (primary) or indirect (secondary) healing. Direct healing occurs with absolute stability of fixation and compression across the fracture site. Haversian osteons are able to cross the plane of contact of fracture without obvious change in shape or direction, thus no callous formation occurs. With a residual fracture gap and absolute stability (i.e. no relative motion), healing across the fracture is far less likely. Relative stability confers micromotion about a fracture site, stimulating a sequential sequence of tissue differentiation, resorption of the fracture and uniting of the fracture fragments by callus. Too much strain, however, and the induction of resorption prevails, leading to non-union. When relative stability and thus indirect bone healing is the goal of a fixation construct, whether that be a splint, external/internal fixator, or flexible internal fixation (e.g. intramedullary rod), it is important to consider the degree of strain that may occur across the fracture site. As demonstrated in the figures above, multiple fracture lines share the displacement and are thus more tolerant to instability while simple fractures must bear the full shift.

Figure A demonstrates a simple fracture whereas Figure B demonstrates multiple fracture fragments.

Perren provides a review of the evolution of internal fixation of long bone fractures. He highlights the strain theory, which was developed according to the bone healing that takes place under certain conditions. According to the strain theory, the amount of mobility allowed depends less on the displacement of the fragments alone than on the relation of the width of the fracture gap (L) and displacement (δL); ε = δL/L. The amount of strain (ε i.e. relative deformation) optimally ranges between the minimum required for the induction of callus and the maximum which allows bony bridging.

The fracture gap in Figure A increases by 100%, whereas in figure B the total displacement is shared equally among the fracture gaps, so there is only a 20% increase in strain at fracture site Y (5mm to 6mm).
