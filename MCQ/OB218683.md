## Question

![[q218683-b-jonesfx-planus.jpg]]

## Options

1. Lower risk of nonunion
2. Higher risk of refracture
3. Lower risk of refracture
4. Earlier return to sport
5. Delayed return to sport

## Correct option

**2. Higher risk of refracture**

## Answer explanation

Both patients sustained Jones fractures of the fifth metatarsal base. The patient in Figure A has a cavus foot and is therefore at a higher risk of refracture and nonunion, even after successful treatment (Answer 2).

Fractures of the fifth metatarsal base are one of the most common injuries seen in running athletes. These are divided into Zone 1, Zone 2, and Zone 3 injuries. Zone 1 injuries occur at the tip of the fifth metatarsal base, and are usually managed nonoperatively. Zone 3 injuries occur in the diaphyseal portion of the fifth metatarsal shaft, and are usually managed without surgery except in refractory cases or nonunion. Zone 2 injuries occur at the meta-diaphyseal junction and are known as Jones fractures. These have 25% risk of non-union and are commonly fixed in high-demand patients such as athletes because of a higher rate of union and lower risk of refracture. However, patients with cavus feet are at higher risk of nonunion or refracture after successful treatment.

*Ruta and Parker published in 2020 review on the management of Jones fractures in athletes.* From a biomechanical standpoint, **the authors found** that hindfoot varus, forefoot adduction, cavus foot, and genu varum contribute to increased load to the foot’s lateral column. Those at the greatest risk for a Jones fracture are typically individuals with long, straight, narrow fifth metatarsals and an adducted forefoot.

*Chloros et al. published a 2020 update on management, complications, and outcomes of fifth metatarsal fractures.* **The authors state** that refractures or nonunions occur in up to 12% of intramedullary screw fixation cases; however, the refracture rate in elite athletes may be as high as 30% in those treated with intramedullary screws and 10.5% in those treated with plate fixation. Failure to simultaneously address causative factors such as pes cavus has been documented as a possible cause of fracture or fixation failure.

*Metzl et al. published a 2022 JAAOS review on the diagnosis and management of Jones fractures.* As previous studies have demonstrated, hindfoot varus, pes cavus, and metatarsus adductus are risk factors for Jones fractures. In cases of more severe deformity, a nonunion, or revision surgery, surgical correction of the associated foot deformity should be considered. In patients with forefoot-driven hindfoot varus, adding a dorsiflexion osteotomy of the first metatarsal can help restore the alignment and balance of the foot. In patients with heel varus, a calcaneal osteotomy is needed.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of a cavus foot with a Jones fracture. Figure B is a lateral radiograph of a neutral foot with a Jones fracture.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: A cavus foot has not been associated with a decreased risk of nonunion.
Answer 3: A cavus foot is associated with a higher risk of refracture.
Answers 4 and 5: A cavus foot is not associated with an earlier or delayed return to sport.
