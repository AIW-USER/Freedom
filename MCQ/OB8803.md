## Question

A 35-year-old female undergoes an arthroscopic shoulder procedure. Postoperatively, full active shoulder range of motion is encouraged but she is instructed to avoid active forearm supination with the elbow at 90° of flexion. Following this procedure, which long-term alteration in upper extremity function is expected?

## Options

1. Decreased elbow flexion strength
2. Decreased forearm supination strength
3. Decreased shoulder abduction strength
4. Decreased shoulder forward elevation strength
5. No change in shoulder or elbow strength

## Correct option

**5. No change in shoulder or elbow strength**

## Answer explanation

The patient underwent a long head of the biceps (LHB) tenodesis, which does not affect shoulder strength, nor compromise elbow or forearm strength long-term. In the early post-operative period, active forearm supination with the elbow at 90° of flexion and resisted elbow flexion are avoided until the tenodesis has healed.

The LHB originates on the supraglenoid tubercle and superior labrum and inserts distally with the short head of the biceps onto the radial tuberosity. It functions to flex the elbow and supinate the forearm. Proximal tendonitis of the LHB is a common cause of anterior shoulder pain. When recalcitrant to conservative measures, surgical options include tenotomy or tenodesis of the LHB. While both relieve pain and preserve elbow/forearm strength, tenotomy may result in a cosmetic (“popeye”) deformity and should be discussed preoperatively with patients.

*Levy et al. examined 10 shoulders with dynamic electromyography (EMG) to delineate the function of the LHB at the shoulder with the elbow and forearm position fixed in a long arm brace.* No electrical activity was identified in the LHB with isolated shoulder range of motion (ROM) in all planes. **The authors concluded** that any bicipital function at the shoulder must either be a passive role of the tendon or tension associated with elbow and forearm activity.

*Shank et al. compared elbow flexion and forearm supination strength following LHB tenotomy or tenodesis.* No statistically significant difference was found in either elbow flexion or forearm supination strength when LHB tenotomy, LHB tenodesis and a nonoperative asymptomatic control group were compared, both dominant and nondominant limbs. **The authors concluded** that one may select either LHB tenotomy or tenodesis for treatment of LHB pathology.

*Yamaguchi et al. performed an EMG analysis of 44 shoulders with and without rotator cuff tears (RCTs) to determine LHB activity, with the elbow and forearm position fixed.* Some reports have suggested that the LHB functions as a humeral head depressor and that this activity may increase in the presence of a RCT. However, no significant LHB activity was observed in all shoulders, with and without RCT. **The authors concluded** that any function of the LHB at the shoulder does not involve active contractions.

Incorrect Answers:
*Answers 1 + 2: As reviewed above (Shank et al.), there is no significant loss of elbow flexion strength or forearm supination strength following LHB tenodesis.*
Answers 3 + 4: The LHB does not contribute to shoulder ROM or strength; therefore, shoulder strength would not be affected by LHB tenodesis.
