## Question

A 13-year-old girl presents with 6 months of activity-related distal thigh pain without systemic symptoms. Radiographs of the symptomatic region are demonstrated in figure A. The lesion measures 4.0 cm craniocaudally and occupies just over half of the transverse bone diameter. Despite rest, activity modification, and physical therapy, her pain persists and limits sports participation. What is the most appropriate management?

![[q220925-a-standard-compressed-ikke-ossifiserende-knokkelfibr.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Observation with repeat radiographs in 6 months
2. Long-leg casting and protected weight-bearing
3. Curettage and bone grafting
4. Sclerotherapy
5. Wide excision with reconstruction

## Correct option

**3. Curettage and bone grafting**

## Answer explanation

Although the vast majority of non-ossifying fibromas (NOF), particularly those that are smaller and asymptomatic, are managed with observation alone, curettage and bone grafting is indicated for a persistently symptomatic, large non-ossifying fibroma in a weight-bearing bone with cortical thinning and lesion size suggestive of increased fracture risk (Answer 3).

NOF is a common benign, cortically based, eccentric metaphyseal lesion in children/adolescents, often discovered incidentally and most frequently in the distal femur, followed by the proximal and then distal tibia. Diagnosis is usually made via plain radiographs, obviating the need for advanced imaging or biopsy for lesions without atypical characteristics; lesions commonly have a narrow transition with a sclerotic rim and may display cortical thinning, enlarging with growth and gradually reossifying near maturity. Standard care is observation with interval radiographs; surgery, typically in the form of curettage and bone grafting, is considered for symptomatic lesions, large lesions with extensive cortical involvement, or those with high fracture risk.

*Baghdadi et al. retrospectively reviewed a series of 84 pediatric patients with distal tibia NOFs, of which 25 presented with fracture and 59 without fracture.* **They found** that larger size, Ritschl stage B, and cortical thinning were significant predictors of fracture. **They concluded** that size and cortical features should guide intervention, presented a scoring system and management algorithm based on radiographs, and advocated for reserving advanced imaging for equivocal cases.

*Betsy et al. reviewed metaphyseal fibrous defects/NOFs.* **They noted** that large, symptomatic lesions should be treated with biopsy, curettage, and grafting; they cited commonly used fracture-risk thresholds (>50% bone diameter; >33 mm in weight-bearing bones), while also noting limitations of absolute size criteria. **They concluded** monitoring is aimed at avoiding pathologic fracture, with most pathologic fractures nondisplaced and initially casted, then curetted/grafted after healing.

Wootton-Gorges provided a broad review of MRI of primary bone tumors and tumor-like conditions in children. In regard to NOF/fibrous cortical defect imaging, they described these lesions are eccentric, cortically based with marginal sclerosis; fibrous cortical defects are <2 cm and NOFs are larger. On MRI, NOFs are low signal on T1, whereas T2 signal varies by the degree of hypercellular fibrous tissue, higher during development and lower with healing. Gadolinium contrast produces a thin, enhancing rim.

Figure A demonstrates an AP radiograph of the distal femur, with an eccentric, metaphyseal lesion with a "bubbly" appearance and local cortical thinning with appreciable periosteal reaction.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Observation is appropriate for typical, asymptomatic or smaller NOFs; this patient has persistent pain, large size (>~50% diameter), and cortical thinning—features that meet operative indications.
Answer choice 2: Pathologic fractures through NOFs may be treated with casting; casting alone does not address a persistently painful, high-risk lesion without fracture.
Answer choice 4: Sclerotherapy is not a standard treatment modality for NOF; symptomatic large lesions are managed with curettage and grafting.
Answer choice 5: Wide excision is excessive for a benign NOF, which is managed intralesionally with curettage when surgery is indicated.
