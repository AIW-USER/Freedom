## Question

A 35-year-old man presents with a 14-month history of progressive right hip pain with catching, locking, and decreased internal rotation. Imaging demonstrates multiple intra-articular calcified loose bodies, and he undergoes arthroscopic loose body removal with partial synovectomy. His radiograph is shown in Figure A. He initially improves but develops recurrent mechanical symptoms 3 years later. Which of the following statements regarding recurrence in this patient is most accurate?

## Options

1. The arthroscopic approach has a significantly higher recurrence rate compared with open synovectomy and should have been avoided
2. Recurrence is a recognized outcome after surgical treatment and is associated with incomplete removal of diseased synovium
3. Recurrence after 3 years is pathognomonic for malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma and requires wide resection
4. The presence of ring-and-arc calcification indicates a higher likelihood of recurrence compared with non-calcified disease
5. Hip synovial chondromatosis has recurrence rates exceeding 50%, making it the joint with the highest recurrence risk

## Correct option

**2. Recurrence is a recognized outcome after surgical treatment and is associated with incomplete removal of diseased synovium**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with hip synovial chondromatosis and undergoes arthroscopic partial synovectomy, with the development of recurrent symptoms 3 years later. Recurrence rates following arthroscopic surgery range from 7.1% to 25%, with incomplete synovial removal being a key risk factor (Answer 2).

Synovial chondromatosis is a benign disorder characterized by metaplastic transformation of synovial tissue into cartilaginous nodules that may detach, mineralize, and form intra-articular loose bodies. The knee and hip are the most commonly affected joints, with patients typically presenting in the third to fifth decades of life with pain, mechanical symptoms, and restricted range of motion. Characteristic radiographic findings include multiple intra-articular calcifications with “ring-and-arc” chondroid mineralization, while extrinsic bone erosion may be present without implying malignancy. Standard treatment consists of surgical loose body removal with synovectomy. Recurrence is well described, and malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma is rare.

*Neumann et al. performed a topical review and described synovial chondromatosis as a condition of unknown etiology characterized by synovial metaplasia that produces cartilaginous nodules, which subsequently detach, mineralize, and may ossify.* The knee is the most commonly affected joint, and patients may present asymptomatically or with pain, swelling, and limited range of motion. Plain radiographs showing mineralized nodules are pathognomonic when present. Treatment involves arthroscopic or open removal of loose bodies with or without synovectomy to prevent articular destruction and relieve symptoms.

*Murphey et al. detail characteristic imaging features, most commonly multiple intra-articular calcifications with “ring-and-arc” chondroid mineralization on radiographs.* MRI typically shows low-to-intermediate T1 signal and very high T2 signal with hypointense calcifications, while CT best delineates calcified loose bodies and associated extrinsic bone erosion. Primary disease is distinguished from secondary synovial chondromatosis by uniform loose body size and absence of underlying degenerative joint disease, whereas secondary disease demonstrates concentric growth rings.

*Blitzer and Scarano reported a case demonstrating successful arthroscopic management of hip synovial osteochondromatosis in a 61-year-old woman, with rapid recovery and minimal morbidity.* Nearly all loose bodies were removed arthroscopically, illustrating that hip arthroscopy can function as both a diagnostic and therapeutic modality for this condition. Their report supports arthroscopy as a minimally invasive alternative to open arthrotomy with synovectomy, with the potential advantages of faster rehabilitation and reduced surgical morbidity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While arthroscopic approaches may have technical limitations in accessing the peripheral compartment, the reported recurrence rate after hip arthroscopy is approximately 7.1%, not exceeding 30%
Answer 3: Recurrence alone does not indicate malignant transformation; malignancy is suggested by multiple recurrences and marrow invasion, occurring in only approximately 5% of cases.
Answer 4: Ring-and-arc calcification is pathognomonic for synovial chondromatosis but does not predict recurrence risk, which is instead associated with incomplete synovial removal.
Answer 5: There is no established evidence that the hip has higher recurrence rates compared to other joints; overall recurrence rates for synovial chondromatosis range from 3% to 23% regardless of joint location.
