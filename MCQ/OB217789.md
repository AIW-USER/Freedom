## Question

A 20-year-old collegiate javelin thrower reports medial-sided elbow pain with intermittent paresthesia of the little and ring finger and grip strength weakness. On examination, there is decreased sensation in the ulnar nerve distribution of the hand. While palpating the medial elbow through elbow range of motion, two "snaps" are appreciated. He has tried 6 weeks of nighttime splinting with only mild improvement in his symptoms. Which of the following interventions would be indicated at this time?

## Options

1. Continued nighttime splinting for at least another 6 weeks
2. Begin daytime splinting for at least 3 weeks
3. Cortisone injection
4. In situ ulnar nerve release
5. Ulnar nerve release with anterior transposition

## Correct option

**5. Ulnar nerve release with anterior transposition**

## Answer explanation

This patient has cubital tunnel syndrome with a dislocating ulnar nerve (two "snaps" are the ulnar nerve and the medial triceps). In the throwing athlete, this problem is best addressed with cubital tunnel release with anterior transposition.

Cubital tunnel syndrome is a compressive neuropathy of the ulnar nerve caused by anatomic compression in the medial elbow. Diagnosis is made clinically with the presence of sensory changes to the ring and little fingers, intrinsic muscle weakness, and a positive Tinel's sign over the cubital tunnel. Treatment may be nonoperative modalities such as bracing or surgical decompression depending on the severity and duration of symptoms, and the success of nonoperative treatment. Ulnar nerve decompression and anterior transposition is indicated in those patients that have failed an in situ release, are a throwing athlete, or in the patient with poor ulnar nerve bed from a tumor, osteophyte, or heterotopic bone.

*Posner et al. reviewed compressive neuropathy of the ulnar nerve in the upper limb.* **They reported** that initial treatment for acute and subacute neuropathy at the elbow is nonsurgical but for chronic neuropathy associated with muscle weakness, or neuropathy that does not respond to conservative measures, surgery is usually necessary. **They concluded** that ulnar nerve transpositions are often successful with low complication rates, and are preferred in certain situations.

*Henn et al. reviewed the outcomes following surgical management of unstable and stable ulnar nerves at the elbow in young patients.* **They reported** that in 45% of these patients, the ulnar nerve was either subluxated or perched on the medial epicondyle with elbow flexion and made up the "unstable" cohort. **They noted** that at an average of 5.6 years following surgery, the unstable cohort had a significantly lower QuickDASH score (6.4 vs 18.6) and a significantly higher VAS for treatment satisfaction (8.7 vs 5.9) compared with the stable cohort. The unstable cohort was also significantly less likely to experience residual symptoms (43% vs 94%), persistent numbness (39% vs 44%), or persistent tingling (22% vs 56%) compared with the stable cohort. **They concluded** that surgical management of young patients with symptomatic, unstable ulnar nerves results in superior subjective outcomes compared with surgery in young patients with stable ulnar nerves.

*Zimmerman et al. retrospectively reviewed 142 patients treated with cubital tunnel release and anterior submuscular transposition.* **They reported** that submuscular transposition is a safe and durable option for primary ulnar neuropathy at the elbow. **They concluded** that good or excellent results are achieved in the vast majority of patients and there is a low complication rate.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Nighttime splinting for 3 weeks is adequate time to assess for improvement in symptoms. Additional time is unlikely to improve the symptoms in the throwing athlete
Answer 2: Daytime splinting is not indicated in cubital tunnel syndrome
Answer 3: Cortisone injections are typically not utilized in cubital tunnel syndrome due to risk to the nerve and the effectiveness of surgical release +/- transposition after failed nonoperative management
Answer 4: In the throwing athlete with an unstable ulnar nerve, an anterior transposition should be performed as the unstable nature of the nerve may persist and result in continued symptoms following in situ release
