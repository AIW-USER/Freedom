## Question

You are consulted on a 64-year-old male who has sustained an unstable bimalleolar ankle fracture. He has a past medical history of poorly controlled type II diabetes (A1c 9.8) with profound neuropathy. He is uncertain when the injury originally occurred, but he has soft-tissue ulcerations overlying the medial and lateral aspects of his ankle. After discussion with the patient, you decide to treat the patient's injury with a tibiotalocalcaneal fusion with a hindfoot nail. Which of the following is the optimal fusion position of the patient's foot and ankle?

## Options

1. 5-10° of dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation
2. 5-10° of plantarflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation
3. Neutral dorsiflexion, 10-20° of external rotation, 10° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of anterior talar translation
4. Neutral dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation
5. Neutral dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, neutral hindfoot valgus/varus and 0-5mm of posterior tibial translation

## Correct option

**4. Neutral dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation**

## Answer explanation

The optimal foot and ankle fusion position when performing a hindfoot and ankle fusion is neutral dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation (Answer 4).

The optimal treatment of ankle fractures in diabetics is hotly debated, and a complicated commonly encountered clinical scenario. The underlying microvascular damage imparted by poorly controlled diabetes impairs wound and fracture healing. Furthermore, neuropathy often results in a loss of protective sensation. Finally, these patients often have poor bone quality when compared to non-diabetic age- and gender-matched patients. As a result of these pathophysiologic changes, diabetic patients are at a much higher risk of a number of complications following both nonoperative and operative management including delayed union and prolonged healing, hardware failure, infection, lower functional outcomes, and the need for future amputation.

A variety of treatment options exist for these patients, with no "gold standard" treatment currently identified. That said, the implant construct must offer enhanced fixation and careful attention must be paid to preserving the often tenuous soft-tissue envelope. These patients always require a prolonged period of non-weight bearing following surgery, if successful union is to be achieved. Recently, there has been a paradigm shift towards the utilization of hindfoot fusion nails in the acute and sub-acute treatment of unstable ankle fractures in diabetic patients. Proponents of this technique highlight the limited open nature of the surgical procedure coupled with high degrees of stability as benefits over traditional open reduction and internal fixation or hybrid constructs. When performing an arthrodesis, careful attention must be paid to the position of the foot and ankle, as an improper fusion position can place increased stress on the knee while limiting the ability of the foot to naturally compensate for the loss of tibiotalar and subtalar motion.

*Nash et al. performed a systematic review investigating the current concepts and challenges in managing ankle fractures in the presence of diabetes.* *Their study ultimately included data from 40 studies.* **The authors note** that the presence of diabetes, especially poorly controlled diabetes, presents an increased risk of complications. Concerningly, these patients also do poorly with non-operative management. **The authors note** that external fixation + definitive fixation should be avoided as this technique is associated with high complication rates and that all attempts should be made to minimize insult to the surrounding soft tissue while still ensuring high levels of implant construct stability.

*Ebaugh et al. investigated the outcomes of primary tibiotalocalcaneal nailing for complicated diabetic ankle fractures.* *Their study included 27 patients with poorly controlled diabetes who underwent tibiotalocalcaneal nailing as their primary treatment.* *The mean follow-up was 888 days and the average patient age was 66 with an average A1c of 7.4.* The overall limb salvage rate was 96%, and 88% of patients achieved successful union. The surgical complication rate was 18.5%, with no instances of malunion, symptomatic nonunion or Charcot arthropathy. Promisingly, 81% of patients were able to maintain independent ambulation.

*Vaudreuil et al. investigated limb salvage options after failed initial operative management of bimalleolar ankle fractures in patients with diabetic neuropathy.* *Their study included 17 patients with a median follow-up time of 20 months.* *Overall, 14/17 patients achieved successful limb salvage, all 14 of which eventually required a fused ankle joint.* **The authors conclude** that even when non-fusion strategies are undertaken, many patients will eventually end up with a clinically fused ankle joint. Furthermore, primary fusion procedures were associated with a high rate of limb salvage and a decreased number of operations.

Incorrect Answers
Answers 1,2,3,5: The optimal ankle and hindfoot alignment when performing a tibiotalocalcaneal fusion is neutral dorsiflexion, 5-10° of external rotation, 5° of hindfoot valgus, and 0-5mm of posterior talar translation.
