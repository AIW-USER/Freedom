## Question

A healthy 55-year-old female is involved in a motor vehicle collision and sustains the injury shown in Figure A. She is evaluated in the emergency room and placed into a coaptation splint, then transitioned to a Sarmiento brace at her 1-week outpatient office appointment. She misses her six-week appointment and presents 12 weeks post-injury complaining of persistent pain. Persistent motion at the fracture site is noted on her physical exam. Her most recent radiographs are shown in Figure B. Which of the following is most predictive of progression to nonunion?

## Options

1. Persistent radial nerve palsy at 8 weeks
2. Absence of radiographic callus at 3 weeks
3. Persistent fracture mobility at 6 weeks
4. Non-dominant extremity
5. Dominant extremity

## Correct option

**3. Persistent fracture mobility at 6 weeks**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a midshaft humerus fracture and has a high risk of progressing to a nonunion given the absence of callus on radiographs and mobility at the fracture site at her 12-week follow-up despite a trial of nonoperative management in a functional brace. Of the choices listed, the most predictive factor of progression to eventual nonunion is persistent fracture site mobility at 6 weeks (Answer 3).

Humeral shaft fractures are common, comprising 3-5% of all extremity fractures. Most isolated fractures are amenable to nonoperative management, usually in the form of functional bracing such as the Sarmiento brace. However, primary nonunion occurs in 2-33% of cases managed nonoperatively, and 5-10% of cases managed operatively. There are several risk factors for nonunion, including metabolic abnormalities (Vitamin D deficiency is the most common), infection, smoking, obesity, unstable fracture patterns, shoulder or elbow stiffness, and open fractures. Persistent motion at the fracture site 6 weeks post-injury is also an extremely sensitive and specific predictor of impending nonunion. The gold standard for a nonunited humeral shaft fracture is surgical intervention in the form of an open reduction internal fixation using compression plating with or without bone graft.

*Toivanen et al. published a 2005 retrospective case series of 93 patients with humeral shaft fractures undergoing functional brace treatment.* *The authors evaluated each patient 6 weeks following injury - if there were no radiological or clinical signs of consolidation, surgery was performed.* If the fracture stabilized and bony callus was visible, bracing was continued. The patient was followed until fracture union. 72 of the 93 patients (77%) healed without significant malalignment, whereas 21 patients (23%) failed bracing and required surgical fixation. Of note, there were more nonunions in patients with proximal 1/3 shaft fractures compared to those with middle or distal 1/3 shaft fractures.

*Driesman et al. published a 2017 retrospective cohort study assessing if the presence of fracture site mobility at various time points predicted humeral shaft fracture progression to radiographic nonunion in patients managed nonoperatively using a functional brace.* Clinical examination for fracture stability was routinely performed on patients by the treating physicians and documented. Patients were followed until union or underwent surgery for persistent fracture mobility. 87% of patients healed their fracture within our study cohort by 6 months. **The authors found** that if the humeral shaft fracture site was mobile at the 6-week follow-up visit, it identified future fracture nonunion with 82% sensitivity and 99% specificity (only 1 patient with motion at 6 weeks proceeded to fracture union). When patients had both an absence of callus formation and motion at 6 weeks (n = 7), all proceeded to nonunion (sensitivity 63.6%, specificity 100%, PPV 100%).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, 4, 5: concomitant radial nerve palsy, absence of callus at 3 weeks, and hand dominance have not been associated with an increased risk of nonunion.
