## Options

1. Methylprednisolone bolus 30mg/kg over 15 minutes with maintenance infusion of 5.4 mg/kg per hour infused for 23 hours
2. Delaying surgical decompression at least 5 days to avoid a "second hit" by operating in the acute inflammatory window
3. Administration of methylprednisolone and urgent (< 24 hours) decompression
4. Urgent surgical decompression (< 24 hours)
5. Observation in a hard collar and early mobilization

## Correct option

**4. Urgent surgical decompression (< 24 hours)**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with a C6 burst fracture with neurological deficits. Early decompression (< 24 hours) following traumatic spinal cord injury has been shown to improve neurologic outcomes compared with delayed (>/ 24 hours) management.

Following a traumatic cervical spinal cord injury, the surgeon's role is to decompress the spinal cord and provide stabilization to allow for early transfers and mobilization. Multiple studies have demonstrated that early (< 24 hours from the time of injury) decompression is safe and is associated with the greatest chance for neurologic recovery. Delaying surgery in these patients is associated with increased pulmonary complications, decubitus ulcers, and DVT. Prior studies advocated for the use of corticosteroids to minimize secondary injury in these patients. However, this has largely been abandoned due to the potential risks of steroids and the lack of compelling evidence demonstrating improved neurologic outcomes to justify their use.

*Fehlings et al. conducted a multicenter, prospective, cohort study (STASCIS) in adults with traumatic cervical cord injuries.* *They specifically sought to determine if the timing of decompression (< 24 or >/ 24 hours after injury) improves neurologic outcomes based on the ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS).* **They concluded** that decompression within 24 hours following injury was safe and resulted in greater neurologic outcomes compared to delaying surgery.

*El Tecle et al. also evaluated the timing of decompression following cervical cord injuries.* *They performed a systematic literature review utilizing 14 pre-clinical and 30 clinical studies meeting their inclusion criteria.* **They concluded** that evidence favors early decompression and that there is significant variability in defining early versus late decompression in the literature.

*Evaniew et. al. performed a propensity score-matched cohort study with data from a multi-center registry evaluating the NASCIS-II regimen of MPSS use for treatment of acute spinal cord injury.* Importantly, in their analysis they controlled for anatomic level and severity of injury. **They found** that MPSS did not improve motor recovery in this cohort, and that those who did receive an infusion had higher rate of total complications including urinary tract infections, decubitus ulcers, and pneumonias. The authors were in agreement with guidelines against routine administration of MPSS in acute SCI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3: The use of steroids in spinal cord injuries are controversial due to their potential side-effects and lack of clear evidence supporting neurological improvement; thus steroids are not advised. The NASCIS trials were 3 multicenter, prospective, randomized, double-blinded trials that found no statistically significant differences in neurologic outcomes between methylprednisolone (low- and high-dose) and placebo following spinal cord injury.
Answer 2: Early decompression (< 24 hours) has been proven to be safe in prospective cohort studies.
Answer 5: This injury demands early (< 24 hours) surgical decompression and stabilization to give the patient the best chance for neurologic recovery and avoiding complications associated with prolonged immobility.
