## Question

When formulating a Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) copolymer for an orthopedic fixation device, how does adjusting the monomer ratio affect the material's degradation profile?

## Options

1. Increasing the proportion of the PGA monomer consistently increases both the overall dissolving time and the long-term mechanical strength.
2. A 50:50 ratio of PLA to PGA results in the fastest degradation time compared to either homopolymer or other copolymer ratios.
3. A higher concentration of PLA accelerates hydrolysis, causing the implant to dissolve completely within 2 to 3 weeks.
4. The degradation rate of PLGA is solely determined by the implant's size and is completely independent of the PLA to PGA ratio.

## Correct option

**2. A 50:50 ratio of PLA to PGA results in the fastest degradation time compared to either homopolymer or other copolymer ratios.**

## Answer explanation

Combining these two monomers in equal parts disrupts the structural regularity, creating a highly amorphous material that allows water to penetrate easily, leading to the fastest rate of hydrolysis.

Comprehensive Review of Biodegradable Orthopedic Implant Materials

Biodegradable (or bioresorbable) materials have revolutionized orthopedics by eliminating the need for secondary surgical procedures to remove hardware (like plates, screws, and pins) once a fracture has healed. An ideal biodegradable implant provides sufficient mechanical strength to support the healing bone and then gradually degrades at a rate matching the formation of new bone, transferring the load back to the natural skeleton to prevent "stress shielding."

These materials generally fall into three categories: Polymers, Metals, and Ceramics/Composites.

1. Synthetic Biodegradable Polymers

Polymers are the most widely used biodegradable materials in orthopedics, primarily for low-load bearing applications (e.g., suture anchors, interference screws, meniscal darts). They degrade mainly through hydrolysis (reaction with water).

Polyglycolic Acid (PGA)

 - Properties: Highly crystalline, rigid, and hydrophilic (absorbs water easily).

 - Mechanical Strength: Very high initial strength (Tensile modulus ~7 GPa). It is the strongest of the common biodegradable polymers initially.

 - Degradation Time: * Strength Loss: Rapid. Loses about 50% of its mechanical strength within 2 to 4 weeks.

 - Mass Loss (Complete Resorption): 6 to 12 months.

 - Clinical Use: Best for applications requiring high initial strength but where long-term support is not needed. Because of its rapid degradation, it can sometimes cause a localized inflammatory response.

Polylactic Acid (PLA) / Poly-L-lactic Acid (PLLA)

 - Properties: Exists in several forms, but PLLA is the most common for orthopedics. It contains a hydrophobic (water-repelling) methyl group, making it highly resistant to water penetration. It is semi-crystalline.

 - Mechanical Strength: High tensile strength, though slightly lower initial stiffness than PGA (Modulus ~2.7 - 4 GPa).

 - Degradation Time: * Strength Loss: Slow. Maintains functional strength for 3 to 6 months.

 - Mass Loss: Very slow, taking 2 to 5 years (and sometimes longer) to completely resorb.

 - Clinical Use: Preferred for fractures that take longer to heal (e.g., ligament reconstruction screws, fracture plates in pediatric patients).

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)

 - Properties: A copolymer made by combining PLA and PGA. Its properties are highly tunable based on the monomer ratio.

 - Mechanical Strength: Moderate, depending on the ratio.

 - Degradation Time: A 50:50 ratio of PLA to PGA creates a highly amorphous structure that degrades faster than either pure PGA or pure PLLA (complete mass loss in 1 to 2 months). Increasing the PLA ratio slows degradation.

 - Clinical Use: Drug delivery systems within orthopedics, coatings, and specific fixation devices where a targeted degradation window between PGA and PLLA is desired.

Polycaprolactone (PCL)

 - Properties: Semi-crystalline, highly rubbery, and exceptionally tough but flexible.

 - Mechanical Strength: Very low tensile strength and stiffness compared to PGA/PLA (Modulus ~0.4 GPa). Not suitable alone for load-bearing.

 - Degradation Time: Very slow due to high hydrophobicity. Complete resorption takes 2 to 4 years.

 - Clinical Use: Rarely used alone for fixation. It is highly valued in tissue engineering scaffolds, 3D printing of bone grafts, or blended with ceramics to increase their toughness.

2. Biodegradable Metals

While polymers are great for soft tissue fixation and pediatric fractures, they lack the mechanical strength needed for major load-bearing fractures (like a femur or tibia fracture). Biodegradable metals bridge this gap.

Magnesium (Mg) and its Alloys

 - Properties: Mg is biologically essential. Its most remarkable feature is its elastic modulus (~45 GPa), which is incredibly close to human cortical bone (~15-25 GPa). This near-perfect match prevents "stress shielding" (where the implant takes all the weight, causing the surrounding bone to weaken).

 - Mechanical Strength: High enough for load-bearing orthopedic applications. Much stronger than polymers.

 - Degradation Time: Degrades via corrosion in bodily fluids in 3 to 12 months, depending on the specific alloy used (e.g., alloying with Zinc or Calcium slows degradation).

 - Challenges: Mg degrades rapidly and produces hydrogen gas bubbles as a byproduct. If gas is produced faster than the body can absorb it, it can cause tissue separation. Modern Mg-alloys focus on controlling this corrosion rate.

Zinc (Zn) and Iron (Fe)

 - Note: These are largely experimental or early-clinical in orthopedics compared to Mg.

 - Iron (Fe): Extremely strong but degrades too slowly in the body, and its corrosion products can be bulky and hard for the body to clear.

 - Zinc (Zn): Has a degradation rate sitting perfectly between rapid Magnesium and slow Iron. However, its mechanical strength is slightly lower than ideal, requiring alloying to improve performance.

3. Biodegradable Ceramics

Ceramics are rarely used as standalone fixation devices (like screws) because they are highly brittle. However, they are outstanding for bone regeneration and are often used as void fillers, coatings, or in composites with polymers.

Beta-Tricalcium Phosphate (β-TCP)

 - Properties: Highly osteoconductive (provides a scaffold for new bone cells to grow onto).

 - Mechanical Strength: High compressive strength but low tensile strength (brittle).

 - Degradation Time: Resorbs in 6 to 18 months. Crucially, its degradation rate closely matches the rate of new bone formation.

 - Clinical Use: Bone graft substitutes, filling bone cysts, or blended with PLLA/PCL to make composite screws that are both strong and osteoconductive.

Calcium Sulfate (Plaster of Paris)

 - Properties: One of the oldest biomaterials. Osteoconductive.

 - Mechanical Strength: Very low.

 - Degradation Time: Very rapid, absorbing in 1 to 2 months, which is often faster than new bone can form.

 - Clinical Use: Used as a void filler or combined with slower-degrading materials like TCP to create biphasic bone substitutes.
