## Correct option

**3. Proximal humerus**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a pathologic fracture through a unicameral bone cyst (UBC) of the proximal femur. The most common location for these lesions is the proximal humerus (52% of cases), followed by the femur (25%) and calcaneus (6%).

UBCs, also known as solitary bone cysts, are benign, single-chambered, fluid-filled lesions of unknown etiology. They occur predominantly in children and adolescents before skeletal maturity and represent around 3% of all primary tumors in the first 2 decades of life. UBCs are typically asymptomatic until a fracture occurs, usually with minor trauma, resulting in the classic “fallen leaf” sign indicating a free-floating fragment within the cystic cavity. Depending on the location of the lesion, these may be treated with or without surgery. Lesions of the proximal femur have a high rate of re-fracture and nonunion and are preferentially treated with curettage, bone grafting, and internal fixation. Lesions in non-weight-bearing extremities (e.g., proximal humerus) may be treated nonoperatively; 15% of lesions fill in with native bone following an acute fracture. As a patient approaches skeletal maturity, a UBC will often decrease in size and may heal after growth is complete. Fracture healing usually does not lead to cyst resolution and requires close follow-up while in the active phase due to recurrence and risk of fracture or growth arrest.

*Wilke et al. published a 2017 retrospective review to evaluate if internal fixation (1) reduced the risk of further procedures for treating a UBC and (2) increased the rate of radiographic union.* Thirty-six patients treated for a unicameral bone cyst of the proximal femur were reviewed between 1974 and 2014. Those who did not undergo internal fixation had a higher rate of undergoing additional surgery. There was no difference in time to radiographic healing; however, time to return to normal activities was reduced if patients did undergo internal fixation.

*Pretell-Mazzini et al. published a 2014 JAAOS review on the general characteristics and management controversies of UBCs.* *The authors describe the Dormans and Pill classification of six proximal femur UBCs based on the cyst's location and size and the lateral buttress's presence or absence.* Three characteristics of the lesion are important when considering fracture constructs: the presence of a lateral buttress, femoral neck bone stock, and skeletal maturity. Generally, side plate constructs are required in the setting of a compromised lateral buttress. Lesions with adequate femoral neck bone stock are amenable to constructs with cannulated screws. Lesions with inadequate femoral neck bone stock in skeletally immature patients are best treated with smooth K-wire fixation; however physeal-crossing cannulated screws may be used in skeletally mature patients.

*Ruiz-Arellanos et al. published a 2024 JBJS systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the treatment and outcomes of 4,973 UBCs.* **The authors found** that cyst filling with bone substitute and decompression with or without curettage was a highly successful technique for treating UBCs. Decompressing the cyst wall after injection, rather than the specific compound used for injection, was also more likely to enhance healing rates. Additionally, packing the defect with bone substitute demonstrated a higher success rate than autograft, making it the recommended option for patients with UBCs.
