## Question

A 3-year-old male sustains the injury shown in Figure A and an immediate hip spica is placed. Over the next 24-hours the patient is noted to become agitated with increasing analgesia requirements and decreased movement of his left toes. All of the following factors may lead to this condition EXCEPT:

![[q5379-a-original-femur-break-xray-f.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Spica casting with knee flexed 105-degrees
2. Immediate cast placement in the emergency department under conscious sedation
3. Initial placement of short leg cast for traction to aid in fracture reduction
4. Firm cast mold in popliteal fossa to prevent fracture displacement
5. High energy injury

## Correct option

**2. Immediate cast placement in the emergency department under conscious sedation**

## Answer explanation

Compartment syndrome is a devastating complication of pediatric lower extremity fractures. Improper spica casting technique may increase the risk for the development of compartment syndrome after low energy femoral shaft fractures. Immediate spica cast application in the emergency department with adequate sedation has not been shown to be associated with an increased risk for compartment syndrome.

Spica casting is an effective treatment for femoral shaft fractures in patients aged <7 months to 5 years with <2 to 3cm of shortening. The cast should first be placed on the body and thigh, followed by the lower leg in order to decrease the risk of compartment syndrome. The 90/90 technique of spica casting in which the short leg cast is placed first and traction is applied to reduce the fracture has been shown to increase the risk for compartment syndrome and skin complications. The elevation of the leg also leads to hypoperfusion, ischemia, and rebound swelling.

*Mansour et al performed a retrospective review evaluating application of a spica cast in the emergency department versus the operating room for pediatric femur fractures.* There was no significant difference in the rate of loss of reduction requiring revision casting or operative treatment, the need for cast wedging, or minor skin breakdown. Additionally, spica casting in the OR delayed time to cast placement, lengthened hospital stay, and the cost of casting in the OR was 3 times higher than casting in the ER.

*Mubarak et al evaluated 9 cases of low energy femur fractures treated with 90/90 spica casts that developed leg compartment syndrome.* **The authors proposed** that the technique of initial short leg cast placement and use of cast for traction while applying the 90/90 spica cast increases the risk for compartment syndrome. The most severe damage was to the superficial and deep compartments of the leg.

*Infante et al retrospectively reviewed patients weighing between 10 and 100lbs with femoral shaft fractures who were placed in immediate hip spica casts.* All fractures united within 8 weeks and no significant residual angular deformity was present in any of the patients studied. **The authors concluded** that immediate closed reduction and placement of a well-molded hip spica cast is a safe and reliable treatment for closed femur fractures.

Figure A demonstrates a left spiral femoral shaft fracture in a skeletally immature individual. Illustration A demonstrates 90/90 spica cast application technique.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1 and 4: Pressure on the popliteal fossa with excessive cast molding or knee flexion >90 degrees may increase the risk for compartment syndrome.
Answer 3: Longitudinal traction after short leg cast application can generate pressure on the skin and underlying muscle, increasing the risk for compartment syndrome.
Answer 5: High energy injury mechanisms place patients at increased risk for compartment syndrome irrespective of spica cast technique.
