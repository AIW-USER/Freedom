## Question

A 22-year-old male with a history of IV drug abuse presents to the ED with a painful and swollen sternoclavicular (SC) joint. His temperature is 101.3º F. His remaining vital signs are stable. Radiographs do not demonstrate any fracture or dislocation. Labs are drawn and demonstrate significantly elevated CRP and ESR. You are concerned this patient may have septic arthritis of the SC joint. Which of the following is true regarding this condition?

## Options

1. Advanced imaging (CT/MRI) should be routinely obtained
2. The most common causative organism is Pseudomonas aeruginosa
3. Aspiration alone is effective in treating septic arthritis of the sternoclavicular joint
4. The most common presenting symptom of sternoclavicular joint septic arthritis is shoulder pain
5. Surgical resection of the sternoclavicular joint should be avoided at the time of incision and drainage

## Correct option

**1. Advanced imaging (CT/MRI) should be routinely obtained**

## Answer explanation

Sternoclavicular (SC) joint septic arthritis should be treated with incision and drainage (I&D), but advanced imaging should be obtained preoperatively to rule out retrosternal abscess, chest wall phlegmon, and identify the extent of associated osteomyelitis.

SC joint septic arthritis is relatively uncommon, accounting for about 1% of all septic joints. However, it is more common in IV drug users and other at risk populations, including diabetics and those with renal insufficiency. The most common presenting symptom is chest wall tenderness and the most common causative organism is Staphylococcus aureus. Advanced imaging is important before surgery because there is a high incidence of retrosternal abscess or chest wall phlegmon associated with SC joint infection. It is necessary to identify these findings preoperatively, as they may require consultation with a general or cardiothoracic surgeon. MRI may be more useful in identifying these changes in addition to associated osteomyelitis, but CT is also useful to identify abscess in cases where MRI may not be available.

*Ross et al. reviewed the management of SC septic arthritis, examining 170 cases.* **They noted** chest pain was the most common presenting symptom, with high rates of associated complication, including osteomyelitis (55%), chest wall abscess (25%) and mediastinitis (13%). They reinforce that CT or MRI should be routinely obtained to assess for these conditions and that the sternoclavicular joint should be completely resected to avoid reinfection.

*Von Glinski et al. reviewed the surgical management of SC joint infections,* **noting** a high percentage of diabetic patients (61.5%) in their population. **They note** that all patients received a preoperative CT scan and all were treated with SC joint resection. **They concluded** that resection of the SJ joint led to satisfactory results and that less invasive methods of drainage may lead to recurrence.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The most common causative organism is Staphylococcus aureus.
Answer 3: Aspiration alone is ineffective in definitively managing SC joint septic arthritis. If aspiration is positive for infection, thorough debridement of the joint should be performed.
Answer 4: The most common presenting symptom is chest wall tenderness about the SC joint, though shoulder pain can be present at a much lower rate.
Answer 5: Surgical resection of the SC joint demonstrates the best outcome and lowest rates of recurrence given the high rates of associated osteomyelitis.
