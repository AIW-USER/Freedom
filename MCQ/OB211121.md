## Question

A 23-year-old soccer player with a history of multiple ankle sprains presents with posterolateral ankle pain that has been present for the past 3 months. On physical exam, the patient has pain over the posterolateral ankle with plantar flexion, but no pain is elicited with combined passive dorsiflexion and eversion of the ankle. He denies any numbness or tingling in his foot or sensation of ankle instability. The pain has been managed with rest, physical therapy and intra-articular steroid injection with only marginal improvement. A current MRI is shown in Figures 1 and 2. What is the best next step?

## Options

1. Peroneal brevis tubularization
2. Arthroscopic excision of os trigonum
3. Flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon debridement
4. EMG to evaluate for tarsal tunnel syndrome
5. Subtalar loose body removal

## Correct option

**2. Arthroscopic excision of os trigonum**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is most consistent with Os trigonum syndrome, with characteristic posterolateral ankle pain exacerbated by plantar flexion and increased signal on T2 MRI about the Os trigonum.

An Os trigonum is an accessory ossicle separated from the posterolateral tubercle of the talus that is present in 10-25% of the population. While generally asymptomatic, an os trigonum may become symptomatic on occasion in active individuals, such as ballet dancers and soccer players, given their strenuous lower extremity demands involving repetitive plantar flexion. The Os trigonum lies lateral to the FHL tendon and may fracture or contribute to local inflammation, leading to localized pain, FHL entrapment, or FHL irritation. The etiology of FHL irritation secondary to an Os trigonum can be differentiated from isolated FHL tendonitis by examination, whereas isolated FHL tendonitis generally presents with posteroMEDIAL ankle pain and triggering of the FHL tendon.

*Nault et al. present an overview of Os trigonum syndrome.* **The authors note** that this pathology is predominately seen in ballet dancers and soccer players due to plantar flexion stresses and is often most symptomatic with push-off maneuvers. Symptoms typically have been found to improve with rest or activity modification. **They found** that Os trigonum syndrome was furthermore often associated with pathology of the flexor hallucis longus tendon, and while symptoms generally resolved with conservative management, both arthroscopic and open surgical excision were successful in treating the underlying pathology.

*Weiss et al. evaluated the clinical outcomes following arthroscopic excision of symptomatic Os trigonum in a series of 24 patients who had failed non-operative management.* *The authors utilized a posteromedial viewing portal and burr through a posterolateral working portal.* *At final 26-month follow-up,* **they found** that isolated Os trigonum excisions resulted in significant improvement in AOFAS scores in 21 of the 24 patients and full return to normal activities without limitations at an average of 1.5 months post-operatively. The only complication reported was a transient neuropraxia of the calcaneal branch of the posterior tibial nerve.

Figure A is a T1 weighted sagittal view of the ankle showing an Os trigonum. Figure B is a T2 weighted sagittal view of the ankle showing inflammation with a fluid signal noted around the Os trigonum relative to the posterior talar body.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: While posterolateral ankle pain can be a sign of peroneal tendon pathology, this patient denies ankle instability and has no pain with passive dorsiflexion and eversion.
Answer 3: FHL tendonitis may be associated with an Os trigonum, but the treatment of this would be to address the Os as the inciting pathology and not debridement of the FHL tendon. FHL tendonitis generally presents with posteroMEDIAL ankle pain rather than posteroLATERAL ankle pain.
Answer 4: Tarsal tunnel syndrome often presents with medial foot and heel pain with neurologic symptoms along the plantar-medial aspect of the foot.
Answer 5: Subtalar arthritis or a subtalar loose body may lead to pain with inversion and eversion in the posterolateral ankle. However, this patient has no pain with eversion of the ankle, and there is no evidence of a loose body on MRI.
