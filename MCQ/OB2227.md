## Question

A 9-year-old boy who sustained the injury in Figure A following a motor vehicle accident. He has been followed yearly for a continued leg length discrepancy. At the age of 14, with a LLD of 4.2cm, the decision is made to proceed with an epiphysiodesis of the contralateral distal femur and proximal tibia. Fusion of the growth plate was achieved. At 18 years of age, he is found to have an apparent LLD of 3cm. What is the most common reason for this persistent LLD?

![[q2227-a-distal-femur-peds.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Error in the timing of surgery
2. Failure to use epiphysiodesis of the ipsilateral proximal tibia
3. Compensatory overgrowth of the contralateral limb
4. Failure to address proximal femur physis
5. Result of inadvertent joint penetration

## Correct option

**1. Error in the timing of surgery**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a persistent leg length discrepancy because there was an error in timing to perform the epiphysiodesis. The decision to proceed with this particular surgery would likely allow only a 1.2 cm correction given the amount of growth remaining. Errors in timing are by far the most common cause of persistent LLD.

In general, lower extremity growth ceases for boys and girls at 16 and 14 years, respectively. Growth of the lower extremity for the proximal femur, distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal tibia averages are 3, 9, 6, and 5mm per year, respectively. This patient sustained a distal femur fracture with apparent physeal growth arrest. Five years following the injury he has nearly a 4.5 cm LLD. As the distal femur contributes approximately 9mm of growth per year (9mm per year x 5 years = 45mm or 4.5 cm), achieving equal leg lengths at maturity would require contralateral arrest long before the age of 14. As the boy is now skeletally mature at 18 years of age, a limb lengthening procedure is likely indicated to correct for the LLD.

*Blair et al. retrospectively reviewed 67 distal femoral and proximal tibial epiphysiodesis over a 14 year period for correction of leg length inequality.* **They reported** that the vast majority of failures were secondary to errors in timing surgery due to incorrect use of Green and Anderson growth prediction tables. **They concluded** that the primary causes of failure would have been decreased by more careful follow-up and the use of the Moseley Graph.

*Stanitski et al. review the assessment and treatment of limb-length inequality in the growing child.* **They report** that in the immature patient, consistent longitudinal data are essential to avoid pitfalls in the projection of ultimate length difference. They discuss common therapeutic options ranging from no treatment to surgical shortening or lengthening procedures. **They conclude** that the current indication for lengthening is a disparity exceeding 5 to 6 cm, while epiphysiodesis or shortening is useful for smaller discrepancies or for residual differences following a contralateral lengthening.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of a distal femur physeal injury with the complete displacement of the epiphysis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Epiphysiodesis of the ipsilateral proximal tibia would result further shorten the limb and not indicated.
Answer 3: There was no compensatory overgrowth of the contralateral limb. The contralateral limb resumed expected growth.
Answer 4: Addressing the proximal femur would result in 3mm of correction per year and would be unnecessary if the timing of the surgery performed was done correctly.
Answer 5: There is no evidence of inadvertent joint penetration. This may result in chondrolysis, but would not influence LLD.
