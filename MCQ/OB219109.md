## Question

A 75-year-old, right-hand-dominant female has had shoulder pain for 10 years and has failed conservative treatment with physical therapy and corticosteroid injections. An AP radiograph of her right shoulder is shown in Figure A. On examination, her range of motion is limited with forward flexion and abduction both to approximately 70°. She externally rotates to 10° and internally rotates to her right hip. Crepitus is noted with motion on examination, which does cause pain. Which of the following is true regarding her best surgical option at this time?

![[q219109-a-rotator-cuff-arthropathy.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Outcomes rely on an intact coracoacromial arch
2. Poor outcomes are seen in setting of supraspinatus atrophy
3. Improves pain, but does not improve function significantly
4. Expected to improve pain and range of motion even in setting of preoperative pseudoparalysis
5. High rates of component loosening expected

## Correct option

**4. Expected to improve pain and range of motion even in setting of preoperative pseudoparalysis**

## Answer explanation

This patient has rotator cuff tear arthropathy, which would be best treated with a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA), given its ability to improve her pain and function.

Rotator cuff arthropathy results from an insufficient rotator cuff which leads to superior migration of the humeral head and abnormal contact pressures on the glenohumeral joint. Patients complain of pain, crepitus and limitations in motion, both actively and passively. Many patients are pseudoparalytic, meaning they are unable to actively abduct their shoulder. Many patients are initially treated non-operatively with success using physical therapy and steroid injections but in order to ultimately address the pathology, surgery may be required. Surgical options for this challenging problem have evolved with the advent of rTSA. Previously, hemiarthroplasty with a large head was seen to be the best surgical option, but it has been well established that this does not do as good of a job reestablishing shoulder function and range of motion (ROM) as an rTSA.

*Petrillo et al. reviewed using rTSA for irreparable rotator cuff tears and cuff tear arthropathy.* **They noted** a statistically significant improvement in outcome scores and range of motion postoperatively globally in this population. This, however, was not without complication, as 17% of patients had a complication ranging from heterotopic ossification to others causing the need for revision.

*Leung et al. compared the functional outcome of hemiarthroplasty to rTSA for cuff tear arthropathy in 56 cases.* **They noted** that while postoperative outcome scores improved in both groups, scores were significantly better in the rTSA group at 2-year follow-up than the hemiarthroplasty group. Specifically, the active elevation range of motion was noted be significantly better in the rTSA group at all time points in this study.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of a right shoulder demonstrating rotator cuff arthropathy with glenohumeral arthritis secondary to superior migration of the humeral head and acetabularization of the acromion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Arthroscopic debridement and hemiarthroplasty both rely on an intact coracoacromial arch to prevent anterosuperior escape of the humeral head. rTSA has been demonstrated to have better functional improvements in this population compared to hemiarthroplasty.
Answer 2: Supraspinatus atrophy is a contraindication to rotator cuff repair in this population and would represent a reason for performing a rTSA.
Answer 3: Studies have shown that rTSA reliably improves pain and functional outcomes, including range of motion.
Answer 5: High rates of component loosening would be expected in this population if an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty system were to be used as the glenoid fixation is exposed to a much higher degree of stress through the "rocking horse" phenomenon. Rates of loosening are low with the use of modern-day rTSA constructs.
