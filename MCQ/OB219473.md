## Question

A 24-year-old right-hand-dominant male presents to the emergency department for evaluation of left shoulder pain following a fall onto his shoulder while participating in jiu-jitsu. On examination, there is skin tenting with posterior shoulder fullness. Plain radiographs are obtained and shown in Figure A. The muscle most commonly involved with this injury is innervated by what nerve?

![[q219473-a-screenshot-2024-02-11-at-9-49-49-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Axillary nerve
2. Cranial nerve VII
3. Cranial nerve XI
4. Medial and lateral pectoral nerves
5. Suprascapular nerve

## Correct option

**3. Cranial nerve XI**

## Answer explanation

This 24-year-old male has a type IV acromioclavicular (AC) joint separation following a direct fall onto the left shoulder while participating in jiu-jitsu. Type IV AC joint separations are commonly irreducible as they pierce the fascia of the trapezius, a muscle innervated by cranial nerve XI/spinal accessory nerve (Answer 3).

Acromioclavicular joint separations ("shoulder separations") are commonly encountered in practice, accounting for approximately 9% of all shoulder girdle injuries and 40-50% of shoulder injuries in sports. The condition involves injury to the AC and possibly the coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments, depending on injury severity. These injuries are most often characterized via the Rockwood classification, where the injury is categorized into six separate types based on the integrity of AC and CC ligaments, as well as the direction of clavicle displacement (Illustrations A & B). Shoulder separation injuries that involve posterior displacement of the clavicle are considered type IV injuries and often require surgical intervention as they pierce through the trapezial fascia, a muscle innervated by the spinal accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI).

*Simovitch et al. reviewed AC joint separations, focusing on the biomechanics, diagnosis, and management of these injuries.* **They report** type I and II injuries are most amenable to nonoperative treatment, while types IV through VI often require operative intervention to restore the CC interval. Treatment of type III injuries remains a debatable topic, however, more recent data suggests non-operative treatment is the most appropriate initial course of management.

*Duffett et al. performed a qualitative review of four randomized controlled trials examining the most appropriate treatment of type III AC joint injuries.* All included RCTs demonstrate similar outcomes between operative and nonoperative treatment. **The authors conclude** that nonoperative treatment, at a minimum, is non-inferior to operative treatment and allows the patient to return to previous function in a faster manner compared to their operative counterparts.

Figure A demonstrates a Type IV shoulder separation with posterior displacement of the clavicle relative to the AC joint. Illustration A details the Rockwood Classification, while Illustration B exhibits the pathoanatomy associated with each type of injury.

Answer 1: The axillary nerve innervates the teres minor and deltoid muscles, both of which are not associated with this type of injury.
Answer 2: Cranial nerve VII, otherwise known as the facial nerve, innervates several facial muscles, as well as the platysma.
Answer 4: The medial and lateral pectoral nerves innervate the pectoralis major, while the medial pectoral nerve alone innervates the pectoralis minor muscle. Both muscles are not associated with type IV AC joint injuries.
Answer 5: The suprascapular nerve innervates the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. These muscles are not associated with AC joint separations.
