## Question

A 23-year-old male presents to the orthopedic clinic for follow-up. 12 weeks ago, he suffered an accidental laceration to the left volar wrist while using a box-cutting knife. The emergency room consult documented an intact median and ulnar nerve exam. The patient underwent a primary repair of the FDS and FDP to the ring finger. In the clinic today, he cannot fully extend the wrist and simultaneously extend all fingers. He has full passive flexion of the wrist and fingers. However, when actively closing his fingers into a fist the small and long fingers do not contact the palm. Which of the events listed below is most likely to explain the current function of his left hand?

## Options

1. Shortening of the ring finger FDP 0.5 cm during repair
2. Shortening of the ring finger FDP 2 cm during repair
3. Rupture of the ring finger FDP repair postoperatively
4. Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury in zone 1 of Guyon’s canal
5. Iatrogenic median nerve injury in the carpal tunnel

## Correct option

**2. Shortening of the ring finger FDP 2 cm during repair**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with active flexion lag of the fingers adjacent to a repaired FDP tendon, known as the quadriga effect. Over-advancement of the FDP greater than 1 cm is associated with this outcome.

Several interconnections of the FDP tendons of the fingers result in shared biomechanics between the fingers. The quadriga phenomenon was named because of the resemblance to the reins of the 4 horses that would pull a Roman chariot. If 1 of the 4 horses had a tight rein, that animal would take most of the strain, leaving slack in the remaining reins. Classically, interconnection has been attributed to the shared muscle belly of the long, ring, and little fingers. However, several anatomy studies have shown additional connections including cross-linking of tendon fibers in the distal forearm and interconnected synovial sheaths with the carpal tunnel. Shortening of 1 of the FDP tendons results in increased tightness across that “rein”, leaving slack in the remaining FDP tendons. This is why a patient with a shortened FDP of the ring finger would have limited active flexion of the long and small fingers.

*Malerich et al. performed a cadaver study involving 9 fresh specimens.* The FDP was advanced in 0.5 cm increments at its distal insertion site. The flexion lag of the adjacent digits was measured. **They reported** the upper limit of advancement for the long, ring, and small finger profundi without affecting the flexion of the adjacent finger was 1 cm. **The authors conclude** that shortening greater than 1 cm is not recommended during the primary repair of FDP.

*Schreuders published a review of the quadriga phenomenon.* **The author notes** that although the small, ring, and long fingers share a common muscle belly, significant interconnections exist between all finger profundi. **They note** that some anatomical variations have been found that connect the flexor pollicis longus to the FDP of the index finger. **The authors conclude** that a thorough understanding of the anatomy of interconnections between profundi tendons is needed for appropriate surgical repair and post-operative therapy.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1- In finger by 0.5 cm should not result in any significant decrease in adjacent finger flexion
Answer 3- A rupture of the repaired tendon would decrease active flexion over the affected finger, not the adjacent fingers.
Answer 4- An iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury would result in the denervation of the intrinsic muscles and an intrinsic minus (claw hand) hand across the ulnar 2 digits.
Answer 5- An iatrogenic median nerve injury would result in denervation to the 1st and 2nd lumbricals and thenar musculature.
