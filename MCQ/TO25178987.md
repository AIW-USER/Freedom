Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Carpal Instability

## Question

In Carpal Instability Adaptive (CIA) secondary to a distal radius malunion with dorsal tilt, what is the primary compensation mechanism of the lunate?

## Options

1. The lunate flexes to maintain the center of gravity
2. The lunate tilts dorsally (extends) to realign with the tilted radial joint surface
3. The lunate translocates ulnarly
4. The lunate remains neutral while the scaphoid flexes

## Correct option

**2. The lunate tilts dorsally (extends) to realign with the tilted radial joint surface**

## Answer explanation

The lunate adapts to the new slope of the
radius; this is not a ligamentous failure but a positional adaptation to bony
deformity.

Carpal
Instability Adaptive (CIA)
Definition
CIA refers to
carpal malalignment that occurs secondary to pathology outside the carpus
itself — the carpal bones and their intrinsic and extrinsic ligaments are
normal, but the carpus adopts an abnormal posture as an adaptive response to
altered skeletal geometry proximal or distal to it. The instability is a
consequence of an extrinsic deformity rather than intrinsic ligamentous
pathology.
Pathophysiology
The most common
cause is a malunited distal radius fracture with residual dorsal
angulation (loss of normal volar tilt). The normal volar tilt of the distal
radius articular surface (approximately 11° of volar inclination) positions the
proximal carpal row in a balanced neutral alignment. When a distal radius malunion
produces dorsal angulation of the articular surface, the carpus adapts by
assuming a DISI pattern — the lunate extends to accommodate the dorsally
tilted radial articular surface. This adaptive DISI is not caused by SL
ligament rupture (the SL ligament is intact) but by the altered geometry of the
platform on which the carpus sits. The scapholunate angle may be increased,
mimicking SL dissociation, but the SL gap is normal and the Watson test is
negative.
Other causes
include malunion of carpal fractures (particularly scaphoid malunion with
humpback deformity — the flexed, shortened scaphoid alters proximal row
mechanics), malunited metacarpal fractures affecting distal row alignment, and
Madelung's deformity (increased volar tilt of the distal radius producing
adaptive VISI pattern).
Clinical Features
Patients present
with wrist pain, stiffness, reduced grip strength, and limited range of motion.
The history typically reveals a preceding distal radius fracture treated
conservatively or with inadequate reduction. Examination demonstrates
restricted wrist extension (due to dorsal angulation of the radius), midcarpal
prominence, and tenderness at the dorsal wrist. Importantly, specific
instability tests (Watson test, ballottement tests) are negative because
the intrinsic ligaments are intact.
Radiographic Findings
The key
radiographic feature is the distal radius malunion — loss of volar tilt
(dorsal angulation), loss of radial inclination, radial shortening, and
incongruity of the DRUJ. The carpus demonstrates a DISI pattern (increased
scapholunate angle, extended lunate) that is secondary to the altered radial
geometry. The SL gap is normal, distinguishing adaptive DISI from true SL
dissociation.
Management
The treatment
principle in CIA is to correct the underlying skeletal deformity — once
normal geometry is restored, the carpal malalignment resolves spontaneously
because the ligaments are intact.
Corrective
Osteotomy of the Distal Radius: An opening wedge osteotomy through the malunion site, restoring normal
volar tilt, radial inclination, and radial length. A structural bone graft
(iliac crest autograft, distal radius allograft, or synthetic bone substitute)
fills the opening wedge, and fixation is achieved with a volar locking plate.
Once the articular surface geometry is restored, the carpus "resets"
to its normal alignment — the adaptive DISI resolves as the lunate returns to
neutral on the correctly tilted radial platform.
Scaphoid
Malunion Correction: For
adaptive carpal instability secondary to scaphoid humpback malunion, a volar
wedge bone graft (corticocancellous iliac crest graft or vascularised
medial femoral condyle graft) is interposed at the malunion site, restoring
scaphoid length, correcting the flexion deformity, and normalising proximal row
mechanics. Fixation with a headless compression screw (Herbert or Acutrak
screw).
The critical
distinction from CID is that no carpal ligament reconstruction or
intercarpal fusion is required — the ligaments are competent, and
correction of the extrinsic deformity is both necessary and sufficient.
