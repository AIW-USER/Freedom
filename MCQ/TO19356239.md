## Question

You are the team doctor of a soccer team. A 15 years old athlete is getting repeated hamstring injury. What kind of training should he be doing [FNB 2023]

## Options

1. Balance board exercises
2. Eccentric
3. Isotonic
4. Isometric

## Correct option

**2. Eccentric**

## Answer explanation

Ans: B

EXPLANATION: -

The three muscles at the back of the thigh—semitendinosus and gracilis medially and biceps femoris laterally, are called the hamstrings. They originate at ischial tuberosity and insert on upper leg such that they cross two joints (hip and knee), which makes them prone to injury.

![[TO19356239-1.png]]

Hamstring injuries are very common in football players, sprinters, gymnasts. In the large majority of hamstring strains, injury to Biceps femoris is most common (~83%) Semimembranosus injuries are uncommon (~12%) and Semitendinosus is rarer (~5%).

There are 2 types of Hamstring strains:

Type I Hamstring strain:

• Occur during high speed running
• Usually involves long head of biceps femoris –proximal muscle tendon junction
• More marked acute decline in function
• Shorter rehabilitation

Type II Hamstring strain:

![[TO19356239-2.png]]

• Excessive lengthening of hamstring with hip flexed and knee extended – high kicking, split
• Slow speed , stretch injuries
• Usually involves semimembranosus close to ischial tuberosity
• Less dramatic acute decline in function
• Longer rehabilitation

Diagnosis: A meticulous history taking and clinical evaluation is the mainstay. Clinically, majority of these patients present with a sudden onset of posterior thigh pain after high speed running or other sport activity. Active knee flexion against resistance in the prone
position is painful. USG and MRI (better in detecting muscle edema hemorrhage and tear) are commonly used to support the diagnosis. MRI may help in grading the injury using BAMIC classification for muscle injuries.

The following is the British Athletics Muscle Injury Classification (BAMIC)

Location Categories (A-D):
• A: Myofascial (muscle surface/fascia)
• B: Muscle-tendon junction (where muscle meets tendon)
• C: Intra-tendinous (within the tendon)
• D: Tendon avulsion (tendon completely torn from attachment)

Grade Description Severity Location (A-D)
0 No structural damage, muscle soreness Mild N/A
1 Small tear, minimal muscle fiber disruption Mild A-D
2 Moderate tear, partial muscle fiber disruption Moderate A-D
3 Extensive tear, significant muscle fiber damage Severe A-D
4 Full-thickness tear, complete rupture Very Severe A-D

Prognosis:

• MRI negative hamstring strain – early return to play (RTP)
• More proximal injury, closer to ischial tuberosity – prolonged time to RTP
• Increased craniocaudal length of lesion on MRI – greater time to RTP
• Time to pain-free walking (>1 day , 4 times more likely to RTP after >3 weeks)
• Days to pain-free jogging : 1-2 days - < 2 weeks ; 3-5 days- > 2 weeks ; >5 days - >4 weeks
• Injury to intramuscular conjoint tendon , RTP delayed up to 3-4 months
• Pain on passive straight leg raise test and longer length of hamstring tenderness- greater the time to RTP
• Forced to stop training / playing within 5 mins of the injury and greater maximum pain during injury – greater the time to RTP

Treatment and Rehabilitation:

• Acute phase: Isometric strengthening, ice, compression, elevation, NSAIDs on need basis. Isometric strengthening is muscle contraction in static position (no joint movement) is ideal exercise during acute phase when pain is present.
• Subacute phase: Pain free walking once attained, muscle strengthening and conditioning is resumed. From isometric one moves to isotonic exercises in this phase. Basically, isotonic exercises (where joint movement is there but tone is kept constant during exercises) include both concentric and eccentric strengthening. Concentric involves improving strength in contracted position, while eccentric involves strengthening in stretched position. Here the emphasis is on eccentric strengthening, and the Askling L- Protocol and Nordic hamstring curls.
