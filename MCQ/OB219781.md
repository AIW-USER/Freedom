## Question

A 61-year-old male arrives in the postoperative care unit following a right total knee arthroplasty, and upon examination is found to have an absent dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial artery pulses. Vascular surgery is consulted, and a CT angiogram reveals an injury to the popliteal artery consistent with a blunt traumatic injury. What retractor position is most likely to result in this injury?

## Options

1. Insertion of a posterior retractor 1cm into the posterior soft tissue, medial to the PCL
2. Insertion of a lateral retractor around the fibular head 3cm into the posterior soft tissues
3. Insertion of a medial retractor along the tibial plateau 3cm into the posterior soft tissue
4. Insertion of a posterior retractor 1cm into the posterior soft tissue, lateral to the PCL
5. Insertion of the lamina spreader between the medial tibial plateau and femoral condyle

## Correct option

**4. Insertion of a posterior retractor 1cm into the posterior soft tissue, lateral to the PCL**

## Answer explanation

The most likely cause of this patient's popliteal artery injury is the insertion of the posterior retractor 1cm or greater into the posterior soft tissues and lateral to the PCL (answer choice 4).

Popliteal artery injuries during total knee arthroplasty are a rare but devastating complication occurring in 0.017%-0.2% of total knee arthroplasties. Arterial injuries are either direct laceration of the artery during dissection of the posterior compartment, or blunt injury resulting in excessive traction from either retractor placement or knee hyperextension. The anatomic course of the popliteal artery contributes to the risk of injury as the artery lies directly behind the posterior capsule, and lateral to the PCL. The popliteal artery is furthest away from the posterior tibial plateau when the knee is between 60° to 90° of flexion. Therefore, performing total knee arthroplasty in hyperextension places the popliteal artery at increased risk for injury due to its relative position to the posterior plateau during hyperextension. Hyperextension of the knee also increases the traction placed on the popliteal artery, which can contribute to arterial injury. Prompt management identification and management of these injuries perioperatively or postoperatively are critical for reducing morbidity and mortality. These injuries highlight the importance of postoperative pulse checks in PACU along with a high index of suspicion.

*Matsen Ko et al.* *Performed a retrospective review of trends, costs, and risk factors associated with popliteal artery injuries during total knee arthroplasty using the Nationwide Inpatient Sample from 1998 to 2009, reviewing 1,120, 508 patients.* **The authors found** a rate of injury of 0.057%, and the rate to be relatively constant over the study period with significant risk factors including revision surgery, peripheral artery disease, weight loss, renal failure, coagulopathy, and metastatic cancer. **The authors concluded** that popliteal artery injuries carry a high rate of morbidity and mortality, and are associated with increased risk of revision surgery, greater length of stay, hospital charges, and mortality.

*Farrington et al.* *Performed an analysis of 45 knees in 32 patients with primary osteoarthritis to evaluate the distance between the popliteal artery and posterior tibial cortex in various degrees of flexion using duplex ultrasound.* **The authors found** the distance from the posterior plateau to the popliteal artery to be greatest at 60° to 90°. **The authors concluded** the safest position for the popliteal artery during total knee arthroplasty is during knee flexion.

*Ninomiya et al.* Performed a review of popliteal artery injury and the relevant anatomy during total knee arthroplasty by reviewing 50 MRI knee imaging studies. **The authors found** that the popliteal artery was consistently found to be a lateral structure relative to the joint line and that placement of the posterior retractor lateral to the PCL and deeper than 1cm increased the risk of popliteal artery injury. **The authors concluded** that the hyperflexion of the knee and placement of the posterior retractor lateral to the PCL or deeper than 1cm are risk factors for popliteal artery injury and that the artery is least at risk with medial placement of the posterior retractor.

Illustration A: The axial cut of the knee depicts the relationship of the PCL relative to the popliteal artery. The popliteal artery can be seen in the posterior aspect of the knee, just lateral to midline. The PCL can also be seen in the posterior aspect of the femoral condyle , medial to the course of the popliteal artery.

Answers 1: The popliteal artery is lateral to the PCL and medial retractor placement is considered a safe area
Answer 2: Lateral retractor placement around the fibular head may result in injury to the common peroneal nerve which can run within 3cm of the posterior aspect of the fibular head
Answer 3: Medial retractor placement around the tibial plateau may result in stretch injury to the saphenous nerve which can run 2 to 3 centimeters posterior to the medial border of the tibia
Answer 5: Placement of the lamina spreader between the medial femoral condyle and medial tibial plateau is not associated with increased risk for popliteal artery injury..
