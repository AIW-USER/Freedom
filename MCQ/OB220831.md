## Question

A 7-year-old male presents to your clinic. His parents are concerned because he has always been “bowlegged,” but they feel this has worsened over the past few years. His radiographs are shown in Figure A. He undergoes a hemiepiphysiodesis using a titanium plate with two solid screws. His early postoperative recovery is uneventful, but at 6 months, he returns to the clinic, and you notice the screws have broken. Which of the following may have prevented this complication?

![[q220831-a-ob-blounts-1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Nothing, this is an expected outcome of the surgery
2. Using four screws instead of two
3. Placing cannulated screws instead of solid screws
4. Utilizing stainless steel implants
5. Waiting until the patient was nearing skeletal maturity prior to hemiepiphysiodesis

## Correct option

**4. Utilizing stainless steel implants**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with evidence of Blount’s disease, characterized by the genu varum seen on his radiographs. Temporary hemiepiphysiodesis is often utilized to tether the lateral physis while allowing growth on the medial side of the knee to ultimately correct his deformity. Complications can occur after this procedure, and use of stainless steel implants may lessen the risk of screw/plate breakage when compared to titanium implants (Answer 4).

Blount’s disease is thought to occur secondary to a pathologic posteromedial physis. As a result, the tibia gradually develops a classic deformity, characterized by genu varum, procurvatum, and internal tibia torsion. Blount’s disease can be classified based on the age of onset. The infantile form occurs typically before age 5, and is typically more severe and can be bilateral. Adolescent Blount’s typically occurs later, may be less severe, and can be associated with a femoral deformity. It is important to note that genu varum is normal in infants less than two years of age. Multiple treatment options exist depending on the severity of the deformity and the patient’s age. Osteotomies were historically the gold standard, but now are largely reserved for patients at or near skeletal maturity or who have failed a prior tethering procedure. Hemiepiphysiodesis, utilizing staples, plates, or screws, is another surgical option for patients with remaining growth potential. Tethering of the lateral physis allows the medial physis to “catch up” and gradually correct the deformity with time. The main complications with this technique involve undercorrection (if the patient does not have enough growth remaining to correct the deformity), rebound deformity, and hardware failure/breakage. Several possible risk factors for hardware failure have been proposed to include the use of cannulated screws, the use of titanium plates, obesity, and a larger preoperative deformity.

*Fan et al. conducted a systematic review that looked at risk factors for failure of temporary hemiepiphysiodesis in patients with Blount’s disease.* *They included 53 patients from 8 papers.* Overall, 51% of patients achieved neutral alignment at final follow. However, **they also found** a relatively high rate of unplanned reoperation (49%) with hemiepiphysiodesis. Overall, the literature was mixed regarding risk factors for failure, but they did find no statistically significant difference between staples versus plate fixation with regard to the rate of unplanned reoperation.

*Jain et al. performed a retrospective cohort study of 61 limbs (in 40 patients) with Blount’s disease, with a mean follow-up time of 38 months.* *All patients underwent plate fixation with either titanium or stainless steel plates, and with either 2 or 4 screws.* **They found** an overall failure rate 41%, with 28% of those occurring due to hardware failure. When stratified by plate material, **they found** a 5.9x higher rate of failure with titanium plates when compared to stainless steel plates. **They found** no differences between failure rates with the number of screws used for fixation. **The authors concluded** that the use of titanium plates for hemiepiphysiodesis may place patients at a higher risk for hardware failure, particularly in higher-risk patients with obesity and larger preoperative deformities.

Figure A demonstrates a standing AP radiograph of a child with Blount’s disease of the left tibia.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Hardware failure, while relatively common, should not be expected after this surgery, particularly if the patient has residual deformity that still needs correction
*Answer 2: The use of 2 versus 4 screws is not a risk factor for hardware failure, as described by Jain et al.*
Answer 3: Cannulated screws are biomechanically weaker than solid screws, and are therefore thought to be a risk factor for hardware failure.
Answer 5: While earlier age of onset at the time of surgery may be a risk factor for hardware failure, the literature surrounding this is mixed. Additionally, waiting until the patient nears skeletal maturity may not allow enough time for guided growth to correct his deformity.
