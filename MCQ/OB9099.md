## Question

## Options

1. Continued immobilization in a semi-rigid cervical orthosis for 6-8 weeks
2. Posterior occipital-cervical fusion with iliac crest bone graft
3. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the odontoid process with an anterior odontoid screw
4. Resection of the odontoid process through a transoral approach
5. Halo vest immobilization for 6-8 weeks

## Correct option

**3. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the odontoid process with an anterior odontoid screw**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a Type II odontoid fracture with risk factors for nonunion (smoker, delay in treatment > 4 days). The most appropriate treatment would be surgical fixation, with ORIF using anterior screw osteosynthesis being the most appropriate fixation method in this patient given the fracture pattern.

Odontoid fractures can be classified based on the anatomic position of the fracture within the dens itself. Type II is a fracture that occurs at the base of the odontoid as it attaches to the body of C2. Type II fractures can be managed nonsurgically or surgically with halo immobilization, ORIF (odontoid screw fixation), or posterior atlantoaxial arthrodesis. Management with halo vest immobilization is usually considered if the initial dens displacement is < 6mm, if the reduction is performed within 1 week of the injury and is able to be maintained, and if the patient is younger than 60 years old. Halo vest immobilization can lead to a healing rate of more than 90%. Anterior odontoid single screw fixation is often better tolerated than skeletal halo fixation and is also noted to preserve the normal rotation at C1/C2. Studies have shown a decreased risk of malunion and nonunion rates in the treatment of type II odontoid fractures with anterior odontoid screw fixation. Osteoporosis, short neck and barrel-chested anatomy, and fractures that are more than 4 weeks old preclude anterior odontoid fixation. Posterior surgical fusion techniques, on the other hand, provide high fusion success rates but do so at the expense of cervical rotation, with up to 50% of rotation lost with this technique.

*Shilpakar et al. performed a review on the management of acute odontoid fractures.* **They report** that candidates for anterior odontoid screw fixation are limited to those with acute Type II fractures in which the transverse ligament is intact, the fracture can be reduced into alignment to allow for adequate screw placement, and the fracture line is amenable to screw fixation. Exclusion criteria for anterior odontoid screw fixation included chronic fractures (due to a higher nonunion rate), short-necked obese patients—with increased AP chest diameter (such as those with COPD or emphysema, due to difficulty with screw trajectory), longitudinal or oblique fractures of the dens (due to difficulty aligning the fracture from the lagging effect of the screw), and severe irreducible fracture retrolisthesis.

*Subach et al. performed a retrospective review of 26 consecutive patients who underwent odontoid screw fixation in order to further define the safety and efficacy of the technique.* **They found** that radiographic fusion was evident in 25 of 26 patients (96%) at a mean follow-up period of 30 months. No complications related to the surgical approach were identified, and all patients remained in neurologically stable condition. However, two complications (8%) related to the instrumentation occurred, with patient requiring external immobilization because of suboptimal screw placement and one patient requiring posterior atlantoaxial arthrodesis because of inadequate fracture reduction. **They concluded** that single-screw anterior odontoid fixation was associated with a relatively low complication rate and a high fusion rate, and should therefore be the preferred treatment method for acute Type II odontoid fractures.

*Fountas et al. performed a retrospective study on the fusion rate of a group of 38 patients having undergone anterior screw fixation for type II and "shallow" type III odontoid fractures.* **They found** that 87.1% of patients showed satisfactory bony fusion and no evidence of abnormal movement at the fracture site. **They concluded** that anterior odontoid screw fixation comprises a safe therapeutic modality with high stability and low mechanical failure rates at short-term and long-term follow-up.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Continued immobilization in a semi-rigid cervical orthosis for 6-8 weeks is an appropriate treatment option for Type II odontoid fractures in elderly patients who are not surgical candidates, which does not apply to this patient.
Answer 2: Posterior occipital-cervical fusion with iliac crest bone graft can be used for Type II odontoid fractures with risk factors for nonunion; however, given that this patient has acceptable alignment, minimal displacement, and appropriate fracture obliquity (anterosuperior to posteroinferior), anterior screw osteosynthesis is the more appropriate method of treatment.
Answer 4: Resection of the odontoid process through a transoral approach can be performed for fractures with severe posterior displacement associated with spinal cord compression and neurologic deficits, which is not the case in this patient.
Answer 5: Halo vest immobilization can be used for Type II odontoid fractures in young patients with no risk factors for nonunion; this patient, however, has risk factors for nonunion so halo vest immobilization is not the most appropriate method of treatment.
