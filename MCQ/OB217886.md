## Question

You are performing a right-sided anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction on a 23-year-old male patient. He underwent appropriate pre-surgical rehabilitation, and had full range of motion prior to surgery. You have prepared the lateral wall, and centered your femoral tunnel 9 mm anterior to the intercondylar ridge, at the 10 o'clock position. Further on in the surgical case, you center the tibial tunnel at the native ACL footprint, in line with the posterior border of the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus. The surgery is completed without complication. Assuming that the patient is compliant with post-operative restrictions and physical therapy, what would be his expected physical examination findings one-year after surgery?

## Options

1. Negative pivot shift, knee range of motion from 0 to 75 degrees
2. Negative pivot shift, knee range of motion from 0 to 120 degrees
3. Negative pivot shift, knee range of motion from 20 to 125 degrees
4. Positive pivot shift, knee range of motion from 0 to 120 degrees
5. Positive pivot shift, knee range of motion from 20 to 125 degrees

## Correct option

**1. Negative pivot shift, knee range of motion from 0 to 75 degrees**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent an anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction during which his femoral tunnel was placed too far anterior. The most likely sequelae of an overly-anterior femoral tunnel is loss of flexion in the setting of a normal pivot shift (Answer 1).

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures in the United States. With the appropriate surgical technique, ACLR is predictably able to restore knee stability and allow for high rates of return to sporting activity. The most common complications following ACLR occur secondary to tunnel malposition. On the femoral side, a vertical tunnel will lead to residual rotational instability, and too anterior of a tunnel will lead to a knee that is tight in flexion. On the tibial side, too anterior of a tunnel will lead to a knee that is tight in flexion and a graft that impinges on the roof of the notch. Too posterior a tibial tunnel will lead to a graft that impinges on the PCL. Illustration A demonstrates the bony landmarks for the native ACL footprint.

*Trojani et al. reported on the results of a multi-institution, 10-year study of outcomes following ACLR.* *293 patients were available for statistical analysis.* Overall, the most common mode of failure following ACLR was femoral tunnel malposition, present in 36% of patients who experienced treatment failure. The most common type of femoral malposition was a tunnel placed overly anterior.

*Battaglia and Miller reported on their technique for managing bony deficiency in the setting of revision ACLR.* As part of their review, the authors describe the most common causes of failure following ACLR. **They note** that 75% of failures following ACLR are due to a technical error and that 70% of these errors are due to tunnel malposition. **The authors highlight** the importance of recognizing this malposition during revision ACLR, and offer a surgical technique on how to optimize outcomes in the setting of failed primary ACLR.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: This represents normal knee range of motion and kinematics, which would be expected following appropriate tunnel placement.
Answer 3: This knee has signs of graft impingement, which would be expected with too anterior of a tibial tunnel. With graft impingement, the graft impinges on the roof of the intercondylar notch, and can lead to graft attrition and/or loss of extension.
Answers 4 and 5: A positive pivot shift occurs secondary to a femoral tunnel placed more vertically within the notch.
