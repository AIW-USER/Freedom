## Question

The 'Comma Sign' is a critical arthroscopic landmark for identifying the retracted superolateral corner of the subscapularis. Which two structures primarily constitute this tissue complex?

## Options

1. MGHL and SGHL
2. LHB and CHL
3. CHL and Supraspinatus
4. SGHL and CHL

## Correct option

**4. SGHL and CHL**

## Answer explanation

The 'comma' is formed by the junction of the superior glenohumeral ligament and the coracohumeral ligament as they pull away from the bicipital groove.

Arthroscopic subscapularis repair:

1. Classification (Lafosse)

The Lafosse classification is the gold standard for describing subscapularis tears. It dictates both the prognosis and the surgical approach (single vs. double row, need for extensive releases).

2. Crucial Anatomy: The "Comma Sign" and Biceps Pulley

The "comma sign," first described by Lo and Burkhart, is the absolute key to finding a retracted subscapularis tendon.

When the superior subscapularis tears off the lesser tuberosity, the medial sling of the biceps pulley—which consists of the superior glenohumeral ligament (SGHL) and the coracohumeral ligament (CHL)—tears with it.

Instead of floating away, this SGHL/CHL complex remains attached to the superolateral corner of the torn subscapularis tendon. As the tendon retracts medially and inferiorly, this capsuloligamentous tissue drapes over the superior border of the subscapularis, forming a distinct, comma-shaped arc.

Clinical Pearl: If you are lost looking for a retracted Type III or IV tear, find the comma tissue. If you trace the comma inferiorly, it will lead you directly to the superolateral corner of the subscapularis tendon.

Because the subscapularis forms the anterior wall of the bicipital groove, almost all significant subscapularis tears result in biceps pulley destruction and subsequent biceps subluxation or tearing. Routine biceps tenodesis (or tenotomy) is highly recommended during subscapularis repair to eliminate this pain generator and protect the repair.

3. The 3-Sided Release for Retracted Tears

A retracted subscapularis (Type III/IV) will not reach the footprint without tension. Surgeon must perform a sequential 3-sided release.

 - Anterior Release (Coracohumeral Ligament):

Working in the subcoracoid space (viewing from the posterior portal, working through the anterolateral portal), surgeon must dissect the adhesions between the anterior surface of the subscapularis and the base of the coracoid. Releasing the contracted CHL here is critical for lateral excursion.

 - Superior Release (Rotator Interval):

Carefully separate the superior border of the subscapularis from the supraspinatus and the contracted rotator interval tissue. Surgeon must separate the "comma tissue" from the base of the coracoid to free the superolateral corner.

 - Posterior/Intra-articular Release (Capsule & MGHL):

The subscapularis is intimately blended with the anterior capsule. To gain maximum excursion, one must perform a capsulotomy, separating the subscapularis tendon from the underlying anterior capsule and the middle glenohumeral ligament (MGHL) down to the level of the 6 o'clock position on the glenoid.

4. Surgical Technique: Repair Steps

Once mobilized, the repair must recreate the broad footprint on the lesser tuberosity.

1.Manage the Long Head of the Biceps: Usually via tenodesis.

Address the biceps first. If left intact, a subluxating biceps will mechanically rub against and potentially fail your subscapularis repair. Perform a tenodesis low in the groove or a tenotomy.

2.Prepare the Lesser Tuberosity: Use a 70-degree scope for best visualization.

Clear the soft tissue down to a bleeding bone bed on the lesser tuberosity. The 70-degree arthroscope from the posterior portal provides the optimal "look-down" view of the footprint.

3.Place Traction on the Tendon: Traction sutures aid visualization.

Pass a traction suture through the superolateral corner of the subscapularis (often right at the base of the comma tissue) and pull it out an accessory anterolateral portal to maintain tension and visibility.

4.Anchor Placement and Suture Passing: Start inferior and work superior.

Place the most inferior anchor first. Pass the sutures through the inferior portion of the tendon using a penetrating bird-beak or suture lasso. Working inferior to superior prevents the superior sutures from obscuring your view of the inferior footprint.

5.Tie the Knots (or secure knotless): Ensure arm is in internal rotation.

Tie the sutures (or impact knotless anchors) with the arm held in internal rotation to remove all tension from the repair site. For Type III or IV tears, a modified double-row technique (using the initial sutures to bridge over the tendon to a lateral row) provides superior biomechanical footprint compression.

Answer: 4
