## Question

## Options

1. Shorter duration of ICU stay
2. Longer duration of total hospital stay
3. Equivalent duration of total hospital stay
4. Superior long-term respiratory function
5. Equivalent risk of developing pneumonia

## Correct option

**1. Shorter duration of ICU stay**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with multiple segmental rib fractures resulting in a flail segment and subsequently underwent open reduction internal fixation. Rib plating has demonstrated superior early outcomes compared to nonoperative management, including a shorter duration of ICU stay.

Rib fractures are common in the trauma population, especially those sustaining blunt chest wall injuries. Flail chest injuries occur in roughly 10% of those with blunt thoracic trauma and have reported mortality rates of up to 33%. The paradoxical motion of the flail segment is a result of negative-pressure ventilation, and the altered motion mechanics of the chest wall in the setting of a flail segment can lead to debilitating pain during the normal ventilation cycle. This leads to decreased respiratory function and effort, which in turn leads to increased pulmonary complications including pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and potentially the need for intubation.

Rib fractures were historically treated with nonoperative management, including aggressive pulmonary toilet, pain control, and ventilatory support. However, recent data have strongly demonstrated the benefits of operative intervention compared to nonsurgical methods. Underutilized in previous years, there has been a trend toward open reduction and internal fixation, which are indicated for flail segments, chest wall deformities, and symptomatic malunion/nonunion.

*Marasco et al. conducted a 2013 prospective randomized controlled trial comparing outcomes between operative versus nonoperative treatment of traumatic flail chest injuries.* *46 patients were randomized into either operative or nonoperative interventions.* Resorbable copolymer plates and bicortical screws were used in the operative group, and current best practice mechanical ventilator management was used in the nonoperative group. Patients in the operative group had significantly shorter ICU stays and lesser requirements for noninvasive ventilation after extubation compared to the nonoperative group. Additionally, more patients in the nonoperative group required tracheostomies. No differences in spirometry at 3 months or quality of life at 6 months were noted between the two groups.

*Fowler et al. published a review article on the surgical management of rib fractures, reviewing the surgical techniques, indications, approaches, and implants.* Surgical fixation of chest wall injuries has been historically underutilized, despite growing evidence demonstrating its benefits. Indications for surgical rib fracture fixation at this time are considered to be relative, without any absolute indications for internal fixation at the time of publication. Relative indications include flail chest, chest wall deformity, and a symptomatic nonunion/malunion. There are multiple approaches and implants used in surgical fixation, however, **the authors reported** their successful experience with locking plates.

*Coughlin et al. performed a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials investigating the short and long-term clinical outcomes between operative and nonoperative management of rib fractures.* Surgical fixation of rib fractures had a clear clinical benefit over non-operative management, resulting in shorter ICU and total hospital stays, decreased need for subsequent intubation, and decreased risk of developing pneumonia. However, the study found no statistically significant difference in the overall mortality rate or any postoperative spirometry parameters, despite pneumonia being one of the primary etiologies behind mortality related to chest injury. **The authors believe** that fixation reduces the rate of mortality; however, there were insufficient numbers of patients currently enrolled in RCTs for this to be shown with statistical significance.

*Swart et al. performed a systematic literature review and meta-analysis on outcomes after operative fixation of flail chest injuries.* The results were then applied to a decision analysis model comparing the costs and outcomes of operative fixation versus nonoperative treatment. **The authors found** that operative treatment decreased mortality, pneumonia, and tracheotomy, as well as time in ICU and total hospital length of stay. Operative fixation was associated with higher costs than nonoperative treatment ($23,682 vs. $8629 per case, respectively) and superior outcomes (32.60 quality-adjusted life year (QALY) vs. 30.84 QALY), giving it an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $8577/QALY, well under the contemporary suggested threshold of $100,000/QALY.

Figure A is an AP radiograph demonstrating multiple rib fractures. Figure B is an intraoperative photograph of a rib plating procedure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2&3: Surgical fixation of flail segments is associated with a shorter duration of total hospital stay compared to nonoperative treatment.
Answer 4: Surgical fixation of flail segments is associated with equivalent long-term respiratory function compared to nonoperative treatment.
Answer 5: Surgical fixation of flail segments is associated with a significant decrease in the risk of pneumonia compared to nonoperative treatment.
