## Question

A 13 year-old-male presents with a transphyseal femoral neck fracture after he was injured in a motor vehicle collision. He subsequently underwent closed reduction and transphyseal screw fixation of his fracture 28 hours after presenting to the hospital, with anatomic alignment and reduction achieved. Which of the following patient clinical or treatment characteristics will have the most impact on the development of avascular necrosis of the femoral head?

## Options

1. Anatomic reduction of the fracture
2. Closed (rather than open) reduction of the fracture
3. Utilization of solely screw fixation
4. Time at which surgery was performed
5. Transphyseal screw fixation

## Correct option

**1. Anatomic reduction of the fracture**

## Answer explanation

Obtaining an anatomic fracture reduction is the most significant factor contributing to the risk of avascular necrosis following a pediatric femoral neck fracture (answer choice 1).

Pediatric proximal femur fractures are rare injuries that occur as a result of high-energy trauma or low-energy trauma in the setting of abnormal bone stock. Fractures are classified by the Delbet system, which groups fractures by their location: Type I - Transphyseal, Type II - Transcervical, Type III - Cervicotrochanteric/basicervical, Type IV - Intertrochanteric. Avascular necrosis is the most common complication of this injury and occurs as a result of injury to the superior reticular branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery. Patients who unfortunately develop this complication do not present until 2 to 12 months after injury and do so with an insidious onset of groin pain, ipsilateral knee pain, or reduced hip range of motion. Risk of avascular necrosis is highest in Delbet Type I fracture and decreases as the location of the fracture gets more distal (Type II > Type III > Type IV). Additional risk factors for the development of avascular necrosis include inadequate reduction and increasing age. Though it is thought that a relationship between surgical timing and avascular necrosis risk exists, the literature is conflicting at best. Patients who develop avascular necrosis may benefit from core decompression or free vascular fibular grafting.

*Lark et al. published a review on managing postoperative complications of pediatric hip fractures.* *They review the most common complication (avascular necrosis of the femoral head) as well as other less common complications (nonunion, coxa vara, and physeal closure).* *They specifically review the clinical presentation (groin pain 2-12 months after injury with reduced range of motion), the need for MRI for diagnosis, risk factors, and treatment strategies, which include core decompression, free vascular fibular grafting, proximal femoral osteotomy, and diphosphonate therapy, and hip reconstruction.* **They conclude** that anatomic reduction with a stable construct is the most important thing a clinician can do to prevent osteonecrosis.

*Yeranosian et al. published a systematic review of pediatric femoral neck fractures and assessed factors affecting outcomes.* **They reported** a cumulative 23% avascular necrosis rate, 10.8% nonunion rate, 18.5% coxa vara rate, 21.8% premature physeal closure rate, and a revision surgery rate of 19.2%. **They found** that a higher Delbet classification was largely associated with complication risk, and timely treatment is imperative to reduce the risk of avascular necrosis. **They concluded** that the majority of the literature about this injury is fairly limited due to the retrospective nature of existing studies, and future studies should aim to be prospective and multicenter in nature.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 2: Neither closed nor open reduction is associated with a greater risk of avascular necrosis as long as anatomical reduction is achieved.
Answer 3: Utilization of solely screw fixation is not a risk factor for avascular necrosis as long as anatomical reduction is achieved.
Answer 4: A clear relationship between delayed surgical timing and risk of developing avascular necrosis has yet to be definitively established, as current literature is conflicting.
Answer 5: Though transphyseal screw fixation is associated with a greater risk of growth arrest, it is not associated with an increased risk of avascular necrosis as long as an anatomic reduction is achieved.
