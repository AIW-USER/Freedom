## Question

Which is true about athletic Pubalgia (FNB Pattern Paper 2016)

## Options

1. More common in female athletes
2. Bilateral radiating pain along the inner side of the thigh
3. Main involved muscle is transversus abdominis
4. Fulcrum is the pubis symphysis.

## Correct option

**4. Fulcrum is the pubis symphysis.**

## Answer explanation

Athletic Pubalgia, also known as sports hernia, Gilmore’s groin, or core muscle injury (CMI). is defined as chronic lower abdominal and groin pain without the presence of a true hernia.

Pathology: The pubic symphysis acts as a fulcrum point from where this pathology origins. It is a common attachment site for the rectus abdominis and adductor longus. The confluence of the rectus abdominis, the conjoint tendon (formed by the internal oblique and transversus abdominis) and external oblique form the pubic aponeurosis, which is also confluent with the adductor longus and gracilis at the pubic symphysis. Adductor and rectus abdominis have opposing actions on the pubis symphysis. Imbalance between the opposing forces of the adductor longus and rectus abdominis at the pubic symphysis fulcrum point is thought to be implicated as the origin mechanism of athletic pubalgia.

Signs and symptoms: Chronic lower abdominal and groin pain in high-level athletes is the main presenting complaint. Athletes usually present with the complaint of exercise-related unilateral lower abdomen and anterior groin pain that may radiate to the perineum, inner thigh, and scrotum. Pain is mostly relieved with rest. However, even with resolution of symptoms after a period of rest, the pain often returns with return to play. Forces across the pelvis increase as muscle strength increases, which may explain why athletes are commonly affected. Activities that can lead to athletic pubalgia involve running, kicking, cutting and twisting movements, and explosive turns and changes in direction. It is more common in male athletes than females due to a narrower pelvis that cause greater shifts in force and less stability than the wider female pelvis

Treatment: Physical therapy should include core strengthening and stabilization, restoration of pelvic tilt and postural training. Pharmacological treatments include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories and oral steroid taper. Injections include selective corticosteroid or platelet-rich plasma injections into the rectus abdominis and/or adductor longus origin. If a patient does not improve in 3 months with conservative treatment then surgery is advised. Principles of operative management include reinforcement of the posterior wall and fixation of the rectus abdominis or conjoint tendon. Most also recommend adductor tenotomy when adductor pain and dysfunction is present.

Answer : D
