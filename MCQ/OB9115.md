## Options

1. < 15%
2. 16-30%
3. 31-50%
4. 51-70%
5. > 70%

## Correct option

**1. < 15%**

## Answer explanation

Mortality following an isolated pelvic ring injury has been found to be anywhere from 1-15%.

Isolated injuries of the pelvis or acetabulum account for approximately 3-8% of all fractures. The type of pelvic fracture is a predictor of associated injury, blood requirements, and overall mortality. Pelvic ring injuries associated with hemodynamic instability and shock can have mortality rates as high as 25-52%. Combined acetabular and pelvic ring injuries routinely have higher ISS and transfusion requirements.

*Halvorson et al. wrote a review article reporting on acetabular and pelvic ring injuries.* **They found** isolated pelvic ring injuries to have a mortality rate of 1.5-13%. **They also reported** that combined acetabular and pelvic ring injuries had mortality rates similar to isolated pelvic ring injuries (~13%).

*Manson et al. performed a retrospective review of 1,248 patients to assess the ability of the Young-Burgess classification to predict patient mortality.* Over the 7-year data collection period, **they found** an overall mortality rate of 9.1% for the entire study group. The ability of the Young-Burgess classification scheme to predict mortality only approached statistical significance at p=0.07.

Figure A is an AP radiograph showing a right-sided sacral fracture and superior and inferior rami fractures. Figures B and C are CT images demonstrating the right-sided sacral and rami fractures. Illustration A is an artist's rendition of the Young-Burgess Classification for pelvic ring injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-5: These percentages do not represent the mortality rates for isolated pelvic ring injuries.
