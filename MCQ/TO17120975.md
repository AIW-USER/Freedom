## Question

All of the following indices of Acromion morphology are measured on Outlet view radiographs Except

## Options

1. Acromion type
2. Acromion slope
3. Acromion index
4. Acromio-humeral interval

## Correct option

**3. Acromion index**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C

Acromion index is best measured on a true AP radiograph.

INDICES TO STUDY ACROMION MORPHOLOGY

The pathogenesis of Rotator cuff tears have long been thought to be related to Extrinsic impingement linked to the morphology of the acromion. While bursal-side cuff tears have invariably been associated with severe degenerative acromial changes, degenerative cuff lesions are aggravated by friction and a rubbing mechanism with the undersurface of the acromion, which leads to the development of a complete tear. So, there has always been keen interest of researchers to study acromial morphology.

Acromial morphology is usually assessed through five commonly used parameters in radiographs including the acromial type, acromial slope, acromial tilt, lateral acromial angle and acromial index.

Acromial type

The acromial type is classified according to Bigliani (1986) classification. Type I represents a flat, type II a curved, and type III a hooked undersurface of the acromion on outlet-view radiographs. Acromion type has indicated a greater likelihood of a cuff tear in those individuals with type-III acromion. This association is most likely because the shape of a type-III acromion is described as being hooked. A hooked acromion differs from the type-I and -II acromions due to its increased anterior and inferior extension of the acromial bony process. This increased anterior and inferior extension is thought to decrease the subacromial space, thereby increasing the likelihood of extrinsic degeneration of the rotator cuff tendons.

Acromial slope

The acromial slope (AS) is also measured on outlet-view radiographs. One line is drawn connecting the most anterior point of the inferior acromion and the midway point on the inferior acromion. Another line is drawn connecting the most posterior point of the inferior acromion with the same midway point. The angle (δ) formed by these 2 lines represented the AS. It is in the range of 23° to 45° in healthy adults. The acromial slope angle indicates curvature of the acromion in a posteroinferior-anterosuperior direction. A small acromial slope angle indicates a steep acromion, which would develop an ineffective contact point between the sidewall of the humeral head and the sidewall of the acromion which could lead to an increased incidence of rotator cuff tears.

Acromial tilt

The acromial tilt (AT) is again measured on outlet-view radiographs. One line is drawn connecting the most posterior point of the inferior acromion to the most anterior point of the inferior acromion. Another line is drawn connecting the same most posterior point of the inferior acromion to the inferior tip of the coracoid process. The resulting angle (β) represents the AT. A lower tilt angle is associated with a higher incidence of rotator cuff tears.

Fig: Acromial tilt angle

Lateral acromial angle

The lateral acromial angle (LAA): One line is drawn along the superior and inferior-most lateral points of the glenoid and represents the glenoid surface. Another line is drawn parallel to the acromion under surface. The angle between these 2 lines (a) represented the LAA. The normal lateral acromion angle is in the range of 64° to 99°. Higher angles are found in patients with an intact tendon and lower angles in patients with tendon discontinuity. All shoulders with an acromion angle less than 70° have a high likelihood of full-thickness rotator cuff tear.

Acromion index

The acromion index (AI) is measured on true anteroposterior radiographs. The distance from the glenoid plane to the acromion (GA) is divided by the distance from the glenoid plane to the lateral aspect of the humeral head (GH). The larger the extension of the acromion, the higher the AI. A larger acromial index is also associated with a greater likelihood of a cuff tear. This greater prevalence of cuff tears in persons with higher acromial index may be due to an alteration in the force vector of the deltoid muscle causing an increased superiorly directed force of the humeral head into the acromion and coracoacromial arch. The change in the deltoid force vector in turn might affect the ability of the stabilizing cuff muscles to compress and stabilize the humeral head in the glenoid with shoulder elevation. Due to a possible change in the length-tension relationships of the deltoid and cuff muscles in individuals with larger acromial indices, the muscles may not function optimally and may be unable to maintain the humeral head in a centred position. This, in turn, might decrease the subacromial space, leading to compression of the rotator cuff tendons and subsequent tears.

Fig: Acromion index

Acromio-humeral interval (AHI)

It is the distance between the inferior aspect of the acromion and the most superior point of the humeral head on outlet view radiograph. The AHI has been proven to be closely related to rotator cuff injury. The risk of rotator cuff injury is increased when the AHI is smaller due to decreased subacromial space.
