## Question

A 66-year-old recreational pickleball player falls directly onto his shoulder during a match and sustains an acute, traumatic tear of his rotator cuff. He presents to your clinic as a referral from the emergency room for discussion of surgical intervention. The patient's son is a medical student and encourages his father to ask about the possibility of using platelet-rich plasma (PRP) as an adjunct during the procedure, as he had recently seen this done during one of his rotations. Which of the following statements about PRP and its use during rotator cuff tear surgery is true?

## Options

1. Has 10-15 times the platelet concentration of normal plasma and creates a fibrin matrix to augment the repaired rotator cuff tendons
2. May provide no additional benefit when combined with surgical intervention for rotator cuff tears
3. Primarily results in the production of aggrecan and polypeptides that stablize the matrix surrounding repaired rotator cuff tendons
4. Requires a sample of whole blood to be sent to the lab with 24 hour processing time prior to surgery
5. The point-of-care production is regulated by the FDA in the same fashion as all human cellular and tissue-based products

## Correct option

**2. May provide no additional benefit when combined with surgical intervention for rotator cuff tears**

## Answer explanation

Though the use of PRP as an augment to rotator cuff repair appears safe, at this point, there remains a lack of evidence to support the use of PRP for the augmentation of rotator cuff repair.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) consists of a plasma sample from one's own blood that is enriched with autologous platelets, typically in the range of 3-5 times that of normal whole blood. It is prepared, typically in the clinic or operating room, by centrifugation of blood to separate a concentrated and platelet-rich plasma layer from a sample of whole blood. It has been used in numerous settings in the orthopaedic care model given its postulated effects on the inflammatory cascade and adjunct healing properties, but its true efficacy is controversial due to the small amount of high-level studies in the literature.

*Lamplot et al. published a practical guide for the current use of biologic therapies in sports medicine.* **The authors note** that, to date, clinical trials of PRP used in an effort to augment rotator cuff healing have reported equivocal results. **They conclude** that although its use appears safe (due to only 2 cases of reported infection and no additional complications), there remains a lack of evidence to support the use of PRP for the augmentation of rotator cuff repair.

*Fu et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the use of PRP and fibrin matrix to assist in the healing and repair of rotator cuff injuries.* *The review included 320 patients across 11 studies with the primary outcome being a functional score change from pre- to post-treatment and the secondary outcome being a visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score change from pre- to post-treatment.* **The authors concluded** that the results of their meta-analysis do not support the use of platelet-rich plasma/platelet-rich fibrin matrix in patients with rotator cuff injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: PRP has a platelet concentration of 3-5 times that of whole blood, not 10-15 times. Of note, concentrations of > 5x have been associated with inhibition of healing.
Answer 3: Though the exact mechanism by which PRP works is not known, its primary effects are postulated to be via the numerous growth factors released from platelets (i.e., TGF-B, VEGF, PDGF) that stimulate cytokine release and affect the inflammatory milieu, not through the production of aggrecans and structural polypeptides.
Answer 4: The process does require a sample of whole blood, but it can be processed in one of several proprietary in-office or in-OR devices that allow for centrifugation, processing, and use within minutes.
Answer 5: The point-of-care production of PRP is performed on machines that have received 510(k), or fast-tracked clearance. The administration of PRP is technically exempted from the same regulatory processes that govern other human cellular and tissue-based products under the FDA's 21 CFR 1271 of the Code of Regulations. Under these regulations, certain products including blood products such as PRP are exempt and therefore do not follow the FDA's traditional regulatory pathway that includes animal studies and clinical trials.
