## Question

An 18-month-old patient presents to clinic for follow up evaluation. The patient has a known history of infantile idiopathic scoliosis. X-rays are obtained at today’s visit. Which of the following measure on Figure A correctly corresponds to the rib vertebral angle difference (of Mehta)?

![[q218614-a-screen-shot-2023-02-28-at-7-35-20-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Angle Y + Angle X
2. Angle W – Angle Y
3. Angle Y + Angle W
4. Angle X – Angle Y
5. Angle X – Angle W

## Correct option

**4. Angle X – Angle Y**

## Answer explanation

The rib vertebral angle difference (RVAD) is calculated by first drawing a line perpendicular to the apical vertebral endplate. Then two lines are drawn from the mid-neck to the mid head of the corresponding ribs. The difference between the values of these rib vertebral angles on the concave and convex sides of the curve is the rib vertebral angle difference, or RVAD (Answer 4).

Infantile Idiopathic Scoliosis (IIS) is a coronal plane spinal deformity which most commonly presents in children ages 3 years or less. Treatment can be observation, bracing, or surgical management depending on the skeletal maturity of the patient, magnitude of deformity, and curve progression. When considering likelihood of deformity progression, there are three radiographic parameters to consider. First is curve magnitude, similar to other forms of scoliosis. Cobb angles >20° are associated with progression. For those curves LESS than 20°, the next thing to consider is rib phase, which is either Phase 1 or Phase 2. Rib phase is determined by looking at the apical vertebrae (the apex vertebrae in the curve), and then looking at the convex rib head position with respect to the apical vertebrae. If it overlaps, this is considered a Phase 2 rib and portends a high risk of progression. If it is a Phase 1 rib (i.e., the apical rib head on the convex side does NOT overlap the apical vertebral body on an anteroposterior radiograph), then the next consideration is the RVAD. An RVAD > 20° is linked to high rate of progression.

Karol provides a review on the current literature regarding early-onset scoliosis (EOS) (note that early-onset scoliosis is a broader category that includes scoliosis in children < 10 years, which includes infantile idiopathic scoliosis, juvenile idiopathic scoliosis, congenital scoliosis, neurogenic scoliosis, and syndromic scoliosis). They report treatment of EOS is customized to the particular disorder, and that while lack of treatment has been shown to lead to increased mortality, extensive early definitive fusion may lead to thoracic insufficiency. They conclude that delaying definitive surgery and the use of growing instrumentation may provide benefit in maintaining pulmonary health.

*Thorsness et al. provide a review on the nonsurgical management of EOS.* **They report** that the goal of both nonsurgical and surgical management is to control or correct the spinal curve to allow appropriate pulmonary development while delaying definitive fusion until an appropriate skeletal age. **They further discuss** that although more commonly used to delay surgery, serial cast correction using the Cotrel and Morel elongation-derotation-flexion technique may result in complete correction in patients with infantile idiopathic scoliosis and smaller curve magnitudes.

Incorrect Answers:
