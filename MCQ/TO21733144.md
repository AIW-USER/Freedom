Chapter: Arthroscopy MCQs — Meniscus Injuries

## Question

Snapping knee syndrome in discoid meniscus is seen in which type

## Options

1. Complete discoid meniscus
2. Incomplete
3. Wrisberg type
4. Ring shaped discoid variant

## Correct option

**3. Wrisberg type**

## Answer explanation

Answer: 3

EXPLANATION: -

Discoid meniscus is the most frequent congenital malformation of the menisci, and primarily affects the lateral meniscus.

The ultrastructure of the discoid meniscus is characterized by lower collagen density, with a disorganized network that predisposes it to breakage. Histologically, the discoid meniscus presents mucinous alterations, similar to those found in degenerative meniscus. Morphologically, the Discoid meniscus has increased thickness, enlarged surface, and decreased peripheral vascularity compared to the normal lateral meniscus.

CLASSIFICATION: Watanabe et al first proposed the classification in 1969 based on the arthroscopic appearance. They classified as complete, incomplete, and Wrisberg, according to the degree of coverage of the lateral tibial plateau and presence of normal posterior attachment.

![[TO21733144-1.jpg]]

Complete (type I) refers to a block-shaped meniscus covering the entire tibial plateau with normal posterior attachment.

Incomplete (type II) refers to a semilunar-shaped meniscus covering up to 80% of the tibial plateau with normal posterior attachment.

Wrisberg type (type III) is more normally shaped but is most unstable compared to the normal meniscus because it lacks the usual posterior attachments (coronary ligament or popliteomeniscal fascicles) and has the posterior meniscofemoral ligament (Wrisberg ligament) only. This is the reason behind snapping knee syndrome being very common in this category.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Discoid menisci are usually asymptomatic unless they are unstable or torn. Snapping (often a thud) and pain are common symptoms, with occasional limitations of extension (locking) if they become symptomatic. “Snapping knee” is basically seen with unstable discoid meniscus. It is usually related to the less common Wrisberg ligament type, but it can also be seen when a torn stable discoid meniscus becomes unstable due to spread of a tear that includes the posterior tibial attachment. An audible palpable or visible snap (meniscal thud) on terminal extension (10–20°) along with pain swelling and locking, in the absence of a traumatic cause, are the chief presentations of young children with this syndrome.
