## Question

A 22-year-old college outfielder has had months of shoulder pain that has not improved despite a modified throwing program and therapy. He is diagnosed with the SLAP tear and agrees to undergo surgery. What associated finding is shown in Figure A and what is the appropriate treatment?

![[q9037-a-supraglenoid-cyst.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Articular-sided supraspinatus tear, rotator cuff debridement followed by SLAP debridement
2. Buford complex, repair followed by SLAP debridement
3. Supraglenoid cyst, decompression with SLAP repair
4. Hill-Sachs lesion, remplissage followed by SLAP debridement
5. Reverse Hill-Sachs lesion, remplissage followed by SLAP repair

## Correct option

**3. Supraglenoid cyst, decompression with SLAP repair**

## Answer explanation

The structure seen is a supraglenoid cyst, one of many associated findings with SLAP and labral tears, while sublabral clefts and Buford complexes are also commonly seen. This patient is most likely to have a Type II SLAP tear with the biceps anchor detached, which should be repaired.

Paralabral cysts can range in symptoms, from being asymptomatic, to causing pain and weakness due mass effect on the surrounding structures. Most notable of these are supraglenoid cysts within either the suprascapular or spinoglenoid notches, which may impinge on the traversing suprascapular nerve and cause a palsy. If a cyst is within the suprascapular notch both the supra- and infraspinatus muscles will be involved, whereas a spinoglenoid cyst will affect only the infraspinatus muscle. Treatment of these involves conservative modalities and rehab if there is no structural mass on MRI. If there is other structural pathology, as in this case, concurrent decompression while repairing the labral tear has been described with good results. SLAP tears are repaired with suture anchors to the superior glenoid after a bed has been prepared through debridement.

*Piasecki et al. reviewed suprascapular neuropathies pathophysiology and management.* The suprascapular nerve passes on average 3cm medial to the supraglenoid tubercle and placing the nerve in a vulnerable position for compression. Careful history and physical exam in addition to electrodiagnostic studies and advanced imaging are warranted in the workup. An initial course of nonsurgical treatment is reasonable even in the setting of a structural cause of entrapment as cysts may be a transient and self-limiting phenomenon. If this has failed, open or arthroscopic decompression have shown good results, with arthroscopy having the added advantage of addressing intra-articular pathology without the morbidity.

*Weber et al. reviewed the ABOS Part II database to evaluate SLAP diagnosis and treatment rates.* **They found** significantly higher incidence of SLAP diagnosis and treatment compared to previously published prevalence rates. This highlights a possible trend of over diagnosis and treating normal labral variants as pathologic.

*Tuite et al. evaluated the reliability of MR arthrogram in detecting normal labral variants by reviewing a series MR arthrograms and comparing these to intra-operative findings.* **They found** sublabral clefts to be common and most areas of high signal were extension of normal labral variants and not labral tears. This highlights the difficulty of imaging to diagnose labral pathology.

Incorrect Answers: 1, 2, 4, and 5 are not seen in the image although they can be seen with type II SLAP tears, however these tears should be repaired rather than debrided.
Answer 1- Supraspinatus tears are seen in overhead throwing athletes and is associated with internal impingement
Answer 2- Buford complex is a cordlike- MGHL with an absent labrum at the anterosuperior region. Repairing it may significantly limit external rotation and be a source of pain
Answer 4- Hill-Sachs lesions are seen with anterior instability and typically large defects that engage the glenoid are addressed surgically
Answer 5- Reverse Hill-Sachs lesions are seen with posterior instability and surgery is reserved for defects over 20% of the articular surface
