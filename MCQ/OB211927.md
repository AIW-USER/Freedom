## Question

A 74-year-old-female presents to the emergency department after slipping in the bathroom. She denies loss of consciousness. On exam, she endorses posterior neck and right wrist pain. She has difficulty extending her right wrist but is otherwise neurologically intact. Imaging of the cervical spine is performed and pertinent views are shown in Figures A and B. How should her neck injury be addressed at this time?

## Options

1. Occipitocervical fusion
2. C1-C2 fusion
3. Halo orthosis
4. Hard cervical collar
5. Further assessment with flexion-extension radiographs

## Correct option

**4. Hard cervical collar**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a C1 (atlas) fracture without signs of radiographic instability; treatment at this time is with a hard cervical collar. The right wrist weakness should be further investigated with extremity radiographs, as this injury would not cause isolated right wrist extension weakness (C6).

Atlas fractures are most often caused by axial loading. The stability of isolated atlas fractures is determined by the integrity of the transverse atlantal ligament (TAL). Therefore, it is essential to assess these injuries with an AP open-mouth odontoid view or coronal CT to measure the lateral mass displacement (relative to the C2 lateral masses) or atlantodens interval. However, these measurements may be misleading due to a rebound effect after the initial injury. Fracture variants such as unilateral sagittal split lateral mass fractures and plough fractures may also be considered unstable. Stable injuries can be treated in a rigid cervical collar and unstable injuries should be treated C1-C2 arthrodesis.

*Bransford et al. reviewed upper cervical spine trauma.* **They state** that the management of atlas fractures is dictated by TAL integrity. If the lateral mass displacement is less than 7 mm, treatment in a hard cervical collar is indicated; >/7 mm warrants C1-C2 arthrodesis.

*Dvorak et al. performed a retrospective study looking at long-term health-related quality of life (HRQOL) outcomes in patients who sustained Jefferson-type fractures.* *They performed radiographic analysis and had patients fill out the Short Form (SF)-36 and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons/North American Spine Society (AAOS/NASS) outcome instruments at the final follow-up.* **They concluded** that patients do not return to their pre-injury health status and that those with other injuries and significant osseous lateral mass displacement (>/7 mm total) do worse.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Surgical management of this injury would consist of C1-C2 fusion, which would result in a 50% loss of cervical rotation; fusing to the occiput would result in loss of an additional 50% of cervical flexion-extension and should be avoided.
Answer 2: If the patient fails non-operative management, C1-C2 posterior spinal instrumented fusion can be considered.
Answer 3: Elderly patients should never be placed in a halo orthosis, as this is associated with a higher risk of mortality.
Answer 5: Flexion-extension radiographs are almost never indicated in an emergency setting and have the risk of propagating a neurological injury.
