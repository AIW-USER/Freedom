## Question

It's 8 pm on a Sunday evening when a 22-year-old male presents to the trauma bay after being involved in a motor vehicle accident. The patient's orthopaedic injuries are shown in Figures A and B. His fast exam is positive with a CT scan confirming a liver laceration requiring an emergent ablation with interventional radiology. His serum lactate is 6 mmol/L (normal < 1mmol/L) and base excess is -8.1 mmol/L (normal -2 to +2 mol/L). The trauma team states he could be ready for the OR in 2-3 hours to allow interventional radiology to perform their procedure first. Otherwise, the trauma team does not have any operative interventions planned. Which of the following is true regarding this injury?

## Options

1. This injury pattern should be staged with external fixator placement followed by definitive surgery in 1 week
2. Definitive open reduction internal fixation should be performed immediately after the interventional radiology procedure in 2-3 hours
3. Surgery should be delayed to allow time for adequate resuscitation
4. Single implant constructs to treat both fractures have shown definitive benefit over independent fracture fixation with separate implants
5. The patient should be treated with a long stem hip replacement bypassing the distal fracture by two cortical diameters

## Correct option

**3. Surgery should be delayed to allow time for adequate resuscitation**

## Answer explanation

This is a young patient with combined ipsilateral femoral neck and shaft fractures. Regarding femoral neck fractures, a shorter time to fixation has been correlated with a decreased risk of complications (i.e. nonunion), however, treatment of other life-threatening injuries and adequate resuscitation takes precedence (Answer 3).

Femoral shaft fractures in young patients are most often the result of high-energy traumatic events. It is imperative to obtain dedicated ipsilateral hip imaging (radiographs and CT scan), as there is an approximate 10% risk of there being a concomitant femoral neck fracture. There is a consensus that femoral neck fractures in young patients (<40 years) should undergo operative fixation to avoid the long-term complications of early-age arthroplasty. Further, multiple studies have shown that urgent timing of fixation (<24 hours) helps mitigate the risk of long-term complications of fixation such as nonunion and avascular necrosis. Therefore, these injuries should undergo operative fixation as soon as reasonably possible. Unfortunately, these high-energy injury patients often have other potentially life-threatening injuries (e.g. liver laceration) which preclude emergent (<6 hours) fixation. Once the patient is stabilized and adequately resuscitated, he should proceed to the operative theatre for definitive fixation. Resuscitation via the early appropriate care protocol recommends definitive fixation once initial acidosis has improved to at least one of the following: lactate ≤4.0 mmol/L, pH ≥7.25, or base excess between -5.5 mmol/L and +5.5 mmol/L. Many orthopaedic traumatologists will wait until the following morning to also allow for optimal staffing and dedicated orthopaedic radiology technicians present to facilitate the technically demanding procedure.

*Papakostidis and colleagues performed a systematic review and meta-analysis following the PRISMA guidelines to evaluate the effect of timing of internal fixation of intracapsular femoral neck fractures and late complications.* Based on their included studies (n=7), **they found** that delayed fixation (>24 hours) significantly increased the risk for nonunion but not the risk for avascular necrosis. **The authors concluded** that the fixation of intracapsular neck fractures should be done as early as possible to mitigate the risk of nonunion.

*Slobogean and colleagues performed a survey-based study evaluating surgeon preferences in the management of isolated femoral neck fractures in younger patients.* With 540 respondents, **they reported** that multiple cannulated screws are the preferred treatment for most surgeons treating nondisplaced fractures with an equal divide in preference between multiple screws and the sliding hip screw for displaced fractures. The majority of respondents choose to perform an open reduction with internal fixation between 8 and 24 hours. **The authors concluded** that the lack of surgeon consensus and the high rates of fracture complications associated with the fixation of young femoral neck fractures supports the need for definitive clinical trials to optimize patient-reported outcomes.

Jones and Walker provided a comprehensive review regarding the treatment of ipsilateral femoral neck and shaft fractures. The authors emphasize the importance of avoiding missing these injuries by obtaining appropriate pre- and intra-operative imaging. They further discuss the various single- versus independent fixation constructs. The authors conclude that while there is a lack of consensus regarding the optimal treatment construct, timely anatomic reduction of the femoral neck fracture and restoration of the length, alignment, and rotation of the femoral shaft fracture is of paramount importance.

Figure A shows AP radiographic imaging of the right-sided femoral shaft fracture. Figure B shows the same patient’s pelvic CT scan demonstrating a femoral neck fracture with posterior comminution. Illustrations A and B demonstrate this patient’s final operative fixation which was performed 12 hours after presentation to allow for adequate resuscitation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Isolated femoral shaft fractures in a polytrauma patient with other life-threatening injuries may be staged with external fixator placement followed by definitive surgery in 1 week. However, a concomitant ipsilateral femoral neck fracture warrants special consideration with urgency for early definitive fixation.
Answer 2: Delaying fixation and putting it on as the first case in the morning allows adequate time for resuscitation after the interventional radiology procedure. Further, it allows for appropriate perioperative staffing to be present to facilitate the technical demands of the procedure.
Answer 4: At this time, no study exists demonstrating superior outcomes using single- versus independent fixation implants for ipsilateral femoral shaft and neck fractures. Independent fracture fixation implants (i.e. Illustration B) have a slightly lower rate of mal-reduction; however, this has not been correlated with improved outcomes compared to single-construct implants (i.e. a single intramedullary reconstruction nail).
Answer 5: With the exception of highly comminuted fractures not amenable to fixation, performing total hip arthroplasty on a patient in their 20s is not advisable due to the long-term complications that would likely require multiple revision surgeries over their life span. This patient’s fracture should undergo operative fixation.
