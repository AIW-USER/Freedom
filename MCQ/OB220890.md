## Question

A 25-year-old professional dancer reports to the clinic with a four-month history of pain at the lateral aspect of her foot. She obtained radiographs at her primary physician's office, and she was provided a compression wrap while being told to cease dance practice for six weeks. Today, her radiograph is shown in Figure A. Which of the following risk factors increased her risk of fracture nonunion?

## Options

1. Fracture location
2. Use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
3. High dietary calcium intake
4. Early weightbearing
5. Female sex

## Correct option

**1. Fracture location**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a 5th metatarsal base fracture nonunion. If treated nonoperatively, fractures distal to the 4th-5th metatarsal articulation (Type III) have an increased risk of nonunion (Answer 1).

Fifth metatarsal base fractures are common foot injuries that can vary in severity and treatment approach, classified into three main types based on the location and mechanism of injury. Type 1 fractures, also known as pseudo-Jones or avulsion fractures, occur at the tuberosity of the base and are typically caused by an inversion injury with plantar flexion, where the lateral band of the plantar aponeurosis or the fibularis brevis tendon pulls off a bone fragment. Type 2 fractures, known as Jones fractures, are acute fractures at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction, just distal to the tuberosity, and are at high risk for nonunion due to limited blood supply. Type 3 fractures are proximal diaphyseal stress fractures that develop over time due to repetitive stress and also have a high risk of nonunion because of their location in a watershed area of blood supply. Treatment strategies for these fractures range from conservative management with immobilization for non-displaced fractures to surgical intervention with internal fixation, especially in cases with a high risk of nonunion or in high-demand athletes.

*Solan et al. provide an in-depth analysis of the treatment strategies for fractures of the 5th metatarsal, which are the most common foot fractures, particularly in active populations.* **They recommend** functional management without immobilization for Type 1 fractures, cast immobilization and follow-up for Type 2 fractures, and considering surgical fixation for Type 3 fractures, especially in athletes or patients with foot deformities due to the higher risk of nonunion. The authors prefer a cannulated 4.5-mm screw for fixation, avoiding bone grafts and washers to reduce the risk of symptomatic hardware. They also discuss the challenges of diagnosing and treating nonunions, with surgical fixation being the favored intervention for established nonunions or refractures.

*Chloros et al. review fifth metatarsal fractures.* *Nonoperative treatment is typically successful for Zone 1 fractures, while Zone 2 and 3 fractures, often termed "Jones" fractures, have more controversial treatment approaches and may require surgery.* Surgical treatment, usually anatomic reduction and intramedullary fixation with a screw, is considered the gold standard, particularly for high-performance athletes. However, complications such as hardware failure, refracture, and sural nerve injury can occur. The anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of the fifth metatarsal influence the healing potential and management of these fractures.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 2: NSAID use has not been shown to increase the risk of fracture nonunion in the fifth metatarsal base.
Answer 3: High dietary calcium intake has not been shown to increase the risk of fracture nonunion
Answer 4: Early weight-bearing has not been shown to increase the risk of nonunion in stable 5th metatarsal base fractures
Answer 5: Sex has not been shown to be an independent risk factor for 5th metatarsal base fracture nonunion
