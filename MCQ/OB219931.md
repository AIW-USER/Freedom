## Question

A 45-year-old recreational tennis player presents for 3 months of lateral elbow pain exacerbated by hitting backhands. She has been reading about several nonoperative modalities for lateral epicondylitis, particularly corticosteroid injections and platelet-rich plasma injections. What can you tell the patient about the comparative benefit of these treatment options?

## Options

1. Platelet-rich plasma decreases time to return to previous level of sport
2. Corticosteroid injections lead to faster long-term symptom resolution
3. There is no consistent difference between these treatment modalities and placebo
4. Platelet-rich plasma is most beneficial if it is leukocyte poor
5. Physical therapy is the only intervention that consistently out performs placebo and observation

## Correct option

**3. There is no consistent difference between these treatment modalities and placebo**

## Answer explanation

There has not been a nonoperative treatment option for lateral epicondylitis that has consistently performed better than observation/placebo alone (Answer 3). No matter the nonoperative treatment plan, recovery can take up to a year.

Contrary to common belief, lateral epicondylitis is primarily a degenerative disorder rather than an inflammatory one. The pathogenesis of this disorder involves repetitive microtrauma and failure of tendon healing, rather than true inflammatory etiologies. There are several nonoperative treatment options for lateral epicondylitis, including physical therapy, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections, corticosteroid injections, ultrasound, nitroglycerin patches, and bracing. While some of these interventions may relieve short-term pain, none have consistently outperformed placebo/observation alone. Regardless of the constellation of treatment options selected for the patient, recovery can take 6-12 months. Nonoperative management is successful in 80-90% of patients, and only 2-4% ultimately choose to undergo a surgical intervention.

*Cutts and colleagues wrote a review on the epidemiology and treatment outcomes of lateral epicondylitis.* **They report** that lateral epicondylitis is primarily a degenerative rather than an inflammatory process. They review several treatment options, **emphasizing** that current evidence lacks the consistency and duration of follow-up needed to demonstrate that any nonoperative treatment is superior to observation/placebo alone.

*Dragoo and colleagues reviewed the use of biologics for the elbow.* **They found** that the literature on PRP for lateral epicondylitis shows conflicting results for its efficacy. Several studies did not have a control group or describe their PRP formulation. **They recommend** the use of leukocyte-rich PRP based on randomized controlled trials, but additional studies are needed to provide more definitive data.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 4: PRP, particularly leukocyte-rich, has been a promising biologic option for lateral epicondylitis. However, there is conflicting evidence supporting its efficacy, significant heterogeneity between the PRP preparations studied, and a lack of long-term follow-up.
Answer 2: Corticosteroid injections can provide short-term pain relief. However, there is no convincing evidence they improve long-term outcomes, and recent studies have suggested they can also lead to skin complications
Answer 5: Physical therapy and bracing are typical first-line options for lateral epicondylitis, but there has not been convincing evidence that they accelerate symptom resolution.
