## Question

A 4-year-old girl comes to the ED with 3-day history of leg pain and progressive inability to bear weight following a trip and fall while playing with her dog. Physical exam is notable for erythema around the distal aspect of her left leg. Temperature is 39.0°C (102.2°F). Laboratory studies show a white blood cell count of 16,200/mm3, serum C-reactive protein level of 15.4 mg/L, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 52 mm/hr. Her X-rays are shown in Figure A and B. She is started on an empiric cephalosporin. She appears to improve on day 1, but becomes progressively more lethargic on days 2 and 3 without a significant change in her CRP. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO)
2. Brodie abscess
3. Subperiosteal abscess
4. Ewing sarcoma
5. Atypical organism

## Correct option

**3. Subperiosteal abscess**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with signs and symptoms suggestive of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis. Failure to improve following a course of IV antibiotics should prompt an investigation for other infectious sequelae, including septic arthritis or a subperiosteal abscess (answer 3).

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) may present subtle radiographic evidence of focal demineralization and sclerotic changes, although radiographs may appear normal as well. MRI is the gold standard imaging modality for initial diagnosis, and first-line treatment is primarily nonoperative with IV antibiotics. Failure to improve following a course of antibiotic therapy may suggest extension of the infection into an adjacent joint, classically described as those with intracapsular metaphyses (shoulder, elbow, hip, ankle); or into an adjacent subperiosteal space resulting in a subperiosteal abscess. To evaluate for these sequelae, MRI again is the gold standard imaging study, and management at this point involves surgical drainage and debridement.

*Stone et al. published a 2016 10-year retrospective review of their experience with pediatric tibial osteomyelitis.* *The authors reviewed the clinical records of 191 patients to compare their experience with that in the literature, and to determine any factors that may aid diagnosis and/or improve treatment outcomes.* Compared to the literature at the time of this study, which cites a high surgical intervention rate of 49.0-92.3% for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, in this study only 43.8% of patients required any form of surgery to the tibia, although 18.8% needed more than 1 intervention.

*Funk et al. published a 2017 review on the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of pediatric AHO.* *The authors cite the rate of adjacent septic arthritis and osteomyelitis as 33%.* Historically, the sequela-prone child was suspected of having contiguous osteomyelitis and septic arthritis due to persistent elevation of inflammatory markers after initial drainage of septic arthritis, and was considered to have a poor prognosis compared with those who had isolated septic arthritis.

*Quick et al. published a 2018 comparison study following the creation of a multidisciplinary guideline for the treatment of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) and septic arthritis (SA) in response to updates in evidence-based literature in the field and existing provider variability in treatment.* *The study compared cases before versus after the implementation of the guideline to determine the concrete effects the guideline had on the care of patients with AHO and SA.* Following implementation of the guideline, the group found a significant decrease in the length of intravenous antibiotic treatment, a decrease in peripherally inserted central catheter use, and an increase in bacterial identification.

Figures A and B are the radiographs of a skeletally immature patient with AHO. There is no periosteal reaction; however, there is diffuse soft tissue edema involving the distal thigh and proximal leg.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient is presenting with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis. CRMO does not typically present with infectious complications such as subperiosteal abscesses or septic arthritis.
Answer 2: Brodie abscesses are typically associated with infectious osteomyelitis of a more subacute or chronic duration (mean time to diagnosis 12 weeks), and have a more discernible radiographic appearance.
Answer 4: Ewing sarcoma can present similar to infectious etiologies, however, Ewing would also demonstrate radiographic findings of permeative bone destruction and identifiable periosteal reaction (i.e. "sunburst" or "onion-skin" patterns).
Answer 5: While an infection with an atypical organism is possible, the patient would not likely have experienced transient initial improvement.
