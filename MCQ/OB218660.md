## Question

A 9-year-old boy sustains the injury shown in Figure A after tripping and falling on the playground. He undergoes closed reduction and application of a long leg cast in the emergency department. Which of the following deformities would be most likely to develop?

![[q218660-a-tibfib.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Procurvatum
2. External rotation
3. Varus
4. Valgus
5. Neutral

## Correct option

**4. Valgus**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained midshaft tibial and fibular fractures. Fractures involving both bones have a propensity to drift into valgus.

Tibial shaft fractures are the third most common long bone fracture in children (second most common in non-accidental trauma), occurring at a mean age of 8 years and with a slight male predominance. Closed, length-stable fractures that fall within tolerances following closed reduction may be treated with the application of a long leg cast and early weight bearing. However, post-casting deformity may result from nonoperative treatment. Isolated tibial shaft fractures with intact fibulae tend to fall into varus, whereas fractures involving both the tibia and fibula tend to fall into valgus and procurvatum. The valgus deformity is due to both the pull of the lateral compartment musculature as well as the pull of the pes anserinus medially. The procurvatum deformity is due to the pull of the superficial and deep compartment musculature. Applying a gentle varus mold during the application of a long leg cast may be employed to combat the valgus deformity, although overzealous varus molds may lead to varus malunions and poor outcomes. Residual deformities seen on follow-up imaging may be corrected with cast wedging, provided that the fracture site retains some mobility. Failure of cast wedging may require cast removal and repeat manipulation, or operative intervention.

*Silva et al. published a 2012 randomized prospective controlled trial comparing the effectiveness of treating tibial shaft fractures through two methods of nonoperative treatment in long leg casts: non-weightbearing with the knee flexed at 60 degrees versus weight bearing as tolerated with the knee flexed at 10 degrees.* **The authors found** that the latter group demonstrated superior functional improvement and standing skills at 6 weeks compared to the non-weightbearing group. Additionally, early weight bearing did not lead to an increased rate of coronal or sagittal displacement, shortening, or need for cast wedging.

*Raducha et al. published a 2019 review of tibial shaft fractures in children and adolescents.* *The authors review the mechanism, clinical evaluation, imaging, and treatment modalities for these common fractures.* *The authors acknowledge the greater propensity for isolated tibial shaft fractures to fall into varus, and for those involving both the tibia and fibular shaft to fall into valgus.* Valgus and varus molds performed at the time of casting may serve to prevent or lessen these post-reduction deformities, respectively. Despite this, interval displacement of the fracture may require wedging of the cast in an office setting. If wedging is unsuccessful or the cast remains overly loose after the swelling subsides, then the patient should be scheduled for cast removal and re-reduction or operative intervention.

Figure A contains AP and lateral radiographs of a right-sided fracture involving both the tibia and fibula. Illustration A depicts the surrounding musculature contributing to deforming forces in injuries involving both intact and fractured fibulae.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 2, 3, and 5: The fracture involves both the tibial and fibular shafts and is most likely to develop a valgus deformity.
