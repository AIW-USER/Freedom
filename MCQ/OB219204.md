## Question

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) represents a computerized system for collecting patient-reported outcome measures that utilizes computer adaptive testing (CAT) software and item response theory (IRT) to reduce the floor and ceiling effects of data collection. When comparing the use of PROMIS to legacy patient-reported outcomes measures applied to foot and ankle surgery outcomes, which of the following statements is most true of PROMIS?

## Options

1. Demonstrates equivalent ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months post-operatively
2. PROMIS requires at least two years (24 months) of clinical follow-up data in order to accurately identify statistically significant differences in post-operative outcomes
3. Demonstrates inferior ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months post-operatively
4. PROMIS cannot be applied to foot and ankle surgery outcomes because the measure is not validated for use in lower extremity surgery
5. Demonstrates superior ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months post-operatively

## Correct option

**5. Demonstrates superior ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months post-operatively**

## Answer explanation

Comparative studies between PROMIS of the lower extremity and the FAOS (Foot and Ankle Outcome Score) and OMAS (Olerud-Molander Ankle Score) have demonstrated that the PROMIS lower extremity outcome measure demonstrates a superior ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months after ankle fracture surgery.

PROMIS is a computerized system for collecting patient-reported outcome measures and was devised in order to decrease survey burden, diminish floor and ceiling effects associated with the data collection process, and improve the ability to detect a minimal clinical significant difference (MCID) among included patients. The system does this by employing a computer adaptive testing (CAT) model that incorporates item response theory (IRT), which creates a dynamic test in which subsequent questions are selected based on a patient’s answer to preceding questions. Each individual’s test is thereby customized, limiting the number of questions administered to each patient and theoretically providing a more precise representation of a patient’s state. The use of PROMIS measures is currently validated in the settings of foot and ankle surgery, upper extremity surgery, and spine surgery.

*Gausden et al. reviewed 132 patients who underwent osteosynthesis for an unstable ankle fracture between 2013-2016 and completed legacy outcome scores (Foot and Ankle Outcome Score [FAOS], Olerud and Molander Ankle Score [OMAS], and Weber Score) along with the PROMIS Physical Function (PF) and PROMIS Lower Extremity (LE) CATs.* **The authors found** no observed floor or ceiling effects in either the PROMIS PF or the PROMIS LE scores, with clinically significant changes in the PROMIS LE score detected in patients between 6-month and 12-month postoperatively, whereas the reported OMAS score and Weber scores did not identify a clinically significant difference between patients during the same time frame. **They concluded** that the PROMIS LE was superior for evaluating patients following ankle fracture surgery in terms of lower floor and ceiling effects and greater ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in patients between time points following surgery.

*Brodke et al. reviewed PROMIS for orthopaedic outcomes measurement.* **The authors note** that many historically or commonly used patient-reported outcome measures in orthopaedics are narrow in scope and are limited by the burden associated with their administration, making them useful only for specific populations, with the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) being developed to overcome these limitations. **They also note** that PROMIS has been validated in patient populations with orthopaedic disorders of the foot and ankle, upper extremity, and spine and has demonstrated a marked improvement in measurement characteristics and reduced patient administrative burden, leading them to conclude that PROMIS Physical Function measures are useful for assessing orthopaedic outcomes and are superior to legacy measures in several key populations.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: PROMIS has been shown to demonstrate a superior ability to distinguish clinically significant changes in function at 6 and 12 months postoperatively, is validated for use in foot and ankle surgery, and does not require > 2 years of data to be applied in this setting.
