## Options

1. Popliteal artery
2. Medial geniculate arteries
3. Medial sural artery
4. Posterior tibial artery
5. Peroneal artery

## Correct option

**3. Medial sural artery**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a type IIIB open tibial shaft fracture necessitating soft tissue coverage. Given that the injury is in the proximal third of the tibia, a gastrocnemius rotational flap was selected. This pedicled flap is supplied by the medial sural artery (Answer 3).

Open tibial shaft fractures follow the typical Gustilo-Anderson grading system for open fractures; type IIIB fractures involve soft tissue defects that are not amenable to primary closure and require a separate soft tissue coverage procedure. Multiple options exist for various regions of the leg, but typically, proximal 1/3 defects are best treated with a rotational gastrocnemius flap, middle 1/3 defects with a rotational soleus flap, and distal 1/3 defects with a free flap. For gastrocnemius flaps, either head may be used, but the medial muscle belly is usually larger and more mobile, and does not carry the same risk of iatrogenic peroneal nerve injury as the lateral gastrocnemius. The blood supply to the medial gastrocnemius is the medial sural artery, which is a branch of the popliteal artery.

*Tornetta et al. performed a 1993 randomized, prospective study comparing early outcomes following external fixation with non-reamed locked nails in type IIIB open tibial fractures.* The group found that nailed fractures were consistently easier to manage, especially in terms of soft-tissue procedures and bone grafting. Nailing was preferred by patients over external fixation and does not require the same high level of compliance as external fixation. The only factors against nailing were a longer operating time and a greater need for fluoroscopy. The group concluded that locked non-reamed nailing is the treatment of choice for type IIIB open tibial fractures.

*Qiu et al. performed a 2018 systematic review of the current literature regarding the timing of soft tissue coverage for open extremity injuries.* Marko Godina previously suggested in 1986 that soft tissue coverage of traumatic extremity wounds within 72 h of injury optimizes outcomes. **The authors found** that an infection rate was 7.7% if coverage was performed <72 h after injury and 11.6% if >72 h after injury, supporting Godina’s original recommendation.

*Khundkar published a 2019 review of soft tissue coverage options following lower extremity trauma.* For defects around the upper third of the tibia, a pedicled gastrocnemius muscle flap is usually the flap of choice. The medial gastrocnemius muscle is often bigger and has a longer reach than the lateral, and care must be taken to preserve its vascular pedicle, the sural artery (arising from the popliteal artery at or just above the joint line) which enters the muscle proximally.

Figure A is a clinical photograph and radiographs of a Type IIIB open tibial shaft fracture. Figure B consists of clinical photographs of a medial gastrocnemius rotational flap. Illustration A is a depiction of the sural artery to a pedicled medial gastrocnemius flap.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While the popliteal artery does branch off into the sural arteries, it does not directly supply the medial gastrocnemius.
Answer 2: The medial geniculate arteries are not the dominant blood supply to the medial gastrocnemius. Together with the lateral geniculate arteries, they supply the knee joint and patella.
Answer 4: The posterior tibial artery arises distal to the branch point of the sural artery and is not the dominant blood supply to the medial gastrocnemius.
Answer 5: The peroneal artery branches off the tibioperoneal trunk, which is a continuation of the popliteal artery distal to the branch point of the anterior tibial artery. It is not the dominant blood supply to the medial gastrocnemius.
