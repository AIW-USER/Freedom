## Question

A 37-year-old female presents to the emergency room one month after her right thigh was rolled between the side of a forklift machine and a wall when a coworker was backing up on the factory floor. She was able to ambulate immediately after the event, however, has noted continued dull pain and swelling in her thigh. She denies any fevers, chills, increasing erythema, or drainage. On exam, she does not have any overlying skin changes but has palpable, deep fluctuance with presenting radiographs shown in Figure A. A CT scan with contrast is obtained in the emergency department shown in Figures B and C. Which of the following is the next best step in management?

## Options

1. MRI of the entire femur and pelvis followed by a core needle biopsy
2. Obtain a CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast
3. Admit patient for broad-spectrum antibiotics with trending inflammatory markers
4. Percutaneous needle decompression utilizing a large bore needle
5. Perform operative open irrigation and debridement with the placement of a drain

## Correct option

**5. Perform operative open irrigation and debridement with the placement of a drain**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a shearing force injury to the soft tissue of her thigh with a large fluid collection between the subdermal adipose tissue and the respective underlying fascia. These findings are consistent with a Morel-Lavallée lesion which, given its exceedingly large size and continued symptomatology for this patient, should undergo open irrigation and debridement with long-term drain placement to prevent recurrence.

The Morel-Lavallée lesion is a closed traumatic soft tissue degloving injury most encountered about the thigh, hip, and pelvic regions. It is caused by a shearing force with consequential separation of the subdermal adipose tissue from its underlying fascial tissue. Thereby, this results in a large dead space formation between the two layers that fill with hemolymphatic serosanguinous fluid and fatty debris. If left untreated, local inflammation can result in the formation of a pseudocapsule (i.e. pseudocyst) as the body attempts to sequester the fluid-filled space. In such settings, continued dull pain and contour deformities of the extremity are common. Smaller, asymptomatic lesions may be treated with nonoperative treatments including compression wrapping. Larger, symptomatic lesions and chronic lesions are best served with operative irrigation and debridement. Very importantly, it is best practice to utilize a limited approach (Illustration A) to avoid overlying skin necrosis, given its subdermal vascular plexus has already been compromised from the injury. A long-term drain should be left in place to prevent re-accumulation.

*Scolaro and colleagues provided a comprehensive review regarding the diagnosis and treatment of Morel-Lavallée lesions.* In general, **the authors recommend** nonsurgical management if the lesion is remote from a fracture, is not fluctuant on palpation, and is not painful or bothersome to the patient. If the lesion is notably large, resides in the area of an anticipated surgical incision, lies adjacent to an open wound, or is persistently symptomatic, **they recommend** surgical debridement. **They conclude** by highlighting the unacceptably high recurrence rates with needle decompression and advocate for limited open debridement and placement of a long-term drain when surgical management is pursued.

*Powers and colleagues provided a case report and literature review on Morel-Lavallée lesions.* **They note** that these closed injuries may lack overlying skin changes or take weeks to develop which collectively contribute to 1/3 of these lesions being initially missed. **The authors also highlight** the benefit of obtaining an MRI to determine if pseudocapsule formation has occurred, which they purport is best treated with open decompression and pseudocapsule debridement. **They conclude** that the patient’s history, physical examination, and pseudocapsular formation on MRI may be utilized in conjunction to establish a diagnosis of these potentially missed injuries (i.e. avoid the misdiagnosis of neoplasm).

Figure A demonstrates AP radiographic imaging of this patient’s proximal femur showing no apparent fractures. Figures B and C are images from the contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrating a notably large fluid collection between the subdermal adipose tissue and the fascia over the trochanteric region extending distally into the thigh. Illustration A demonstrates the limited, two-incision open approach to the treatment of Morel-Lavallée lesions. Two interconnecting incisions are made at each end of the underlying lesion with a yankauer utilized for suction/ irrigation (blue dotted line) and a canal brush employed for debridement. A drain should be placed thereafter to prevent fluid re-accumulation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Obtaining an MRI of the entire femur and pelvis followed by a core needle biopsy would be an appropriate workup in the setting of a potential soft tissue sarcoma.
Answer 2: Obtaining a CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast is a part of the appropriate workup in the setting of a destructive bone lesion in an adult patient (aged >40 years). Primary differential diagnoses would include metastases (by far the most common), myeloma, or lymphoma.
Answer 3: Admitting the patient for broad-spectrum antibiotics with trending of their inflammatory markers would be appropriate in the setting of an abscess. While Morel-Lavallée lesions have a potential risk for becoming infected, this patient does not demonstrate any concerns for such in the question stem.
Answer 4: Percutaneous needle decompression utilizing a large bore needle has an unacceptably high rate of recurrence and therefore not recommended as a treatment for Morel-Lavallée lesions.
