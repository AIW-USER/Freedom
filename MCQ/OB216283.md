## Question

A 39-year-old male presents as a trauma activation after a motor vehicle collision. He is found to have a posterior pelvic ring injury on pelvic radiographs and computed tomography (CT). The decision is made to perform percutaneous sacro-iliac (SI) screw placement. Which of the following is true regarding this technique?

## Options

1. SI joint disruptions require a horizontal and transverse oriented screw
2. Sacral fractures require a caudal-to-cranial and posterior-to-anterior screw trajectory
3. The iliac cortical density accurately represents the alar slope in patients with sacral dysmorphism
4. Placement of the guide wire posterior to the iliac cortical density on lateral fluoroscopic views ensures safe screw placement in normal sacra
5. The inferior gluteal artery is most at risk with percutaneous SI screw placement

## Correct option

**4. Placement of the guide wire posterior to the iliac cortical density on lateral fluoroscopic views ensures safe screw placement in normal sacra**

## Answer explanation

The iliac cortical density on lateral sacral fluoroscopic views represents the sacral slope in non-dysmorphic sacra. Screw placement posterior to this ensures a safe screw trajectory that does not risk injury to the L5 nerve root.

Posterior pelvic ring injuries can be adequately stabilized with percutaneous SI screws. This technique has multiple advantages over open reduction and plating including shorter operative time, decreased blood loss, lower wound complications, and lower infection rates. Proper screw placement is highly dependent on high-quality imaging of the pelvis intraoperatively. The iliac cortical density (ICD) is visualized on lateral fluoroscopic views of the sacrum. This landmark represents the sacral alar slope, which the L5 nerve root is draped over. Placement of the screw posterior to this landmark ensures safe screw placement.

*Routt et al. performed a cadaveric study and retrospective case series of percutaneous sacroiliac screw placement.* **They reported** anatomic variations in the sacral ala are common and can be easily identified with pelvic outlet and lateral sacral radiographs. **They concluded** the lateral sacral image details the sacral alar slope for appropriate SI screw placement.

*Iorio et al. reviewed the indications, preoperative planning, and techniques of percutaneous SI screw placement.* **They stated** the iliac cortical density parallels the anterior border of the SI joint and represents the alar slope. **They concluded** near anatomic alignment of the posterior pelvic ring can improve anterior pelvic ring reduction and can improve long-term function.

Incorrect answers
Answer 1: SI joint disruption injuries require a caudal-to-cranial and posterior-to-anterior screw trajectory to provide adequate compression and reduction. A horizontal-transverse oriented screw is preferred for sacral fractures.
Answer 2: Sacral fractures require a transverse and horizontally oriented screw trajectory to provide compression across the fracture site. In some cases, a positional screw may be chosen to avoid iatrogenic injury to the exiting nerve roots from over-compressing the fracture. A caudal-to-cranial and posterior-to-anterior oriented screw is preferred for SI joint disruptions.
Answer 3: Patients with sacral dysmorphism have abnormal anatomy of the upper sacral vertebrae. The ICD is nonlinear with the sacral slope and can be posterior and caudal and may lead to incorrect screw placement if not recognized.
Answer 5: The deep superior branch of the superior gluteal artery is the vascular structure most at risk with SI screw placement.
