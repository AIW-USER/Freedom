## Question

A three-month-old female is seen in the orthopedic offices two weeks after being placed in a Pavlik harness for developmental dysplasia of bilateral hips. The mother was concerned that her daughter is moving her legs less than usual. On exam, the child is unable to extend her knees spontaneously or in response to gentle stimulation of her feet. Pathology of which anatomic structure labeled A-E in Figure A would be responsible for the physical exam findings noted above?

![[q5846-a-pelvis-ct.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. A
2. B
3. C
4. D
5. E

## Correct option

**2. B**

## Answer explanation

The patient is experiencing a transient femoral nerve palsy. The structure labeled B in Figure A is pointing to the femoral nerve.

Children less than 6 months of age with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) and an Ortolani positive physical exam finding are usually initially treated with a Pavlik harness. This harness has been designed to aide in keeping a reducible hip located within the acetabulum. One of the complications of the Pavlik harness is placing the hip in excessive flexion leading to a transient femoral nerve palsy. This nerve palsy can present with quadriceps weakness and the inability to extend the knee.

*Murnaghan et al.* **note** that a femoral nerve palsy during Pavlik harness use for DDH is strongly predictive of treatment failure. They also mention that when a femoral nerve palsy is noted, there is an increased likelihood of the need for a closed or open reduction of the pathologic hip(s).

*Mubarak et al. reported transient femoral nerve palsy suspected to be secondary to excessive hip flexion in a Pavlik harness.* **They recommend** that hip flexion greater than 120 degrees should only be used if necessary to achieve reduction. Once reduction is able to be obtained, then the flexion should be reduced to approximately 90 degrees or less.
