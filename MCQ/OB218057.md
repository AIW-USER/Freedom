## Question

A 40-year-old carpenter presents to the clinic with chronic thumb pain, particularly when grasping objects such as his hammer at work. He states that he works through the pain but can tell he is developing some limitations over the past year. On exam, the patient is found to have painful motion of his thumb metacarpophalangeal joint, painful grind test, and laxity with an ulnar-to-radial applied force at the joint line in full flexion and extension. He has not seen a provider for his hand previously. His radiographs today are shown in Figure A. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Thumb spica brace for 4-6 weeks with topical and oral NSAIDs
2. Arthroscopic evaluation with synovectomy and osteophyte removal
3. Radial collateral ligament reconstruction
4. Open debridement with implantation of silastic metacarpophalangeal joint arthroplasty
5. Joint preparation and metacarpophalangeal joint fusion using headless compression screws

## Correct option

**1. Thumb spica brace for 4-6 weeks with topical and oral NSAIDs**

## Answer explanation

This patient has chronic thumb radial collateral ligament insufficiency with resultant clinical and radiographic findings of post-traumatic thumb metacarpophalangeal joint (MCPJ) arthritis. He should initially be managed with conservative treatment (Answer 1).

Thumb MCPJ arthritis can occur in the setting of primary osteoarthritis, post-traumatic arthritis (as seen in this patient), or as a musculoskeletal manifestation of rheumatoid arthritis. It is far less common as an isolated condition (except in laborers) and is more often seen in conjunction with basilar (carpometacarpal) thumb arthritis or interphalangeal thumb arthritis. Patients often complain of pain and weakness, particularly when performing grasping activities (i.e. holding a hammer or front wheel walker). As with any form of arthritis, initial management should include a trial of NSAIDs (both topical and/or oral), activity modifications, occupational therapy, and potential corticosteroid injection. Thumb spica bracing for 4-6 weeks has been shown to be of some clinical benefit for these patients when combined with topical and oral NSAIDs. If these patients fail conservative management, they are indicated to undergo operative treatment. Fusion is the hallmark procedure that relieves pain, restores joint stability, and maintains the thumb’s ability to perform opposition. There are a variety of techniques and implants to perform thumb MCPJ fusion, however, direct comparison studies are lacking. Unfortunately, many patients who undergo fusion eventually develop adjacent joint disease (interphalangeal or carpometacarpal), which should be discussed with patients as a potential long-term disadvantage of fusion. For this reason, arthroplasty procedures using silastic or metal-on-poly implants have gained some popularity, as they can be converted to fusion should they fail. However, arthroplasty implants are generally contraindicated in the ligamentous deficient thumb and in laborers (as seen in this patient). Patients should be counseled on the risks and benefits of each procedure to participate in the shared decision-making procedure for optimal expectations and treatment outcomes.

Berger and Meals provided a current concept review regarding the management of arthritis in the three thumb joints. They primarily focus on the management of basilar thumb arthritis and lack of optimal surgical technique consensus. The authors also address that thumb MCPJ arthritis is most reliably treated with fusion in approximately 5-20 degrees of flexion and 15 degrees of internal rotation (pronation). They conclude that fusion procedures are the primary surgical treatment for both MCPJ and interphalangeal thumb joint arthritis.

*Earp and colleagues provided a comprehensive review specific to thumb MCPJ arthritis.* *They mention that while fusion remains the benchmark procedure for degenerative disease, arthroplasty should be considered in cases that involve arthritis of the entire thumb axis (multiple thumb joints).* *They cite arthroplasty’s ability to reduce pain, preserve motion, and limit the progression of adjacent joint disease.* However, **they note** limitations in large comparative studies to fusion. **The authors conclude** that each patient should undergo an initial trial of nonsurgical management followed by consideration for operative procedures.

Figure A demonstrates this patient's advanced thumb MCPJ arthritis with some ulnar subluxation of his proximal phalanx secondary to prior radial collateral ligament injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: There is limited evidence of its use, however, arthroscopic evaluation with synovectomy has been a described treatment option in early thumb MCPJ osteoarthritis. The primary indications are to obtain tissue biopsy for the diagnosis of inflammatory arthropathy and to improve the range of motion.
Answer 3: Radial collateral ligament reconstruction could help restore ligamentous stability, however, would not address the progressive osteoarthritis demonstrated on radiographs. If conservative management fails, he will most likely benefit from a fusion procedure.
Answer 4: Silastic and metal-on-poly metacarpophalangeal joint arthroplasty implants serve to (1) preserve joint motion and (2) prevent adjacent joint degeneration seen after thumb MCPJ fusions. Importantly, these implants rely on the ligamentous stability of the thumb, and this patient appears to have a chronic radial collateral ligament deficiency. Should he fail conservative management, he would be best served with a fusion procedure.
Answer 5: Given this patient’s ligamentous laxity and joint subluxation on radiographic evaluation, he would be best served with a fusion if he fails conservative management.
