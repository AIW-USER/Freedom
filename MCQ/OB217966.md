## Question

A 46-year-old male presents with left shoulder weakness and pain radiating to the anterior aspect of his shoulder. He states it started after a car accident over a year ago where he dislocated his shoulder. A formal reduction in the emergency department was required. He has undergone a trial of physical therapy, NSAIDS, and activity modifications, however, his symptoms are recalcitrant to conservative measures. His MRI images are shown in Figure A. ## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a subacute left-sided subscapularis rotator cuff tear after a motor vehicle accident one year ago. The upper and lower subscapular nerves (Label C, as shown in Illustration A) provide motor innervation to the subscapularis musculature.

Rotator cuff tears may result secondary to degenerative or traumatic etiologies. Subscapularis rotator cuff tears are generally considered uncommon, however, they present with anterior shoulder pain and bicep tendon subluxation. Provocative physical examination findings include weakness in the belly press test or lift-off test. While partial tears may be managed with an initial trial of physical therapy, NSAIDS, and activity modifications, full-thickness tears in the young-age population should be treated with an acute repair. Chronic, irreparable injuries may require latissimus or pectoralis major tendon transfer in younger patients or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty in elderly cohorts.

*Mall and colleagues performed a systematic review of the outcomes of acute rotator cuff repairs.* **They found** that traumatic rotator cuff tears were more likely to occur in younger, male patients who suffer trauma to an abducted, externally rotated arm. The tears were often considered large and involved the subscapularis tendon. **They conclude** that while more evidence is needed, acute repairs appear to have some clinical benefit over trials of nonoperative care.

*Lee and colleagues provided an updated, comprehensive review regarding subscapular tendon tears.* *They reviewed the different treatment options and discussed a trend toward arthroscopic repairs over the historic open repair techniques.* *They also reviewed newer surgical options including pectoralis tendon transfer and anterior capsule reconstruction.* **They conclude** that there are numerous surgical treatment options that should be chosen based on individual patient functional demands.

Figure A shows coronal and axial T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging of a left shoulder with a subscapularis rotator cuff tear and bicep tendon subluxation (right-sided image). Figure B is an unlabeled pictorial image of the brachial plexus that is labeled in Illustration A.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Label A is referring to the axillary nerve, which supplies motor innervation to the teres minor musculature of the rotator cuff. It additionally supplies motor innervation to the deltoid musculature.
Answer 2: Label B is referring to the lateral pectoral nerve, which supplies motor innervation primarily to the pectoralis major musculature with some additional contribution to the pectoralis minor musculature.
Answer 4: Label D is referring to the suprascapular nerve, which supplies motor innervation to the supraspinatus and infraspinatus musculature of the rotator cuff.
Answer 5: Label E is referring to the long thoracic nerve, which innervates the serratus anterior musculature.
