## Question

A 45-year-old Veterans Administration beneficiary presents with a painful elbow and arm. He states he was picking up a television three months ago when he felt a pop in his left arm. Magnetic resonance imaging in Figure A confirms his diagnosis and he undergoes single-incision reconstruction with semitendinosus allograft augmentation. At his three-month follow-up visit, he continues to complain of burning pain in the radial aspect of his forearm. Which of the following would be the most likely sequelae of his current state?

![[q217919-a-figure-a-biceps-tear.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Gradual resolution
2. Mild improvement of symptoms with residual sensory deficit
3. Eventual bicep tendon re-rupture
4. No change in symptoms resulting in need for neuroma excision alone
5. Worsening of symptoms requiring neuroma excision and cable autografting

## Correct option

**1. Gradual resolution**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent a biceps tendon reconstruction through a single-incision approach and is now experiencing a neuropraxia injury to his lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN). The vast majority of these cases will resolve spontaneously without the need for further intervention.

 The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN) is the terminal sensory branch of the musculocutaneous nerve. It is more commonly injured in single-incision than the double-incision approach to distal biceps repair/reconstruction. This is likely secondary to the increased traction force required for the single-incision approach. Some degree of traction neuropraxia is common and will typically resolve by six months postoperative; however, symptoms may take up to two years to resolve. Continued observation would be most appropriate at this time, with the expected eventual resolution of symptoms.

*Schmidt et al. reviewed the anatomy of the biceps tendon and the anatomic considerations in primary and chronic tendon tears.* *They stress the importance of repairing the biceps tendon to the more ulnar-sided aspect of its anatomic footprint to achieve maximal biomechanical function.* **They conclude** that inaccurate placement of the tendon on the proximal radius following surgical repair has been shown to result in profound alterations in the biceps’ native function.

*Grewal et al. performed a randomized control to compare the functional outcomes and complication rates between single-incision biceps tendon repair (using two suture anchors) and two-incision biceps tendon repair (using trans osseous drill holes).* **They found** no difference in functional outcome scores between the two groups. However, there was a significantly increased risk for transient neuropraxia of the LABCN in the single-incision group. The vast majority of these LABCN neuropraxia cases were resolved (17/19 patients) at two-year follow-up. **They conclude** that both techniques described are highly effective and the surgical approach should be at the discretion of the surgeon.

*Ford et al. performed a retrospective comparative treatment study in over 900 patients treated operatively for distal biceps tendon ruptures.* **They report** a 21.5% minor complication rate (LABCN neuropraxia), 7.5% major complication rate (PIN palsy, synostosis), and 4.5% reoperation rate overall. The LABCN palsy was specifically seen in 13% of patients postoperatively and these patients did not require additional surgery. **They conclude** by confirming nerve injuries were more common in the single-incision group while heterotopic ossification was more common in the double-incision group.

Figure A displays the sagittal (left) and coronal (right) images from this patient's MRI confirming a distal biceps tendon rupture with proximal retraction to the mid-diaphyseal region of the humerus.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Some patients have postoperative LABCN neuropraxia symptoms that do not resolve. However, thankfully these are the minority of patients.
Answer 3: The patient is experiencing LABCN neuropraxia. This has not been associated with risk for bicep tendon re-rupture and overall both complications occur in the minority of patients.
Answers 4&5: Continuation of symptoms with neuroma formation would be unlikely to occur. However, in rare cases, traditional neuroma management could be considered. This includes neuroma excision with tension-free repair, traction resection, or transposition.
