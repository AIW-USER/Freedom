## Question

A 3-week-old is brought into the emergency room after her mom said she tripped on a dropped dish towel and fell onto her new baby's leg four days ago. Since that time, she notes that she hasn't been moving it as much, the knee is red and warmer than the other side, and she cries every time she touches or bathes the extremity. ## Options

1. Admit to the hospital and consult social services
2. Application of a Pavlik harness and routine discharge
3. Consult pediatrics and admit for workup of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia and congenital syphilis
4. Spica casting and admission to the hospital for observation and cast care teaching
5. Workup for infection to include septic knee and osteomyelitis given radiographic findings

## Correct option

**1. Admit to the hospital and consult social services**

## Answer explanation

The infant has radiographic evidence of a distal femur metaphyseal corner fracture, which is considered pathognomonic for child abuse, making admission to the hospital with a consult to social services and appropriate fracture care the best next steps in her treatment (Answer 1).

Although the most common orthopaedic presenting complaint for non-accidental trauma is a single long bone fracture, metaphyseal corner fractures are a finding considered pathognomonic for child abuse. Importantly, abuse must be considered if the child is < 3 years old, and especially if present before walking age. Transverse fractures are more predictive of non-accidental trauma compared to spiral or oblique fractures. Other medical conditions and comorbidities must be considered when non-accidental trauma has been ruled out, such as spondylometaphyseal dysplasia and congenital syphilis, though these occur much less commonly.

*Zhao et al. performed a national inpatient database study looking at the predictors for non-accidental trauma (NAT) in a child with a fracture.* **The authors found** that of the 57,183 pediatric fracture cases, 881 (1.54%) had a concurrent diagnosis of NAT. Based on multivariate analysis, younger age, black race, intracranial injury, concomitant rib fracture, and burns were positive predictors of NAT in a child with a fracture. **They concluded** by creating an estimated probability chart that can be used in the clinical workup of a child with a fracture and possible NAT.

*Jayakumar et al. reviewed the orthopaedic aspects of pediatric non-accidental injury (NAI).* **The authors note** that no fracture in isolation is pathognomonic of NAI, but specific abuse-related injuries include multiple fractures, particularly at various stages of healing, metaphyseal corner and bucket-handle fractures and fractures of ribs. **They concluded** that Orthopaedic surgeons must systematically assess, recognize, and act on the indicators for NAI in conjunction with the pediatric multidisciplinary team.

*Thompson et al. performed a biomechanical investigation of the classic metaphyseal lesion using an immature porcine model.* *The authors used twenty-four pelvic limb specimens from 7-day-old and 3-day-old piglets tested in lateral bending (varus and valgus) using an electromechanical testing machine and found that fractures resembling classic metaphyseal lesions were identified in 12 of the 24 specimens.* **They concluded** that metaphyseal fractures, consistent with classic metaphyseal lesions, resulted from a single loading event delivering varus or valgus bending to the stifle (knee).

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of a skeletally immature femur showing a metaphyseal corner fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 4: Application of a Pavlik harness may be a reasonable treatment for an infantile femur fracture, but the patient should not be discharged without an appropriate NAT workup, given the presence of a metaphyseal corner fracture.
Answers 3 and 5: The radiographic findings are more consistent with a metaphyseal corner fracture than with early signs of osteomyelitis or metabolic disorder.
