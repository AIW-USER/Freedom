## Question

A 75-year-old female with rotator cuff arthropathy presents to your clinic for follow-up. She is no longer getting relief from physical therapy and occasional corticosteroid injections and would like to move forward with surgery. You recommend performing a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA), given her insufficient rotator cuff. Postoperatively, you analyze your radiographs and note that your baseplate is positioned within the upper 50% of the glenoid. Intraoperatively, the construct was stable and well fixed. Given these findings, which of the following complications is this patient most at risk for?

## Options

1. Dislocation
2. Glenoid baseplate loosening
3. Acromial stress fracture
4. Scapular notching
5. Periprosthetic humerus fracture

## Correct option

**4. Scapular notching**

## Answer explanation

Superior positioning of the glenoid baseplate in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) increases the risk for scapular notching, especially if the glenosphere does not cover the inferior glenoid.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty improves function for patients with rotator cuff arthroplasty and severe glenoid deformity. It does so by moving the shoulder's center of rotation medially and inferiorly to allow for the deltoid to power the arm. While rTSA implants do significantly improve function in this patient population, complications are possible and can be devastating in some situations. One of the most common complications is scapular notching and a primary risk factor is superior placement of the glenoid baseplate which creates abnormal impingement of the humeral neck and the inferior scapula. Scapular notching was more common in Grammont style prosthesis where the neck-shaft angle was 155°, as this decreased the lateral offset of the humeral prosthesis. Conversely, in the 135° prosthesis the scapular notching rate is lower. In addition to inferior positioning of the glenoid baseplate, lateralization of the glenoid baseplate or glenosphere can help decrease notching by increasing the offset of the humeral component.

*Cheung et al. published a review of complications in rTSA.* *They started by discussing general indications for rTSA and provided an overview of potential complications.* As for scapular notching, **they note** that the incidence seems particularly dependent on glenosphere offset, glenosphere position, bone loss and the longevity of the prosthesis.

*Parada et al. compared complications and their frequency between anatomic (aTSA) and rTSA.* **They noted** total complication rates of 10.7% for aTSA and 8.9% for rTSA, with revision rates of 5.6% and 2.9%, respectively. The most causes of these complications included rotator cuff tears and glenoid loosening for aTSA and acromial stress fractures and instability for rRTSA.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Superior glenoid baseplate positioning does not directly increase the risk for dislocation in a rTSA.
Answer 2: The risk of glenoid baseplate loosening is increased with lateralization of the baseplate, given more stress being placed through the implant.
Answer 3: Acromial stress fracture would be less likely to occur with the baseplate positioned more superiorly, as this decreases distalization forces of the deltoid.
Answer 5: Glenoid baseplate positioning does not alter the risk for periprosthetic humerus fracture after rTSA.
