## Question

## Options

1. Electromyography (EMG) of bilateral lower extremities
2. Posterior laminectomy and combined posterior and transforaminal interbody fusion at L3-L4
3. Right L3-L4 microforaminotomy and facetectomy
4. Revision laminectomy and instrumentation of L5-S1 with revision bone grafting
5. Referral to physical therapy

## Correct option

**1. Electromyography (EMG) of bilateral lower extremities**

## Answer explanation

This patient has vague radicular symptoms with radiographic evidence of adjacent segmental degeneration. However, her symptoms of L5/S1 distribution weakness, sensory changes, and radiographic findings do not correlate. Thus, an EMG is the best next option to further diagnose nerve root dysfunction (Answer 1).

Adjacent segment disease (ASDis) is the clinical manifestation of adjacent segment degeneration (ASDeg), caused by increased stress and strain on the cranial or caudal segments adjacent to a prior fusion or arthroplasty procedure. This phenomenon is due to increased compensatory motion at the levels above or below a prior fusion procedure. Arthroplasty devices have been demonstrated to reduce the risk of ASDeg, but not eliminate it. Most patients who undergo lumbar fusion procedures progress to some level of adjacent segment degeneration, but not all are symptomatic. It is critical to correlate patient complaints with radiographic findings in the selection of surgical and nonsurgical treatment options for degenerative spinal disease.

*Min et al. performed a retrospective review of 48 patients who underwent instrumented lumbar fusion at L4-L5 and had minimal adjacent segment degeneration preoperatively.* **They found** that 62.5% of patients were found to have degenerative changes in their adjacent segments at a mean follow-up of 44.6 months. Notably, **they found** no difference in clinical outcome measures at 1-year and final follow-up between patients with ASDeg and those without. They stress that radiographic evidence of ASDeg does not correlate with poor outcomes.

*Harrop et al. performed a systematic review of lumbar adjacent segment degeneration and disease after arthrodesis and arthroplasty procedures.* **They note** that studies vary in the reported incidence of ASDeg after lumbar arthrodesis, with most large series reporting an incidence at long-term follow-up of about 30-40%. **They report** that systematic analysis found a total risk of 9% for ASDeg after arthroplasty, with only 1% of patients being symptomatic. **The authors conclude** that the overall quality of evidence in the current literature regarding this topic is poor, and it is important to elucidate the difference between ASDeg and ASDis in selecting treatment.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral standing radiographs of the lumbar spine with evidence of prior L4-S1 combined posterior and transforaminal interbody fusion, with evidence of L3-L4 disc height loss and spondylosis consistent with adjacent segment degeneration. Figure C is a selected sagittal MRI sequence demonstrating severe adjacent segment degeneration at L3-L4. Figure D is a sagittal CT cut of the lumbar spine demonstrating a successful combined interbody and posterior fusion at L4-L5 and L5-S1.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2, 3, 4: The patient's symptoms do not correlate to the L3-L4 distribution, and thus, further workup should be performed before selecting a surgical treatment for adjacent segment disease or revision surgery.
Answer 5: Physical therapy is unlikely to help this patient, who has had symptoms for many years, and further workup should be performed.
