## Question

A 4-year-old boy presents to the ER after falling off a trampoline with the injury shown in Figure A. Examination reveals 2+ radial pulse with a well perfused hand. He is taken to the operating room for closed reduction and percutaneous pinning. The fragment is anatomically reduced. Following the procedure, the surgeon notices the hand is paler and cooler than the contralateral side with diminished pulses. Which of the following is the next best step in management?

![[q215088-a-schiii.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Splint in extension and observation
2. Remove pins and reassess vascular status
3. Angiogram of the brachial artery
4. Remove the pins and pin the fracture in the initial displaced position
5. Explore the brachial artery while fracture remains reduced and pinned.

## Correct option

**2. Remove pins and reassess vascular status**

## Answer explanation

This child has an extension Gartland type III supracondylar fracture of the humerus with an initial well-perfused extremity that loses perfusion following reduction/pinning. The next best step in management is to remove the pins and to reassess the vascular status.

Type III supracondylar humerus fractures can be associated with a vascular injury in up to ~15% of cases. These vascular injuries are most often transient and improve following reduction and stabilization of the injury. Patients with pulseless and poorly perfused hands should be taken to the operating room on an emergent basis. Following reduction and pinning, the vascular status of the extremity should be assessed and if the patient has persistently poor perfusion, fixation removal and vascular exploration are recommended. If a previously perfused hand loses perfusion following reduction and pinning, it is recommended the pins be removed immediately and the vascular status be reassessed.

*Franklin et al. reviewed pediatric supracondylar fractures with neurovascular compromise.* **They noted**: (1) observation for 24-48 hours for the pulseless, well-perfused hand, (2) exploration of pulseless hands with AIN/median nerve palsy, and (3) if a hand is not perfused or compartments are firm, emergent treatment should be considered.

*Choi et al. performed a review of 1255 pediatric supracondylar fractures treated at a single institution.* There were 33 pulseless fractures (2.6%). All 24 well-perfused patients did well postoperatively without vascular repair, although 10 remained pulseless. Of the 9 poorly perfused patients, 4 underwent vascular repair, and 2 developed compartment syndrome. **They concluded** that patients with poor perfusion were at increased risk of vascular repair and compartment syndrome.

Figures A is the lateral elbow radiographs showing a Gartland type III supracondylar fracture of the humerus.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3: Removal of the pins is required since the patient losses perfusion followed reduction and pinning.
Answer 4: Pinning an unreduced fracture is never indicated.
Answer 5: If exploration is to be performed, the pins need to be removed to allow for adequate visualization of possible entrapped vessels.
