Chapter: Pediatric Orthopedics MCQs — Cerebral Palsy (CP)

## Question

A 12-year-old child with GMFCS Level III spastic diplegia presents with "Apparent Equinus." Clinical exam and 3D Gait Analysis show:
• Ankle: Neutral dorsiflexion in stance, but high electromyography (EMG) activity in the gastrocnemius.
• Knee: 35° of persistent flexion during mid-stance.
• Hip: 20° of persistent flexion.
• Imaging: Patella Alta is noted on lateral X-ray (Insall-Salvati ratio of 1.4).
What is the most appropriate surgical strategy for this child's "equinus"?

## Options

1. Lengthening of the Achilles tendon to allow the heel to touch the ground.
2. Distal Femoral Extension Osteotomy (DFEO) and Patellar Tendon Advancement (PTA).
3. Isolated Hamstring lengthening (Semitendinosus and Gracilis).
4. Gastrocnemius recession combined with a Solid AFO.

## Correct option

**2. Distal Femoral Extension Osteotomy (DFEO) and Patellar Tendon Advancement (PTA).**

## Answer explanation

Recognizing Apparent Equinus: This is the most common "trap" in CP surgery. The child looks
like they are in equinus because they are walking on their toes, but the ankle is actually in neutral or even dorsiflexion relative to the leg. The "equinus" is a result of the knee flexion deformity.

The Logic of DFEO/PTA: At age 12, with a 35° knee flexion deformity and Patella Alta, soft tissue hamstring lengthening alone (Choice C) is often insufficient due to capsular contracture and bony changes. A Distal Femoral Extension Osteotomy
corrects the fixed contracture, and the Patellar Tendon Advancement
restores the lever arm of the quadriceps, which is often stretched out (functional weakness) due to chronic crouch.

If TA is lengthened the in "Apparent Equinus," this destroy the last bit of stability the child has (the plantarflexion-knee extension couple), causing them to collapse into a severe, non-ambulatory crouch gait.

Answer : b
