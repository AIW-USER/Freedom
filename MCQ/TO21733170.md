Chapter: Spine MCQs — Spondylolisthesis

## Question

Management of a 2 months old bilateral pars defect should ideally be

## Options

1. Hyperextension brace
2. Antilordotic brace
3. Fusion and instrumentation
4. Observation

## Correct option

**2. Antilordotic brace**

## Answer explanation

Ans: B
EXPLANATION: -

Pars interarticularis defect (also called as SPONDYLOLYSIS) refers to a unilateral or bilateral overuse or fatigue stress fracture involving the pars interarticularis of the posterior vertebral arch. It is a common cause of axial back pain in adolescents, especially in the case of young athletes.

Stress reactions in the pars precede fractures and likely represent a grey area of a physiologic stress response transitioning to a pathologic lysis.

Morita et al. defined pars fractures into 3 stages: early, progressive, and terminal. Early fractures are hairline defects with focal bony resorption progressive fractures have wider defects and the presence of small fragments. Terminal stage fractures exhibit sclerotic change and sometimes lead to pseudarthrosis.

Defects may progress from unilateral to bilateral, with 80% of defects bilateral and 20% unilateral. Adolescent athletes may present with a unilateral defect due to asymmetric axial loading. Once a unilateral defect occurs, stresses increase at the contralateral pars, likely explaining the greater proportion of bilateral cases. L5 is the most common location for spondylolysis (85–95%), and the remaining cases are typically observed at L4 or L3.

The goal in treatment of early and progressive defects is to achieve bony union through immobilization, while in terminal defects, the goal is to limit pain from synovitis by non surgical methods and ultimately achieve bony union through surgical means. So treatment is always conservative initially, while surgical intervention is reserved for refractory cases in patients with debilitating pain for more than 6–12 months.
Positive predictors of pars defects spontaneously healing include unilateral defects, earlier fracture stage, and lumbar level other than L5
It is important to differentiate acute from chronic pars defects when selecting management: acute lesions include stress reactions and early/progressive fractures, while chronic defects are non-unions that will not heal spontaneously. So indications for surgery can be summed up as persistent pain after 6–12 months of conservative treatment, development of neurologic deficits or worsening symptomatic spondylolisthesis.

There are 2 principal surgical methods that can be used for spondylolysis: spinal fusion or direct pars repair. Direct repair of the pars has become the preferred intervention for pars defects after conservative measures fail. Direct repair does not restrict range of motion in the involved segments, can restore normal anatomy, and has good clinical functional outcomes. Contraindications for direct repair include facet joint arthropathy, significant degenerative disk disease, and associated spondylolisthesis. Various methods may be utilized for direct pars repair, but pedicle screw-based systems and percutaneous placement of a direct pars screw appear to perform best.

Indications for spinal fusion: Meyerding grade 3 or 4, significant degenerative disc disease, dysplastic pars anatomy.

Spinal fusion increases stability and reduces translation in the sagittal plane. While spinal fusion provides excellent stability, its intersegmental fixation causes loss of natural range of motion and carries significant risk of Adjacent Segment Degeneration: 36% of patients who undergo posterior lumbar fusion will require additional surgery at 10 years postoperatively due to Adjacent Segment Degeneration. Additionally, spinal fusion should be avoided in professional athletes if possible due to lower chances of returning to prior athletic level as well as necessity to take a prolonged period of time off from athletics
