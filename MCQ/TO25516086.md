Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Pes Planus - Flat Foot

## Question

An adult patient presents with a severe, painful rigid pes planus deformity classified as Stage III adult-acquired flatfoot deformity (AAFD / Progressive Collapse Foot Deformity). Clinical examination reveals a completely rigid, non-reducible hindfoot eversion deformity with severe subfibular impingement. What is the most appropriate surgical reconstruction strategy for this patient?

## Options

1. Lateral column lengthening (Evans osteotomy) combined with a cotton osteotomy
2. Isolated posterior tibial tendon reconstruction with an FDL transfer and spring ligament repair
3. Triple arthrodesis (talocalcaneal, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid fusion) with appropriate structural realignments
4. Isolated subtalar joint fusion without addressing the midtarsal complex

## Correct option

**3. Triple arthrodesis (talocalcaneal, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid fusion) with appropriate structural realignments**

## Answer explanation

In adults, rigid pes planus is most commonly caused by Stage III or IV posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD), post-traumatic deformity, or inflammatory arthropathy. By definition, the deformity is no longer passively correctable, and the hindfoot joints (subtalar, talonavicular) have developed secondary degenerative changes.

Non-Operative Management:

Non-operative treatment in adult rigid flatfoot is primarily palliative — it does not correct the deformity but aims to accommodate it and alleviate symptoms.

A rigid UCBL orthosis or Arizona brace (a custom-moulded, rigid ankle-foot orthosis that extends above the malleoli, providing circumferential support to the rigid hindfoot) accommodates the fixed deformity and redistributes pressure. Rocker-bottom shoe modifications reduce the demands on the stiff foot during gait. Activity modification, NSAIDs, and corticosteroid injection into the subtalar or talonavicular joint may provide symptomatic relief. A patellar tendon-bearing (PTB) brace can offload the foot in severe cases.

Non-operative management is appropriate for patients who are elderly, low-demand, medically unfit for surgery, or who prefer to avoid surgery despite persistent symptoms.

Operative Management:

Surgery is indicated for persistent pain and functional limitation despite adequate non-operative management in a patient fit for and willing to undergo surgical reconstruction.

Stage III PTTD (Rigid Flatfoot, No Ankle Involvement):

Triple arthrodesis remains the gold standard for rigid adult flatfoot — it provides reliable pain relief, correction of the deformity to a plantigrade position, and a stable platform for weight-bearing. The procedure involves fusion of the subtalar, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid joints through one or two incisions (medial and lateral), with realignment of the hindfoot from its valgus, abducted, dorsiflexed position to a neutral or slightly valgus (5°), adducted, plantigrade position. Fixation is with multiple cannulated screws across each joint. Bone graft (from the resected joint surfaces or supplemental iliac crest autograft) fills any defects. Non-weight-bearing for 6–8 weeks is standard, with transition to protected weight-bearing in a boot until radiographic union (typically 12–16 weeks).

Double arthrodesis
(talonavicular + calcaneocuboid) — an alternative that preserves the subtalar joint. May be appropriate when subtalar arthrosis is minimal and the primary deformity is at the talonavicular level.

Isolated subtalar arthrodesis — rarely sufficient as a standalone procedure for rigid flatfoot (because the talonavicular joint is usually also degenerated and contributing to the deformity), but may be appropriate in isolated subtalar coalition or post-traumatic subtalar arthritis.

Stage IV PTTD (Rigid Flatfoot with Ankle Valgus):

When the deformity has progressed to involve the ankle joint (deltoid ligament attenuation producing lateral talar tilt within the ankle mortise), correction of the hindfoot alone is insufficient — the ankle valgus will persist or progress.

Triple arthrodesis combined with deltoid ligament reconstruction may be attempted if the ankle articular cartilage is preserved. Pantalar arthrodesis (triple arthrodesis + ankle fusion) is the salvage for end-stage deformity with ankle arthritis — provides a painless, stable, plantigrade foot and ankle but eliminates all hindfoot and ankle motion. Tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis using a retrograde intramedullary nail provides an efficient single-construct fixation for combined ankle and subtalar fusion.

Principles Common to All Surgical Management

Restoration of plantigrade, braceable foot — the primary goal of all surgical intervention is to produce a foot that is pain-free, stable, plantigrade (flat on the ground), and braceable (can be accommodated in standard or modified footwear). Absolute correction of all anatomical parameters is less important than achieving a functional, shoeable foot.

Correction of all components of the deformity — hindfoot valgus, forefoot abduction, and arch collapse must all be addressed. Correcting only one component (e.g., subtalar valgus without addressing talonavicular subluxation) produces an incompletely corrected, still-symptomatic foot.

Gastrocnemius or Achilles lengthening — equinus contracture (inability to achieve 10° of ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended) is frequently associated with rigid pes planus and acts as a deforming force that drives the foot into pronation. A gastrocnemius recession (Strayer procedure) or percutaneous Achilles lengthening should be performed concurrently if equinus is present, to eliminate this deforming force and protect the reconstruction.

Management of concurrent deformity — forefoot varus (a supination deformity of the forefoot relative to the hindfoot that becomes apparent after hindfoot correction) should be addressed with a Cotton osteotomy (dorsal opening wedge osteotomy of the medial cuneiform to plantarflex the first ray) to ensure a plantigrade forefoot. Failure to address forefoot varus results in lateral border overload and persistent symptoms.

Answer: 3
