## Question

A 24-year-old professional hockey player presents with severe right foot pain after he was hit directly in the right medial foot with the puck. There is a large ecchymosis with swelling over the medial foot that is exquisitely tender. The patient is unable to bear weight on the right foot. Figures A-D are the current radiographs. ## Options

1. Progressive weight bearing as tolerated
2. Non-weightbearing in a short leg cast
3. MRI of the right foot
4. Return to play
5. Bilateral weight-bearing radiographs

## Correct option

**3. MRI of the right foot**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a direct blow injury mechanism to the right foot with normal radiographs. An MRI of the affected foot should be performed to identify an occult fracture or high-grade bone lesion.

Hockey injuries affect the lower extremity 20-30% of the time and can be the result of high energy contact mechanisms. Direct blow by the puck has a unique injury pattern, resulting in trabecular bone stress fractures with associated bone marrow edema that is not evident on traditional radiographs. Occult fractures of the medial malleolus, medial talar neck, medial cuneiform, and 1st metatarsal which have a high risk of progression if not addressed early. Specifically, injuries to the medial malleolus can progress to frank displaced fractures that require open reduction and internal fixation. Players that sustained a debilitating injury from a direct puck blow should undergo MRI imaging if initial radiographs are unremarkable.

*Baker et al. performed a retrospective study of 31 acute foot injuries in 27 professional hockey players that underwent MRI imaging following normal radiographs.* **They reported** 17 injuries that occurred from a direct blow which caused 6 fractures and 6 high-grade bone lesions localized to the medial bones (medial malleolus, navicular, and 1st metatarsal) in 14 cases, which was associated with more severe bone injury than other injury mechanisms. **The authors concluded** that most fractures and bone injuries to the medial foot are the result of a direct blow mechanism and are associated with a prolonged recovery.

*Polites et al. performed a retrospective study of pediatric hockey players presenting with injuries over 16 years.* **They reported** there to be 168 injuries in 155 children with extremity injuries and traumatic brain injuries being the most common and the result of intentional contact and 23.2% of injuries requiring a major procedure. **They concluded** that trauma associated with hockey can be severe and necessitates close evaluation.

Figures A-C are the AP, oblique, and lateral radiographs of the right foot without any abnormal anatomy of fractures. Figure D is the mortise view radiograph of the right ankle without any obvious injuries or abnormalities.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Given that there could be an occult fracture from the given injury mechanism, progressive weight-bearing as tolerated could worsen the injury.
Answer 2: Placement in a short leg cast and non-weightbearing without a diagnosis would not be preferred.
Answer 4: Direct blow injury mechanisms to the foot in hockey players can be associated with occult fractures and high-grade bone lesions that are not evident on plain radiographs. It would be erroneous to allow the patient to return to play without fully evaluating for an occult injury with an MRI.
Answer 5: Bilateral weight-bearing radiographs would be useful for evaluating for a Lisfranc fracture. However, the injury mechanism is inconsistent with this injury.
