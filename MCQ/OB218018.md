## Question

A 22-year-old male who is a former college football player presents to your office with complaints of knee pain and instability. He previously underwent hamstring autograft ACL reconstruction his freshman year of college, but states his knee has never felt, "quite right." Knee radiographs demonstrate no arthritic changes. Full length standing radiographs demonstrate a normal mechanical axis bilaterally with a posterior tibial slope of 7°. Varus stress radiographs show an increase in lateral joint space of 5mm compared to the contralateral knee. On examination, he has a 3B Lachman, negative posterior drawer, no laxity with valgus stress, and significant laxity with varus stress at 30° but not at 90°. Dial testing demonstrates a 10° increase in external rotation at 30° but not at 90°. You schedule him for revision ACL reconstruction using a BPTB autograft. What is the most likely reason this revision procedure will fail?

## Options

1. Graft choice
2. Gender
3. Concomitant posterolateral corner injury
4. Excessive posterior tibial slope
5. Proximal tibia varus deformity

## Correct option

**3. Concomitant posterolateral corner injury**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an injury to his posterolateral corner (PLC) in addition to his anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture, which increases the risk of ACL reconstruction failure.

ACL tears are very common, especially in the young, athletic population. They more commonly occur in females secondary to valgus and internal rotation forces through the knee. They can be associated with tears of the lateral/medial menisci as well as the posterior cruciate ligament or collateral ligaments. The PLC consists of the lateral collateral ligament (LCL), popliteus, and the popliteofibular ligament and injury to these structures can sometimes be difficult to identify on MRI. The dial test can be used to differentiate PLC injuries from combined PLC and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries. If there is an increase in external rotation at 30° but not 90°, this indicates an isolated PLC injury, whereas if external rotation is increased at both degrees of flexion, there is a combined PLC/PCL injury. If injuries to the PLC are not addressed concomitantly at the time of ACL reconstruction/revision, there has been a clear increase in the risk of graft re-rupture due to the increased stresses placed through the ACL graft.

*Kennedy et al. reviewed surgical management of knees with injuries to the ACL and PLC.* **They note** that the primary function of the PLC is to prevent excessive varus and external rotation of the knee and varus stress radiographs can be helpful in diagnosing these injuries. They describe an anatomic-based reconstruction of the ACL/PLC in order to restore stability of the knee and minimize stress on the ACL graft.

*Ranawat et al. discussed evaluation and management of PLC injuries of the knee in a JAAOS review article.* **They note** that PLC injuries have been increasingly recognized and serve as a cause of increased failure rates after ACL and PCL reconstructions. Acute intervention of PLC injuries has been shown to result in better outcomes than delayed reconstruction of chronic PLC injuries, therefore prompt attention is preferred.

*Fanelli et al. evaluated treatment of combined ACL/PCL/PLC injuries,* **noting** that the most important initial steps included ensuring tibiofemoral joint reduction and documentation of neurovascular status. Physical examination, imaging studies, examination under anesthesia, and diagnostic arthroscopy were all recommended to formulate an appropriate diagnosis. **They ultimately suggested** that all 3 ligament complexes should be surgically addressed in these cases.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Autografts demonstrate a lower risk for failure in both the primary and revision ACL reconstruction setting.
Answer 2: Females have a higher rate of re-rupture than males.
Answer 4: Excessive posterior tibial slope >12° is thought to be a risk factor for ACL re-rupture.
Answer 5: On full-length standing films, this patient did not demonstrate significant varus deformity. His deformity is dynamic in nature, due to incompetence of the posterolateral corner ligamentous complex, as demonstrated on stress examination.
