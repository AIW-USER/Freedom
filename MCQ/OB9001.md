## Question

A 12-year-old patient that developed torticollis 10 weeks ago has undergone treatment including 2 weeks of halter traction followed by 3 weeks of halo traction with sedative medications. Despite these efforts, that patient continues to have torticollis with figures A-C showing the CT scan of the cervical spine at the end of halo traction therapy. What is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Increase dose of sedative medications
2. C1-2 arthrodesis
3. Increased traction weight with follow-up CT in 2 weeks
4. Immobilization in a halo vest
5. Conversion to C-collar

## Correct option

**2. C1-2 arthrodesis**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a persistent Fielding and Hawkins type 1 atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation (AARS) that has failed to reduce with adequate sedation and traction based on the clinical exam and CT imaging. The most appropriate next step would be a C1-2 arthrodesis.

Atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation is a rare presentation in the pediatric population that usually presents after upper respiratory infections, but can also follow traumatic events. Patients typically present with new-onset fixed torticollis. Classification is based on the Fielding and Hawkins system, with most cases being type 1. The vast majority of cases resolve with conservative measures consisting of C-collar immobilization, NSAIDs, or halter traction. Cases with persistent or recurrent torticollis are best managed with a posterior C1-2 arthrodesis.

*Neal et al.* *2015 reviewed the treatment of AARS in pediatric patients.* **The authors proposed** a treatment algorithm based on the chronicity of the subluxation. Presentations greater than two weeks are treated with halter traction, benzodiazepines, and NSAIDs. If torticollis persists, then halo traction for an additional two weeks. Posterior C1-2 arthrodesis is recommended if there is persistent subluxation after two weeks of halo traction.

*Rahimi et al.* *2003 performed a retrospective study of 23 pediatric patients presenting with atlantoaxial instability, of which six patients had an AARS mostly due to trauma.* *Most cases resolved with immobilization or with 48-72 hours of cervical traction.* *The author's recommended the use of dynamic CT during injury evaluation, and recommend initial treatment consisting of rigid immobilization for 6 weeks.*

Figures A-C demonstrate axial CT scans of the cervical spine with atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation. Illustration A depicts the Fielding classification for AARS. Illustration B depicts the recommended treatment algorithm by Neal et al. for atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Sedative medications such as benzodiazepines are useful in the reduction of AARS, but increasing the dose after 2 weeks of halter and 2 weeks of halo traction will not likely achieve reduction.
Answer 3: Adding additional weight to traction after five weeks total of traction will be unlikely to achieve a reduction.
Answer 4: Immobilization in a halo vest would be favorable if the patient achieved a reduction after halo traction. However, in this case, the patient is subluxated.
Answer 5: Immobilization with a C-collar is preferred in acute presentations or after reduction with halter traction. However, this patient is still not reduced and if the reduction was achieved after halo traction, halo orthosis would be indicated.
