## Options

1. Conversion to a sliding hip screw
2. Implant removal and conversion to total hip arthroplasty
3. Placement of 7.0 mm partially-threaded screw perpendicular to the fracture site
4. Valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy with 130° angled blade plate
5. Varus intertrochanteric osteotomy with 95° angled blade plate

## Correct option

**4. Valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy with 130° angled blade plate**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a femoral neck nonunion from a previous vertically oriented femoral neck fracture, which is best treated with a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy.

Femoral neck fractures in young patients have different treatment considerations than elderly patients. Often, a high-energy mechanism such as a fall from a height or high-speed motor vehicle collision leads to a more vertically oriented fracture plane. Fixation with this fracture morphology can be difficult to achieve compression and is prone to hardware failure from the shear forces acting on the construct with weight-bearing. Complications of femoral neck fractures can include nonunion. In cases of nonunion, treatment with a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy has been reported to have a high subsquent union rate. Mechanically, this functions to redirect the fracture plane more horizontally and convert shear forces into compressive forces with weight-bearing. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head is another devastating complication of femoral neck fractures and is often treated with arthroplasty when there is significant femoral head collapse.

*Said et al. retrospectively reported 36 cases of femoral neck nonunions treated with valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy.* *The authors used a 130° angled blade-plate which resulted in fracture union in 97% of patients at an average of 4 months time-to-union.* **The authors recommended** performing a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy for relatively young patients with femoral neck nonunions.

*Deakin et al. reviewed the management of femoral neck nonunions with valgus osteotomy.* The published literature reports union rates between 80-90% with valgus osteotomy with avascular necrosis occurring in up to 20% of cases. **They recommended** careful patient selection for valgus osteotomies as some patients may be best treated with arthroplasty.

*Singh et al. retrospectively studied reconstruction nail (RN) and sliding hip screw (SHS) with compression plate fixation for ipsilateral femoral neck and shaft fractures in 27 patients.* **The authors reported** the average time to fracture union for the femoral neck fracture was 15.2 weeks and 17.1 weeks in the SHS and RN, respectively, with no difference in femoral shaft time to union clinical results between the two groups. **They concluded** that both fixation strategies are effective and the use of the SHS with compression plating may be technically easier, but femoral neck fixation should be performed before shaft fixation.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Conversion to a sliding hip screw would not likely lead to fracture healing in this patient due to the vertically oriented fracture line. Internal fixation has already failed.
Answer 2: A total hip arthroplasty is a salvage treatment option for a femoral neck nonunion and avascular necrosis, but should be avoided in a relatively young patient.
Answer 3: Placing a 7.0 mm partially-threaded screw perpendicular to the fracture would provide a lag effect to the fracture, but after 9 months this would not have a high success rate.
Answer 5: Inducing a varus deformity to the proximal femur would not improve union success like a valgus osteotomy as this would add more shear stress across the nonunion site.
