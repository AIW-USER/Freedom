## Question

A 7-year-old girl presents to your clinic complaining of 4 months of occasional back pain during gymnastics practice. Close-up radiographs are shown in Figure A. Which of the following is true with respect to the pathology seen in the radiograph?

![[q998-a-spondylolysis.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. 40% of patients will progress to spondylolisthesis
2. Likely unrelated to gymnastics and activity restriction is unlikely to improve symptoms
3. Unilateral lesions have the same rate of spondylolisthesis as bilateral lesions.
4. Quadriceps stretching may help with resolution of pain.
5. She will likely not need surgery in her lifetime for this pathology

## Correct option

**5. She will likely not need surgery in her lifetime for this pathology**

## Answer explanation

The patient in the vignette has an L5 pars defect (spondylolysis). A pars defect has been shown to rarely progress to high-grade spondylolisthesis requiring surgical management.

Spondylolysis is one of the most frequent causes of back pain in the pediatric population with a prevalence of 4-6% in the general pediatric population. Those who perform repetitive hyperextension activities such as gymnastics, weightlifters and football lineman have a very high prevalence of this condition, up to 47% in some studies. Around 15% of patients diagnosed with spondylolysis progress to spondylolisthesis on follow-up studies (all with bilateral defects, as unilateral defects do not advance to spondylolisthesis). Treatment generally comprises of non-operative management with activity modifications, NSAIDs, physical therapy (focusing on hamstring and core exercises) and TLSO if they remain symptomatic. Operative management is rare and reserved for persistently symptomatic patients having failed non-operative care.

*Beutler et al. performed a prospective 45-year follow-up of 30 patients found to have pars defects as children.* **They noted** that those with unilateral defects never progressed to spondylolisthesis over the course of follow-up. **They also noted** that there was no association between slip progression and low back pain. Finally, **they concluded** that there was no difference between the SF-36 scores of their cohort and those of the general population of the same age.

*Lemoine et al. evaluated the prevalence of spondylolysis with respect to spinopelvic parameters in children under eight requiring CT evaluation for unrelated lumbar conditions.* *In 717 patients,* **they found** 25 cases of spondylolysis (16 unilateral and 9 bilateral). **They noted** that unilateral spondylolysis was significantly associated with normal pelvic incidence and bilateral spondylolysis was associated with high pelvic incidence.

Figure A is the Lateral lumbar radiograph showing an L5 pars defect. Illustration A is the same radiograph as Figure A with a circle indicating the pars defect.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Around 15% of patients with spondylolysis progress to spondylolisthesis
Answer 2: Patients performing repetitive hyperextension activities such as gymnastics and weightlifting are at increased risk for developing symptomatic spondylolysis and a period of such activity session can often improve symptoms significantly
Answer 3: Bilateral pars defects have a much higher rate of progressing to spondylolisthesis (~18-20%) than unilateral lesions (~0%)
Answer 4: Patients with spondylolisthesis and spondylolysis typically have hamstring tightness (not quadriceps) and improve with physical therapy focused on hamstring exercises.
