KLIPPEL FEIL SYNDROME
Klippel Feil Syndrome (KFS) was first described by Maurice Klippel and Andre Feil in 1912. The condition is characterized by congenital fusion of two or more cervical vertebrae due to failure of segmentation. There is failure of normal division of cervical somites at 3–8 weeks of gestation. The most common fusion levels are C2-C3 and C5-C6, however, it may even involve the craniocervical junction (occiput C1-C2).
Exact etiology is unclear, and sporadic inheritance pattern in commonest. However, Klippel-Feil syndrome could likely be caused by heritable mutations in the GDF6, GDF3, and MEOX1 genes. GDF6 and GDF3 influence embryonic bone development. The MEOX1 gene encodes the homeobox protein MOX1, which regulates vertebral separation. GDF6 and GDF3 abnormalities are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, while MEOX1 mutations are autosomal recessive.

Classifications
The Samartzis classification (2006) scheme is frequently used to describe Klippel-Feil syndrome fusions as it carries a prognostic value. Type I involves a single congenitally fused segment, whereas type II involves multiple, noncontiguous, congenitally fused segments. Type III consists of multiple, contiguous, congenitally fused segments. About 25% of patients with Klippel-Feil syndrome present with type I deformities, while 50% have type II, and another 25% develop type III deformities.

KFS classification is an alternative for classifying the condition but is less frequently used.

Clinical Features
Incidence is roughly 1 in 40,000 live births. Mean age of onset is around 15 years and the condition has slight female preponderance. The classical triad consists of short-webbed neck, low posterior hairline and restriction of neck movements. Segment of spine near the fused part becomes hypermobile, causing degenerative changes, which may predispose individuals to spinal stenosis, neurological deficits, and cervical spinal deformities and instability. Scoliosis (congenital or idiopathic) occurs in 60% of patients. Other associated conditions include genitourinary abnormalities (structural abnormalities such as double collecting system, horseshoe-shaped kidney, renal aplasia, etc.), congenital heart defects, hearing loss, synkinesis, ocular anomalies and cervical canal stenosis.
Sprengel deformity of shoulder accompanies the syndrome in 50% cases. Torticollis (head tilt) could also co exist in some cases masking the short neck or making its identification difficult.
Wildervanck syndrome (cervico-ocular-acoustic dysplasia): Here the triad consists of: -
• Klippel-Feil syndrome
• Congenital ossicular anomalies; usually diffuse ossicular ankylosis and sensorineural deafness
• Duane syndrome (ocular motility disturbance due to fibrosis of extraocular muscles

Treatment
Conservative management tailored to symptoms with lifestyle modifications is the mainstay in management. Symptomatic children with chronic pain, myelopathy features, cervical instability, basilar invagination or progressive scoliosis deformity may require surgical decompressions and fusions. Patients should be advised to avoid collision sports, especially those with fusions above C3 (even if asymptomatic).
