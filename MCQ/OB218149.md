## Question

A 32-year-old female complains of numbness and tingling in her right hand which often wakes her up at night. On physical examination, she has a negative Tinel sign over the cubital tunnel, negative Froment sign, positive Durkan compression test, and positive Phalen test. Which of the following muscles would demonstrate weakness compared to the unaffected, left side?

## Options

1. Adductor pollicis
2. Abductor pollicis brevis
3. Flexor pollicis longus
4. Pronator quadratus
5. Flexor digitorum profundus to the index finger

## Correct option

**2. Abductor pollicis brevis**

## Answer explanation

This patient is experiencing carpal tunnel syndrome, as indicated by her physical examination with positive signs using provocative testing. The abductor pollicis brevis (Answer 2) is the only muscle listed that is solely innervated distal to the transverse carpal ligament by the median nerve and would be affected by median nerve compression within the carpal tunnel.

Carpal tunnel release is one of the most commonly performed surgeries in the United States each year. Therefore, a thorough understanding of normal anatomy and common variants is paramount in preventing complications during surgical release. The median nerve innervates most of the forearm flexor musculature proximal to the carpal tunnel, apart from the flexor carpi ulnaris and the ulnar-most flexor digitorum profundus musculature (ulnar nerve innervation). On the contrary, the ulnar nerve supplies most of the hand and intrinsic musculature distal to the carpal tunnel, apart from the (1) opponens pollicis, (2) abductor pollicis brevis, (3) superficial flexor pollicis brevis, and (4) radial ½ of the lumbricals (#1-3 supplied by the recurrent motor branch, #4 supplied by the palmar digital branches). When performing a carpal tunnel release, it is recommended to incise the transverse carpal ligament as ulnarly as possible to avoid iatrogenic injury to common anatomic variants of the recurrent branch of the median nerve (Illustration A). If it is transected, the majority of the thenar musculature would be denervated leading to considerable loss of hand function.

Kleopas provided a review article regarding the diagnosis and treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome. The author discusses that manual labor, obesity, pregnancy, and family history all predispose patients to developing carpal tunnel syndrome. They further mention various treatment options and when to consider early operative intervention over conservative management to halt disease progression (i.e. initial presentations with thenar atrophy). They conclude that surgical decompression of the carpal tunnel via open or endoscopic means is safe, effective, and well-tolerated by patients.

*Padua and colleagues provided a similar review article regarding carpal tunnel syndrome.* *The authors discuss various diagnostic modalities beginning with a thorough history and physical examination.* *They further discuss nerve conduction studies as a potential clinical adjunct in the setting of ambiguous cases or, more importantly, to establish the severity of disease and potential for recovery after carpal tunnel release.* **The authors conclude** by reviewing treatment modalities and confirming that surgical release is the most definitive treatment option with the lowest risk for recurrence.

*Seiler and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of normal palmar anatomy and variants that impact median nerve decompression.* *They discuss the importance of where to make the surgical approach, given that the carpal tunnel is immediately bordered by Guyon’s canal ulnarly, the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve radially, and the superficial palmar arch distally.* *The authors further discuss normal anatomic variants and conclude that a better understanding of anatomic variations should lead to improved safety and surgical outcomes.*

Illustration A is an image from the Seiler article outlining that the recurrent motor branch of the median nerve branches in an extra-ligamentous pattern in ~50% of patients, sub-ligamentous in ~30% of patients, and trans-ligamentous in ~20% of patients.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The adductor pollicis is innervated by the ulnar nerve.
Answers 3&4: The flexor pollicis longus and pronator quadratus are innervated by the anterior interosseous nerve, a branch of the median nerve that terminates proximal to the carpal tunnel. Therefore, both muscles would not be affected in the setting of carpal tunnel syndrome.
Answer 5: Flexor digitorum profundus to the index finger is innervated by a branch of the median nerve proximal to the carpal tunnel. Therefore, it would not be affected in the setting of carpal tunnel syndrome.
