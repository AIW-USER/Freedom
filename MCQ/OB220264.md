## Options

1. Transition to a functional brace and initiation of range of motion exercises
2. Cast reapplication in situ and 1-week follow-up
3. Repeat closed reduction, casting, and 1-week follow-up
4. Cast wedging
5. Open reduction internal fixation of the radius and ulna

## Correct option

**5. Open reduction internal fixation of the radius and ulna**

## Answer explanation

This patient, despite having an acceptable initial reduction in the cast, has since displaced and developed a malunion of her both bone forearm fracture, hampering her ability to pronosupinate. The best course of action at this time is open reduction and internal fixation (Answer 5).

In patients over the age of 10 years, tolerances for both bone forearm fractures include residual angulation of <10º, malrotation of <30º, and no bayonet apposition. As long as the fracture remains within these parameters, closed treatment in a cast is appropriate. However, interval displacement leading to loss of reduction and unacceptable alignment is an indication for surgical intervention. Additionally, this patient has developed an interval healing callus - therefore, open reduction is required to remove the callus and obtain anatomic alignment before surgical fixation.

As highlighted in the Rockwood and Wilkins textbook, in children younger than 10 years of age, acceptable reduction parameters for forearm fractures are less than 15º of angulation, 100% translation, and less than 45º of malrotation. Weekly monitoring for loss of reduction and unstable fractures requiring further intervention is warranted. When acceptable alignment can be maintained, good outcomes can be expected in this age group. In patients older than 10 years, angulation of less than 10º, and malrotation of 30º, without bayonet apposition, can be accepted. When surgical treatment is indicated, plating of one or both bones is acceptable.

*Ploegmakers et al. investigated the tolerances of angulation in the non-operative treatment of pediatric forearm fractures.* *They constructed graphs to depict age plotted against the limit of acceptable angular deformity (in degrees) for various fractures, including plastic deformation, distal radial physeal fractures, torus fractures, radial neck, and distal and middle both bone forearm fractures.* **They concluded** that their findings in this meta-analysis are helpful in the decision-making process of acceptance of angular deformities in pediatric forearm fractures.

*Pace published a 2016 JAAOS review on treatment recommendations for pediatric both bone forearm fractures.* **The author recommended** surgical intervention when angulation of >10° in the proximal shaft and midshaft, or >15° in the distal shaft, persists after attempts at closed reduction in girls aged >8 years and boys aged >10 years with 2 or more years of remaining growth. Although difficult to quantify, surgery can be considered in the setting of malrotation >30°.

Figure E is a 6-week post-injury radiograph of the subsequent malunion with interval callus formation at both the ulna and radius fracture sites. Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: This patient has a limited range of motion secondary to the loss of her radial bow. Transitioning to a functional brace and initiating range of motion exercises or reapplying a cast in situ are not the best next steps.
Answers 3 and 4: Attempts at a closed reduction either manually or via cast wedging are likely to be unsuccessful due to the presence of healing callus. Open removal of the callus and osteoclasis is required to realign the fracture.
