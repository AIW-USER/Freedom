## Question

A 9-year-old girl was playing basketball and suffered a twisting injury to her right knee with her foot planted. She had immediate inability to bear weight, and by the time she was taken to the training room there was visible swelling about her knee. Given her age, which of the following is true regarding appropriate initial imaging for her injury?

## Options

1. MRI should be done right away to assess for ligamentous injury
2. A stress radiograph should be done rule out physeal injuries
3. CT scan is needed to rule out tibial spine avulsion
4. AP, lateral, and oblique radiographs of the knee
5. A notch view of her knee is needed to rule out tibial tubercle avulsion

## Correct option

**4. AP, lateral, and oblique radiographs of the knee**

## Answer explanation

Initial evaluation of a torsional knee injury in a skeletally immature patient should include non-stress radiographs of the knee. This is typically sufficient to diagnose pediatric fracture patterns (such as distal femoral physeal fractures) about the knee.

Skeletally immature patients have a variety of injuries about the knee that differ from adults. These include distal femoral physeal fractures, tibial tubercle fractures, and tibial spine avulsions. Ligamentous injuries can also occur similar to adults. Radiographs, such as AP, lateral, and oblique views should be part of the initial evaluation of these patients and typically can detect these fracture patterns when present. Other views to consider are sunrise views to assess the patellofemoral joint or notch views if concerned for an OCD. Stress radiographs to confirm non-displaced Salter-I distal femoral physeal fractures can be done, but have fallen out of favor due to patient discomfort and the need for sedation to properly stress the knee. If a Salter-I fracture is suspected and confirmation is required, current recommendations would be to protect the knee in a long leg cast and obtain repeat radiographs in 2-3 weeks. Alternatively, MRI is a sensitive test for non-displaced physeal fractures, but is not routinely ordered for this purpose.

*Mayer et al. review the evaluation and management of pediatric knee injuries including distal femoral physeal fractures.* *They comment that standard radiographs including an oblique projection should be routinely done during the initial evaluation, and are often diagnostic of even non-displaced physeal fractures.* CT and MRI should not be part of routine evaluation for pediatric knee injuries and ordered only when needed to confirm diagnosis not made with the screening plain films.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: MRI can be used when history and physical exam findings suggest ligamentous injury, but are not part of the initial evaluation
Answer 2: Stress radiographs have fallen out of favor and are not part of routine initial evaluation for these injuries.
Answer 3: Tibial spine avulsions are typically visible on standard radiographic views. CT can be useful for confirmation of equivocal cases or surgical decision making, but is not always necessary.
Answer 5: Tibial tubercle avulsion fractures are best seen on a lateral projection of the knee.
