## Question

A 32-year-old hemophiliac patient presents after falling off a roof while at work with severe right thigh pain and inability to walk. Radiographs are shown in figures A and B. On physical examination, the patient has very tense compartments and is complaining of paresthesias in the right lower extremity. The patient's pain is uncontrolled with intravenous opioid analgesics. Laboratory analysis reveals the patient to have a factor VIII activity of 5% and very high titers of factor VIII inhibitor. What is the best treatment option at this time?

## Options

1. IMN of the fracture, thigh fasciotomy, and blood replenishment as needed
2. Replenish factor VIII level to 100% followed by IMN of the fracture and fasciotomy
3. Replenish factor VIII level to 50% followed by IMN of the fracture and replenishment of blood products as needed
4. Administer factor VIIa immediately and proceed with fasciotomy and IMN of the fracture
5. Immediate fasciotomy and plating of fracture with factor VIII replenishment at the time of surgery

## Correct option

**4. Administer factor VIIa immediately and proceed with fasciotomy and IMN of the fracture**

## Answer explanation

The ideal treatment approach for this patient would be to achieve hemostasis with factor VIIa, given the high titers of factor VIII inhibitor, and proceed immediately with a thigh fasciotomy and intramedullary nail of the fracture.

Hemophilia is an X-linked recessive genetic defect that results in abnormally low levels of factor VIII (hemophilia A) or factor IX (hemophilia B). Hemophilia A is the most common, affecting 1/5000 males. This can lead to multiple orthopedic manifestations, including recurrent large joint hemarthroses leading to severe hemophilic arthropathy, intramuscular hematomas potentially causing acute compartment syndrome or acute nerve entrapment syndromes, and fractures secondary to osteopenia. Treatment involves factor VIII replacement and maintenance of at least 50%. Some patients may develop high levels of factor VIII inhibitor due to an immunological response to chronic factor VIII replacement. In acute bleeding episodes, these patients can be given activated factor VII, which bypasses factor VIII mechanism of action and provides hemostasis.

*Dumontier et al. performed a retrospective study of 17 hemophiliac patients presenting with neurologic deficits of the upper extremity.* **The authors reported** that 12 patients presented with acute compartment syndrome and five patients presented with acute nerve entrapment syndrome. **The authors concluded** that factor replacement and splinting is the first step in the management of acute compartment syndrome and nerve entrapment in hemophiliac patients with surgical management indicated after no improvement after 24 hours.

*Carr et al. reported a case of a patient with Hemophilia A with high titers of factor VIII inhibitor that underwent a total knee arthroplasty for severe hemophilic arthropathy.* **The authors reported** that hemostatic control was successful using recombinant factor VIIa, 85 mcg/kg over 3-5 minutes, given immediately prior to surgery and repeated every 2 hours for the first 48 hours after surgery. **The authors recommended** that the tourniquet be released immediately following factor VIIa infusion and aggressive physical therapy initiated in the early postoperative period in patients requiring factor VIIa.

*Chuansumrit et al. reported on a case series of five Hemophiliac patients with high titers of factor VIII inhibitor presenting with acute bleeding episodes treated with factor VIIa.* *Of the nine bleeding episodes included in the study, 2/3 episodes had bleeding controlled with a continuous infusion of factor VIIa and 3/6 episodes were controlled with bolus injections of factor VIIa.* **They also reported** that continuous infusion reduced the total dose of factor VIIa by 50%. **They concluded** that the use of factor VIIa by either bolus or continuous infusion was effective at controlling bleeding episodes in hemophiliac patients with high titer levels of factor VIII inhibitor.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs of the right femur demonstrating a comminuted transverse shaft fracture with varus malalignment and shortening. Illustration A is a diagram of the coagulation cascade. Note that activated factor VII bypasses factor VIII mechanism of action.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The patient has a very low factor VIII activity, which would make bleeding control very difficult in this case. The patient's coagulation deficits need to be addressed in order to control the worsening of compartment syndrome and permit surgical intervention.
Answers 2 and 3: Factor VIII replenishment is unlikely to be effective in this patient given the high factor VIII inhibitor levels. Postponing surgery in this patient that is developing compartment syndrome could potentially compromise his limb. Addressing the coagulation defects in this patient in an urgent fashion combined with emergent surgical intervention is indicated.
Answer 5: Plating of this fracture is not the optimal treatment option. The standard of care for a transverse femoral shaft fracture is a reamed, statically-lock intramedullary nail. Additionally, addressing the coagulation defect after surgical intervention would expose this patient to a high amount of intraoperative blood loss.
