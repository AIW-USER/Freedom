## Question

A 19-year-old collegiate defensive back walks off the field with vague pain in his left arm following a tackle. He has not had any issues with the left arm prior to this play but did have a right shoulder instability event 2 months prior for which he has been playing with a harness. As the sideline physician, what physical exam finding would lead you to keep him from immediately returning to play at this time?

## Options

1. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion with 4/5 deltoid strength
2. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion but pain with greater tuberosity palpation
3. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion but pain with AC joint palpation
4. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion but with minor pain with O'Brien's test
5. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion but with minor pain with dynamic labral shear test

## Correct option

**1. Full active and painless shoulder range of motion with 4/5 deltoid strength**

## Answer explanation

Of all the above physical exam findings, deltoid weakness would signify some degree of nerve injury, in this case, a stinger, for which the team physician should keep him from immediately returning to play at this time.

The situation above is common for any team or community physician covering youth sporting events. Though examination can be difficult in these situations, motor evaluation is paramount to screen for potentially catastrophic injuries not readily apparent at first evaluation. Transient brachial plexopathies, or stingers, are due to tension across the peripheral nerves of the plexus and can manifest with variable degrees of motor weakness involving the deltoid and biceps. This can be subtle and confused for shoulder pathology so diligent examination is crucial. Decision on return to play may be considered if motor deficits have completely resolved, which is often the case for a stinger.

*Vaccarro et al. penned a literature review in 2002 regarding return to play decision making for athletes with cervical spine pathology.* At the time there was no consensus on guidelines for return to play criteria involving spinal injuries. **They note** the significant burden of both noninvolved and involved parties on this process but advocate for the clinician to rely closely on the radiographic extent of injury, physical exam, and the patent's recovery response.

*Weinberg et al. reviewed the management of stingers.* *They described the most common injury mechanism being traction to the brachial plexus due to neck abduction to the contralateral shoulder with or without shoulder depression.* These injuries are usually self-limited neurapraxias resolving in minutes. **They caution** against allowing the return to play for athletes with prolonged symptoms, and urge disqualification from event participation if bilateral symptoms exist or the player has had recurrent stinger symptoms.

*Safran et al. comprehensively reviewed the various neurological disorders about the shoulder.* Etiology and natural history vary significantly but there is a large overlap in symptoms, making history and physical exam essential for a prompt diagnosis. In regards to returning to play following a stinger, full and painless shoulder motion with symmetric strength is required, in addition to the absence of neck pain.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2- A rotator cuff contusion does not require removal from play unless the player feels he cannot protect himself during competition.
Answer 3- An AC separation does not require removal from play unless this prevents the player from the full function of the arm.
Answers 4 and 5- Biceps and/or superior labral pathology can be disabling but with full shoulder strength, range of motion, and no instability findings, does not necessitate disqualification from play.
