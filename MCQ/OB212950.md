## Question

A 13-year-old male sustains the injury shown in Figure A. He is admitted at 10 pm with plans for surgical treatment in the morning. Overnight, he requires an increase in narcotics to control his pain. On physical examination, he has diffuse tenderness to palpation and is unable to comply with the examination. He appears distressed and his leg appears more swollen than when he was admitted. What is the next best step in management?

![[q212950-a-tibial-tubercle.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Emergent vascular exploration
2. Emergent fasciotomies with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF)
3. Elevation and observation
4. Emergent closed reduction and percutaneous screw placement
5. Urgent ORIF only

## Correct option

**2. Emergent fasciotomies with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a tibial tubercle fracture and has developed compartment syndrome. The next best step in treatment would be emergent fasciotomies with ORIF.

Fractures of the tibial tubercle have a well-known association with compartment syndrome even though these injuries are usually not associated with high-energy mechanisms. This is due to an injury to the recurrent anterior tibial artery which causes bleeding into the anterior compartment. Once compartment syndrome develops, emergent fasciotomies become necessary.

*Livingston et al. performed a review of pediatric acute compartment syndrome.* **They report** that the three As (anxiety, agitation, increased analgesic requirement) have supplanted the classic adult signs. **They conclude** that outcomes in children after fasciotomy are satisfactory and the diagnosis should not be missed or delayed.

*Schiller et al. performed a review of lower extremity avulsion fractures in the pediatric and adolescent athlete.* **They report** that avulsion injuries such as the tibial tubercle usually require immediate surgical fixation due to the risk of compartment syndrome. **They conclude** that these injuries should be treated surgically as these fractures rarely heal due to the strong contraction of the quadriceps tendon causing fracture displacement.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the knee demonstrating a tibial tubercle fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient is showing signs and symptoms of compartment syndrome and requires fasciotomies.
Answer 3: The patient is demonstrating signs of compartment syndrome and as such, there is no role for conservative management.
Answer 4: While closed reduction and percutaneous screw placement may be appropriate for some tibial tubercle fractures, this patient is showing signs and symptoms of compartment syndrome and requires fasciotomies.
Answer 5: While ORIF is appropriate treatment of the tibial tubercle fracture, this patient is showing signs and symptoms of compartment syndrome and requires fasciotomies in addition to ORIF.
