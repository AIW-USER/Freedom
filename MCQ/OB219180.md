## Question

A 36-year-old male is involved in a motor vehicle collision that required prolonged extrication. He is sedated and intubated upon early arrival to the emergency department due to his deteriorating clinical condition. He undergoes emergent open laparotomy for a severe splenic laceration and remains on sympathomimetic medications for stabilization. The ICU nurse noticed increasing swelling in his right calf and a radiograph was obtained (Figure A). The junior orthopedic resident evaluates the patient and is concerned about acute compartment syndrome. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Monitor serial myoglobinuria levels
2. Serial physical examinations with manual palpation of leg compartments
3. Measure intra-compartmental pressures
4. Perform emergent fasciotomies at the bedside through a single-incision approach
5. Proceed to the OR for emergent intramedullary nailing and two-incision fasciotomies

## Correct option

**3. Measure intra-compartmental pressures**

## Answer explanation

Compartment syndrome can be diagnosed in the awake and alert patient through corroboration of the patient’s history and serial physical examinations. However, establishing the diagnosis in obtunded patients is more difficult and reliant upon performing intra-compartmental pressure monitoring (Answer 3).

Acute compartment syndrome occurs within a defined muscle compartment where the compartmental pressure rises to a level that decreases the blood supply and perfusion to the muscle within the compartment. Regardless of the anatomic region involved, younger age (20s-30s) has consistently been a risk factor, owed to relatively high muscle bulk and thus reduced swelling capacity within the fascial compartments. Pain and swelling of the involved extremity are early signs, particularly if the patient demonstrates increasing analgesic requirements. In awake and alert patients, the diagnosis can be established via serial physical examinations without needle manometry. However, there is growing evidence for the lack of reliability in the physical examination and most authors recommend measuring compartment pressures in all patients. In the obtunded/sedated patient, needle manometry becomes necessary to establish the diagnosis with a pressure differential of <30 mmHg diagnostic for compartment syndrome.

*Duckworth and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of the diagnosis of compartment syndrome.* *They review that pressure differential (“delta-P”) can be calculated by the difference in the patient’s diastolic blood pressure and the measured compartment pressure.* If the pressure differential is less than 30 mmHg, it is diagnostic for compartment syndrome and the patient should undergo emergent fasciotomies. **The authors also highlight** that repeated needle manometry over 2 hours, or continuous compartmental pressure monitoring, is a superior diagnostic modality to a single measurement alone. **They conclude** that all patients with potential acute compartment syndrome should undergo continuous compartmental monitoring for 2 hours, as it serves as the most reliable diagnostic modality.

*Shuler and colleagues sought to determine the sensitivity of manual palpation for detecting critical pressure elevations in the leg compartments most frequently involved in clinical compartment syndrome.* *They utilized fresh cadaveric models with artificially induced compartment syndrome and had various orthopedic residents/attendings palpate the cadavers and record if* **they believed** there was underlying compartment syndrome. **The authors reported** that manual palpation had a sensitivity of 24% and a specificity of 55% for diagnosing compartment syndrome. **They concluded** that manual detection of compartment firmness associated with critical elevations in compartmental pressure is poor, highlighting the need for other diagnostic modalities, particularly in obtunded patients.

Osborn and Schmidt provided the AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) on the management of acute compartment syndrome. After reviewing 3,600 abstracts and more than 480 full-text articles, 15 recommendations were included in the CPG. The working group found very limited evidence in the management of obtunded or intoxicated patients and recommended repeated or continuous intra-compartmental pressure measurements to rule out acute compartment syndrome. The authors concluded that physical examination alone can result in multiple false positive cases of acute compartment syndrome and more high-quality research is needed to establish a gold-standard diagnostic modality algorithm.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is very limited evidence of utilizing myoglobinuria in the diagnosis of compartment syndrome. Serum lactate and troponin levels are considered more appropriate laboratory tests, however, still have inferior diagnostic value to repeated or continuous needle manometry.
Answer 2: Serial physical examinations with manual palpation of leg compartments would be considered appropriate if the patient was awake and alert.
Answer 4&5: Fasciotomies are not without iatrogenic risks, and a reliable diagnostic modality should be employed before proceeding to the operative theatre. All obtunded patients should undergo repeated or continuous compartment pressure monitoring before performing fasciotomies.
