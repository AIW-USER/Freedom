## Question

## Options

1. Corticosteroid injection of the plantar fascia
2. Flexor hallucis longus tendon repair
3. Open reduction and internal fixation of the visualized fracture
4. Posterior tibialis tendon reconstruction with autograft
5. Use of a University of California, Berkely (UCBL) orthosis with activity

## Correct option

**5. Use of a University of California, Berkely (UCBL) orthosis with activity**

## Answer explanation

The patient has an incidental accessory navicular seen on the imaging provided in Figures A-C, which should initially be treated conservatively with shoe wear modifications or orthoses such as the University of California, Berkely (UCBL) orthosis to offload the medial arch when symptomatic.

Adolescent accessory navicular represents a variant of normal that is present in up to 12% of the population and occurs clinically as a plantar medial enlargement of the navicular bone. Though it may coexist with pes planovalgus deformity or posterior tibialis tendon insufficiency (PTTI), the majority of patients are asymptomatic. Symptomatic patients, however, may present after a perceived inversion injury to the ankle or as a result of chronic overuse that typically clinically presents as medial arch pain with or without swelling over the posterior tibialis tendon insertion. First-line treatment involves non-invasive measures including activity restriction, shoe modification, and anti-inflammatory medications. Arch supports can be helpful, but the University of California, Berkely (UCBL) orthosis has shown to be particularly helpful given that it inverts the heel with walking and may directly decrease pressure on the accessory bone, thereby decreasing symptoms with activity. If shoe wear and activity modifications do not help, then a brief period of cast immobilization may be required. Finally, recalcitrant cases refractory to extended non-operative management can be treated with excision of the symptomatic, accessory bone.

*Leonard et al. provide a review of adolescent accessory navicular.* **The authors note** that the condition often becomes symptomatic in young athletes from medial pressure of the accessory ossicle against athletic shoes, causing complaints of pain and tenderness along the medial midfoot region. Though initial management is always non-invasive with shoe wear modifications, **the authors conclude** that in patients with a small symptomatic accessory navicular and a native navicular that is normal in shape with no significant planovalgus deformity, simple excision is reasonable, and in most circumstances, can be accomplished without significantly disrupting the continuity of the posterior tibial tendon.

*Jegal et al. published on accessory navicular (AN) in the athlete versus the general population.* *The authors compared 29 athletes with AN to 50 non-athletes with AN and found that there was a history of trauma in 23 feet (74%) of the athlete group and in only 13 feet (39%) of the non-athlete group, with 34% of the non-athletes improving after conservative treatment, but only 6.9% of athletes showing the same degree of improvement.* **The authors concluded** that the radiologic findings and clinical course of athletes were different from those of the general population, and their symptoms were more often refractory to conservative treatment than those of the non-athlete group.

Figures A-C represent AP, lateral, and oblique radiographs of a foot showing an accessory navicular. Illustration A represents a University of California, Berkely (UCBL) orthosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient has imaging evidence of an accessory navicular, not symptomatic plantar fasciitis.
Answer 2: Flexor hallucis longus release, tenosynovectomy, and repair are treatments for FHL tendinitis, which is not exhibited in this patient.
Answer 3: An accessory navicular can be excised if symptoms are recalcitrant to non-operative management, but ORIF is not indicated.
Answer 4: Posterior tibialis tendon dysfunction is associated with pes planovalgus deformity (flatfoot), which the patient does not appear to have clinically or radiographically.
