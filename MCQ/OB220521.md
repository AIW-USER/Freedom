## Question

A 65-year-old female trips out of her shower after a syncopal episode and sustains a closed tibial shaft fracture. She is evaluated by the orthopedic team and placed in a long-leg splint. The cardiology team evaluates her and finds she has a significant arrhythmia, putting her at high risk for an orthopedic procedure. The patient is requesting alternatives to surgical management of her tibia fracture. Which of the following is a contraindication to treating this tibial shaft fracture in a functional brace?

## Options

1. Same level fibula fracture
2. Transverse tibial shaft fracture
3. Sagittal alignment of 9° on repeat radiographs in the splint
4. 7° of rotational malalignment found on repeat radiographs in the splint
5. Comminuted fracture with tibial shortening of 24 mm

## Correct option

**5. Comminuted fracture with tibial shortening of 24 mm**

## Answer explanation

Tibial shortening of over 10 mm is a contraindication to nonoperative management (answer choice 5), as this can result in a clinically significant leg length discrepancy, potentially causing gait abnormalities, pelvic tilt, and compensatory back or hip pain.

Tibial shaft fractures are among the most common long bone fractures and can be effectively treated with functional bracing in select patients. Functional bracing involves early mobilization and weight-bearing in a removable brace that allows controlled axial loading while maintaining acceptable fracture alignment. Ideal candidates have closed, isolated fractures with acceptable initial alignment (<10° sagittal angulation, <5° coronal angulation, < 1 cm of shortening, >50% cortical apposition, and <10° rotational malalignment). After a period of acute swelling subsides, patients are transitioned to a functional brace that promotes fracture healing through controlled loading, stimulating callus formation. *Sarmiento et al.* **found** that the average time to union with functional bracing was about 18 weeks. Contraindications to functional bracing include open fractures, significant soft tissue injury, fractures with intra-articular extension, unacceptable alignments, and patients unable to comply with brace use.

*Sarmiento et al. published a 1995 study evaluating the efficacy of functional bracing for the treatment of 1,000 closed diaphyseal tibial fractures.* **The researchers found** that patient age and fracture location did not significantly affect healing time. The final limb shortening was 4.28 mm on average (closely matching the initial shortening of 4.25 mm), and 90% of patients exhibited final angular deformities of less than 6°. **The authors concluded** that functional bracing is an effective, low-morbidity, and cost-efficient treatment for closed tibial shaft fractures in select patients.

*Building upon their previous study, Sarmiento et al. reviewed the cohort of 1,000 closed tibial shaft fractures with the aim of correlating fracture and patient characteristics with healing outcomes.* *The study found that neither fracture location nor type significantly influenced healing duration.* Factors affecting a fracture's outcome include the mechanism of injury, initial shortening and angulation, time of introduction of function, and fragment displacement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: A fibular fracture at the same level as the tibial shaft fracture is not a contraindication to functional bracing. On the contrary, an intact fibula has been reported (Sarmiento, 1995) to be a relative contraindication to functional bracing due to the known varus angulation that can result in the tibia.
Answer choice 2: Transverse tibial shaft fractures are certainly not a contraindication to functional bracing in isolation. Different studies have evaluated fracture types for their success with functional bracing. *However, Sarmiento and colleagues have found that even unstable (oblique, spiral, or comminuted) fracture patterns can be successfully treated with functional bracing, assuming reduction parameters are upheld.*
Answer choice 3: Although the maximal degree of acceptable coronal and sagittal angulation is debated, a sagittal alignment of <10° is widely accepted as tolerable for functional bracing. Therefore, a sagittal alignment of 9° would not be a contraindication to functional bracing.
Answer choice 4: A rotational malalignment of <10° is acceptable for tibial shaft fractures being treated nonoperatively.
