## Question

A 55-year-old female smoker falls on icy pavement and has immediate shoulder pain and crepitus. She is seen in the ED and found to have a 4-part proximal humerus fracture. Open reduction and internal fixation is not thought to be feasible based on the fracture pattern, therefore, hemiarthroplasty is employed. On postoperative radiographs, the greater tuberosity is situated proximal to the level of the superior humeral head. What complication is this patient at increased risk for based on these findings?

## Options

1. Greater tuberosity nonunion
2. Tuberosity impingement
3. Overstuffing of glenohumeral joint
4. Over tensioning of the rotator cuff
5. Tuberosity is positioned correctly - no increased risk

## Correct option

**2. Tuberosity impingement**

## Answer explanation

Superior greater tuberosity malpositioning after hemiarthroplasty increases the risk for impingement of the greater tuberosity on the acromion. To avoid this complication, the greater tuberosity should be positioned 6-8mm distally to the superior aspect of the humeral head.

Hemiarthroplasty is used less commonly since reverse shoulder arthroplasty has been popularized, but it can still be useful in young patients or laborers with rotator cuff arthropathy, osteonecrosis or complex proximal humerus fractures. Fixation and proper placement of the tuberosities is an important consideration when hemiarthroplasty is used to treat proximal humerus fractures as the degree of shoulder rotation can be significantly affected postoperatively. The greater tuberosity should be ideally be positioned 6-8mm below the superior aspect of the humeral head. Final tuberosity positioning that is too proximal will lead to impingement of the greater tuberosity on the acromion in abduction. Inferior tuberosity impingement may lead to over tensioning of the rotator cuff, stiffness and lack of motion.

*Boileau et al. reviewed tuberosity malpositioning and migration after hemiarthroplasty for proximal humerus fractures.* **They noted** that tuberosity malpositioning was noted in 27% of patients initially and 50% of patients were noted to have malpositioning at final follow-up. **They concluded** that risk factors for malpositioning included failure of fixation, poor initial position of the prosthesis, poor position of the greater tuberosity and older women >75 with poor bone quality.

*Wiesel et al. reviewed pitfalls of shoulder hemiarthroplasty when used to treat fractures.* **They note** that the two most important technical considerations that affect outcomes are restoration of the patient's humeral head height/version and the healing of the tuberosities. **They conclude** that if these two components are fulfilled, hemiarthroplasty can be effectively used in this population to provide pain relief.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While tuberosity nonunion is possible and of concern after hemiarthroplasty, the position relative to the center of the humeral head does not increase the risk for nonunion.
Answer 3: The position of the greater tuberosity does not affect the size of the hemiarthroplasty implant used or increase the risk for overstuffing.
Answer 4: When the greater tuberosity is fixed too distally, it can lead to over tensioning of the rotator cuff. However, in this case, the tuberosity has been positioned proximally.
Answer 5: The greater tuberosity should be positioned 6-8mm distally to the superior aspect of the humeral head on postoperative radiographs.
