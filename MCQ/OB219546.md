## Question

You are on your pediatric orthopedic surgery rotation and you are counseling patients and parents on the causes of in-toeing. You note that different causes of in-toeing typically resolve by a particular age. When explaining these causes, in what order (by age) do they typically resolve?

## Options

1. Femoral anteversion, internal tibial torsion, metatarsus adductus
2. Metatarsus adductus, femoral anteversion, internal tibial torsion
3. Metatarsus adductus, internal tibial torsion, femoral anteversion
4. Internal tibial torsion, metatarsus adductus, femoral anteversion
5. Metatarsus adductus, external tibial torsion, femoral anteversion

## Correct option

**3. Metatarsus adductus, internal tibial torsion, femoral anteversion**

## Answer explanation

The causes of in-toeing tend to resolve in a stepwise fashion, with metatarsus adductus (MA) resolving first, followed by internal tibial torsion (ITT), and femoral anteversion (FA).

In-toeing in young children is incredibly common and in most cases self-resolves without the need for any surgical intervention. Causes are typically related to a child's age with MA being the most common cause in younger infants and thought to be related to intrauterine packaging. It involves adduction of the forefoot and a kidney bean shape to the foot and the deformity is typically flexible. ITT usually affects toddlers and younger children and is commonly bilateral. It can be diagnosed with an abnormal thigh-foot angle >10°. If ITT hasn't resolved by age 6-8, some would consider derotational tibial osteotomy, but the need for this is quite rare. Femoral anteversion is the other common cause attributable to in-toeing and usually affects older children, self-resolving by around age 10. It can be diagnosed with a significant increase in the degree of internal hip rotation (>70°). Classically, children are able to sit in the 'W' position. Bracing and PT have not been shown to alter the natural history of this condition. If FA persists into teen years and there are issues with hip pain/laxity or patellar instability, a proximal femoral derotational osteotomy can be considered.

*Lincoln and Suen published a review on common rotational variations in children, focusing on causes of in-toeing, out-toeing, and torticollis.* With regards to in-toeing, **they note** that it is usually attributable to MA in infants, ITT in toddlers, and FA in older children < 10 years old. They mention that most of these causes self-resolve without any need for additional treatment.

*Staheli et al. looked at 1,000 normal lower extremities in children and adults to help establish a "normal" rotational profile.* **They note** that intrauterine positioning often causes early abnormalities in lower extremity rotation. **They note** that most rotational abnormalities need no treatment and self-resolve.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,2,4,5: Metatarsus adductus usually resolves by age 4, internal tibial torsion by age 6-7, and femoral anteversion by age 10.
