## Question

A 10 days old neonate is brought to you in the OPD. Picture of the neonate is given below. What will you suggest to parents for lower limb deformities?

![[TO17138308-1.png]]

## Options

1. Serial stretching and splinting
2. Wait until neonate gains 10 kg weight and then posteromedial release for foot deformity
3. Wait until neonate gains 5 kg weight and then serial stretching
4. Wait until age of one month to start serial stretching

## Correct option

**1. Serial stretching and splinting**

## Answer explanation

This is a case of arthrogryposis multiplex congenita.

Agryposis multiplex congenita is a physical finding that can be seen in around 300 different disorders. Arthrogryposis usually is a nonprogressive syndrome characterized by deformed, rigid joints that affect two or more areas of the body.

The involved muscles or muscle groups are atrophied or absent. The involved extremities appear cylindrical, fusiform, or cone-shaped and have diminished skin creases and subcutaneous tissue. Contracture of the joint capsule and periarticular tissues is present. Dislocation of the joints is common, especially of the hip and knee. Sensation and intellect are normal.

Incidence: 1 in 3000 live births.

Lower extremity deformities: rigid clubfoot and fixed extension or flexion contractures of the knees.

Upper extremity deformities: adducted, and internally rotated shoulders; elbow extension contractures; severe, fixed palmar flexion and ulnar-deviated deformities of the wrist; and contractures of the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints with flexed fingers and adducted thumb.

Involvement usually is bilateral but not always symmetric. Scoliosis has been reported to occur in 10% to 30% of patients.

Etiology: neurological (most common, 80% cases), muscular abnormalities, connective tissue disorders, intrauterine space constraint, vascular compromise, maternal disease, and teratogenic exposure.

Treatment: Passive stretching exercises for each contracted joint, should be started after birth and it should be followed by serial splinting with custom thermoplastic splints.

Knee and hip surgery should be done by 6 to 9 months of age. Foot surgery should be done close to the time when the patient normally begins to stand to decrease the incidence of recurrence.

Foot deformities: The Ponseti method of clubfoot casting has been used successfully in patients with arthrogryposis and clubfeet, but a greater number of casts are required than for idiopathic clubfeet, and the relapse rate is high. If casting fails, an extensive posteromedial and posterolateral release is recommended. If the deformity recurs in a young child or is so severe that it cannot be corrected by posteromedial soft-tissue release, talectomy is indicated. Triple arthrodesis may be performed for rigid deformity in adolescents. Gradual correction of the deformity with circular-frame external fixators is an alternative method for obtaining a plantigrade foot.

Knee deformities: Two most common deformities around the knee are a flexion contracture and an extension contracture. Initial treatment of flexion contractures is by serial splinting or casting in progressive degrees of extension. Ambulation is possible with a residual knee flexion contracture of 15 to 20 degrees. If complete correction has not been obtained by 6 to 12 months of age, posterior medial and lateral hamstring lengthening and knee capsulotomies are indicated. Supracondylar extension osteotomy of the distal femur can be done at near skeletal maturity to correct a contracture and allow use of orthoses.

Extension contracture: Contracture of the quadriceps mechanism can cause hyperextension of the knee, which is treated initially by serial casting. If the deformity does not respond to conservative treatment by 6 to 12 months of age, surgical correction by quadriceps plasty is recommended.

Hip deformities: In general, hip deformities should be treated by passive stretching exercises, beginning in infancy. If conservative measures fail, surgical correction of the hip deformity should be delayed until deformities of the knees have been corrected. Mild hip flexion contractures may be accommodated by an increase in lumbar lordosis. Flexion contractures of more than 45 degrees should have surgical release.

Hip dislocation’s: Unilateral dislocation of the hip, whether flexible or rigid, should be reduced surgically and placed in a functional position to avoid potential pelvic obliquity and scoliosis. Traditional recommendations are that bilateral teratologic hip dislocations should not be reduced because reduction may not improve function so the treatment of bilateral dislocations should be individualized.

Upper limb: Correction of upper extremity deformities should be delayed until ambulation has been achieved, usually by 3 to 4 years of age. If surgery is delayed until age 8 years, the use patterns are so well established that the child would not adapt as well to surgical correction. Function may be adequate despite severe deformity in children with arthrogryposis; the benefits of surgical intervention must be carefully weighed against the risk of surgery.

The stiff flexed elbow is not a severe impairment, and surgery is not indicated. Fixed extension elbow deformity, especially if bilateral, is a severe functional impairment. Triceps V-Y lengthening and posterior capsulectomy at 1.5 to 3 years is required to get flexion.

The wrist is usually flexed and in an ulnar-deviated position. Neutral or mild ulnar deviation and dorsiflexion between 5 and 20 degrees proves to be the most satisfactory position. Procedures described for the arthrogrypotic wrist are tendon transfers, proximal row carpectomy, dorsal radial closing wedge osteotomy of the midcarpus, and wrist fusion. Wrist palmar flexion contracture can be corrected with flexor carpi ulnar is lengthening or transfer to the wrist extensors.

Answer: 1
