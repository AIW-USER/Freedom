## Question

True about peroneal tendon tear is

## Options

1. Peroneus longus tear is more common than peroneus brevis tear
2. Pesplanus is a risk factor
3. Rapid forced dorsiflexion combined with inversion of the foot is the most common mechanism of tear

## Correct option

**3. Rapid forced dorsiflexion combined with inversion of the foot is the most common mechanism of tear**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C

EXPLANATION: -

Peroneal Tendon Splits and Tears

Due to its vulnerable position between the fibula and the PL, the PB tendon is most prone to tear. Split lesions of the peroneus brevis tendon can result from two mechanisms:

 The first suggests subluxation of the peroneus brevis tendon occuring as a result of the laxity or tearing of the SPR from chronic ankle instability. In doing so, mechanical attrition occurs over the posterolateral edge of the ﬁbula.

The second mechanism suggests compression of the peroneus brevis tendon between the posterior ﬁbula and peroneus longus tendon causes a split during an inversion injury.

Originally described as an anatomical classification for peroneus longus tears, the Brandes Smith classification is useful for both brevis and longus tears:

1. - zone A encapsulates tears occurring under the superior retinaculum, typically from subluxation or due to the mass effect of a peroneus quartus or low lying muscle brevis belly;

2. - zone B is the region of the inferior peroneal retinaculum, where tears maybe associated with peroneal tubercle hypertrophy;

3. - zone C is at the cuboid notch and is the region where longus pathology occurs most often.

Mechanism of injury: Rapid forced dorsiflexion combined with inversion of the foot is the most common mechanism of superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) tear. SPR tear leads to peroneal instability.

Risk factors:

Anatomical factors: enlarged peroneal tubercle, low-lying muscle belly of peroneus brevis, accessory peroneus quartus or quintus, cavovarus hindfoot alignment, os peroneum, flat or concave retromalleolar sulcus

Injuries: lateral ligament (ATFL/CFL) tear, calcaneal malunion and subfibular impingement

Disease: Charcot-Marie Tooth

Classification: Redfern and Meyerson Peroneal Tendon Tear Classification

Type I Both tendons intact but with partial tearing

Type II One tendon is intact but other is majority torn

Type IIIa Both tendons are majority torn (unusable) and muscle belly has no excursion

Type IIIb Both tendons are majority torn (unusable) but muscle belly has excursion

Treatment: Initial treatment of peroneal tendon tears should entail rest, activity modification, and graduated physical therapy to promote healing. When conservative treatment fails, various surgical techniques have been described based on the extent and nature of the tear.

 Surgical Repair of Tendon Splits

Surgical repair involves accessing the retromalleolar groove via a lateral approach, incising the SPR, and inspecting the tendons. After debriding the split and removing the thinner section, the tendon is tubularized if more than 50% of the brevis remains (Debridement and tubularization). If less than 50% remains post-debridement, a tenodesis with the peroneus longus tendon is performed.

In cases of severe degeneration where reconstruction is not feasible, options include tendon transfer or grafting. A hamstring autograft or allograft is considered if the peroneal muscle belly has adequate excursion. If the muscle belly is scarred, tendon transfer using the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) or flexor digitorum longus (FDL) is preferred. In cases where the tendon sheath is fibrotic, a staged approach is used: initially, excising scar tissue and implanting a silastic rod, followed by tendon repair after the formation of a pseudosynovial sheath.
