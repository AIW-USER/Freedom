## Question

A 55-year-old male is undergoing a distal biceps repair with a single-incision technique. During the preparation of the footprint on the radial tuberosity, the surgeon emphasizes to their assistant the importance of keeping the arm maximally supinated to prevent neurological injury. Which of the following describes the physical examination findings if the nerve that this maneuver attempts to protect was injured?

## Options

1. Decreased sensation over the lateral forearm
2. Decreased sensation over the medial forearm
3. Weakness in finger abduction
4. Weakness in wrist extension
5. Weakness in wrist flexion

## Correct option

**4. Weakness in wrist extension**

## Answer explanation

When performing a single-incision distal biceps repair, the arm is supinated to protect the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN). A PIN injury would manifest as weakness in wrist extension (Answer choice 4).

Distal bicep injuries typically occur at the insertion of the biceps brachii tendon on the radial tuberosity. The mechanism of injury is classically described as a sudden, eccentric contraction of the muscle. Nonoperative treatment is reserved for lower-demand patients, while operative treatment is recommended for active individuals to preserve supination (~50% loss) and flexion (~30% loss) strength. Distal biceps repair can be performed with a single-incision or dual-incision technique. During a single-incision biceps repair, the arm is supinated maximally to protect the PIN. A PIN palsy would present as weakness in wrist extension with radial deviation due to the pull of the extensor carpi radialis longus (innervated by the radial nerve, proximal to PIN) and nonfunctional extensor carpi ulnaris (innervated by PIN).

*Becker et al conducted a cadaver-based anatomical study to determine the optimal drilling angle when performing a single-incision distal biceps repair.* **The authors found** that when the arm is maximally supinated, the PIN crosses the lateral cortex of the radius at the level of the biceps tuberosity from its anterior position proximally. About 1 cm distal to the biceps tuberosity, the PIN is posterior to the radius. Based on these findings, **the authors conclude** that the optimal drill direction is ulnar and proximal.

*Lynch et al conducted a cadaver-based anatomical study to determine the rate of PIN contact when using a bicortical button during distal biceps repair.* Out of the twelve cadavers the authors included, the PIN was caught under the cortical button in one specimen, within 2 mm in eight specimens, and within 6 mm in eleven specimens. **The authors conclude** that bicortical placement of a cortical button during distal biceps repair places the PIN at risk for injury.

*Thumm et al conducted a cadaver-based anatomical study to determine the distance between the PIN and a distally and ulnarly directed guide pin for distal biceps repair.* *They tested three different drilling angles: distal only, distal and 30 degrees ulnar, and 30 degrees ulnar.* **The authors found** that the 30-degree ulnar drilling angle led to the largest distance between the guide pin and the PIN.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. While this is the most common nerve injury during single-incision distal biceps repair, supination of the arm protects the PIN.
Answer 2: This describes the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve.
Answer 3: This describes the ulnar nerve.
Answer 5: This describes the median and anterior interosseous nerve.
