## Question

## Options

1. Insert wire for lag screw
2. Remove the nail and re-ream canal
3. Remove the nail and replace with a smaller diameter nail
4. Remove the nail and assess guide wire bend
5. Tighten the jig to the nail

## Correct option

**4. Remove the nail and assess guide wire bend**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained an intertrochanteric femur fracture that was treated with an intramedullary nail. The nail has been inserted over a guidewire; if the wire cannot be extracted, the nail should be removed and the bend on the guidewire should be evaluated, as the excessive bend on the wire is likely prohibiting its extraction.

Intramedullary nailing of intertrochanteric hip fractures is a common strategy that creates a load-sharing construct and allows for stability and fracture healing. After opening the canal, a ball-tipped guide wire is placed into the canal, usually with a subtle bend on it to allow it to be more easily directed distally into a "center-center" position. Prior to inserting the wire for the lag screw, the central guidewire must be removed from within the nail. If the central guidewire is too bent it may catch on the nail and become difficult to remove. If it becomes stuck, the entire nail along with the central guidewire must be removed and a new central guidewire must be introduced with less bend. It is important to recognize that, in this case, the guidewire is not holding a reduction of the intertrochanteric fracture, therefore it is acceptable to remove to correct the bend if necessary. Once the new guidewire is placed, the nail may be re-inserted. After the nail is re-inserted, the guidewire is removed and the wire for the lag screw may then be inserted.

*Horwitz et al. review nail length in treating intertrochanteric femur fractures.* **They report** that the use of long nails is based on the higher risk of peri-prosthetic diaphyseal fractures associated with short nails. The three indications for long nails are a capacious proximal canal, in the revision of short nails, and pathologic fractures. The general trend is to use short nails as they avoid the freehand distal locking needed for long nails. **They report** that there is no high-quality literature on decision-making with regards to short versus long nails. Though studies are underpowered, the reported peri-prosthetic fracture rates for short nails range from 0-3.3% and 0-1.5% for long nails.

*Riedel et al. analyzed intramedullary nail systems from multiple manufacturers to determine how much a ball-tip guidewire may be bent.* **They report** that the bend angle tolerated by a reamer is 7 degrees compared to 15.5 degrees and 16 degrees for femoral and tibial nails, respectively. **They conclude** that the diameter of the cannula for reamers is smaller than their associated nail; therefore, if a reamer can pass over a bent guidewire then so will the nail.

Figure A is an AP pelvis demonstrating an unstable right intertrochanteric fracture. Figure B is an AP of the hip demonstrating the same. Figure C demonstrates a cephalomedullary nail with a retained guidewire. Illustration A demonstrates a bent guidewire within an intramedullary nail.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Inserting the wire for the lag screw should be performed after the central guidewire is removed.
Answer 2: Re-reaming the canal will not be effective in this case as the guidewire is bent and must be replaced. In some cases, a cortical fragment can be interposed in the canal, blocking the removal of the guidewire and re-reaming may be more appropriate.
Answer 3: Downsizing the nail will not make it easier to remove the guidewire in this instance. Decreasing nail size would be appropriate if the isthmal fit was felt to be too tight for the nail to safely pass.
Answer 5: Ensuring the jig is tight is important for making sure the lag screw crosses through the nail without any divergence, however, it will not help with getting the guidewire out under these circumstances.
