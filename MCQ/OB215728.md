## Answer explanation

This patient's MRI is significant for a bankart lesion. Given his age, the most likely associated injury would be a Hill-Sachs lesion (Figure B).

Traumatic anterior shoulder instability, also referred to as TUBS (Traumatic Unilateral dislocations with a Bankart lesion requiring Surgery), represents traumatic shoulder injuries that generally occur as a result of an anterior force to the shoulder while in an abducted and externally rotated position. The diagnosis is made clinically with the presence of positive anterior instability provocative tests and confirmed with MRI studies that may reveal labrum (bankart) and/or bony injuries of the glenoid (bony bankart) and proximal humerus (Hill-sachs). Treatment may be nonoperative or operative depending on the chronicity of symptoms, recurrence of instability, and the severity of labrum and/or glenoid defects.

*Hintermann et al. prospectively reviewed the arthroscopic findings of the unstable shoulder.* **They report** that in 212 patients who had a minimum of 1 shoulder dislocation, 87% had anterior glenoid labral tears (bankart lesion) and 68% had Hill-Sachs compression fractures; glenohumeral ligament insufficiency, complete rotator cuff tears, posterior glenoid labral tears, and SLAP tears were all less common in younger patients. **They concluded** that Hill-Sachs lesions are the most common finding.

*Kralinger et al. reviewed recurrence with shoulder instability.* **They reported** that the only factor to predict recurrence was an initial dislocation that occurred between the ages of 21 and 30 years; immobilization and physical therapy were not associated with recurrence. **They concluded** that patients in this age group who participate in high-risk sports activities should undergo primary surgical stabilization because of the increased risk of recurrence.

Figure A is an axial MRI image of a bankart lesion. Figure B is a radiograph of a Hill-Sachs lesion. Figure C is a coronal MRI image of a full-thickness rotator cuff tear. Figure D is an axial MRI image of a reverse Hill-Sachs lesion which is seen in cases of posterior shoulder instability. Figure E is an axial CT image demonstrating a coracoid process fracture. Figure F is an AP radiograph of the shoulder demonstrating a greater tuberosity fracture. Illustrations A&B are internal rotation radiographs, in which the humeral head (green), greater tuberosity (yellow), and lesser tuberosity (red) are outlined. Normally, these should be the only structures creating outlines. In this case, an additional Hill-Sachs lesion (blue) is visible.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Full-thickness rotator cuff tears are much more common in elderly patients who sustain a shoulder dislocation
Answer 3: A reverse Hill-Sachs is common after a posterior (not anterior) shoulder instability event
Answer 4: A coracoid process fracture is rare following a shoulder dislocation
Answer 5: An compression fracture of the greater tuberosity (Hill-Sachs) is much more common than a complete fracture of the greater tuberosity following a shoulder instability event
