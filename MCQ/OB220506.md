## Question

A 45-year-old man presents to the orthopedic clinic 3 months after sustaining an anterior shoulder dislocation. He states that his shoulder was reduced in the emergency department approximately 18 hours after the injury. Since then, he has experienced persistent shoulder weakness and decreased sensation over the lateral aspect of his proximal arm. An MRI ordered by his primary care physician showed no evidence of a rotator cuff tear, and he was advised to begin home exercises. On physical examination, he demonstrates reduced abduction strength compared to the opposite arm, but there is no evidence of deltoid muscle contracture. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Continued observation
2. Electrodiagnostic studies
3. Repeat shoulder MRI
4. Operative exploration of the axillary nerve
5. Triceps nerve branch transfer to axillary nerve

## Correct option

**2. Electrodiagnostic studies**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with persistent axillary nerve palsy following an anterior shoulder dislocation. The most appropriate next step in management is electrodiagnostic testing (Answer 2).

The shoulder is the most commonly dislocated major joint, and nerve injury occurs in approximately 21–65% of cases. The axillary nerve is the most frequently affected, and its injury may result in sensory deficits, motor weakness, or both. It innervates the deltoid and teres minor muscles. Although an intact rotator cuff can initiate abduction, axillary nerve dysfunction—particularly deltoid involvement—can lead to significant weakness, especially between 30° and 100° of abduction. Most axillary nerve injuries are neuropraxias that resolve spontaneously, and observation is appropriate in the early post-injury period. However, persistent neurologic deficits—such as weakness and sensory loss at 12 weeks—warrant electrodiagnostic evaluation to determine the severity (i.e., low-, intermediate-, or high-grade injury) and guide further management.

*Avis et al. conducted a literature review and expert consensus to propose an algorithm for managing nerve injuries after anterior shoulder dislocation.* Risk factors for high-grade nerve injury include associated proximal humerus fracture, involvement of multiple nerves, neuropathic pain, and delayed reduction. **The authors recommend** early electrodiagnostic testing and referral to a regional center if no neurologic improvement is seen at 1 week in the presence of any high-risk features (Illustration 1).

*Tiefenboeck et al. analyzed 60 patients with nerve injuries following shoulder dislocation out of a cohort of 15,739.* *Of these, 46 (76.6%) had brachial plexus injuries, and 14 (23.3%) had isolated peripheral nerve injuries.* **The authors recommend** electrodiagnostic studies at 14–21 days if no clinical recovery is observed (Illustration 2).

Illustration 1 is a proposed management algorithm from Avis et al. Illustration 2 is a proposed management algorithm from Tiefenboeck et al.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation is no longer appropriate. Both proposed algorithms support electrodiagnostic testing at this point, given the lack of improvement at 12 weeks.
Answer 3: Repeat MRI is not indicated. The initial MRI confirmed an intact rotator cuff, and there has been no clinical change or reinjury since.
Answers 4 and 5: Surgical intervention may eventually be needed, but diagnostic studies must be performed first to determine the extent of nerve injury and guide further treatment.
