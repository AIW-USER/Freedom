## Question

She initially received spinal injections into the left L4-5 foramen with significant relief of pain, however, her most recent injection provided little benefit. On examination, there is 4/5 strength with left ankle dorsiflexion compared to the contralateral side. Radiographs and MRIs are obtained (Figures 1-3). The patient subsequently undergoes surgery (Figures 4 & 5). At her two-week postoperative visit, she reported burning pain and occasional paresthesias to the groin and left inner thigh. ## Answer explanation

This 54-year-old female demonstrated a left L4-5 foraminal stenosis and concomitant spondylolisthesis, which was treated with left L4-5 fusion through the lateral and posterior approaches. The lumbar plexus, and most commonly the genitofemoral nerve, is the most common nerve injured through the lateral approach.

The treatment of symptomatic lumbar stenosis often varies and is dependent on a myriad of factors, including patient-, physician-, and resource-related factors. Several lumbar interbody fusion approaches, namely the anterior (ALIF), lateral or extreme lateral (LLIF or XLIF), oblique (OLIF), transforaminal (TLIF), and posterior (PLIF), have been developed largely to accelerate post-operative recovery to reduce hospital stays and facilitate earlier return to work. Despite the number of approaches, there is no consensus on the best approach concerning fusion rate. Nonetheless, each approach has its unique complications, and lateral approaches involve navigating either anterior or through the psoas to gain access to the lateral lumbar body. As such, the lumbar plexus is a risk for injury during this approach, with the genitofemoral nerve being at risk in particular at the L4-5 level.

*Uribe et al. performed a study examining the lateral transpsoas approach on 20 cadavers, examining the relationship of the lumbar plexus to the psoas and lumbar vertebral body in a sagittal plane.* The genitofemoral nerve in particular was found to travel obliquely within the psoas before emerging anterior from the medial psoas border at the L3-4 level and then coursing along the anteromedial fourth of the L-4 and L-5 vertebral bodies. **The authors conclude** the safe zones for dilator placement is the mid-posterior vertebral body from L1-4, while the mid-point of the vertebral body at the L4-5 disc space is the safest.

*Hijji et al. performed a systematic review of 63 articles (6,819 patients, 11,325 levels fused) examining complication rates in patients undergoing lateral lumbar interbody fusion.* **The authors noted** transient neurologic complications as the most common complication at 36.07%, with 'transient thigh symptoms' being the most common symptom (26.51%). **They additionally note** persistent neurologic symptoms (surpassing 6 months) at a rate of 3.98%. The authors attribute the high neurological complication rate to manipulation of the psoas, and believe the approach can be considered safe as the majority of neurological symptoms resolve with time.
