## Question

A 45-year-old male presents to your office with a chief complaint of elbow pain and weakness. He states he was lifting weights when he felt a pop in his antecubital fossa. On examination, he has ecchymosis in this region with tenderness over the radial tuberosity and weakness with flexion and supination. You diagnose a distal biceps rupture and recommend repair using a suspensory button through a single-incision technique. You review the risks and rehabilitation process with the patient prior to surgery. When describing the most common complication, what are the associated physical examination findings?

## Options

1. Volar/radial forearm numbness
2. Loss of forearm pronation/supination
3. Expanding hematoma
4. Weakness of index finger extension
5. Weakness of forearm supination

## Correct option

**1. Volar/radial forearm numbness**

## Answer explanation

The most common complication after distal biceps repair is palsy of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN).

Distal biceps ruptures tend to occur in middle-aged men with excessive eccentric load to the elbow when flexed. Both elbow supination and flexion strength are affected, with supination being lost to a greater degree. Surgical repair is recommended in most cases and can be performed through a single- or two-incision technique both of which are acceptable but have slightly different complication profiles. The primary surgical complication with either technique is irritation of the LABCN. The nerve is usually just stretched due to excessive retraction and will recover function slowly within several months but does cause numbness or tingling sensations over the volar/radial aspect of the forearm.

*Ford et al. reviewed major complications after distal biceps repairs in 970 cases.* There was a major complication rate of 7.5% overall, most commonly including PIN palsy (1.9%), proximal radioulnar synostosis (1%), re-rupture (1.6%), and heterotopic ossification (0.9%). The two-incision technique had a higher rate of radioulnar synostosis, while rates of sensory neuropraxia including the LABCN were higher in the single-incision technique.

*Dunphy et al. analyzed complications after 784 distal biceps repairs.* **They noted** higher rates of PIN injury, heterotopic bone formation, and reoperation in two-incision patients compared to single-incision. The most common complication was palsy of the LABCN, which occurred in 24% of single-incision techniques compared to 4% of two-incision techniques. The rate of re-rupture in their series was 1.9%.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Radioulnar synostosis can occur more commonly after a two-incision technique secondary to heterotopic bone formation and can lead to loss of forearm pronation/supination. This occurs far less commonly than the irritation of the LABCN.
Answer 3: Laceration of the brachial artery or the uncontrolled bleeding of the radial recurrent arteries can cause an expanding hematoma in the antecubital fossa but is very uncommon.
Answer 4: Weakness of index finger extension would indicate injury to the PIN, which can occur in single incision techniques but is, again, less common than injury to the LABCN.
Answer 5: Re-rupture of the distal biceps repair occurs in less than 4-5% of cases but would lead to weakness of supination and flexion to a lesser degree.
