## Question

A 33-year-old male presents to your clinic with complaints of persistent knee pain that is primarily located over the medial joint line. He reports he was a college baseball catcher and has had pain since college that has worsened in the last year. He also complains of occasional catching and locking and notes his knee will randomly swell. He has been doing physical therapy for 3 months, as prescribed by his primary care physician, without relief from these symptoms. An MRI cut is shown in Figure A. Given his failure of nonoperative treatment, you discuss surgical management with the plan to perform an osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation. Which of the following is true regarding this technique?

## Options

1. Requires a two-stage procedure
2. OCA of the femoral condyles have worse outcomes compared to those of the patellofemoral joint
3. Cannot be used in the setting of uncontained chondral lesions
4. Can be useful in addressing subchondral bone deficiency
5. More easily available than other cartilage restoration techniques

## Correct option

**4. Can be useful in addressing subchondral bone deficiency**

## Answer explanation

Osteochondral allografts (OCA) are particularly useful solutions when addressing cartilage lesions with underlying subchondral bone involvement.

Articular cartilage lesions can occur in any joint and present challenging problems for orthopaedic surgeons and patients alike. They often cause pain, recurrent effusions, and can cause mechanical symptoms in some patients. Different surgical algorithms exist but mechanical alignment, meniscal deficiency, and ligamentous stability should be assessed and addressed first or at the time of cartilage restoration procedures. Options for surgical intervention include chondroplasty, microfracture, osteochondral autograft (OATs), OCA, or matrix autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI). Use of an OCA is helpful in cases where cartilage lesions are large (>4cm^2), uncontained, or when there is significant involvement of the underlying subchondral bone. There is a risk of infection/immune reaction given the allograft tissue, and availability/price can be a deterrent. However, there is a relatively high rate of return (>80%) to activities in young patients who are appropriately indicated.

*Bedi et al. reviewed the management of knee articular cartilage defects.* *They discuss microfracture and its goals to penetrate the subchondral plate and recruit mesenchymal cells to form fibrocartilage.* *They also discuss mosaicplasty (osteochondral autograft), which has concerns with graft availability and donor site morbidity in larger lesions.* OCA transplantation allows for viable articular cartilage and subchondral bone to address large lesions, potentially even involving an entire compartment. Finally, they discuss ACI and other synthetic scaffolds which are still being evaluated.

*Krych et al. reviewed the return to sport after surgical management of knee articular cartilage defects.* *In the overall cohort of 2,549 patients, 76% returned to sport, with the highest rates being in the osteochondral autograft (93%) and OCA (88%) cohorts.* *Patients in the OCA group returned to sports at an average of 9.6 months, which was similar to other groups.*

Figure A is a coronal T2 MRI sequence that demonstrates a large osteochondral defect showing significant underlying bony edema, reflective of subchondral bone deficiency.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: OCA transplantation is a one-stage procedure. ACI is a two-stage procedure where chondrocytes are harvested, multiplied, and implanted.
Answer 2: OCA of the patellofemoral joint has worse outcomes compared to the femoral condyles due to the increased contact pressures that are transmitted through the patellofemoral joint.
Answer 3: While microfracture techniques require a contained defect, an OCA can still be used in the setting of an uncontained defect.
Answer 5: One of the primary difficulties in using an OCA is their accessibility, as they must be matched closely to the recipient and used in a timely manner to maximize chondrocyte viability.
