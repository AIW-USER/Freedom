## Question

A 62-year-old male presents to the emergency department with acute medial knee pain and an inability to fully extend his knee. He reports feeling a "clunk" while squatting into deep flexion to pick up a tool in his garden. He underwent a mobile-bearing medial unicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKR) 8 months ago. Radiographs demonstrate a posterior dislocation of the polyethylene bearing. Which of the following intraoperative technical errors is the most likely primary cause of this specific failure mechanism?

## Options

1. Creating a flexion gap that is 2 mm tighter than the extension gap, leading to excessive rollback pressure.
2. Failing to clear anterior femoral osteophytes, leading to mechanical impingement.
3. Creating a flexion gap that is 2 mm looser than the extension gap, with the bearing sized to the tighter extension space.
4. Cutting the tibial plateau with inadequate (less than native) posterior slope, forcing the bearing out of the joint space.

## Correct option

**3. Creating a flexion gap that is 2 mm looser than the extension gap, with the bearing sized to the tighter extension space.**

## Answer explanation

Creating a flexion gap that is 2 mm looser than the extension gap, with the bearing sized to the tighter extension space.

To master UKR kinematics for exams, you must link the position of the bearing, the arc of motion, and the state of the soft tissues.

Mobile bearings are completely unconstrained; they are held in place purely by the compressive tension of the medial soft-tissue envelope (specifically the MCL) trapping the polyethylene between the femoral and tibial components.

Why C is correct (The Loose Flexion Gap): During normal kinematics, as the knee goes into deep flexion (e.g., squatting), the femoral condyle rolls posteriorly, and the unconstrained bearing glides posteriorly with it. If a surgeon leaves the flexion gap looser (wider) than the extension gap, they are forced to choose a thinner bearing to avoid overstuffing the extension gap and causing a flexion contracture. When this patient squats, the knee enters the wider flexion gap. The compressive tension from the MCL drops to zero, the joint opens up, and the posterior rollback of the femur easily "spits" the thin bearing out the back of the joint. Posterior spin-out in deep flexion due to a loose flexion gap is the most classic presentation of bearing dislocation.

Why the other options are incorrect:

 - A (Tight flexion gap): If the flexion gap is tighter than the extension gap, the bearing will be tightly compressed during flexion. This will not cause a spin-out; instead, it will limit the patient's range of motion (stiffness) and cause severe deep pain during flexion, potentially accelerating posterior tibial slope subsidence, but it will prevent dislocation.

 - B (Anterior osteophytes): Retained anterior osteophytes can cause spin-out, but the timing and direction are different. Anterior impingement pushes the bearing posteriorly, but it happens when the knee comes into terminal extension
 (as the anterior lip of the femur hits the anterior lip of the poly), not during deep flexion (squatting).

 - D (Inadequate posterior slope): A lack of posterior slope (or a reverse/anterior slope) tends to push the bearing anteriorly during flexion. Conversely, cutting excessive
 posterior slope (e.g., >10 degrees) tilts the tibial tray downhill posteriorly, which would contribute to a posterior spin-out in deep flexion.

The biomechanical mechanism of bearing dislocation (spin-out) in an Oxford or similar mobile-bearing Unicondylar Knee Replacement (UKR) is a pure failure of kinematics and soft-tissue tension. Because the polyethylene bearing is unconstrained, it relies entirely on the compressive forces of the soft-tissue envelope (primarily the superficial MCL and capsule) to remain captured between the femoral and tibial components.

If that compressive force drops to zero at any point in the arc of motion, or if an abnormal bony block levers the bearing out of its track, a spin-out occurs.

The Kinematics of the Bearing

In a properly functioning mobile-bearing UKR, the bearing moves with the femoral condyle.

 - During extension: The bearing moves anteriorly.

 - During deep flexion: The bearing moves posteriorly, following the rollback of the femoral condyle.

Because the bearing is fully congruent with the spherical femoral component, any mismatch in the joint gap directly affects the bearing's stability. The most classic presentation is posterior spin-out, which occurs when the patient goes into deep flexion and the bearing slips out the back of the joint.

Primary Surgical Errors Leading to Spin-Out

Bearing dislocation is almost never an implant failure; it is a technical error. For high-end exams and clinical practice, these are the exact surgical missteps that lead to spin-out:

1. The Loose Flexion Gap (Gap Mismatch)

This is the most common cause of posterior spin-out. The fundamental rule of mobile-bearing UKR is that the flexion and extension gaps must be exactly equal.

 - The Error: The surgeon creates a flexion gap that is 1–2 mm wider than the extension gap. Because the bearing size is usually chosen based on the tighter extension gap to prevent a flexion contracture, the implanted bearing will be too thin for the flexion gap.

 - The Biomechanics: When the patient goes into deep flexion, the collateral ligaments do not generate enough tension to compress the joint. The joint opens up, the compressive force drops to zero, and the rollback of the femur easily spits the thin bearing out posteriorly.

2. Impingement

Retained bone or osteophytes act as a mechanical lever against the bearing.

 - Anterior Impingement: Failing to clear anterior femoral or notch osteophytes. As the knee comes into extension, these osteophytes hit the anterior lip of the bearing, pushing it backward and out of the joint.

 - Posterior Impingement: Failing to resect enough posterior condylar bone. In deep flexion, this retained bone pushes the bearing anteriorly.

3. MCL Damage or Over-Release

Unlike Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA), you never release the MCL in a UKR.

 - The Error: Iatrogenic injury to the MCL with the saw blade, or attempting to "balance" a tight medial compartment by pie-crusting the ligament.

 - The Biomechanics: The MCL provides the medial tension required to capture the bearing. If it is attenuated, the medial compartment will open in valgus stress, instantly allowing the bearing to escape.

4. Component Malposition

 - Excessive Posterior Tibial Slope:
 The tibial cut should parallel the patient's native slope (usually 3–7°). If the surgeon cuts too much posterior slope (e.g., >10°), gravity and the mechanics of the joint will cause the bearing to slide downhill (posteriorly) during flexion, leading to a posterior dislocation.

 - Internal Rotation of the Tibial Tray: If the tibial component is internally rotated relative to the femoral tracking path, the bearing will edge-load and twist out of its congruent articulation during the arc of motion.

Answer: 3
