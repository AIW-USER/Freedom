## Question

A 53-year-old female presents to the clinic with complaints regarding her right foot. She has a history of a calcaneus fracture several months ago that she suffered while zip-lining in another country. She was treated nonoperatively at that time. She has a single radiographic printout of her original injury, shown in Figure A, and the CT findings from her original injury, describing a Sanders IV calcaneal fracture. Currently, she complains of deep hindfoot pain, pain walking on uneven ground, inability to wear shoes due to the feeling of a widened heel, and pain at her anterior ankle with attempted dorsiflexion. Based on her original imaging and current symptoms, what is this patient's most likely diagnosis and appropriate treatment?

![[q220932-a-calcaneal-malunion.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Decreased ankle range of motion; tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis
2. Functional ankle instability; lateral ankle ligament reconstruction
3. Subtalar arthritis; Subtalar distraction arthrodesis
4. Weakened plantar flexion; posterior tibial tendon reconstruction
5. Pes planovalgus; Lisfranc arthrodesis

## Correct option

**3. Subtalar arthritis; Subtalar distraction arthrodesis**

## Answer explanation

Based on her original injury (Sanders IV calcaneal fracture), and presenting symptoms (calcaneal widening, subtalar pain, loss of height with anterior impingement), this patient has a calcaneal malunion and subtalar arthritis. The best treatment for her would be a subtalar distraction arthrodesis (answer choice 3).

Calcaneal fractures can be devastating injuries with long-term complications and sequelae with or without surgical intervention. Nonoperative management is reserved for nondisplaced calcaneal fractures or patients with extensive comorbidities (heavy tobacco use, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, systemic illness, etc.). In the absence of contraindications, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is the standard of care to avoid the sequelae of deformity. Conservative management is associated with a 5.5 to 6 times increased rate of subtalar arthritis requiring subtalar arthrodesis. Furthermore, both surgically and nonsurgically treated calcaneal fractures may develop malunion and malalignment. Calcaneal malunions may result in calcaneal widening, loss of height, and lateral impingement. Loss of calcaneal height, in particular, can result in limited dorsiflexion at the ankle and anterior ankle impingement. Subtalar arthritis, in combination with loss of calcaneal height, is best treated with distraction bone block subtalar arthrodesis to correct height, ankle impingement, subfibular impingement, and subtalar arthritis.

*Konovalchuk et al. published a retrospective of 100 patients with calcaneal malunion, in an attempt to identify radiographic parameters that could guide treatment of the malunion.* *The authors used their collected data to create a treatment algorithm, offering either in-situ arthrodesis or more extensive reconstruction options (subtalar arthrodesis with calcaneal osteotomy or subtalar distraction arthrodesis (SDA)) based on radiographic parameters.* Specifically, **the authors found** that a talar declination angle of <6.5° fared better with SDA or calcaneal osteotomy. **The authors concluded** that increased talar declination angle should not be treated with in-situ arthrodesis alone.

*Fletcher et al. published a systematic review of subtalar distraction arthrodesis (SDA) for the treatment of subtalar arthritis.* *Twenty-five studies and 492 feet were included in the study, with the most common etiology stemming from calcaneal fractures.* **The authors note** multiple different surgical techniques, without superiority, that all result in improved functional, clinical, and patient-reported outcomes. **The authors concluded** that late sequelae of calcaneal fractures are the most common indication for SDA, and patients have reliably improved outcomes after SDA.

Figure A, a lateral radiograph of the right foot, demonstrates a calcaneal fracture with posterior facet depression.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Although decreased ankle range of motion is part of her complaints, this is secondary to anterior impingement and not her primary diagnosis. Subtalar arthritis is far more common than tibiotalar arthritis after intra-articular calcaneal fractures, and should be treated with subtalar distraction arthrodesis in this setting, to spare the tibiotalar joint.
Answer choice 2: This patient is not reporting lateral ankle instability and should therefore not be treated with lateral ankle ligament reconstruction in isolation.
Answer choice 4: The posterior tibial tendon can be injured in calcaneal fractures, although less common than subtalar arthritis and peroneal tendon injuries. This patient should be treated with a subtalar distraction arthrodesis for her complaints.
Answer choice 5: Pes planovalgus can occur with calcaneal malunions. However, in this scenario, the treatment is not to address the Lisfranc joint, as this patient has no Lisfranc joint pathology.
