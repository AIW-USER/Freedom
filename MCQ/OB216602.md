## Question

A 71-year-old male falls down three stairs and strikes his head against the handrail. He presents to the ED with severe neck pain without neurologic deficits. Figures A & B demonstrate CT and MRI cuts of the patient's cervical spine, respectively. What is the most appropriate treatment option for this patient?

## Options

1. Hard cervical collar
2. Halo orthosis
3. Anterior odontoid osteosynthesis
4. Posterior C1-2 instrumented fusion
5. Transoral odontoidectomy alone

## Correct option

**4. Posterior C1-2 instrumented fusion**

## Answer explanation

Geriatric odontoid fractures are at high risk for nonunion and higher risk for mortality with nonoperative treatment. Posterior C1-2 arthrodesis would be preferred in this case due to the higher initial fracture displacement and a relatively horizontal fracture pattern.

Type 2 odontoid fractures are the most common fracture of the cervical spine in patients >65 years usually due to low energy falls at standing height. In patients > 65 years of age, there is a high risk of nonunion since these fractures occur in a vascular watershed area between the carotid and vertebral arteries. Studies have shown surgical treatment with a posterior C1-2 fusion increases the union rate and decreases the mortality as compared to nonoperative treatment with cervical immobilization in a hard or soft collar. Halo immobilization is avoided in this age group given the high complication and mortality rate reported in the literature.

*Fehlings et al.* *2013 examined the outcomes of 159 patients over 65 years old with type 2 odontoid fractures.* Non-union rates were 21% and 5% in the non-surgical and surgical groups respectively, but nonunion was not associated with poorer outcomes. Treatment failure occurred at a much higher rate in their study in both the surgical (30%) and non-surgical groups (70%). Initial nonoperative management, male sex, and older age were associated with a greater risk of treatment failure.

*Schroeder et al.* *2015 analyzed 21 articles including 1233 patients on geriatric odontoid fractures.* The forest plots generated in their paper indicated higher short- and long-term mortality in nonoperatively treated patients. Furthermore, the only difference between surgical approaches was increased healing rates with posterior arthrodesis.

Figure A is the coronal CT scan of the cervical spine demonstrating a type II odontoid fracture, while Figure B is a sagittal MRI showing posterior displacement and transverse nature of the fracture. Illustration A demonstrates a diagram showing the location of all 3 types of odontoid fractures, as classified by Anderson and D'Alonzo.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: There are high nonunion rates and mortality rates reported with nonoperative treatment of odontoid fractures in geriatric patients. Further, the posterior displacement of this fracture should be addressed surgically due to the unstable injury pattern.
Answer 2: Halo vests are poorly tolerated in the elderly with high mortality rates reported. Complications include dysphagia, aspiration pneumonia, pin site infections, and pin migration into the skull.
Answer 3: Anterior odontoid osteosynthesis is reserved for fractures with acceptable alignment and minimal displacement. This fracture pattern has a high degree of posterior displacement with a relatively transverse fracture pattern, which may cause fracture displacement with an anterior lag screw.
Answer 5: Transoral odontoidectomy is indicated when there are neurologic symptoms from cord compression by the fracture fragment. However, in isolation, this would result in greater instability of the cervical spine.
