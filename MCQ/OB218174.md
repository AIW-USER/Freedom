## Question

A 42-year-old male construction worker is injured at work when a heavy piece of steel falls on his arm and forcefully extends his elbow against resistance. On presentation to your clinic, you note a palpable defect in the antecubital fossa and positive hook test. You recommend repair of his distal biceps rupture using button fixation through a single incision technique. As a part of the informed consent process, you discuss risks of surgery with him. The most commonly injured nerve during this procedure originates from which of the following locations on the brachial plexus?

## Options

1. Posterior cord
2. Lateral cord
3. Medial cord
4. Inferior trunk
5. Radial nerve

## Correct option

**2. Lateral cord**

## Answer explanation

The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN) is the terminal branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, which originates from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus.

Distal biceps ruptures typically occur due to sudden, eccentric contraction of the biceps. Repair is usually recommended in active individuals because of the loss of supination strength without an intact biceps tendon. Surgical repair can be performed through either a one- or two-incision technique with certain complications more likely to occur with each approach. Injury to the LABCN is the most common complication of either approach but is more common in the single-incision approach and is usually secondary to excessive traction/retraction. Injury to the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) is also possible and is more likely to occur using a two-incision approach. Radio-ulnar synostosis is a dreaded complication of the two-incision technique, although the incidence may be decreasing with the use of an intermuscular interval rather than exposure of the ulna.

*Morrison et al. attempted to compare/contrast different methods for fixation of ruptured distal biceps tendons.* **They discuss** that the two-incision technique is reliable, generally safe, and effective but does run the risk of PIN injury and radio-ulnar synostosis. Moreover, **they note** that the single-incision technique does not significantly increase the risk of PIN injury while decreasing the risk of synostosis.

*Grewal et al. randomized one- vs. two-incision repair of distal biceps ruptures and looked at outcome scores and complications.* *There were no differences in outcome scores or strength at final follow-up.* However, the one-incision technique was associated with more transient neuropraxias of the LABCN (19/47) compared to the two-incision technique (3/43).
