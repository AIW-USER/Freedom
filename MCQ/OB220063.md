## Question

An 18-year-old high school pitcher presents with a two-week history of decreased pitch velocity and a "dead-arm" feeling when transitioning from the late-cocking to the early acceleration phase of his pitching motion. On exam, he has reduced motion with the throwing arm in abduction and internal rotation, with a reduced total arc of motion that differs from the contralateral side by 45°. Which of the following pathologies would the treating surgeon be most likely to identify on a diagnostic arthroscopy of the affected shoulder?

## Options

1. Bursal-sided partial thickness rotator cuff tear
2. Full-thickness tear of the supraspinatus
3. Increased glenoid anteversion
4. Superior Labrum from Anterior to Posterior (SLAP) lesion
5. Tendinopathy of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT)

## Correct option

**4. Superior Labrum from Anterior to Posterior (SLAP) lesion**

## Answer explanation

The patient has clinical evidence of glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD), which is most commonly associated with a SLAP tear when combined clinically with decreased pitch velocity and a "dead-arm" phenomenon (Answer 4).

Glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) results in the loss of internal rotation of the glenohumeral joint compared to the contralateral shoulder, most commonly seen in the throwing athlete. The diagnosis is made clinically with an exam typically showing a decrease in internal rotation and a decrease in the total arc of rotation compared to the contralateral shoulder. Newer research has found that loss of total arc of motion, and more importantly a failure to gain external rotation in the throwing shoulder, may be more important than a clinical loss of internal rotation. Treatment most commonly consists of physical therapy focusing on posteroinferior capsular stretching via sleeper stretches (Illustration A); however, concomitant shoulder pathology may require surgical management when symptoms fail to improve with conservative management. For instance, throwers with GIRD are 25% more likely to have a SLAP lesion, which occurs via the "peel-back mechanism," in which the biceps anchor and posterosuperior labrum peel back along the glenoid neck during the late cocking phase of the throwing motion (Illustration B). Given the pathological tightening of the posterior and posteroinferior capsule, the resultant posterosuperior translation of the humeral head in abduction and external rotation also results in the sequelae of internal impingement, including partial-thickness articular-sided rotator cuff tears.

*Wilk et al. published a prospective study looking at how deficits in glenohumeral passive range of motion increase the risk of shoulder injury in professional baseball pitchers.* *The authors performed a total of 505 examinations on 296 professional pitchers and found highly significant side-to-side differences in range of motion measurements, with pitchers showing insufficient external rotation (<5° greater external rotation in the throwing shoulder) being 2.2 times more likely to be placed on the disabled list for a shoulder injury and 4.0 times more likely to require shoulder surgery.* **They concluded** that insufficient shoulder external rotation on the throwing side and loss of total arc of motion increased the likelihood of shoulder injury and shoulder surgery.

*Tyler et al. reviewed the role of preseason strength and range of motion in alleviating risk factors for shoulder and elbow injuries in high school baseball pitchers.* *The authors measured preseason strength and ROM in 101 pitchers from 4 different high schools over 4 seasons and found a trend for supraspinatus weakness to be associated with increased injury risk and, paradoxically, pitchers with no IR loss were at increased risk compared with pitchers with ≥20° loss.* **They concluded** that although excessive loss of IR is thought to be a risk factor for injury, the opposite was the case in this study, with the absence of IR loss in high school pitchers possibly indicating inadequate prior exposure to pitching, resulting in increased injury risk.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Partial-thickness articular-sided rotator cuff tears may be found in the setting of GIRD and internal impingement.
Answer 3: Years of competitive throwing may lead to compensatory glenoid changes causing increased retroversion, not anteversion.
Answer 5: Though tendinopathy of the LHBT is one of the most common causes of shoulder pain in all comers, the dead-arm experienced by this thrower is more likely due to a SLAP lesion causing biceps anchor instability.
