## Question

## Options

1. The rotator cuff repair being a worker’s compensation claim case
2. Size of tear/length of retraction
3. Degree of fatty infiltration within rotator cuff muscle belly
4. The patient age at the time of repair
5. Performing a concomitant distal clavicle resection at the time of rotator cuff repair

## Correct option

**5. Performing a concomitant distal clavicle resection at the time of rotator cuff repair**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a chronic, right-sided full-thickness supraspinatus rotator cuff tear and acromioclavicular (AC) joint arthritis that has failed conservative management. He is indicated to undergo rotator cuff repair, for which a concomitant distal clavicle resection has not been shown to significantly improve long-term patient-reported outcome scores (Answer 5).

Rotator cuff tears are exceedingly common in the older adult population after the age of fifty. Rotator cuff pathology is the leading cause of shoulder-related disability seen by orthopedic surgeons. Approximately 250,000 rotator cuff repairs are performed in the United States each year, and the number of cases has continued to gradually rise over time. Therefore, optimizing outcomes and managing patient expectations is of utmost importance in this patient population. Chronic rotator cuff tears are by far the most commonly encountered in practice, and acute tears warrant separate discussion. However, patients with chronic rotator cuff tears should initially be managed with conservative treatment that includes physical therapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications, activity modifications, and potentially a corticosteroid injection. If these modalities fail, the patient should be indicated to undergo rotator cuff repair. Negative predictive factors include active smoking, severe obesity, increasing tear size, increasing muscular fatty infiltration, significantly older age (70+ years), and pre-existing rotator cuff arthropathy. Patients with concomitant acromioclavicular joint arthritis are often treated with additional distal clavicle resection at the time of rotator cuff repair. However, multiple recent randomized-control trials have shown no long-term improvement in pain, patient-reported outcome scores, shoulder range of motion, or need for revision surgery when performing rotator cuff repair and distal clavicle resection compared to performing rotator cuff repair alone. Other factors which have shown no difference in long-term outcomes include performing a mini-open rotator cuff repair over an all-arthroscopic repair (although mini-open incurs worse initial postoperative pain/function), waiting 6 weeks after surgery to begin physical therapy, and type of repair (single vs double row).

*Park and colleagues performed a randomized control trial comparing the treatment of full-thickness or high-grade rotator cuff tears with symptomatic AC joint arthritis.* One group (n=26) was treated with rotator cuff repair and distal clavicle resection while the other group (n=32) was treated with isolated rotator cuff repair. With a minimum 2-year follow-up, there was no difference in patient-reported functional outcome scores nor any difference in residual AC joint tenderness. **The authors concluded** that arthroscopic distal clavicle resection at the time of rotator cuff repair should be carefully considered given the equivocal findings at long-term follow-up and potential risks in performing the distal clavicle resection.

*Wang and colleagues performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized control trials looking at the effect of distal clavicle resection in patients undergoing rotator cuff repair.* **They identified** three studies totaling 208 patients and found that additional distal clavicle resection did not have any clinical benefit on long-term outcomes (24 months post-op). **The authors concluded** that routine distal clavicle resection in the setting of rotator cuff repair does not result in improved outcomes for patients. **They further emphasized** that distal clavicle resection may cause AC joint instability in patients with rotator cuff tears and is therefore not recommended in patients with rotator cuff tears and concomitant AC joint arthritis.

Incorrect Answer Choices:
Answer 1: Worker’s compensation claims incur lower functional outcomes in rotator cuff repairs when compared to the general population. In general, this is true for almost all other musculoskeletal cases (spine fusions, meniscal debridements, etc.), not just rotator cuff repairs.
Answer 2: Rotator cuff tear size can be subclassified into small (0-1cm), medium (1-3cm), large (3-5cm), and massive (>5cm or involving >2 tendons). In general, the larger the tear, the lower the expected post-operative functional outcome after operative repair.
Answer 3: The Goutallier classification is commonly used as it quantifies the amount of fatty degeneration of rotator cuff muscles. An increasing proportion of fat infiltration inversely correlates to the likelihood that a rotator cuff repair will be successful. Therefore, significant fatty infiltration will likely result in rotator cuff repair failure and worse post-operative functional outcome scores.
Answer 4: The older the patient is at the time of repair, the higher the risk for retear and the worse expected functional outcomes. While this may be due to confounding factors, patients in their 50s have better outcomes than much older patients in their 70s.
