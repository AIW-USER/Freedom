## Question

A 2-year-old girl sustains a cervical spine fracture and a halo vest is recommended. What would be the most optimal pin-torque configuration for this child?

## Options

1. Four pins with a torque of 6 to 8 in-lbs
2. Four pins with a torque of 2 to 4 in-lbs
3. Eight pins with a torque of 2 to 4 ft-lbs
4. Eight pins with a torque of 2 to 4 in-lbs
5. Eight pins with a torque of 6 to 8 in-lbs

## Correct option

**4. Eight pins with a torque of 2 to 4 in-lbs**

## Answer explanation

For pediatric patients requiring halo application, constructs should include 6 – 12 pins placed at low insertional torque (2 – 4 in-lbs) to optimize stability while avoid skull penetration.

The halo vest is frequently used in the setting of pediatric cervical trauma and for post-operative immobilization. Advantages of halo application over Minerva casting in children include ease of application, more rigid immobilization, better positioning, fewer skin and chewing problems, and the ability for earlier mobilization, which may be important to minimize the risk of respiratory compromise in the multiple-trauma patient. The parameters of the halo ring must be adjusted based on the patient’s age, due to considerations such as activity level, skeletal maturity, cortical thickness, and risk for complications related to immobilization. For younger patients, typical pediatric halo constructs include anywhere from 6 – 12 pins placed with low insertional torque (2 – 4 in-lbs), compared with adolescents and adults where 4 pins are most commonly placed with higher insertional torque (6 – 8 in-lbs). There is not a general consensus on the ideal number of pins. Pin site infection and pin loosening are the most common complications attributable to halo vest application.

*Bono et al. review the indications and technique for application of the halo fixator.* *The authors specifically discuss special considerations in the pediatric population.* Namely **the authors advocated** for a CT scan prior to placement in order to facilitate pin site planning and avoid immaturely fused sutures and areas with thin cortices which may lead to cranial penetration. **They recommended** for 10 – 12 pins applied at 2 in-lb, avoiding the thin temporal bone and frontal sinuses. However **they note** that while more pins are essential in young children, the threshold for multi-pin arrangements is not well-established.

*Dormans et al. evaluated complications in young children following halo vest immobilization in conjunction with cervical spine arthrodesis or for cervical spine trauma.* **The authors reported** a 68% complication rate, most commonly pin tract infections. Simple superficial pin tract infection and loosening was more frequent in children under 10 years old, and both complications were more common of anterior pin sites. More uncommon complications included dural penetration, transient cranial nerve palsy, and cosmetic issues. Interestingly, younger patients with multiple-pin arrangements had a similar rate of complications to children with the standard four-pin construct.

Answer 2: The insertional torque shown here is appropriate, however literature supports a minimum of 6-8 pins in young children.
Answer 3: Eight pins would be appropriate for a young child, however the torque here is incorrectly expressed in ft-lbs.
