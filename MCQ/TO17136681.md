Chapter: Trauma MCQs — DVT PE Fat Embolism

## Question

True regarding fat embolism syndrome is (FNB Pattern Paper 2017)

## Options

1. Heparinization is used as treatment
2. CPAP and PEEP are recommended setting for management of hypoxia
3. High dose steroids improves the prognosis
4. In a polytrauma patient fixation of fractures should be delayed to avoid second hit

## Correct option

**2. CPAP and PEEP are recommended setting for management of hypoxia**

## Answer explanation

Fat embolism syndrome: Sub clinical FES is seen in almost all long bone fractures and manifests as a decreased PaO2, minor hematological changes with no clinical signs or symptoms of respiratory insufficiency. In clinically apparent FES pulmonary, cerebral and cutaneous manifestations are mainly seen.

Common causes: Traumatic fracture of femur, pelvis, and tibia and postoperatively, after hip and knee arthroplasty and intramedullary nailing.

 Rare causes: Crush injury, severe burn, bone marrow interventions (transplant, biopsy), liposuction, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, hemoglobinopathies, corticosteroid therapy, acute pancreatitis, fatty liver etc.

Risk factors for the development of FES: Young age, polytrauma patient with multiple fractures, closed fractures, multiple fractures, and conservative therapy for long-bone fractures.

Factors which increase the risk of FES after intramedullary nailing: Over-zealous nailing of the medullary cavity, reaming of the medullary cavity, increased velocity of reaming and increase in the gap between nail and cortical bone.

Clinical features of FES: Fat embolism syndrome can present in a wide variety of severity and symptoms. Usually presented with a delay of 12-72 h, the classical triad consists of respiratory distress, cerebral signs and petechiae.

Respiratory symptoms: They represent the initial symptoms and range from Hypoxemia, tachypnoea, and dyspnoea to respiratory failure, requiring mechanical ventilation. In the absence of ongoing embolism and infection recovery occurs in 3 days.

Neurological features: They result from cerebral hypoxia and range from irritability, anxiety, agitation, confusion, delirium, and coma. Usually these symptoms are transient and fully reversible.

 Cutaneous manifestations: Usually skin manifestations are the last symptoms to develop in fat embolism syndrome. Petechial rashes are pathognomonic features of FES and develop due to extravasations of RBCs from over distended capillaries. They are self-limiting and appear within 36 hours and disappear within a week. Petechial rashes are usually seen over the upper portions of the chest and axillae, conjunctiva, oral cavity and shoulders. Petechiae are thought to develop due to extravasations of RBCs from over distended capillaries

Fever and tachycardia are non-specific but seen in almost all cases of FES. Jaundice and oligo/anuria may occur.

Diagnosis: Diagnosis is mainly clinical. An early diagnosis can be made by close monitoring of pulse oximetry and ABG analysis. Arterial Blood gas analysis initially reveals hypoxia with hypocarbia and respiratory alkalosis and Later on, respiratory acidosis due to respiratory muscle fatigue. An increased alveolar- arterial shunt fraction, in the absence of ARDS, within 24-48 h is strongly suggestive of FES

Initially normal, the radiological abnormalities develop gradually as a diffuse fluffy bilateral infiltrates (Snow storm appearance), predominantly in bases and periphery.

Gurd and Wilson criteria for fat embolism: One of the three major manifestations i.e., respiratory insufficiency, cerebral involvement or petechiae along with four of the five minor criteria (pyrexia, tachycardia, fall in hematocrit or platelet values, increasing ESR, retinal changes, and presence of fat globules in sputum or urine) are considered to be consistent with a diagnosis of FES.

Treatment: Patient should be transferred to the ICU. CVP measurement is a useful adjunct to monitor pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. Albumin is a preferred agent to restore intravascular volume as it also has lipophilic action. Hypoxia should be managed by oxygen inhalation using face masks or high flow gas delivery systems. PaO2 may also be improved by Continuous positive pressure ventilation (CPAP) without increasing FiO2. If a FiO2 of >60% and CPAP >10 cm are required to achieve a PaO2, then mechanical ventilation with Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) should be considered. There is insufficient data to support the use of corticosteroids in FES. Routine use of heparin is not recommended due to high risk of bleeding in polytrauma patients.

Answer : B
