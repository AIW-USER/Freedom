## Question

A 17-year-old hockey goalie presents with longstanding activity-related right hip pain. On physical examination, he has limited hip internal rotation. He also has pain with flexion, adduction, and internal rotation of his hip. Figure A is the AP radiograph of the right hip. This radiographic finding is most suggestive of which of the following?

![[q214264-a-crossover-sign.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Acetabular protrusio
2. Acetabular retroversion in cam impingement
3. Acetabular retroversion in pincer impingement
4. Femoral anteversion
5. Femoral retroversion

## Correct option

**3. Acetabular retroversion in pincer impingement**

## Answer explanation

This patient has symptoms and physical exam findings consistent with femoroacetabular impingement (FAI). Figure A demonstrates the crossover sign which is used in the diagnosis of FAI and suggests acetabular retroversion in pincer impingement.

Several measurements can be used to assess FAI. These most commonly include the use of the alpha angle to quantify the head-neck junction deformity, and the crossover sign to assess native acetabular version. The crossover sign is seen on an AP radiograph of the hip or pelvis. Normally, the shadows of the anterior and posterior acetabular walls should meet superiorly and laterally. In cases of acetabular retroversion, the crossover of the shadows occurs more distally. If the crossover sign is present it is most suggestive of acetabular retroversion, which may be a cause of pincer impingement.

*Clohisy et al. reviewed plain radiographic evaluation of the adult hip.* **They reported** that the lateral center-edge angle, anterior center-edge angle, acetabular index of depth to width, femoral head extrusion index, and the Tönnis angle have all been used in the diagnosis of acetabular dysplasia. **They concluded** that measurements of acetabular version, the head-neck offset, and the alpha angle have been used in the diagnosis of FAI.

Nötzli et al. reviewed MRI imaging of patients with positive impingement tests and compared them with asymptomatic controls to evaluate concavity at the femoral head-neck junction. They reported that alpha angles for symptomatic and asymptomatic patients were 74 and 42 degrees, respectively. They concluded that symptomatic hips with impingement have significantly less concavity at the femoral head-neck junction than normal hips.

Figure A is the AP radiograph of the right hip demonstrating a crossover sign.

Illustration A is a lateral of the hip demonstrating the calculation of the alpha angle. Illustration B is an AP pelvis radiograph demonstrating the Tönnis angle. Acetabula having a Tönnis angle of 0° to 10° are considered normal, whereas those having an angle of >10° or <0° are considered to have increased and decreased inclination, respectively. Illustration C is an AP radiograph of the hip demonstrating the lateral center-edge angle. Values of <25° may indicate inadequate coverage of the femoral head. Illustration D is a false-profile radiographic view of the hip demonstrating the anterior center-edge angle. Values of <20° can be indicative of structural instability. Figure E illustrates the crossover sign of acetabular retroversion. The red and blue lines depict the anterior and posterior walls of the acetabulum, respectively. The blue line (posterior wall) crosses over the anterior line. These lines should not normally cross.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: Acetabular protrusio is seen on standard AP imaging when the acetabulum and femoral head are displaced medial to the ilioischial line and are not related to FAI
Answer 2: The crossover sign does suggest acetabular retroversion. However, it is most suggestive of a pincer lesion in the setting of FAI
Answer 4: Femoral anteversion is typically evaluated clinically in pediatric patients. However, to measure true femoral anteversion, a CT scan is typically performed
Answer 5: Femoral retroversion is commonly evaluated with CT imaging
