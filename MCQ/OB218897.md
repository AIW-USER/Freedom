## Question

A 27-year-old male presents with a 3-month history of right upper extremity swelling that has been waxing and waning in nature. He denies any pain, fevers, or chills. The patient states the swelling started after a day of carrying heavy boxes during a move. He has no medical comorbidities and the toxicology screen is negative. ## Options

1. Discharge on NSAIDs, physical therapy referral, and follow up with his primary care provider
2. Consult interventional radiology for drain placement
3. Bedside incision and drainage in the emergency department
4. Schedule for outpatient biceps tendon repair versus reconstruction
5. CT-guided core needle biopsy

## Correct option

**5. CT-guided core needle biopsy**

## Answer explanation

This patient's nonspecific history of symptoms and irregular, painless subfascial mass are concerning for an underlying soft tissue sarcoma. Accordingly, the patient should undergo an appropriate workup to prevent delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Despite the possibility of a partial biceps tendon rupture from a recent move, the history of a painless mass in his arm is concerning for potential neoplasm. There are multiple masqueraders of soft tissue sarcoma (STS) including hematoma, abscess, and osteomyelitis. Obtaining a thorough history and physical examination, ordering prudent diagnostic testing, and carefully scrutinizing imaging findings collectively assist in making the appropriate diagnosis. Given that this patient presents with a large, painless mass deep to fascia, he should be admitted and worked up for a STS. This includes cross-sectional imaging (MRI with contrast preferred) of the entire upper extremity followed by a well-planned CT-guided core needle biopsy to establish a tissue diagnosis. Additional imaging as part of the staging protocol includes a chest CT scan to rule out hematogenous spread to the lungs.

Crago and Lee from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Cancer Center provided a comprehensive review on the management of soft tissue tumors in the extremity. They discuss that the mainstay treatment for extremity soft tissue sarcomas includes limb-sparing surgery with supplemental radiation (for tumors at increased risk for local recurrence) or chemotherapy (for tumors at increased risk for metastasis). The authors conclude that given the approximately 100 histologic subtypes of soft tissue sarcoma with variable biology, nuances in therapeutic algorithms highlight the importance of multidisciplinary care teams with experience in sarcoma management.

Dr. Roland from MD Anderson Cancer Center provided a similar review regarding the treatment of soft tissue sarcomas of the extremities. She highlights that careful evaluation of patients with soft tissue masses is critical for preventing delays in diagnosis and treatment. The author concludes that multimodal therapy including margin-negative surgery with radiation therapy is associated with favorable local recurrence rates, although distance recurrence remains an issue within the field of high-grade sarcomas.

Figure A shows AP and lateral radiographic images showing no underlying fracture. Figure B shows coronal and axial imaging from this patient’s CT scan with IV contrast showing a considerable sized, heterogenous mass within the anterior compartment of his arm. Figure C shows axial and sagittal MRI imaging from this patient displaying a heterogenous mass with irregular enhancing nodular soft tissue and a large cystic/necrotic component consistent with a soft tissue sarcoma. He later underwent a core needle biopsy revealing an underlying pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This would be an appropriate treatment plan in the setting of biceps tendonitis.
Answer 2: Placing a subfascial drain by interventional radiology would be considered appropriate for recurring abscesses or Morel-Lavallee lesions.
Answer 3: If misdiagnosed, needle decompression or incision of this STS in the emergency department would result in significant morbidity and a more complex reconstructive surgery later on.
Answer 4: Scheduling the patient for outpatient biceps tendon repair versus reconstruction would be appropriate in the setting of a partial biceps tendon tear that failed conservative management.
