## Answer explanation

Ans: B

Terminology: Decubitus ulcers, also known as bedsores and pressure sores, have long been difficult to define appropriately. “Pressure sores” was the older term used commonly in the UK but the term fails to describe pressure injuries that are not open wounds (such as blisters and non-blanching erythema) as they are not true sores, but only “pressure damage”. “Pressure ulcers” is a term used widely in the USA and other countries and has been accepted as the Europe-wide term by the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EPUAP). They are also known as ‘bedsores’, ‘decubitus ulcers BUT these names are now rarely used as it is recognised that these ulcers are not caused by lying or being in bed. The areas that are particularly prone to pressure sores are those that cover the bony prominences.

Constant pressure resulting in ‘distortion or deformation damage’ is probably the most accurate description of a pressure ulcer. The revised definition by National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP) now describes them as a pressure injury occurring over a bony prominence or under a medical or other device.

Pathogenesis: They are caused by impaired blood supply and tissue malnutrition owing to prolonged pressure over skin, soft tissue, muscle, and bone. Prolonged pressure along with tissue compression can lead to capillary bed occlusion and local ischemia. Without adequate blood flow, toxic metabolites accumulate locally, increasing the rate of cell death. This leads to ulceration and necrosis of skin and underlying tissue. The skin overlying the sacrum and hips is most often affected (67%), but decubitus ulcers may also be observed over the occiput, helices, elbows, and lower extremities (25%), including heels and ankles. The sores can develop within hours to days of constant pressure. The tissues are capable of sustaining pressure on the arterial side of around 30-32 mm hg for only a small duration of time. But when pressure increases even slightly above this capillary filling pressure, it causes tissue death and ulceration in a very rapid manner.

The majority of people affected with pressure sores are those having health conditions (mental or physical) that encourage immobility. Several other health conditions that influence blood supply and capillary perfusion, such as type-2 diabetes, can also make a person more vulnerable to pressure ulcers. Classification: Several classification systems for decubitus ulcers have been described: Daniel’s, Shea’s, and the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP). The NPUAP was revised in the year 2016. The revised staging system uses the term injury instead of ulcer and denotes stages using Arabic numerals rather than Roman numerals. The revised definition of a pressure injury now describes the injuries as usually occurring over a bony prominence or under a medical or other device.

Treatment: The goal of all the various treatment options is to; minimize the pressure exerted on the ulcer, minimize contact of the ulcer with a hard surface, decrease moisture, and to keep it as aseptic or least septic as possible. The choice of treatment options should be according to the stage/grade of the ulcer, and what the purpose of the treatment should be (decreasing moisture, removal of necrotic tissue, controlling bacteraemia). Debridement would be required each and every time slough, discharge or necrotic tissue is seen in the ulcer bed. Debridement should involve removal not only removal of surface contamination rather a meticulous excision of all dead tissue. Laser and low frequency ultrasound waves have also been employed in debridement assistance.

Dressings used are usually occlusive, so the ulcers heal better in a moist environment. If the ulcer is clean and dry, occlusive dressings are usually changed weekly, and more frequent changes are avoided as dressing changes remove healthy cells along with debris. Contaminated or weeping wounds however, require more frequent dressing changes, sometimes every few hours.

Some specialized dressings to speed up healing process: -

- Hydrocolloid dressings: These contain a special gel that encourages the growth of new skin cells in the ulcer and keeps the nearby healthy area of skin dry.
- Alginate dressings: These are made from seaweed that contains sodium and calcium known to speed up the healing process. Honey-impregnated alginate dressings are known to accomplish total wound healing effectively in pressure ulcers.
- Nano silver dressings: These use the antibacterial property of silver to clean the ulcer.
- Creams and ointments: Topical preparations, such as cream and ointments, containing antiseptics are frequently used to hasten healing in pressure sores.

In heavily contaminated ulcers with presence of heavy slough and discharge, a wiser option is to utilize the Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT). NPWT also called as Vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) is a sealed, negative pressure (suction pressure -75 to -125 mm-Hg) dressing applied over the wound that helps draw the wound edges together, removes infectious material by suction effect, increases local blood flow and actively promotes granulation tissue formation.

Antibiotic use: All pressure sores do not require antibiotics. Longstanding pressure ulcers are frequently colonised by micro-organisms in a biofilm that protects them from effects of conventional antibiotics. Hence, antibiotics should be used as per culture reports and usually only prescribed to treat an infected pressure ulcer with slough and discharge. Topical antibiotics should be avoided because their use may increase antibiotic resistance and allergy. Rather Antiseptic cream may be applied topically to clear out any bacteria more rapidly.

Also, one must not also forget, that wound healing requires adequate protein, iron, Vitamin-C and zinc. Supplements may be prescribed if they are deficient in the diet.

And above all, prevention is clearly the best treatment which involves good skincare in high-risk patients using, pressure dispersion cushions, and appropriate support surfaces. Support surfaces decrease the amount of pressure on the wound and must be advised. Support surfaces can be either static (e.g., air, foam, and water mattress overlays) or dynamic (e.g. alternating air overlay).
