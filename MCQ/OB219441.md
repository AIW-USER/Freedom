## Question

A 79-year-old female undergoes uncomplicated fixation of a stable intertrochanteric femur fracture, shown in Figure A. A short cephalomedullary nail is inserted through a standard trochanteric entry point and is fixed with a helical blade and distal locking screw, and the operation is performed with the patient supine on a fracture table. Which of the following alterations to the outlined surgical plan would decrease the potential for intraoperative injury to the hip abductor musculature?

![[q219441-a-intertrochanteric-fracture-8.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Use of a long cephalomedullary nail with a trochanteric entry point
2. Patient positioned in the lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table
3. Use of a short cephalomedullary nail with a lag screw
4. Use of a sliding hip screw
5. Use of a short cephalomedullary nail without a distal locking screw

## Correct option

**4. Use of a sliding hip screw**

## Answer explanation

Reaming for insertion of a cephalomedullary nail through a trochanteric entry point risks violation of the gluteus medius tendon insertion. The use of a sliding hip screw avoids iatrogenic injury to the hip abductors (Answer 4).

Stable intertrochanteric femur fractures can be effectively treated with either a sliding hip screw or cephalomedullary nail construct.
 Cephalomedullary nail fixation has risen in popularity, in part due to its ease of use and versatility to address various fracture patterns, decreased perceived blood loss, and load-sharing design. A potential drawback of this technique is the violation of abductor musculature incurred during the preparation and insertion of the implant. Open-reduction and internal fixation with a sliding hip screw can be used in stable fracture patterns with an intact lateral wall and avoids iatrogenic damage to the hip abductors during implant insertion.

*McConnell et al. performed a cadaveric study investigating the extent of gluteus medius tendon damage sustained during cephalomedullary nail insertion.* An average of 27% of the tendon insertion was disrupted by reaming for implant insertion. **The authors concluded** that damage to the gluteus medius tendon was unavoidable when implanting the cephalomedullary nail utilized within their investigation and this should be a recognized cause for postoperative morbidity.

*Recognizing the high rate of gluteus medius tendon insertion disruption identified in the literature, Perez et al. investigated the use of a modified medial trochanteric portal to decrease abductor musculature violation during antegrade femoral nail insertion.* *In their cadaveric study,* **the authors found** that this modified entry portal avoided visible damage to gluteus medius tendon insertion in all nine cases in which accurate localization was achieved. **They concluded** that this technique may decrease the potential morbidity associated with abductor injury using traditional lateral trochanteric entry portals.

Figure A demonstrates a stable left intertrochanteric femur fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A long cephalomedullary nail with a standard trochanteric entry point would still risk hip abductor violation for reaming and insertion.
Answer 2: Patient positioning would not change the starting point necessary for nail insertion and would therefore have little effect on potential injury to the hip abductor musculature.
Answer 3: The potential benefits and drawbacks of using a lag screw versus a helical blade are controversial. Neither strategy impacts the potential morbidity associated with hip abductor violation during reaming and nail insertion.
Answer 5: Benefits of distal locking screw insertion reported in the literature include added rotational stability and protection against peri-implant fractures. The use of a distal locking screw does not impact potential hip abductor injury during surgery.
