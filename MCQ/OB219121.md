## Question

A 90-year-old female with an extensive cardiac history falls while tending to her garden and presents to the emergency room with an intertrochanteric femur fracture. She has a history of coronary artery disease, states she has had several stents placed in the past, and is regularly taking aspirin and another anticoagulant with the mechanism of action depicted in Figure A. She is seen by the internist in the emergency room for pre-operative clearance. Which statement is most correct regarding the next best step in management given the patient's regular use of anticoagulants?

## Options

1. Administer Vitamin K and proceed with surgery in the next 24 hours
2. Administer Idarucizumab and proceed with surgery as soon as possible
3. Give fresh frozen plasma and proceed with surgery within 48 hours
4. Make no changes to the patient's anticoagulation and proceed when optimized
5. Stop all anticoagulants and proceed with surgery after 48 hours

## Correct option

**4. Make no changes to the patient's anticoagulation and proceed when optimized**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a hip fracture and is on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and clopidogrel (Plavix), which works via the mechanism shown in Figure A. It is safe to continue both medications and proceed with fixation as soon as the patient is medically clear, ideally within 24-48 hours.

Elderly patients with hip fractures, whether intracapsular femoral neck fractures or intertrochanteric femur fractures, often have numerous medical comorbidities and may be on anticoagulants. In the emergency setting, use of anticoagulants should not delay hip fracture surgery, as the literature has shown mortality benefit out to one year for expeditious fracture fixation (within 48 hours), while a delay > 48 hours has shown an increased risk of mortality.

*You et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of the safety of expedited-surgery protocols in anticoagulant-treated patients with hip fractures.* *The authors included 14 studies and found that the use of expedited surgery protocols (within 48 hours) significantly reduced the time to surgery for patients with hip fractures who were on anticoagulants and was not associated with increased surgical duration or 30-day mortality risk.* **They concluded** that performing hip fracture surgery within 48 hours in direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)-treated patients is safe, albeit with a small increase in blood transfusion risk.

*Collinge et al. reviewed the effects of Clopidogrel (Plavix) and other oral anticoagulants on early hip fracture surgery.* *A total of 1,036 patients were reviewed, of which 74 were on Plavix, 253 on aspirin alone, 90 on warfarin, and 619 on no anticoagulation.* **The authors found** that Clopidogrel only versus aspirin versus both aspirin and clopidogrel, versus no anticoagulant versus warfarin showed no significant differences in estimated blood loss, transfusion requirement, final blood count, bleeding, perioperative complications, or mortality. **They concluded** that patients undergoing early hip fracture surgery who are taking clopidogrel, aspirin, or warfarin (with a regulated international normalized ratio) are not at substantially increased risk for bleeding, bleeding complications, or mortality.

Figure A demonstrates the mechanism of action of clopidogrel (Plavix), which selectively and irreversibly inhibits the binding of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to its platelet P2Y12 receptor, thereby inhibiting platelet aggregation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Vitamin K is a reversal agent for Warfarin, which typically acts as a Vitamin K epoxide reductase complex inhibitor in the clotting factor synthesis chain. The depicted mechanism of action in Figure A is for the anti-platelet clopidogrel, not Warfarin.
Answer 2: Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody that acts as the reversal agent for Dabigatran (Pradaxa), which itself is a direct thrombin inhibitor and does not work through the G-protein coupled cascade demonstrated in Figure A.
Answer 3: There is no indication to give fresh frozen plasma (FFP) prior to proceeding with fixation when the patient is on anti-platelet therapy.
Answer 5: Dual antiplatelet therapy does not need to be stopped prior to urgent fracture fixation in the setting of elderly hip fractures, as above.
