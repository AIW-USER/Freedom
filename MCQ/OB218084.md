## Question

Debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) is one of the proposed treatment options for total hip (THA) and total knee (TKA) periprosthetic joint infections (PJI). Which of the following patients with confirmed PJI would be considered the most appropriate candidate for a DAIR procedure?

## Options

1. 68F, 8 months from initial THA, pain never resolved from initial surgery, MRSA on aspiration
2. 63F, 2 years from initial TKA, 2-day history worsening knee pain, knee aspiration reveals E-coli
3. 75F, 2 years from initial THA, 3 weeks hip pain, MSSA on aspiration, femoral stem subsidence on xray
4. 72M, 3 years from initial TKA, 3x3cm medial knee open wound requiring gastrocnemius flap
5. 65M, 5 years from initial TKA, 6 months of knee pain with MSSA on aspiration

## Correct option

**2. 63F, 2 years from initial TKA, 2-day history worsening knee pain, knee aspiration reveals E-coli**

## Answer explanation

Patients with early post-operative or acute-hematogenous (Answer 2) periprosthetic joint infections are considered potential candidates for DAIR procedures with modular component exchange. Chronic periprosthetic joint infections should be treated with the removal of all implant components via one-stage or two-stage revision arthroplasty.

There are three major types of hip/knee periprosthetic joint infections reported in the literature (note: timelines may vary according to individual studies):

(1) Early post-operative periprosthetic joint infections (“acute PJIs”) which occur within 30 days of initial TKA/THA
(2) Chronic periprosthetic joint infections (“chronic PJIs”) which occur >30 days of initial TKA/THA and when the patient’s symptoms have been present for > 1 month (i.e. there has been sufficient time for bacteria to form a glycocalyx biofilm)
(3) Acute hematogenous periprosthetic joint infections (“acute-hematogenous PJIs” or “late-acute PJIs”) which occur > 30 days of initial TKA/THA and when the patient's symptoms have been present for <3-4 weeks

In the setting of early post-operative and acute-hematogenous PJIs, there has been compelling evidence reported that debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) procedure can sufficiently control infections while maintaining functional outcomes similar to the initial TKA/THA surgery. In these cases, the modular components (i.e. polyethylene in TKAs or the polyethylene and femoral head in THA) are exchanged, the fixed components are aggressively scrubbed clean, and a partial synovectomy with localized debridement is performed. In this manner, DAIR can be a less morbid, more cost-effective, and patient function-sparing procedure compared to one- or two-stage revision arthroplasty. DAIR procedure reported success rates are highly dependent on host- and bacterial- factors and ultimately predicated on well-indicated cases. Factors consistently shown to improve the likelihood of success in DAIR procedures include exchanging any modular components during debridement, performing DAIR within 7 days of onset of symptoms, adding rifampin in staphylococcus PJI, and the use of fluoroquinolones in gram-negative PJIs. Absolute contraindications to DAIR procedures include loose implants, poor soft tissue coverage, and/or a compromised bone-cement mantle. Of note, DAIR procedures are not recommended for chronic PJIs of primary implants as they are less successful at eradicating chronic infections, even when combined with an extended period of antibiotics (12+ weeks).

*Shohat and colleagues provided an updated overview of the definition of periprosthetic hip and knee infections on behalf of the International Consensus on Orthopedic Infections.* *They provided an updated International Consensus Meeting (ICM) criteria for both acute and chronic PJIs (Illustration A).* *The authors acknowledged that the chronic PJI criterion has been well-validated by numerous studies and multi-centered patient cohorts.* However, they acknowledged that their acute PJI criterion was primarily based on prior ICM updates and not well validated. Therefore, **they proposed** that the same threshold criteria for chronic PJIs be applied in the acute PJI setting as well. **They conclude** by acknowledging other limitations of the updated criteria (i.e. did not include next-generation sequencing and some newer technologies).

*Argenson and colleagues provided an updated, exhaustive overview of the utilization of DAIR procedures in hip and knee arthroplasty on behalf of the International Consensus on Orthopedic Infections.* **They emphasize** DAIR procedure utility only in well-indicated patients specifically for early post-operative PJI (KLIC Score, Illustration B) and acute-hematogenous PJI (Crime-80 Score, Illustration C). **The authors further highlight** host- and bacterial- predictors for successful and unsuccessful DAIR procedures while continuing to stress avoiding DAIR procedures for chronic PJIs.

*Chotanaphuti and colleagues provided an updated overview of the treatment algorithms conundrums in hip and knee arthroplasty on behalf of the International Consensus on Orthopedic Infections.* *They discuss the lack of definitive evidence for the optimal treatment of bilateral PJIs or PJIs presenting with overt sepsis.* *They do, however, provide a consensus treatment algorithm for the management of a persistent draining wound after TKA or THA (Illustration 4).*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1. This patient has pain that has been present since surgery (>1 month) with a virulent bacteria known to have high DAIR failure rates to due bacterial antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation. This patient would be best treated with a two-staged revision arthroplasty procedure.
Answer 3. While this patient may have an acute-hematogenous PJI, femoral stem subsidence (i.e. loose implant) is an absolute contraindication to performing a DAIR procedure. This patient would be best treated with a one-stage or two-stage revision arthroplasty procedure.
Answer 4. DAIR procedures are contraindicated in PJIs that require flap soft tissue coverage, as there is an extremely high rate of failure (>85%) in such cases. Those patients should be treated with a two-staged revision arthroplasty (recommended static spacer for soft tissue rest and to prevent knee ROM leading to flap tension failure).
Answer 5. This patient has a chronic PJI given his duration of symptoms and remote history of initial arthroplasty. Chronic PJIs are best treated with one- or two-stage revision arthroplasty.
