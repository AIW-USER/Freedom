## Question

A 10-year-old female is referred for insidious onset of low back pain. The pain is non-radiating but has been worsening over the last 8 months. She was seen by her primary care doctor who ordered the X-ray in Figure A 6 months ago. What is the most common gait pattern seen in patients with this diagnosis?

![[q8811-a-progression-copy.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral shift toward the right leg during stance phase
2. Shortened stride length bilaterally with flexion at hips and knees
3. High knee height during swing phase and audible slap on heel strike of right leg
4. Backwards lurch during stance phase, locking the hip in extension
5. Narrow base with short strides and dragging both feet during swing phase

## Correct option

**2. Shortened stride length bilaterally with flexion at hips and knees**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a spondylolitic defect causing spondylolisthesis. The most common gait is with shortened stride length with flexion at the hips and knees. This pathology can initially be managed conservatively with physical therapy for paraspinal and core musculature strengthening, followed by surgical fusion should nonoperative management fail.

Spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis are common causes of low back pain in children. Activities involving repetitive extension and rotation of the lumbar spine place the pars under significant stress. Some studies have shown incidence of spondylolysis to be nearly 50% in elite level gymnasts and divers. Spondylolithesis falls on the spectrum from pars stress reaction, to spondylolysis, and then spondylolithesis. Risk of progression and development of neurologic symptoms is associated with dysplastic facets at the L5-S1 level.

*Cavalier et al reviewed spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis in pediatric population.* **They found** hamstring tightness and contractures causing hip and knee flexion to be a common physical exam finding. They hypothesize the etiology of which is the vertical orientation of the sacrum causing a retroverted pelvis and compensatory hyperlordosis, rather than due to nerve root involvement. Also seen is limited extension of the knee while supine and the hip at 90 degrees (popliteal angle). These together will cause a shortened stride with a hunched over posture. **They also recommend** further examination to evaluate possible nerve root involvement; motor and sensory function, deep tendon reflexes, straight leg raise test, and rectal tone.

*Cheung et al reviewed treatment options for pediatric spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis.* *Most cases respond to a period of restricted activity and physiotherapy, followed by bracing.* For those that fail conservative measures, **they recommended** repair through either wiring, pedicle screw-hook, or screw direct repair for single or multilevel low grade, mobile slips at levels L1-L4, For high-grade slips or for those with neurologic symptoms, fusion is indicated. There is no consensus whether reduction should be performed as it will correct sagittal alignment but has a higher risk of nerve root injury. Either fusion technique is associated with L5 nerve root injury and pseudarthrosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1 is describing an abductor lurch, seen with gluteus medius weakness
Answer 3 is seen in individuals with a foot drop
Answer 4 is describing a gluteus maximus gait pattern which occurs most commonly with inferior gluteal nerve damage
Answer 5 is describing a diplegic gait often seen in cerebral palsy
