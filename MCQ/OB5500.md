## Question

A 32-year-old woman presents with knee pain after a fall. An injury radiograph is shown in Figure A. She is taken to the operating room for intralesional curettage with adjuvant therapy. Intraoperative histology and an immediate postoperative radiograph are shown in Figures B and C respectively. She is subsequently lost to follow-up and returns 2 years later with knee pain. A recent radiograph is shown in Figure D. She is again taken to the operating room. Tissue is sent for histopathological analysis. What is the most likely diagnosis of this second tissue sample?

## Options

1. Aneurysmal bone cyst
2. Benign giant cell tumor of bone
3. Malignant giant cell tumor of bone
4. Reactive fibrous tissue
5. Osteomyelitis

## Correct option

**2. Benign giant cell tumor of bone**

## Answer explanation

This patient had a benign giant cell tumor (GCT) of bone, which usually recurs as benign giant cell tumor of bone (identical pathology).

GCT is an aggressive benign eccentric meta-epiphyseal neoplasm occurring in patients aged 20-40 years with a slight female preponderance. They are most commonly found in the distal femur, proximal tibia, distal radius and sacral arch/wing. Secondary cystic degeneration occurs because they are hypervascular. Most tumors are Enneking stage 2. Local physical adjuvant therapy such as phenol, heat (argon beam), or freezing reduces recurrence after intralesional curettage.

*Raskin et al. reviewed GCT.* The giant cells are characterized by multiple nuclei (>50), nuclei identical to stromal cells, and central nucleoli. The cell of origin is the fibroblastic-osteoblastic mononuclear cell (not the giant cell). Wide resection is an option for recalcitrant/recurrent/aggressive cases especially in expendable bones (clavicle, distal ulna, proximal to mid fibula).

Figure A is an AP radiograph showing pathologic intraarticular fracture through GCT of the lateral femoral condyle. Figure B is a high-power micrograph showing multiple giant cells. Figure C shows the same lesion after ORIF with curettage, bone grafting and packing with cement. Radiolucency at the axilla of the plate suggests residual tumor and incomplete extirpation. Figure D shows local recurrence (arrow).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Recurrence is unlikely to be cystic in nature. Cystic changes occur in hypervascular tumors with central degeneration.
Answer 3: Recurrence is most likely to be benign. Malignant transformation is rare (<1%) and risk is increased by radiotherapy with megavoltage irradiation.
Answer 4: Reactive fibrous tissue is unlikely to be osteolytic and expansile.
Answer 5: There is no evidence for infection. Osteomyelitis (OM) does not typically present with an expansile, osteolytic mass. OM in the presence of hardware is likely to be found in close association with hardware, resulting in hardware loosening and failure.
