## Correct option

**4. Figure D**

## Answer explanation

Hindfoot varus, as seen in Figure D, would place this patient at higher risk for recurrent ankle sprains and chronic instability.

Lateral ankle instability is very common, especially in an athletic population. The usual mechanism of injury includes an inversion injury with a plantarflexed foot that causes strain or rupture of the ATFL +/- CFL/PTFL. Treatment is mostly conservative but with episodes of continued instability, operative intervention may be required and is most commonly addressed with a Brostrom-Gould reconstruction. Prior to any intervention, hindfoot alignment must be evaluated, as hindfoot varus deformity has been shown to place patients at higher risk for persistent instability. Depending on the severity of the deformity, correction may be considered at the time of ligamentous reconstruction.

*Lintz et al. evaluated the relationship between chronic lateral ankle instability and hindfoot varus on weight-bearing CT scans.* **They noted** that patients who had a varus hindfoot had a 35% increased odds ratio for lateral ankle instability for every 1% increase in varus hindfoot deformity. **They conclude** that their results are convincing in demonstrating a relationship between varus hindfoot deformity and chronic lateral ankle instability with other variables held constant.

*Krause et al. reviewed ankle instability in relation to cavovarus deformity.* **They note** that clinicians must have a high degree of suspicion for tibial and hindfoot malalignment in cases of lateral ankle instability. It is their theory that hindfoot realignment should be heavily considered in combination with a ligamentous repair.

Figure A is a lateral ankle radiograph demonstrating pes planus. Figure B is an AP ankle radiograph demonstrating tibiofibular clear space widening and decreased tibiofibular overlap indicative of syndesmotic injury. Figure C is an AP radiograph demonstrating medial talar dome osteochondral defect. Figure D represents a varus hindfoot. Figure E shows a lateral ankle radiograph demonstrating signs of tibiotalar impingement with small osteophytes noted on both the talus and plafond

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Pes planus and hindfoot valgus often occur concomitantly but would not place this patient at increased risk for recurrent ankle instability.
Answer 2: Syndesmotic injury, as represented in Figure B, would not predispose this patient to lateral ankle instability and conversely, would be the result of recurrent ankle injuries.
Answer 3: Talar OCD may occur as a result of recurrent ankle instability but would not predispose this patient to injury.
Answer 5: Tibiotalar impingement, as noted by the tibiotalar osteophytes, is not a known cause of lateral ankle instability.
