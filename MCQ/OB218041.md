## Question

A 25-year-old patient presents to the local emergency department with the injury depicted in Figure A. Given an inability to maintain an acceptable reduction in a splint, you elect to bring the patient to the operating room for closed versus open reduction with percutaneous pin fixation versus internal fixation. While in the operating room, you perform a closed reduction by applying palmar abduction and pronation while applying pressure to the metacarpal base. Through this reduction maneuver, you hope to counteract the deforming forces imparted by which muscles?

![[q218041-a-life-in-fast-lane.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Abductor pollicis brevis, adductor pollicis and extensor pollicis brevis
2. Abductor pollicis brevis, adductor pollicis and extensor pollicis longus
3. Abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis longus and brachioradialis
4. Abductor pollicis longus, adductor pollicis and extensor pollicis longus
5. Abductor pollicis longus, adductor pollicis and brachioradialis

## Correct option

**4. Abductor pollicis longus, adductor pollicis and extensor pollicis longus**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a partial intra-articular base of the thumb metacarpal fracture (Bennett fracture). Three muscles that provide a deforming force at the base of the thumb, and which must be overcome during closed reduction, are the abductor pollicis longus, adductor pollicis, and the extensor pollicis longus (Answer 4). The extensor pollicis brevis also provides a deforming force.

Approximately 80% of thumb fractures involve the metacarpal base. These fractures can be extra-articular, partial intra-articular (Bennett), or complete intra-articular (Rolando). Bennett and Rolando fractures typically occur secondary to an axial force applied to a flexed thumb. The volar-ulnar base fragment remains attached to the trapezium by the volar break ligament, which spans the tuberosity of the trapezium to the volar edge of the first metacarpal (Illustration A).

While extra-articular and minimally (<1 mm) displaced Bennett fractures are often successfully managed nonoperatively, extra- and intra-articular fractures with > 1mm displacement often require percutaneous k-wire fixation or open reduction with internal fixation. Many patients with these injuries, especially those with high degrees of comminution, go on to experience post-traumatic arthritis at the thumb carpal-metacarpal joint.

Carlsen and Moran provide a comprehensive overview of thumb trauma. The authors note that four muscles provide deforming forces at the base of the thumb: the abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis brevis, and the adductor pollicus. They recommend axial traction, palmar abduction, and pronation while applying pressure to the metacarpal base when attempting to close reduce these injuries.

Soyer provides a JAAOS review on fractures of the base of the first metacarpal. The author notes that these injuries can be roughly divided into extra- and intra-articular variants and that intra-articular fractures present treatment challenges due to the multitude of deforming forces acting on the base of the thumb. The author recommends surgical treatment for unstable fractures, and that the goal should be 1mm or less of residual displacement following treatment. The author cautions, however, that malunion of these fractures often results in long-term disability.

Illustration A is a schematic of the ligaments which provide stability to the thumb carpal-metacarpal joint.

Incorrection Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: the abductor pollicis brevis is not a deforming force in the setting of a Bennett fracture.
Answers 3 and 5: the brachioradialis provides a deforming force for certain distal radius fractures, but not the base of the thumb metacarpal fractures.
