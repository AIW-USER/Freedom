## Question

A young arthroplasty surgeon started using a robot for his total knee arthroplasties (TKAs). He enjoys the increased accuracy of the bone cuts but is concerned about the added operative time. The surgeon would like to start implanting cementless total knee components to decrease operative time. Which patient is an ideal candidate for cementless fixation?

## Options

1. 52-year-old male football coach with tricompartmental osteoarthritis and a BMI of 44 kg/m².
2. 61-year-old female librarian with rheumatoid arthritis after failed nonoperative treatments.
3. 69-year-old female with tricompartmental osteoarthritis and a T-score of - 2.7.
4. 59-year-old male with osteoarthritis and a history of radiation therapy to the ipsilateral thigh, knee, and leg for a soft-tissue sarcoma.
5. 75-year-old caucasian female with tricompartmental osteoarthritis after failed nonoperative management.

## Correct option

**1. 52-year-old male football coach with tricompartmental osteoarthritis and a BMI of 44 kg/m².**

## Answer explanation

The ideal candidate for a cementless TKA is a younger, active patient with an elevated BMI (answer choice 1). The other answer choices represent current contraindications to cementless fixation.

Cementless, or press-fit, TKA components provide a promising alternative to cemented fixation, particularly in young, active, and /or obese patients. Several studies have demonstrated similar outcomes for cementless and cemented TKA when properly indicated. Cementless TKA is associated with shorter operative times and increased cost but equivalent short-term and long-term outcome data. With cementless fixation, the end goal is osseointegration. Theoretically, younger, active patients provide an increased chance of successful osseointegration into the implant. Contraindications for cementless TKA are inflammatory arthritis, metabolic bone disease, older age, and previous irradiation. To minimize the risk of aseptic failure, the surgeon should focus on accurate and precise bone cuts. Some authors argue that robotic-assisted surgery allows for more accurate bone cuts.

*Kamath et al. provide a review of cementless fixation in TKA, including its history, indications, technique, current materials, and reported outcomes.* Historically, cementless implants were riddled with issues secondary to poor design and material coating. Currently, newer porous metals (tantalum and titanium, for example) demonstrate improved osseointegration capabilities. This review highlights techniques for successful press-fit fixation, which include protection of resected surfaces, avoidance of unequal defects, and scrutiny of impaction. **The authors note** the importance of appropriate indications, which include younger, active, and obese patients with good bone quality. Overall, this review provides a review of the evolution of cementless TKAs, recommendations for successful implantation, and examples of successful clinical outcomes.

*Sinicrope et al. published a retrospective study comparing the clinical results of cementless vs cemented primary TKA in morbidly obese (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²) patients.* *In total, 193 patients (108 cementless, 85 cemented) patients were included with a minimum follow-up of 5 years.* *This study found that cementless implants had a 99% survivorship rate at 8 years.* Cemented implants had an 88% survivorship at 8 years and an 84% survivorship at 12 years. **The authors concluded** that morbidly obese patients have a significantly lower failure and revision rate with cementless TKAs compared to cemented.

*Nam et al. published a prospective, randomized, controlled trial of patients undergoing TKA with either cemented or cementless fixation TKA of the same design.* *Patients with inflammatory arthritis, BMI > 40 kg/m², infection, or osteoporotic bone were excluded.* One hundred and forty-seven patients were included in the study and followed for 2 years postoperative. **The authors found** decreased operative time but no differences in blood loss or clinical outcomes between the two cohorts. **The authors conclude** that cementless TKA demonstrates equivalent outcomes and survivorship at 2 years when compared to cemented fixation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 2: Inflammatory arthritis is a contraindication to cementless fixation. Rheumatoid arthritis and other forms of inflammatory arthritis can leave cystic bone defects that prohibit the adequate stability needed for press-fit components.
Answer choice 3: Osteoporosis or overall poor bone quality is a contraindication to cementless fixation as adequate bone is critical for press-fit component stability and osseointegration.
Answer choice 4: Irradiated bone is a contraindication to cementless fixation. Similar to osteoporosis, radiation leaves poor bone quality in the host, which makes it unfit for the necessary osseointegration in cementless components.
Answer choice 5: Press-fit or cementless components for TKA have a track record of failure in elderly, caucasian females. For this reason, cemented components will be indicated for this patient.
