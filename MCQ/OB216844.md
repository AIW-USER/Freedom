## Question

A 62-year-old male presents to your office with complaints of left shoulder pain for the past 2 months. He reports that the pain began when he slipped on the ice. Since that time, he has been having difficulty sleeping and managing tasks overhead. An MRI reveals a large rotator cuff tear of the supraspinatus tendon with 2 cm of retraction. He is booked for rotator cuff repair. Which of the following would carry the lowest risk for retear?

## Options

1. Goutallier 1 with double-row trans-osseous equivalent repair
2. Goutallier 1 with single-row repair
3. Goutallier 3 with single row repair
4. Goutallier 4 with double-row trans-osseous equivalent repair
5. Goutallier 4 with single row repair

## Correct option

**1. Goutallier 1 with double-row trans-osseous equivalent repair**

## Answer explanation

Biomechanical studies and systematic reviews have demonstrated that double-row techniques are superior to single-row techniques with respects to retear rates.

Rotator cuff tears are a very common source of shoulder pain and decreased motion that can occur due to both traumatic injuries in young patients as well as degenerative disease in the elderly patient. Diagnosis can be suspected clinically with provocative tests of the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis, but confirmation requires an MRI or CT arthrogram of the shoulder. Treatment can be nonoperative or operative depending on the chronicity of symptoms, severity of the tear, degree of muscle fatty atrophy, patient age and patient activity demands. Several biomechanical studies and systematic reviews have demonstrated a higher retear rate with single row repairs with little to no functional difference compared with double row repairs

*DeHaan et al. reviewed single-row versus double-row arthroscopic rotator cuff repair techniques.* *In this systematic review,* **they reported** that the functional ASES, Constant, and UCLA outcome scores revealed no difference between single- and double-row rotator cuff repairs, and the total retear rate, which included both complete and partial retears, was 43% for the single-row repair and 27% for the double-row repair, representing a trend toward higher failures in the single-row group. **They concluded** that the single-row repairs did not differ from the double-row repairs in functional outcome scores but revealed a trend toward a lower radiographic proven retear rate.

*Millett et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of all available level I randomized controlled trials comparing single-row with double-row repair to statistically compare clinical outcomes and imaging-diagnosed re-tear rates.* **They reported** that single-row repairs resulted in significantly higher re-tear rates compared with double-row repairs, especially with regard to partial-thickness re-tears. **They concluded** that there were no detectable differences in improvement in outcomes scores between single-row and double-row repairs.
