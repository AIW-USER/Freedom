## Question

A concerned mother brings her 3-year-old son to your office to discuss his flat feet. On examination, he has a painless foot with normal subtalar motion. The foot is otherwise plantigrade. An orthotist in their neighborhood manufactured custom-molded arch supports for his shoes. Mom finds the arch supports difficult to use, and notes that the child has nearly outgrown them. She asks if she needs to replace them. The best advice for mom is:

## Options

1. Continue to wear the inserts as they will reliably improve the patient's gait
2. Continue to wear the inserts as they demonstrate reliable radiographic change in the longitudinal arch
3. Discontinue the inserts as there is evidence they will weaken the foot intrinsics and worsen the deformity
4. Continue or discontinue the inserts based on the patient's comfort with them, and the deformity often resolves with time
5. Continue to wear the inserts as they will reliably halt progression of calcaneal valgus

## Correct option

**4. Continue or discontinue the inserts based on the patient's comfort with them, and the deformity often resolves with time**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a painless pes planus. There is no evidence to suggest that orthotics improve the natural course of this deformity, but there is also no strong contraindication to their use.

Flexible pes planus occurs with a prevalence of 44% among school-aged children and gradually decreases in prevalence with age. If flexible on examination (i.e., the arch reconstitutes with toe raise), imaging is not required. Pes planus which fails to resolve or which is not flexible may be an indicator for other structural pathology such as tarsal coalition, and should be investigated further with imaging studies. Orthoses have been historically used to improve the natural history of this disease, but there is a lack of reproducible, strong evidence to support their utility.

*Pfeiffer et. al. evaluated the prevalence of pes planus in pre-school-aged children, aged 3-6 years.* *Among the 835 children studied, the prevalence of a clinical pes planus was 44%, with <1% being pathologic in nature.* The prevalence decreased dramatically with age, with a prevalence of 54% among 3-year-olds, decreasing to 24% among 6-year-olds. Boys were more commonly affected than girls (52% vs 36%).

*Jane MacKenzie et. al. performed a systematic review of non-surgical treatment of flexible pes planus.* **The results demonstrated** generally poor evidence to guide recommendations. Only one randomized controlled trial was available, which demonstrated no difference in outcomes between children who utilized arch supports and those who did not. **The authors concluded** based on the available evidence that orthoses did not immediately improve radiographic parameters, had no clear effect on gait, and demonstrated no other obvious clinical efficacy.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1, 2, 5: There is poor evidence to suggest use of shoe inserts of any type will cause radiographic changes, prevent deformity, or improve gait.
Answer 3: There is no evidence to suggest that use of shoe inserts will cause worsening of the deformity.
