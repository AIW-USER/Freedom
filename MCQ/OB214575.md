## Options

1. He has a > 95% chance to return to baseball-related activities following arthroscopic repair
2. If he returns to baseball activities after arthroscopic repair, he has a > 90% chance of returning to the same level of play
3. He has higher chance to return to the same level of activity after arthroscopic repair since he is not a pitcher
4. He has a < 10% chance to return to baseball-related activities with non-operative management
5. If he returns to baseball activities after non-operative management, he has a < 10% chance of returning to the same level of play

## Correct option

**3. He has higher chance to return to the same level of activity after arthroscopic repair since he is not a pitcher**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a right shoulder type II SLAP tear. Studies have shown that in professional baseball players, nonpitchers have a much higher chance of returning to sport and to the same level of baseball activity than do pitchers after arthroscopic repair.

Repair for Type II SLAP lesions is a procedure that has enjoyed a variable success rate in baseball players. While a certain percentage (60-70%) of athletes return to the same level of play after SLAP repairs, a certain subset of athletes do poorly due to decreased range of motion and the development of post-surgical adhesive capsulitis. Furthermore, there is a significant decrease in the return to play (RTP) rate and the return to prior play (RPP) rate in pitchers as compared to non-pitchers after both operative and non-operative management. The propensity for stiffness after SLAP repair is the reason that SLAP tear debridement with biceps tenotomy or tenodesis is considered the mainstay of treatment in patients greater than 40 years old.

*Thayaparan et al. evaluated the RTP rate after arthroscopic SLAP repair in 270 overhead athletes, of which 146 were pitchers.* Across all patients, there was a 69.0% overall RTP rate, with a mean time to return to sport of 9 months. **The authors noted** that the RTP rate for pitchers was 57.5% compared to 85% in nonpitchers after arthroscopic SLAP repair. **They concluded** that arthroscopic SLAP repair is associated with a fair return to sport, with 69% of individuals undergoing arthroscopic SLAP repair returning to the sport with pitchers having a significantly decreased return to the sport in comparison with nonpitching athletes.

*Gilliam et al. reported on the RTP rates of baseball players after SLAP repair.* *The authors looked at 216 baseball players who had isolated SLAP repair or SLAP repair with debridement of partial-thickness (<25%) rotator cuff tears.* *Of the players, 133 were reached by phone for a follow-up interview at a mean of 78 months.* **They noted** that 62% of players successfully returned to play. RTP rates were 59% for pitchers and 76% for nonpitchers. **They also noted** that the percentage of patients who felt the same or better at follow-up compared to preinjury was significantly higher among nonpitchers (66%) than pitchers (43%). **The authors concluded** that SLAP repair should continue to be considered as an option for SLAP tear treatment only after nonsurgical management has failed.

*Fedoriw et al. performed a retrospective review of 68 players within a single professional baseball organization with persistent shoulder pain with MRI-documented SLAP lesions.* **They noted** that 21 pitchers successfully completed the nonsurgical algorithm with an RTP rate of 40% and an RPP rate of 22%. The RTP rate for 27 pitchers who underwent surgical intervention was 48%, and the RPP rate was 7%. For 10 position players treated nonsurgically, the RTP rate was 39%, and the RPP rate was 26%. The RTP rate for 13 position players who underwent surgical intervention was 85%, with an RPP rate of 54%. **The author concluded** that the RTP and RPP rates were higher for position players than for pitchers and nonsurgical treatment should be considered for professional baseball players with documented SLAP lesions, as it can lead to acceptable RTP and RPP rates.

Figures A and B are the normal right shoulder AP Grashey and scapular Y radiographs. Figures C and D are the T2-weighted post-contrast MRI arthrogram revealing a type II SLAP tear.
