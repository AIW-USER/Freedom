## Question

A 62-year-old male presents to the clinic for continued follow-up of left shoulder pain unresponsive to over-the-counter analgesics, physical therapy, and corticosteroid injections. An MRI of the left shoulder was obtained, demonstrating supraspinatus and infraspinatus tears with 6.2 centimeters of retraction with Goutallier grade II changes in the supraspinatus. There is suspicion of suprascapular nerve entrapment, given the patient's degree of retraction and fatty infiltration. Which of the following is true concerning concomitant suprascapular nerve release with rotator cuff repair?

## Options

1. Pain scores are improved with suprascapular nerve release
2. Range of motion is improved with suprascapular nerve release
3. Complication rates are increased with suprascapular nerve release
4. No difference in functional outcomes or pain with suprascapular nerve release
5. Fatty infiltration is decreased on MRI following suprascapular nerve release

## Correct option

**4. No difference in functional outcomes or pain with suprascapular nerve release**

## Answer explanation

This 62-year-old male sustained a left rotator cuff tear that remained symptomatic despite nonoperative efforts. Incorporation of a suprascapular nerve release with massive rotator cuff repair does not improve postoperative function scores (Answer 4).

Rotator cuff tears (RCTs) are common injuries, estimated to be present in ~30% of the population over 60. Tears that remain symptomatic despite nonoperative efforts typically require operative intervention, with the size of the tear and integrity of the rotator cuff posing as significant factors in determining the type of treatment. Large (3-5 cm retraction) or massive (greater than 5 cm of retraction) RCTs present unique challenges, including suprascapular nerve dysfunction. The entity, estimated to be present in 8-27% of massive RCTs, is postulated to stem from nerve stretching secondary to chronic tendon retraction. The process contributes to fatty infiltration observed in the supraspinatus and infraspinatus (in conjunction with the tear). As such, some believe concomitant suprascapular nerve release can be warranted in this scenario, however recent literature suggests the inclusion of the release fails to improve pain or range of motion any more appreciably than rotator cuff repair alone.

*Ma et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of nine studies totaling 279 patients undergoing rotator cuff repair with or without suprascapular nerve release.* While the group receiving the nerve release did experience improvements in function and pain relief compared to their preoperative scores, this degree of improvement was determined to be similar to those receiving rotator cuff repairs alone. Ultimately, **the authors conclude** the inclusion of the technique does not appear to improve outcomes following rotator cuff repair.

*Arce et al. discuss suprascapular nerve entrapment in the setting of massive RCTs and provide a technique for releasing the nerve during the rotator cuff repair.* In addition to the standard anterior and posterolateral portals during shoulder arthroscopy, the authors include two retro-clavicular portals between the clavicle and scapular spine, which are placed overlying the muscle belly of the supraspinatus, being 7 and 9 cm medial from the edge of the acromion. After identification of the nerve and artery traversing the suprascapular notch, the superior transverse scapular ligament is then released. **The authors conclude** by noting the technique to be challenging and recommend multiple trials of the technique on cadavers before performing in vivo.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 2, 3, and 5: suprascapular nerve release does not appear to portend any appreciable improvement in range of motion or pain compared to repair alone.
