## Question

A 26-year-old female presents to the trauma bay after a head-on motor vehicle collision. She is complaining of right hip pain, and initial radiographs demonstrate a posterior hip dislocation. The hip is reduced under propofol sedation and repeat radiographs are obtained confirming reduction. CT scan is then obtained and does not show any loose bodies but does demonstrate a posterior wall fracture. Which of the following is most accurate in predicting instability of the posterior wall fracture that would indicate an open reduction and internal fixation in this patient?

## Options

1. MRI demonstrating evidence of labral/capsular injury in addition to posterior wall fracture
2. CT scan with 20% posterior wall involvement
3. CT scan showing fracture exiting inferiorly
4. Dynamic evaluation of hip under anesthesia
5. Iliac oblique judet radiogram confirming fracture

## Correct option

**4. Dynamic evaluation of hip under anesthesia**

## Answer explanation

In the setting of posterior wall acetabular fractures, a dynamic evaluation under anesthesia (EUA) is the best test to indicate whether the hip is unstable and helps determine whether open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the fragment is necessary.

Acetabular fractures can occur in younger patients with significant trauma, such as the MVC in this example. The force vector helps determine the fracture pattern and when the force is directed posteriorly, often a posterior wall fragment is involved. This is also quite common in cases of posterior hip dislocations where a significant posteriorly directed force occurs. In order to evaluate the stability of the hip after reduction, an EUA may be performed. In doing this, the hip is positioned in flexion and adduction and axially loaded. An increase in joint space suggests instability and would indicate posterior wall ORIF is beneficial. Traditionally, evidence of a fracture fragment >20% on CT scan was thought to be a good indicator of the need for fixation, though this has become less widely accepted as the predictive values are relatively poor for correlating fragment size with instability.

*Moed et al. looked at the ability to predict hip stability based on CT scans of posterior wall fractures.* *They used the previously described methods including those described by Calkins et al and Keith and* **found** that both had a substantial number of incorrect predictions based on their measurements. Based on their results, **they concluded** that neither method was especially good at predicting instability based on posterior wall fragment size and that there were a number of hips that were both stable and unstable with a posterior wall fragment measuring >20% in size.

*Firoozabadi et al. also looked at radiographic parameters to help determine stability in posterior wall acetabular fractures.* *They performed an EUA and then correlated these results to radiographic findings described by Moed et al. and Keith et al.* **They found** that 23% of unstable hips had a posterior wall fracture that measured <20% in size. Therefore, **they recommended** that posterior wall fracture size <20% was not a reliable indicator of stability.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While the MRI would likely show evidence of capsulolabral injury, this alone is not predictive of further instability and should not be used as an indicator for posterior wall ORIF.
Answer 2: Traditionally, >20% of posterior wall involvement measured by multiple calculation methods was thought to represent an indication for surgical fixation. This being said, newer studies have identified hips as unstable with smaller degrees of posterior wall involvement. Ultimately fracture size should not be used to indicate a patient for ORIF.
Answer 3: Cranial exit of the fracture into the dome has been correlated as a significant predictor for instability but similarly should not be used as a definitive determinant for the need to perform posterior fracture fixation.
Answer 5: Iliac oblique Judet radiographs are best to evaluate the posterior column and anterior wall and should not be used to make operative decisions regarding the posterior column.
