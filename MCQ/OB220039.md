## Options

1. Admit for loading dose of levetiracetam followed by hemiarthroplasty given the risk of dislocation
2. Hemiarthroplasty with combined distal tibial allograft glenoid reconstruction
3. Modified McLauglin procedure with transfer of the subscapularis into the anterior humeral defect
4. Posterior glenoid reconstruction with iliac crest bone graft and humeral head grafting
5. Social work consult and neurology referral to establish control of his seizure disorder

## Correct option

**5. Social work consult and neurology referral to establish control of his seizure disorder**

## Answer explanation

The patient has recurrent posterior instability with both humeral and glenoid bone loss in the setting of an uncontrolled seizure disorder. In order to protect a planned reconstruction surgery, the most appropriate next step would be a consultation to obtain control of his seizures (Answer 5).

Shoulder dislocation in the absence of trauma, especially a posterior dislocation, should warrant a neurologic workup. While operative intervention may eventually be warranted for the shoulder, full neurologic evaluation should be done first to best prevent recurrent dislocation. Once a seizure disorder has been diagnosed and appropriately treated, posterior glenoid bone loss can be treated with a reconstruction procedure utilizing a bone block from the iliac crest autograft or distal tibial allograft. Large reverse hill-sachs lesions on the humeral head may also engage and predispose to recurrence; thus an anterior procedure such as a modified McLaughlin involving transfer of the subscapularis into the defect or open humeral head grafting may be necessary. For chronic, locked posterior dislocations with significant humeral head bone loss, a hemiarthroplasty may be the one effective treatment option, with or without concomitant posterior glenoid reconstruction.

*Robinson et al. reviewed the epidemiology, risk of recurrence, and functional outcome after an acute traumatic posterior dislocation of the shoulder.* *The authors retrospectively reviewed 112 patients who sustained 120 posterior glenohumeral dislocations and found that most dislocations (67%) were produced by a traumatic accident, with most of the remainder produced by seizures, of which twenty patients (twenty-three shoulders) developed recurrent instability.* **They concluded** that the most common complication after this injury is recurrent instability, which occurs at an early stage in 17.7% of shoulders within the first year after dislocation, with risk factors being age less than forty years old, dislocation sustained during a seizure, and those with a large humeral head defect.

*Rouleau et al. performed a systematic review of the literature regarding the incidence of associated injuries in posterior shoulder dislocations.* *The authors reviewed a total of 108 articles including a total of 475 patients (543 shoulders) and found that seizures were reported in 34% of cases, with a majority of dislocations (65%) having associated injuries.* Fracture was the most commonly associated injury, followed by reverse Hill-Sachs and cuff tears; notably, in the absence of fracture or reverse Hill-Sachs injury, the risk of cuff tear increased nearly fivefold. **They concluded** that the amount of associated injuries related to posterior shoulder dislocation is far greater than previously thought.
