## Question

A 10-year-old boy presents to your clinic with a one-week history of atraumatic left knee pain. On exam, his knee is warm and swollen, but he is able to bear weight. He has had a low-grade fever intermittently that improves with Tylenol. His serum white count and C-reactive protein are mildly elevated. His father says they recently got back from a camping trip to the Appalachian mountains two weeks ago, and the boy had a strange rash for a few days after the trip. Which of the following best describes the most causative micro-organism involved in this patient’s disease?

## Options

1. Gram-positive cocci in clusters
2. Polymicrobial
3. Spirochete
4. Gram-negative diplococci
5. Gram-negative coccobacilli

## Correct option

**3. Spirochete**

## Answer explanation

The constellation of mono-arthropathy, history of a low-grade infection with a rash that preceded the knee pain, and recent travel to an endemic area make Lyme disease the most likely cause of this patient's knee pain. Lyme disease is caused by the spirochete Borrelia Burgdorferi (Answer 3).

Differentiating septic arthritis from Lyme arthritis can be challenging diagnostically. However, it is important to differentiate the two from one another as Lyme disease is typically treated medically with antibiotics (i.e., Doxycycline), while true septic arthritis may require formal irrigation and debridement. On the patient's history, clues that point more towards Lyme disease include a history of an antecedent infection 1-2 weeks prior to the development of joint pain, a history of a bull's-eye rash (erythema migrans), and recent travel to the Northeast. In fact, the presence of erythema migrans and a history of travel to an endemic area often prompts treatment for Lyme without any further testing. On examination and laboratory testing, Lyme disease is often less inflammatory and aggressive than septic arthritis; patients with Lyme disease often have lower temperatures, only mild elevation in inflammatory markers, and often can still bear weight on the extremity, unlike patients with septic arthritis. To confirm Lyme disease, ELISA testing is performed, followed by Western blotting of serum antibodies to Lyme pathogens. The mainstay of treatment for Lyme disease is medical. Most patients require a 1-2 week course of antibiotics, usually doxycycline. If the patient is <8-years-old, the recommendation is to avoid tetracyclines, and amoxicillin can be used instead. Synovectomy is performed only in cases of chronic arthritis that do not respond to antibiotics.

*Cruz et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of patients with Lyme arthropathy involving the hip.* *They calculated mean laboratory values for these patients and compared them with those of a cohort of patients with either septic arthritis or transient synovitis.* *They included 12 articles for a total of 88 patients.* *The patients with Lyme disease had a mean serum white count of 10.6 vs 12.4 in patients with septic arthritis.* The mean ESR and CRP values for Lyme patients were 41 and 3.9, compared to 54 and 8.8 in patients with septic arthritis. A greater portion of Lyme disease patients were still able to bear weight compared to septic arthritis patients. **The authors concluded** that while both septic arthritis and Lyme disease can present similarly, Lyme disease typically presents with less inflammatory marker elevation and less aggressively compared to septic arthritis.

*Konopka et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 106 patients diagnosed with Lyme arthritis.* *In their cohort, 10 patients (9.4%) underwent formal irrigation and debridement in the operating room.* These patients were more likely to present with fever and elevations in their inflammatory markers. It took roughly 43 hours for the confirmatory laboratory tests to finalize, which was roughly 8.7 hours after surgery. **The authors concluded** that a high index of suspicion for Lyme arthritis should be maintained in endemic areas, and more efficient testing should be considered to try to avoid surgery in roughly 10% of cases.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes Staph aureus which is a common cause of septic arthritis
Answer 2: Polymicrobial infections are more common when there is an open wound or in patients with diabetes. They are not a common cause of septic arthritis
Answer 4: This describes gonococcal arthritis which would be very unlikely in this 10-year-old boy
Answer 5: This describes Kingella kingae, which can cause arthritis in children. However, Kingella typically infects children under the age of three. Moreover, this patient's history is more classic for Lyme arthritis.
