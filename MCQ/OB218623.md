## Question

A 44-year-old obese (BMI 35.2 kg/m2) male is being seen in your clinic for heel pain. He states that the pain has been present for years and that his symptoms have been recalcitrant to multiple rounds of physical therapy, NSAIDs, and shoewear modifications. A physical exam reveals that he has tenderness to palpation over the Achilles insertion. For which of the following scenarios would Achilles debridement and calcaneal exosectomy alone be the appropriate surgical plan?

## Options

1. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure A, with ankle dorsiflexion of 10° with the knee extended and an MRI which demonstrates 60% degeneration of the Achilles tendon
2. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure A, with ankle dorsiflexion of -10° with the knee extended, which improves to 10° with knee flexion, and an MRI which demonstrates 60% degeneration of the Achilles tendon
3. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure B, with ankle dorsiflexion of 10° with the knee extended and an MRI which demonstrates 60% degeneration of the Achilles tendon
4. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure B, with ankle dorsiflexion of 10° with the knee extended and an MRI which demonstrates 30% degeneration of the Achilles tendon
5. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure B, with ankle dorsiflexion of -10° with the knee extended, which does not improve with knee flexion, and an MRI which demonstrates 30% degeneration of the Achilles tendon

## Correct option

**4. Heel radiographs as shown in Figure B, with ankle dorsiflexion of 10° with the knee extended and an MRI which demonstrates 30% degeneration of the Achilles tendon**

## Answer explanation

This patient has chronic Achilles tendonitis. Achilles debridement and exosectomy alone is appropriate for a patient with the presence of a Haglund deformity (Figure B), no evidence of a gastrocnemius or Achilles contracture, and <50% Achilles degeneration (Answer 4).

Achilles tendonitis is a series of 3 conditions affecting the Achilles tendon which include: insertional Achilles tendonitis, retrocalcaneal bursitis with Haglund deformity, and mid-substance Achilles tendonitis. Most patients will complain of posterior distal leg pain along the Achilles and at its insertion. The diagnosis is usually made clinically, although imaging is helpful in characterizing the subtype of Achilles tendonitis present. Intratendinous calcifications are often present in the chronic setting, and a Haglund deformity is easily visualized as an enlargement of the posterosuperior tuberosity of the calcaneus seen best on the lateral radiograph (Figure B). The initial treatment for Achilles tendonitis is nonoperative with NSAIDs, activity modification, shoe wear modification, and physical therapy. Surgery is reserved for patients with symptoms unresponsive to nonoperative management. The optimal surgical strategy is dependent on a variety of factors. Almost all patients will undergo debridement of the Achilles tendon. In patients with a Haglund deformity, an exosectomy is required to prevent symptom recurrence. Patients with >50% tendon degeneration often necessitate a tendon transfer at the time of debridement. Finally, patients with Achilles or gastrocnemius contractures will also undergo an Achilles lengthening or gastrocnemius recession, respectively.

*Chimenti et al. provide a current concepts review regarding the treatment of insertional Achilles tendinopathy.* Overall, **the authors highlight** the importance of carefully examining the patient, obtaining a thorough history, and reviewing available imaging, as many adjunct procedures are weakly supported by the available evidence. **The authors conclude** that each surgical plan should be carefully tailored to each patient, and aside from open debridement and decompression, additional procedures should not be ubiquitously performed, and should only be performed if needed.

*Hunt et al. investigated the surgical treatment of insertional Achilles tendinopathy with or without FHL transfer.* *This was a prospective, randomized study that ultimately included 18 patients in the control group and 21 in the FHL transfer group.* Overall, **the authors found** no difference in pain, functional outcome, and patient satisfaction when comparing patients treated with Achilles debridement alone versus FHL augmentation. Overall, **the authors conclude** that while FHL transfer confers minimal, if any, loss of function, it does not appear to be necessary for primary cases, and is most likely best reserved for patients with high degrees of tendon degeneration.

Figure A demonstrates a normal lateral foot radiograph, with no Haglund deformity present and no evidence of intratendinous calcifications. Figure B demonstrates a Haglund deformity, seen as an enlargement of the posterosuperior calcaneus, indicative of chronic Achilles tendonitis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient would be best treated with debridement and FHL transfer, given the >50% degeneration present. No exostectomy is required for this patient.
Answer 2: This patient would be best treated with debridement, FHL transfer, and a gastrocnemius recession, given the improvement in dorsiflexion with knee flexion. No exostectomy is required for this patient.
Answer 3: This patient would be best treated with debridement, FHL transfer, and exostectomy.
Answer 5: This patient would be best treated with debridement, Achilles lengthening, and exostectomy.
