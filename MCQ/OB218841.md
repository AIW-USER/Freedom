## Question

A 44-year-old female well known to your clinic is being seen for follow-up after undergoing nonoperative treatment of a wrist sprain. She notes that over the past 6 weeks, her pain has become increasingly more severe and that even attempting to put gloves on causes extreme pain. She also notes that her affected extremity seems to sweat more than the unaffected side, and she reports difficulty opening jars due to weakness in her grip strength. The patient is otherwise healthy, and noted that a sympathetic block administered by her PM&R physician provided marked, albeit temporary, relief. Which of the following diagnoses best encapsulates her constellation of symptoms?

## Options

1. Compartment Syndrome
2. Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Type I
3. Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Type II
4. Median Nerve Neuroma
5. Pyogenic Flexor Tenosynovitis (FTS)

## Correct option

**2. Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Type I**

## Answer explanation

This patient with hyperalgesia to light touch, increased sweating, and weakness is most likely suffering from Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Type I (Answer 2).

CRPS, also known as reflex sympathetic dystrophy (RSD), is an idiopathic condition triggered by an aberrant inflammatory response that leads to sustained sympathetic activity in a continuous reflex arc. Females are more commonly affected than males (4:1), and there is an increasing incidence of CRPS with age. 60% of CRPS cases occur in the upper extremities, while the remaining 40% occur in the lower extremities. Risk factors for the development of CRPS include trauma with an exaggerated response to injury, surgery, prolonged immobilization, anxiety and/or depression, a history of migraines, tobacco use, and a history of fibromyalgia. The diagnosis of CRPS is made clinically through the implementation of the Budapest criteria (Illustration A). There are two major subtypes of CRPS: Type I and Type II. CRPS Type I is the most common form and involves symptoms without demonstratable nerve damage. CRPS Type II is less common and is associated with identifiable nerve damage and an absence of a positive response with sympathetic blocks. The ideal treatment of CRPS is yet to be identified, and a variety of nonoperative and operative interventions are currently being investigated.

Harden and co-authors performed a validation study of the Budapest criteria in the diagnosis of CRPS. Specifically, the authors had concerns over the potential for overdiagnosis with the then-current International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) criteria. Their study included 113 CRPS and 47 non-CRPS neuropathic pain patients. The authors found that while the IASP criteria demonstrated high sensitivity (1.00), the criteria, unfortunately, carried poor specificity (0.41) in the diagnosis of CRPS. On the other hand, the Budapest criteria greatly improved upon the specificity (0.68-0.79) while maintaining exceptional sensitivity (0.99). Overall, the authors' results corroborated the validity of the Budapest criteria as a diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of CRPS.

*Li et al. provided a review investigating CRPS after hand surgery.* **The authors note** that CRPS has the potential to complicate recovery, delay return to work and increase the likelihood of poor outcomes and/or litigation. The authors describe an incidence of CRPS as high as 40% after fasciectomy for Dupuytren contracture and 39% after treatment of a distal radius fracture. **The authors conclude** that while many pharmacologic treatments are available, none have demonstrated clinical superiority as of the time of publication.

*Kessler et al. provide a recent comprehensive review of CRPS.* **The authors highlight** the difficulty of establishing the diagnosis, even when diagnostic tools such as the Budapest criteria are implemented. Furthermore, **the authors highlight** the importance of early detection and interdisciplinary treatment. **The authors conclude** by highlighting the need for continued research into the optimal treatment of this debilitating condition.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: compartment syndrome would most likely present secondary to a crush-type injury with tense intrinsic hand compartments, pain with passive stretch, paresthesias, and pulselessness.
Answer 3: while CRPS Type II may present similarly to CRPS Type I, these patients do not respond to sympathetic blockade, and often have a history of an injury that causes direct nerve damage.
Answer 4: a median neuroma would most likely present with neuropathic pain on the radial aspect of the hand, diminished 2-point discrimination, and focal weakness with thumb palmar abduction.
Answer 5: flexor tenosynovitis (FTS) would most likely present with single-digit fusiform swelling, a resting flexed position, pain with passive extension, and exquisite tenderness over the flexor tendon sheath.
