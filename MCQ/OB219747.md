## Question

A 19-year-old male presents to the clinic for evaluation of left shoulder pain following a fall while snowboarding. Upon falling a few days earlier, the patient reported an audible pop in the shoulder with immediate pain. On exam, there is a palpable deformity with tenderness. There is no posterior fullness or horizontal instability. Radiographs are obtained, as shown in Figures A and B. The patient expresses a strong desire for surgical treatment. When discussing the current literature on surgical intervention for this specific injury, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. Surgical intervention has a higher risk for complications
2. Operative treatment typically results in improved functional outcomes
3. Nonoperative treatment allows for faster return to activity
4. I and III
5. I, II, and III

## Correct option

**4. I and III**

## Answer explanation

This 19-year-old male sustained a left type III acromioclavicular (AC) joint injury, as demonstrated by his plain films. While controversial, recent literature has shown surgical intervention to bestow a higher risk for complications, equivalent functional outcomes at one year, and prolonged return to activity compared to nonoperative treatment (Answer 4).

AC joint injuries are common occurrences, having a high predilection for younger males following a direct blow to the shoulder. The Rockwood classification is the most widely used classification system, as it proficiently categorizes and dictates treatment (Illustration A). Most injuries, which are Rockwood types I-III, can be treated nonoperatively with two to four weeks of sling immobilization followed by therapy. On the contrary, types IV-VI are considered unstable and have traditionally necessitated surgical treatment. However, the ideal treatment for type III injuries is highly debated, and recent literature suggests type IV and V injuries may also be amenable to nonoperative treatment. Moreover, recent studies suggest patients return to similar levels of function. At the same time, nonoperative treatment is less costly, confers less risk for complications, and allows for a more expeditious return to activity.

*Frank et al. provide a review detailing AC joint injuries, discussing the anatomy, biomechanics, evaluation, and treatment.* **The authors note** nonoperative treatment as the mainstay for types I and II, while the ideal treatment of type III remains controversial, largely due to the lack of high-quality investigations. **They note** that of the available studies, the literature suggests equivalent functional outcomes between nonsurgical and surgical interventions.

*Franovic et al. performed a systematic review of 45 studies examining the success rates and costs of treating type III AC joint injuries nonoperatively, compared to their operative counterparts.* **The authors noted** nonoperative treatment yields the lowest cost and highest degree of utility, given their high rates of successful treatment.

*Chang et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies examining operative versus nonoperative treatment of type III-V AC joint injuries.* **The authors noted** a significant improvement in Constant scores following operative treatment compared to the nonoperative group but note that while being statistically significant, the overall difference (3.14 points) likely doesn't make a meaningful clinical difference. Moreover, **the authors noted** an earlier return to work in the nonoperative group by an average of four weeks, while cosmesis was significantly improved, favoring the operative group. Overall, **the authors concluded** no significant differences exist between surgical and nonsurgical groups concerning functional outcomes.

Figures A & B represent AP and axillary radiographs of the left shoulder/AC joint demonstrating a grade III AC joint separation. Illustration A demonstrates the Rockwood Classification.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 5: Operative treatment of type III AC joint injuries does not reliably improve functional outcomes.
