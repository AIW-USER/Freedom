## Question

A 22-year-old male college gymnast presents to the office for evaluation of forearm pain, which has been present for four months. He reports significant pain and tightness in his bilateral forearms after 10 minutes of tumbling, which subsides with cessation of activity. He denies any numbness or tingling during the episodes and has not attempted any treatment prior to evaluation. Exertional compartment syndrome of the bilateral forearms is suspected, and the patient undergoes compartment pressure measurements, which reveal a pressure of 25 mmHg and 32 mmHg in the left and right forearm, respectively, five minutes post-exercise. What is the most appropriate management?

## Options

1. Endoscopic fasciotomy
2. NSAIDs, activity modification, physical therapy emphasizing forearm stretching and endurance
3. Observation with serial evaluations
4. Open fasciotomy alone
5. Open fasciotomy with fasciectomy

## Correct option

**2. NSAIDs, activity modification, physical therapy emphasizing forearm stretching and endurance**

## Answer explanation

This 22-year-old male gymnast has a clinical presentation suggestive of chronic exertional compartment syndrome, with compartment measurements confirming the diagnosis. The most appropriate course of treatment is physical therapy (Answer B) as 1) the patient has not undergone any course of treatment and 2) upper extremity chronic exertional compartment syndrome has high success rates with nonoperative treatment.

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is among the most common exercise-induced conditions and is most often seen in males in the third decade of life. The leg is most commonly affected, followed by the forearm. Each respective site has been linked with certain sports. Leg ECS is associated with running sports, while forearm CECS is seen commonly in motocross, rock climbing, gymnasts, and rowing. While both present with compartment tightness, pain, and weakness, forearm CECS rarely presents with numbness or paresthesias. The definitive diagnosis is made with compartment measurements, with resting pressures > 15 mmHg, immediate post-exercise pressures > 30 mmHg, five-minute post-exercise pressures > 20 mmHg, and/or fifteen-minute post-exercise pressures > 15 mmHg considered diagnostic. All CECS should undergo a trial of nonoperative management (physical therapy focusing on stretching and conditioning) before considering compartmental release. The forearm, in particular, has found success with therapy, with reports of approximately 80% experiencing improvement in symptoms. This success contrasts with leg CECS, where physical therapy has been found to less reliably improve symptoms (<50%).

*Liu et al. reviewed and examined the pathophysiology, diagnostics, treatments, and outcomes seen in CECS.* **They noted** pain, loss of function, and neurological symptoms are the most commonly observed symptoms. While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electromyography (EMG)/nerve conduction studies (NCS), and infrared spectroscopy are commonly employed diagnostic modalities, compartment pressure measurements remain the gold standard. **They conclude** CECS recalcitrant to nonoperative treatment is most amenable to fasciotomy (open, mini-open, or endoscopic) release, with excellent rates of return to previous function.

*Humpherys et al. reviewed CECS of the forearm in motocross riders, an at-risk population due to prolonged gripping.* **They note** forearm pain, decreases in finger motion and grip strength, and swollen forearm as the primary symptoms, while numbness or tingling is rarely present. Intracompartmental pressure evaluation is required, as other diagnostic modalities (MRI, EMG/NCS) are often inconclusive. **They conclude** CECS of the forearm is highly amenable to nonoperative treatment, which focuses on improving rider ergonomics, motorbike setup, and forearm/wrist stretching. Only after three months of nonoperative treatment are patients indicated for fasciotomy release.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 4, and 5: Operative treatment is not indicated at this time, as the patient has not undergone a trial of nonoperative management/physical therapy.
Answer 3: Observation with serial evaluations would not improve the patient’s symptomatology
