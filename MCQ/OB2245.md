## Question

A 3-year-old male presents with inability to bear weight on his right leg for the past 3 days. They deny any known injury at that time. Examination reveals full motion of the right hip, knee, and ankle. He has tenderness to palpation over the anterior tibia with minimal swelling. No erythema is appreciated. His temperature is 99.6°F. He has no leukocytosis and CRP and ESR are normal. Radiographs of the right leg are seen in Figure A. What is the most likely diagnosis?l

![[q2245-a-toddler-fracture.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Subperiosteal abscess
2. Nondisplaced oblique or spiral fracture of the tibia with an intact fibula
3. Osteomyelitis
4. Non-ossifying fibroma
5. Non-accidental trauma

## Correct option

**2. Nondisplaced oblique or spiral fracture of the tibia with an intact fibula**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s presentation is most consistent with a toddler's fracture. A toddler’s fracture is a nondisplaced oblique or spiral fracture of the tibia with an intact fibula.

Despite the negative radiograph the child’s age and presentation are most consistent with a toddler’s fracture. An oblique radiograph may reveal the fracture but often is not needed in making the diagnosis. These injuries are often unwitnessed and require long leg cast immobilization to allow for fracture healing. Repeat imaging following a period of immobilization should demonstrate a healing periosteal reaction. While infectious etiologies need to be considered, they are less likely as the patient is afebrile with unremarkable laboratory values.

*Halsey et al. retrospectively reviewed toddler's fracture with negative radiographs on the presumption that this diagnosis, despite negative radiographs, is appropriate when the history and physical examination are consistent with the diagnosis.* **They found** that 41% of toddler's fractures were confirmed on follow-up radiographs and that no particular characteristics could predict the outcome when comparing the children who demonstrated a toddler's fracture with those who did not. **They concluded** that to avoid delay in the treatment of toddler's fracture, a long-leg cast should be applied to those children with a history of an acute injury, inability to walk or limp, no constitutional signs, and negative radiographs.

*Oudjhane et al. retrospectively reviewed 500 consecutive radiographs of acutely limping infants and toddlers.* **They reported** that 20% had a fracture as the underlying etiology. **They concluded** that fractures of the lower leg, femur, pelvis, and feet may contribute to limping in this cohort and that radiographs of the pelvis and both lower extremities including the feet should be obtained when occult trauma is suspected and the exact area of injury cannot be isolated clinically.

Figure A is the AP radiograph of a toddler's fracture which was interpreted as negative.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3: Infectious etiology such as a subperiosteal abscess and osteomyelitis should be considered. But without recent illness, and as the patient is afebrile with unremarkable laboratory values, infection is less likely.
Answer 4: A non-ossifying fibroma is a benign fibrogenic lesion that is related to dysfunctional ossification and most commonly occurs in children aged 5-15 years. These lesions are often identified on plain radiographs.
Answer 5: Non-accidental trauma (NAT) should always be considered in any pediatric injury. However, given the patient’s age, presentation and radiographs, NAT would be unlikely.
