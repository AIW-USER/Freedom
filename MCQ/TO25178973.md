## Question

In the context of a suspected RAMP lesion, which of the following best describes the diagnostic challenges and the current clinical "gold standard" for identification?

## Options

1. MRI
2. Clinical examination, specifically the McMurray test,
3. Standard anterior portal arthroscopy
4. Arthroscopic evaluation via a posteromedial portal

## Correct option

**4. Arthroscopic evaluation via a posteromedial portal**

## Answer explanation

RAMP lesion: Description and Pathoanatomy

The RAMP lesion is a meniscocapsular separation of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus — a longitudinal tear at the peripheral attachment of the posterior horn to the posterior capsule and the deep fibres of the medial collateral ligament (meniscotibial/coronary ligament). The term was coined by Staubli and Rauschning in 1994, describing a disruption that creates a "ramp-like" step between the posterior horn and the tibial plateau — the torn meniscus lifts away from its capsular attachment, exposing underlying articular cartilage.

The lesion lies in the red-red zone — the most peripheral, fully vascularised zone receiving blood supply from the perimeniscal capillary plexus. This excellent vascularity gives RAMP lesions inherent healing capacity, making repair both feasible and highly successful.

The posterior horn of the medial meniscus is a critical secondary stabiliser against anterior tibial translation in the ACL-deficient knee. When the ACL is ruptured, the medial meniscus assumes the primary role of resisting anterior subluxation, subjecting the posterior horn to increased stress and making it vulnerable to meniscocapsular separation.

Association with ACL Injury

RAMP lesions have a strong association with ACL rupture, with reported incidence of 9–40% in ACL-injured knees. The mechanism involves the pivot-shift injury causing anterior subluxation of the medial tibial plateau, impacting the posterior horn and avulsing it from its capsular attachment. Incidence increases significantly with chronicity of ACL deficiency — delayed ACL reconstruction is associated with higher RAMP lesion prevalence, emphasising the importance of timely surgical intervention.

An unrecognised and unrepaired RAMP lesion at the time of ACL reconstruction results in persistent medial compartment laxity, increased anterior tibial translation, higher ACL graft failure rates, and accelerated progression to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.

Diagnosis

Clinical examination may reveal posteromedial joint line tenderness and a positive McMurray test with posterior pain, though findings are often non-specific and attributed to the concomitant ACL injury.

MRI sensitivity is relatively low
(approximately 48–65%) because the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction is difficult to visualise on standard sequences. Suggestive findings include fluid signal interposed between the posterior horn and capsule on sagittal images, meniscocapsular discontinuity, and perimeniscal oedema.

Arthroscopic diagnosis is the gold standard but requires systematic examination of the posteromedial compartment — a "blind spot" in standard anterior arthroscopy. The arthroscope must be passed through the intercondylar notch (trans-notch/Gillquist view) or through a dedicated posteromedial portal to visualise the tear. Without this deliberate examination, RAMP lesions are frequently missed.

Classification — Thaunat and Fayard (2016)

This is the most widely referenced classification, dividing RAMP lesions into five types based on location and extent:

Type 1 — Partial Meniscocapsular Separation: Incomplete, partial-thickness tear at the meniscocapsular junction without complete separation. Meniscus remains partially attached and relatively stable on probing. The mildest form.

Type 2 — Complete Meniscocapsular Separation: Full-thickness tear through the coronary ligament with complete separation of the posterior horn from the capsule. The meniscus lifts away on probing, exposing the tibial plateau. This is the classic and most common RAMP pattern — meniscal tissue itself is intact.

Type 3 — Meniscocapsular Tear with Meniscal Extension: Tear originates at the meniscocapsular junction but extends into the meniscal substance as a partial longitudinal tear of the posterior horn. More significant structural lesion.

Type 4 — Complete Longitudinal Tear with Meniscocapsular Separation: Full-thickness longitudinal tear through the posterior horn combined with meniscocapsular separation. Produces a large unstable fragment.

Type 5 — Double Longitudinal Tear:
Two parallel longitudinal tears creating a central strip of unstable meniscal tissue. The most severe and uncommon pattern.

Management

Given their peripheral vascular location, the overriding principle is repair rather than resection.

Non-Operative Management: Limited to small, stable Type 1 (partial-thickness) tears discovered incidentally during ACL reconstruction, particularly if <1 cm and stable on probing. The haemarthrosic environment from ACL reconstruction may facilitate spontaneous healing. However, most surgeons favour repair even for partial tears given the simplicity of the technique and the critical importance of posterior horn integrity.

Surgical Repair:
Performed arthroscopically, typically concurrent with ACL reconstruction, through a posteromedial portal established under direct arthroscopic visualisation. The technique involves tear preparation (debridement of tear edges with shaver or rasp to create a bleeding surface promoting healing), followed by suture repair using all-inside suture devices (pre-loaded self-deploying anchors such as FasT-Fix) or inside-out suture technique. Typically 1–3 sutures are placed along the tear length. For Types 3–5, additional sutures address the intra-meniscal component.

Biological augmentation with fibrin clot, PRP, or trephination may enhance healing, though evidence remains evolving.

Concurrent ACL reconstruction is essential — restoration of ACL function protects the meniscal repair by eliminating the abnormal anterior translation and rotational forces that caused the lesion.

Postoperative rehabilitation follows ACL reconstruction protocols with meniscal repair modifications — restricted flexion to 90° for 4–6 weeks, avoidance of deep squatting for 3–4 months, and graduated return to full activity by 6–9 months.

Outcomes are excellent — healing rates exceed 85–95%, reflecting the favourable biology of the peripheral vascularised zone. Combined RAMP repair with ACL reconstruction produces significantly better AP stability and lower re-rupture rates compared to ACL reconstruction with the RAMP lesion left unaddressed.
