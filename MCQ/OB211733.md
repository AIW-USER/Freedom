## Question

Figures A and B are the radiographs of a 62-year-old male who is presenting with worsening right shoulder pain for the past 4 months. He underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair of the right shoulder 14 years ago and was doing well until the past 4 months. His shoulder pain has been being managed by his primary care physician until this point. He has failed two courses of formal physical therapy and received cortisone injections without improvement. Figure C is an MRI obtained prior to consultation. On examination he has weakness with belly-press test and has passive external rotation to 90 degrees with the elbow at the side. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management for this patient?

## Options

1. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair
2. Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty
3. Continued physical therapy
4. Repeat cortisone injection
5. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

## Correct option

**5. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient has end-stage glenohumeral arthritis with evidence of a complete subscapualris tear. As he has failed conservative management; reverse shoulder arthorplasty is indicated in this patient.

Osteoarthritis of the shoulder is common in patients over the age of 60 years. In this process, there is irreversible progressive loss of articular cartilage with hypertrophic reaction of the subchondral bone. The humeral head is often
flattened with osteophyte and subchondral cyst formation, and posterior humeral subluxation with glenoid posterior wear is often evident. In these patients, there is a 5-10% incidence of rotator cuff tears. With advanced glenohumeral OA and evidence of a complete subscapularis tear, a RSA is indicated in this patient as failure of the subscapularis repair following an anatomic TSA would likely lead to failure.

*Randelli et al. performed a systematic literature review to report clinical outcomes of RSA used as a revision surgery following failure of the primary implant due to rotator cuff insufficiency.* **They report** improvements in function and reduction of pain with a high complication rate, up to 62%, and a reoperation rate of 28%. The concluded that RSA use in revision surgery for patients with rotator cuff deficiency is a valid option but should be limited to elderly patients with poor function and severe pain.

*Singh et al. examined the rate of revision in patients undergoing TSA.* In a total of 2588 TSAs, **they report** a five, 10 and 20 year survival rates of 94%, 90%, and 81%, respectively. **They also noted** that men and those with rotator cuff disease had a hazard ratio for revision surgery. **They concluded** that male gender and rotator cuff disease are independent risk factors for revision after TSA.

Figure A is the AP radiograph of the right shoulder which depicts glenohumeral osteoarthritis. Figure B is the lateral radiograph of the right shoulder demonstrating glenohumeral osteoarthritis with posterior glenoid wear. Figure C is the axial MRI image demonstrating a complete subscapularis tear with dislocation of the bicep tendon.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has advanced OA and shoulder arthroplasty is more approriate.
Answer 2: Anatomic TSA is not recommended in this patient as the integrity of the subscapularis tendon is compromised.
Answer 3: This patient has completed several courses of physical therapy without relief and continued therapy is not indicated.
Answer 4: This patient has exhausted conservative management and previous cortisone injections have not provided relief.
