## Question

A 4-year-old boy arrives to the trauma bay as a victim of a drive-by shooting with an isolated injury as shown in Figure A. After initial resuscitation and antibiotic administration, CT-angiography is obtained showing a patent, uninjured superficial femoral artery. The patient is indicated to undergo irrigation and debridement of the open fracture site as well as fracture stabilization. Which of the following would be considered the most appropriate method of treatment given the patient's age and injury shown in Figure A?

![[q218706-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Spica casting
2. Titanium flexible nailing
3. Submuscular plating
4. Piriformis entry rigid femoral nailing
5. Trochanteric entry rigid femoral nailing

## Correct option

**3. Submuscular plating**

## Answer explanation

Given this patient's age and highly comminuted fracture pattern, they are indicated to undergo submuscular bridge plating (Answer C) for fracture stabilization.

Diaphyseal femoral shaft fractures are commonly encountered within the pediatric orthopaedic community. These injuries are often the result of non-accidental trauma (0-2 years), falls (3-6 years), or high-energy motor vehicle accidents (7+ years). As compared to adult femoral shaft fractures, pediatric cases seldom have a concomitant femoral neck fracture or experience hemodynamic instability. Younger patients (<5 years) have the unique advantage of a robust periosteum which provides inherent fracture stability and fast healing. Pavlik harnesses are employed in non-crawling infants (<6 months), whereas crawling and ambulatory toddlers (6 months to 5 years) are most often treated with spica cast immobilization. However, unique circumstances may require alternative treatment methods. In this case, the patient has a comminuted, length-unstable, open fracture at significant risk for mal-alignment and rotational deformity. A submuscular plate can be utilized as a bridge plate construct with intraoperative comparative fluoroscopic imaging to the uninjured, contralateral side to ensure appropriate length, rotation, and alignment (Illustration A). In this case, the hardware was removed 8 months later, demonstrating the robust healing potential of this age group (Illustration B).

Greenhill and Herman provided a comprehensive review article regarding the management of pediatric femoral shaft fractures and controversies. They discuss that when choosing a fixation strategy for older children (5-11 years), fractures can be classified as length stable or length unstable. Length-stable fractures are considered transverse or short oblique (fracture length is less than 2 diaphyseal cortical diameter lengths) and should be treated with flexible nailing. Conversely spiral, long oblique, and comminuted fractures are considered unstable and should be treated with submuscular plating. The authors also highlight that rigid trochanteric entry nailing is appropriate as early as 9 years when the trochanteric physis no longer contributes to proximal femur appositional growth. The authors conclude by providing a common treatment algorithm (Illustration C).

*Weltsch and colleagues performed a survey of experts followed by a retrospective review of pediatric femoral shaft fracture cases at a Level 1 trauma center.* **The authors concluded** that the key to proper treatment involves using the least invasive method to get the best result, resulting in the highest cost-care value. For example, their findings reflected that while a 3-year-old could be treated with elastic nailing, spica casting has similarly great results at a much lower cost.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Spica casting would not reliably keep this highly comminuted fracture in a stable position to heal with appropriate length, rotation, and alignment.
Answer 2: Flexible nails are reserved for stable fracture patterns with direct cortical apposition. Utilizing flexible nails in this patient could lead to unacceptable shortening given the length and degree of fracture comminution.
Answer 4: Piriformis entry rigid nailing has generally been abandoned in skeletally immature patients as there is a significant risk of injuring the medial femoral circumflex artery with resultant avascular necrosis of the femoral head using this starting point.
Answer 5: Trochanteric (i.e. lateral) entry rigid nailing is ideal in older (9+ years) and heavier (49kg+) patients. Disrupting the trochanteric physis before the age of 9 is associated with developing a clinically significant coxa valga deformity.
