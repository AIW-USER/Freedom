## Question

A 17-year-old field hockey player comes to the office complaining of six months of plantar foot pain. She states that the pain is worse after long practices and changes in her shoe wear on and off the field have not made any difference in her pain. Radiographs of her symptomatic foot are shown in Figures A and B. She is initially placed into a short-leg walking cast for six weeks without improvement in her symptoms and elects to undergo an operative procedure for her associated pathology. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Freiberg's Disease
2. Morton's neuroma
3. Mucous cyst
4. Post-traumatic arthritis
5. Synovial sarcoma

## Correct option

**1. Freiberg's Disease**

## Answer explanation

Freiberg's disease is characterized by infarction and fracture of the second metatarsal head, with the pathognomonic imaging findings shown in Figures A and B.

A relatively rare condition, Freiberg's disease is thought to be related to a disruption in the blood supply to (most typically) the second metatarsal head due either to repetitive microtrauma causing stress overloading or to idiopathic osteonecrosis. This ultimately leads to the collapse of the second metatarsal head, as shown in the radiographs in Figures A and B. Smillie's classification (Illustration A) can be used to classify the imaging findings into stages 1-5, ranging from subchondral fractures visible only on MRI (stage 1) to severe arthritic changes and joint space obliteration (stage 5). Early stages of disease that are symptomatic are best treated with non-invasive modalities at first, including NSAIDs, walking cast immobilization, and/or activity/shoe-wear modifications that include stiff-soled shoes with metatarsal pads. If these interventions fail and symptoms persist, metatarsophalangeal joint debridement and loose body removal can be performed. More severe disease may necessitate the use of a dorsal closing wedge osteotomy or DuVries arthroplasty (partial metatarsal head resection).

*Carmont et al. published a current concepts review of Freiberg's disease.* **The authors note** that avascular necrosis of the second metatarsal head was first described in 1914 and has been recognized as the fourth most common osteochondrosis of the body. **They conclude** that nonoperative management in the early stages of the disease consists of the control of symptoms and the prevention of deformity, but that fair evidence supports the use of open surgical debridement as well as dorsiflexion osteotomy when non-invasive modalities fail.

*Helal et al. published a suggested pattern of management for Freiberg's disease.* **The authors note** that pain on weight-bearing is the classical presenting feature, with early-stage treatment being relief from weight-bearing through the use of crutches or a suitable weight-relieving orthosis. **They conclude** that a percentage of good results following early surgery as advocated by Smillie and modified by K wire metatarsal head stabilization may produce results superior to conservative management when symptoms persist.

Figures A and B represent AP and oblique radiographs of the foot showing an infarcted second metatarsal head with sclerosis and fragmentation pathognomonic for Freiberg's disease. Illustration A depicts the Smillie classification.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: A Morton's neuroma would not be evident on radiographs and involves pain at the (typically third) webspace, not over the metatarsal head.
Answer 3: Mucous cysts most commonly occur in the distal interphalangeal joints of the hands in the setting of end-stage arthritis.
Answer 4: Though post-traumatic arthritic changes could look similar radiographically, rapidly progressive, isolated second metatarsal osteoarthritis without a known traumatic inciting event would be unlikely at the given location in a 17-year-old patient.
Answer 5: Synovial sarcoma, though it is most common in the young adult age group and represents the most common sarcoma of the foot, actually occurs most commonly around the knee and radiographically can show soft tissue calcifications that can resemble heterotopic ossification.
