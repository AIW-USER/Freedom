## Question

A 61-year-old male presents to your office with complaints of right knee pain, which has been progressive for several months. The pain is primarily present over the medial joint line and is worse with weight-bearing activities. Radiographs demonstrate moderate to severe osteoarthritis of his knee with approximately 5º of varus deformity. He has not previously had any treatment but states his quality of life is now being affected. What treatment strategy would you most strongly recommend to manage his pain and dysfunction based on current guidelines?

## Options

1. Oral tramadol
2. Hyaluronic acid injections
3. Corticosteroid injections
4. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDS)
5. Oral supplements (i.e glucosamine & chondroitin)

## Correct option

**4. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDS)**

## Answer explanation

In the most updated version of the AAOS clinical practice guidelines (CPG) for knee osteoarthritis (OA), released in 2021, topical and oral NSAIDS both received a strong recommendation for use.

The AAOS CPG are developed by a group of experts on knee OA and its management. They help to advise practitioners on the best practices in management for patients with knee OA. They were most recently updated in 2021 and strong recommendations for use included: supervised and self-managed exercise programs, patient education, oral NSAIDS, topical NSAIDS, and oral acetaminophen. Strong recommendations against use included: oral narcotics and lateral wedge insoles. Other common treatments include intra-articular corticosteroid injections for which a moderate strength recommendation was established and intra-articular hyaluronic acid injections, which were recommended against with moderate strength.

*Brophy et al. published a systematic review outlining the recent 2021 AAOS clinic practice guidelines for the management of knee OA.* *They review all 29 recommendations, with 8 of them being strong recommendations.* Finally, **they note** there is more investigation needed into the use of intra-articular corticosteroid injections, hyaluronic acid injections and platelet-rich plasma injections.

*Schnitzer et al. reviewed the efficacy and safety of NSAIDS compared to a placebo when used over 53 weeks for knee OA.* **They noted** that changes in pain scores were improved compared to placebo at 13 and 26 weeks. **They also concluded** that oral NSAIDS were safe, with no considerable changes in blood pressure or other complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Current AAOS guidelines recommend strongly against tramadol and other narcotic pain medication given the fact they increase adverse events without being effective at improving pain or function for patients with knee OA.
Answer 2: Current AAOS guidelines have a moderate strength recommendation against the use of hyaluronic acid injections for knee OA, as it is felt that patients do not demonstrate a clinically relevant difference when compared to controls.
Answer 3: Current AAOS guidelines have a moderate strength recommendation in favor of corticosteroid injections, with the thought that they may offer a short-term improvement in pain. However, the recommendation is lower in strength than that of oral NSAIDs or acetaminophen.
Answer 5: Current AAOS guidelines have provided a limited recommendation that oral supplements including turmeric, ginger extract, glucosamine, chondroitin, and vitamin D may be helpful in reducing pain and function in knee OA patients, however the evidence remains inconsistent and limited.
