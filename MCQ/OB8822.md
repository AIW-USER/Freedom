## Question

His pain is mildly relieved with NSAIDs and activity modification. He denies any prior history of shoulder pain. ## Options

1. EMG
2. Biopsy
3. CT scan of the chest
4. AP, lateral, and oblique radiographs of the cervical spine
5. Observation

## Correct option

**4. AP, lateral, and oblique radiographs of the cervical spine**

## Answer explanation

The patient has cervical radiculopathy with an incidental, benign-appearing enchondroma of the left proximal humerus. The patient's likely C5 cervical radiculopathy warrants further investigation, while the patient's proximal humerus enchondroma can be observed over time.

Enchondromas are benign growths of hyaline cartilage that are typically asymptomatic and found incidentally. Radiographically, enchondromas are typically metaphyseal and demonstrate a cartilaginous matrix with a classic rings and arcs pattern of calcification. It is important to distinguish a benign enchondroma from a malignant chondrosarcoma. Patients with a chondrosarcoma will typically have insidious onset of pain at rest and a more aggressive appearance on radiographs, which will demonstrate endosteal scalloping, possible cortical breakthrough, bone expansion, or radiolucent areas. While not mandatory, CT can be useful to evaluate for endosteal scalloping, and MRI can help assess for soft tissue involvement.

*Douis et al. describe the classic MRI appearance of enchondroma which is a lobular lesion with intermediate T1-weighted signal and high T2-weighted signal intensity.* Thin, enhancing fibrovascular septae can be seen on T2. Matrix mineralization can appear as punctate areas of signal void.

*Levy et al. performed a retrospective review of 57 patients with solitary enchondromas of the proximal humerus.* *Of the patients that presented with shoulder pain, 82% had findings that correlated with their initial complaint, indicating the incidental nature of enchondromas.* Rotator cuff tendinopathy was most common.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the left shoulder demonstrating a benign-appearing enchondroma in the proximal humerus with chondroid matrix and no cortical scalloping or expansion. Figures B and C are T1-weighted and T2-weighted(respectively) MRIs of the proximal humerus which show a lobular, cartilaginous lesion with intermediate signal on T1 and high intensity on T2 and no evidence of cortical erosion or soft tissue extension. Illustration A demonstrates a chondrosarcoma of the proximal humerus with calcifications, and malignant features such as radiolucency, endosteal scalloping, and cortical expansion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: EMG and/or nerve conduction study may have a high false negative rate and is not the first-line test in evaluating cervical radiculopathy.
Answer 2: Biopsy is not warranted in this benign-appearing lesion.
Answer 3: This is a benign-appearing lesion that does not require a metastatic work-up.
Answer 5: Observation is a reasonable option for the patient's enchondroma. However, the patient is having painful cervical radiculopathy and weakness. Observation alone would be inappropriate.
