Chapter: Trauma MCQs — Morel-Lavallee Lesion

## Question

A 40 years female sustained a road traffic accident and injury. Her X ray showed a trochanteric fracture. Next day of injury she developed fullness and mild pain in the thigh. Clinical picture of her thigh is given below. True about the condition is

## Options

1. Internal fixation should be delayed
2. Fracture fixation should be combined with incision and open debridement
3. Observation until line of demarcation develops and IV antibiotics
4. Immediate fracture fixation and ambulation

## Correct option

**2. Fracture fixation should be combined with incision and open debridement**

## Answer explanation

![[TO17136685-1.jpg]]

This is the typical picture of Morel-Lavallee lesion.

Morel-Lavallee lesion was first described by French physician Maurice Morel-Lavallee in 1853. It is a closed traumatic soft tissue degloving injury characterized by separation of the dermis (skin) and subcutaneous (fat) tissue from the underlying (deep) fascia due to a shearing force. This creates a pocket underneath where considerable bleeding can occur from injured lymphatics and vessels of sub dermal plexus and collect. Owing to absence of resistance, often this subcutaneous hematoma, gets so large that it can seriously threaten the viability of the skin above it. If left untreated, most cases, blood gets replaced by serosanguinous fluid and as lesion matures a pseudocapsule forms sequestering the lesion. In other cases, bacterial infections can occur also leading to pus formation.

Clinically, the lesion is characterised by ecchymosis, swelling, fluctuance and skin hypermobility in the polytrauma patient with underlying fractures. The fractures are usually ones around hip mainly pelvic-acetabular fractures (upto 10% acetabular fractures can have this) and proximal femur fractures, especially in obese individuals in whom there was a shear component to the injury. Proximal lateral thigh, in the peritrochanteric region (most common site) and gluteal region are the common sites of this lesion owing to hypermobile skin in these areas. Skin discoloration at times takes few days to manifest, hence, paraesthesias in the overlying skin are often the earliest indicators in suspected cases. MRI and ultrasonography have been recommended to confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment: Multiple treatment options have been suggested for Morel-Lavallee, including radical incision, which frequently leaves a massive wound, and less invasive methods, such as percutaneous wound drainage. A middle path to follow can be as under:

Small lesions (< than 50 cm3) can be treated by percutaneous drainage, drain placement and compression therapy.

Incision, irrigation and open debridement is the standard treatment for all larger Morel-Lavallee lesions. Single incision technique, with incision centred over the lesion is usually employed in such cases. Closure method is controversial with some advocating primary closure while others preferring a vacuum dressing, drain placement and delayed closure.

In case the associated fracture also needs stabilization, the primary recommendation is to treat the lesion at the same time the fracture is being stabilized (although the lesion is taken as an independent risk factor for infection). Now if this lesion is to be treated at the time of internal fixation, incisions should preferable be placed to avoid the lesion. If that’s not possible then dual incision technique for drainage is recommended where the two incisions are made on proximal most and distal most part of the lesion, leaving areas for fracture stabilization. The proximal incision is closed on primary basis but distal gets a (VAC application and) secondary closure.

Recurrence has been reported in almost 15-20% cases despite open drainage and may need repeated I and D plus use of sclerosants.

Answer: 2
