## Question

A 45-year-old male presents to your clinic with a right elbow injury. He felt a pop in his right elbow as he was catching a bookshelf from falling down in his apartment. He felt immediate pain and swelling that has partially improved over the last 2 weeks. His right elbow MRI shows a partial distal biceps tendon tear. Which labeled region in Figure A signifies the most likely location of the partial tear?

![[q214835-a-distal-biceps.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. A
2. B
3. C
4. D
5. E

## Correct option

**3. C**

## Answer explanation

Partial distal bicep tendon tears most commonly occur at the radial aspect of the radial tuberosity insertion which is signified by the letter C.

Distal biceps tendon tears generally occur due to excessive eccentric tension as the arm is forced from a flexed to an extended position. Partial injuries may also occur with this mechanism or may occur simply due to persistent mechanical attrition due to prono-supination. When the arm is pronated, the sharp margin of the radial aspect of the tuberosity impinges on the tendon and then subsequently releases when the tendon is pulled free during supination. Treatment of partial distal biceps tendon tears is controversial and depends on the patient's age and activity level. Most patients are given a trial of nonsurgical management to include rest and physical therapy. Surgical treatment is indicated in the setting of persistent weakness and pain despite conservative measures.

*Vardakas et al. reported on 7 cases of partial rupture of the distal biceps tendon.* **The authors noted** pain (not weakness) was the main complaint in all 7 patients. The authors initially tried immobilization and physiotherapy in all patients, but no patient improved from conservative treatment. All patients eventually underwent surgical debridement and reattachment and at ~31 months, all patients reported a significant decrease in their pain, and no complications were noted.

Ramsey performs a review of distal biceps tendon anatomy and associated injuries. He notes that the rupture typically occurs at the tendon insertion into the radial tuberosity in an area of preexisting tendon degeneration which is generally the radial aspect of the insertion. He cites that the ability to palpate the tendon in the antecubital fossa may indicate partial tearing of the biceps tendon. He also notes that while MRI is generally not required to diagnose a complete rupture, it may be useful in the case of a partial rupture.

Figure A is a schematic of the radial tuberosity on the proximal radius with the blue footprint indicating the distal biceps insertion.
