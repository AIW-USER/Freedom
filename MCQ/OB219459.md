## Question

A 58-year-old female presents with left foot pain and bunion deformity. After discussing the various available procedures, the patient undergoes a modified Lapidus procedure involving a metatarsocuneiform arthrodesis with a distal modified McBride procedure (Figure A). One year later, the patient presents with forefoot pain and abnormal callous formation along their foot in the area denoted (Figure B). What is the likely cause of this patient’s current symptoms?

## Options

1. Excessive lateral capsular release with overtightening of the medial capsule
2. Functional load transfer from altered forefoot biomechanics
3. Cutaneous nerve injury during soft tissue releases
4. Iatrogenic transecting of the flexor hallucis longus tendon
5. Avascular necrosis secondary to iatrogenic vascular injury

## Correct option

**2. Functional load transfer from altered forefoot biomechanics**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent a Lapidus fusion with a modified Mcbride procedure. The residual shortened first metatarsal is known to cause functional load transfer to the lesser metatarsal heads with resultant forefoot pain during ambulation (i.e. transfer metatarsalgia).

Hallux valgus is a triplanar deformity of the first ray involving (1) valgus positioning in the coronal plane, (2) dorsiflexion in the sagittal plane, and (3) pronation in the axial plane. Dorsiflexion and pronation of the first ray result in effectively off-loading the normal plantar weight-bearing role of the first metatarsal head. As a result, forefoot weight-bearing loads are transferred to lesser metatarsal heads during ambulation. This causes pain along the plantar aspect of the lesser metatarsal heads and may be appreciated on physical exam by pressure-induced callouses (Figure B). A first metatarsocuneiform arthrodesis (Lapidus procedure), when indicated, can correct the underlying valgus and dorsiflexion deformities. However, when it is performed in isolation, this procedure often fails to correct the underlying rotational deformity (i.e. pronation). For this reason, it is now performed alongside a modified McBride procedure (i.e. “Modified Lapidus”), which better corrects the pronation deformity at the time of fusion. Functional shortening of the first metatarsal and/or residual pronation both contribute to transfer metatarsalgia.

*Espinosa and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of the diagnosis and treatment of metatarsalgia.* *They define metatarsalgia as pain in the area across the plantar forefoot beneath the second, third, and fourth metatarsal heads and is most commonly due to the presence of a relatively long second metatarsal.* **The authors conclude** by discussing various treatment options ranging from simple shoe adjustment with adding a metatarsal pad to multiple corrective osteotomies.

*Shi and colleagues comprehensively reviewed the operative approaches to adult hallux valgus deformities.* *The authors review that mild cases are primarily treated with distal chevron osteotomies whereas moderate-to-severe deformities require proximal-based osteotomies or fusion.* **The authors conclude** that no single operative can be universally applied to all patients with hallux valgus and the patient’s goals and expectations should help guide treatment.

Figure A demonstrates pre- and post-operative imaging of this patient’s Lapidus procedure with relative shortening of the first metatarsal. Figure B demonstrates the classic area of plantar-based forefoot pain seen in patients experiencing transfer metatarsalgia.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Hallux varus deformity may be caused during distal soft tissue correction (i.e. modified McBride procedure) by (1) excessive lateral capsular release with overtightening of the medial capsule, (2) lateral sesmoidectomy, or (3) over-resection of the medial first metatarsal head. However, this patient is describing the classic scenario of transfer metatarsalgia.
Answer 3: The terminal branch of the superficial peroneal nerve (i.e. dorsomedial cutaneous nerve) runs directly over the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint and may be injured during soft tissue reconstructive procedures. In such cases, a painful neuroma may form with symptoms involving the distal hallux.
Answer 4: Injury to the flexor hallucis longus tendon would result in a cock up toe deformity, which is not seen in this patient. This complication was commonly seen after Keller resection arthroplasty procedures, resulting in a paradigm shift towards alternative corrective surgeries.
Answer 5: The blood supply to the metatarsal head is laterally based. Therefore, over-aggressive lateral release during modified McBride procedures may result in iatrogenic avascular necrosis of the metatarsal head. However, this patient is describing the classic scenario of transfer metatarsalgia.
