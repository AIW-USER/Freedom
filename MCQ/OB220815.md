## Question

A 20-year-old college lacrosse player is checked from the side, and his foot is forced into a planted, slightly plantar-flexed position. He feels immediate midfoot pain but is able to finish practice. On examination, his foot is swollen without plantar ecchymosis. Midfoot squeeze and pronation-abduction stress produce pain, but initial weight-bearing radiographs are normal. MRI demonstrates moderate-grade edema at the Lisfranc interval with disruption of the dorsal capsular fibers, but no plantar ligament disruption. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Dorsal spanning bridge plate fixation due to ligamentous injury pattern
2. Strict non-weight bearing with immediate cast immobilization for 6 weeks
3. Stress fluoroscopy exam under anesthesia to rule out occult instability
4. Protected weight-bearing in a CAM boot
5. Primary arthrodesis to prevent chronic instability

## Correct option

**4. Protected weight-bearing in a CAM boot**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a stable tarsometatarsal joint injury due to an intact plantar ligament. Therefore, non-operative treatment with protected weight-bearing and immobilization is a suitable option (answer 4).

Lisfranc injuries involve disruption of the tarsometatarsal joint complex, ranging from subtle ligamentous sprains to severe fracture-dislocations. They are frequently misdiagnosed, leading to significant morbidity and long-term loss of function. Management is determined by injury stability, as stable injuries are treated non-operatively with immobilization and protected weight-bearing. In contrast, unstable or displaced injuries require surgical intervention, most commonly open reduction and internal fixation versus primary arthrodesis.

*Weatherford et al. performed a narrative review summarizing the current understanding and management of tarsometatarsal (TMT) joint injuries, including their anatomy, mechanisms, diagnosis, and treatment options.* The main findings highlight that nonsurgical management is appropriate for select patients with stable TMT joints, while surgical intervention, most commonly open reduction and internal fixation, is standard for unstable or displaced injuries, with primary arthrodesis emerging as an alternative for specific cases. **They conclude** that successful outcomes depend on accurate diagnosis, anatomic reduction, and stable fixation to prevent long-term complications such as posttraumatic arthritis and arch collapse.

*O'Neill et al synthesized current concepts in the diagnosis and management of midfoot injuries, including Lisfranc and Chopart joint injuries.* **The authors highlight** that midfoot injuries are often underdiagnosed due to subtle clinical and radiographic findings, and emphasize the importance of weight-bearing radiographs and advanced imaging for accurate assessment. Treatment recommendations are based on injury stability, with nonoperative management reserved for stable injuries and surgical intervention (typically open reduction and internal fixation or primary arthrodesis) indicated for unstable or displaced injuries. **They similarly conclude** that early recognition and anatomic restoration of midfoot alignment are critical to optimizing functional outcomes and minimizing long-term complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Operative fixation is indicated only for unstable Lisfranc injuries. The patient's intact plantar ligament indicates a stable injury pattern and does not require surgical intervention.
Answer 2: Full immobilization is unnecessarily restrictive when MRI confirms plantar ligament integrity and stable alignment, and would delay return to function without improving stability.
Answer 3: Stress testing is reserved for equivocal cases; definitive MRI evidence of ligament continuity eliminates the need for invasive instability assessment.
Answer 5: Arthrodesis is reserved for truly unstable, purely ligamentous Lisfranc injuries or chronic post-traumatic midfoot arthrosis.
