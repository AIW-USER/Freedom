## Question

You are an orthopedic resident reviewing shoulder rehabilitation protocols as a part of your upper extremity rotation. Which patient would be most appropriately indicated for prolonged physical therapy (> 3-6 months) with rotator cuff and periscapular strengthening as opposed to operative intervention?

## Options

1. 55-year-old female with a symptomatic 6 mm bursal-sided rotator cuff tear
2. 21-year-old male baseball pitcher with posterosuperior labral fraying, 15 degrees less internal rotation and 10 degrees less total motion than the contralateral side
3. 37-year-old male with full-thickness rotator cuff tear
4. 21-year-old male football player with first time dislocation and anterior labral tear + 15% glenoid bone loss
5. 17-year-old female high school swimmer with Beighton score = 5 and positive sulcus sign on exam

## Correct option

**5. 17-year-old female high school swimmer with Beighton score = 5 and positive sulcus sign on exam**

## Answer explanation

This patient most likely has multidirectional shoulder instability (MDI), and should be managed nonoperatively with physical therapy for an extended period of time before considering any surgery.

Multidirectional shoulder instability is characterized by instability of the shoulder in two or more planes and is thought to be caused by capsular redundancy of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL). It is most common in younger overhead athletes, as muscular weakness combined with generalized ligamentous laxity in this population typically causes pain in situations of overuse. On examination, a sulcus sign is often appreciated (Illustration A) and anterior/posterior load and shift testing often demonstrate both anterior and posterior laxity. First line management for MDI is a prolonged course of aggressive physical therapy (>3-6 months) in order to strengthen the periscapular muscles and rotator cuff to obtain better control and strength through the full shoulder range. If therapy fails to improve symptoms, capsular shift (open or arthroscopic) can be performed but this is done uncommonly.

*Gaskill et al. reviewed the management of MDI in a JAAOS review article,* **noting** that MDI occurs when both static and dynamic shoulder stabilizers are compromised. They discuss physical examination findings and the highlight the importance of distinguishing laxity from instability. **They note** that initial management is physical therapy and capsular plication +/- rotator interval closure can be performed if other rehabilitation methods fail.

*Jacobson et al. compared the results of open and arthroscopic capsular plication for MDI patients who failed the aforementioned therapy measures.* **They noted** a consistent loss of >40° of external rotation after both open and arthroscopic procedures, but there was no clear evidence to suggest one was superior. **They ultimately concluded** that arthroscopic plication had comparable results and a slight trend towards increased return to sport.

Illustration A demonstrates evidence of a sulcus sign, representative of MDI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A 6-mm (50%) bursal sided rotator cuff tear would be indicated for repair, despite its partial-thickness nature, especially since symptomatic.
Answer 2: A young patient with active internal impingement and likely some would be treated with posteroinferior capsular stretching primarily.
Answer 3: A 37-year-old with a full-thickness rotator cuff tear should be repaired before the tear retracts or cuff muscle atrophy occurs.
Answer 4: Despite a first time dislocation, the combination of a Bankart lesion and significant glenoid bone loss is an indication for surgical stabilization rather than prolonged therapy.
