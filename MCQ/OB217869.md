## Question

A 42-year-old right-hand dominant male presents to your clinic with complaints of ulnar sided wrist pain that is worsened when he plays tennis. He notes a popping sensation that occurs during his backhand. On examination, there is no sign of ecchymosis or swelling. Tenderness to palpation is primarily present over the dorsal and ulnar aspect of the wrist. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Intersection syndrome
2. Dorsal radioulnar ligament tear
3. ECU tendon subluxation
4. Ulnar styloid impaction syndrome
5. Lunotriquetral ligament tear

## Correct option

**3. ECU tendon subluxation**

## Answer explanation

Extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) tendon subluxation can be identified by a snapping or popping sensation that occurs when the wrist is supinated.

ECU tendon subluxation, sometimes termed "snapping ECU," is caused by attenuation and rupture of the ECU subsheath which can occur in combination with ECU tendonitis. When ECU subsheath rupture occurs, the ECU subluxes during supination and typically reduces with pronation. This subluxation event can elicit a prominent snapping or popping sensation that is painful for patients. Ultrasound or MRI can be used to assess these injuries. First line treatment is immobilization in pronation, but ECU subsheath repair or reconstruction may be required in some cases.

*Ghatan et al. investigated the importance of the ECU subsheath in providing stability to the ECU tendon.* **They noted** that even in cases of an intact ECU subsheath, there was some subluxation of the ECU tendon, although this increased dramatically in cases where the ECU subsheath was sectioned, particularly distally. **They also found** that the ECU was most stable in pronation with the wrist in neutral or extended and recommended that supination and wrist flexion be avoided in these patients.

*Inoue et al. discuss surgical intervention strategies in 12 patients with recurrent ECU tendon dislocation.* **They noted** 3 different injury patterns, including Type A: ulnar subsheath rupture, Type B: radial subsheath rupture, and Type C: ulnar periosteal detachment. They go on to discuss treatment strategies for each type, **noting** that all patients studied had a good result.

*Allende et al. performed a general review of ECU wrist lesions, with 9 of the patients in the study having been diagnosed with ECU subluxation or dislocation.* **They noted** a high rate of concomitant ulnar sided injuries in these cases. Additionally, they attempted to classify these ECU and subsheath lesions in order to allow for a treatment algorithm to be derived.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Intersection syndrome is caused by inflammation between the 1st and 2nd extensor compartments with repetitive wrist extension and while it can cause crepitus, it would not be a source of ulnar-sided wrist pain.
Answer 2: The dorsal radioulnar ligament is part of the TFCC. If torn, these injuries may be a source of ulnar sided wrist pain but do not typically elicit a palpable snap or pop with pronosupination.
Answer 4: Ulnar styloid impaction occurs when the ulnar styloid impacts the triquetrum in patients with ulnar positive variance and while causing pain with pronation, would not typically cause snapping or popping.
Answer 5: Lunotriquetral (LT) ligament tears can be a source of ulnar sided wrist pain, worse with pronation but would not elicit a snap or pop typically.
