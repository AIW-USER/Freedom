## Question

A 75-year-old male presents to your office after having a cervical lymph node biopsy performed by an ENT with complaints of posterior shoulder pain and fatigue. A clinical picture is seen in Figure A. He has not had any treatment for this complaint yet. Which of the following nerve has been damaged and is most likely causing the abnormality seen clinically?

![[q217854-a-screen-shot-2022-10-09-at-8-43-08-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Thoracodorsal nerve
2. Suprascapular nerve
3. Long thoracic nerve
4. Spinal accessory nerve
5. Axillary nerve

## Correct option

**4. Spinal accessory nerve**

## Answer explanation

This patient has lateral winging of his right scapula, related to spinal accessory nerve injury after his recent cervical lymph node biopsy.

Scapular winging is related to dysfunction of the scapular stabilizing muscles which usually occurs secondary to denervation. Medial and lateral scapular winging are both possible and defined by the direction of the superomedial corner of the scapula. Lateral winging, specifically, is caused by injury to the spinal accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI). Secondarily, this nerve injury leads to dysfunction of the trapezius and inability of the trapezius to provide a superior and medial force on the scapula, as seen in Figure A. It can occur after trauma, direct laceration or after cervical lymph node biopsy or radial neck dissection. Treatment is initially nonoperative with PT and activity modification but if improvement is not noted, exploration of the spinal accessory nerve +/- repair may be necessary.

*Galano et al. reviewed surgical treatment of the winged scapula,* **noting** that long thoracic and spinal accessory nerve injuries were most commonly linked to these injuries. For long thoracic nerve injury, **they recommended** pectoralis major transfer without the use of graft and for spinal accessory nerve injury they recommended a modified Eden-Lange procedure. **They noted** that both groups showed improvements in range of motion and pain scores.

*Didesch et al. looked at the anatomy, etiology and management of scapular winging.* **They note** that weakness and loss of scapular mechanics are correlated with difficulty with range of motion and activities of daily living. **They note** that acute traumatic tears of the serratus anterior, trapezius, and rhomboids are under-recognized causes of scapular winging in addition to nerve denervation.

Figure A is a clinical picture which demonstrates lateral scapular winging of the right scapula as demonstrated by lateral displacement of the superomedial scapular border.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Thoracodorsal nerve denervation would affect the latissimus dorsi which would not lead to scapular winging.
Answer 2: Suprascapular nerve denervation affects the supraspinatus and infraspinatus and would not result in scapular winging.
Answer 3: Long thoracic nerve denervation results in medial scapular winging.
Answer 5: Axillary nerve denervation would affect the deltoid and teres minor muscles, which would not lead to scapular winging.
