## Question

A 71-year-old female presents to your clinic complaining of right foot and ankle pain. The pain is located primarily medially. Radiographs demonstrate a Meary's angle of 30º with osteophytes present at the ankle joint. There is no notable forefoot abduction. On examination, she is unable to perform a single-leg heel rise. Valgus deformity is noted at the subtalar and tibiotalar joints, neither of which is passively correctable. She tells you that the pain significantly limits her activities of daily living. What is the most appropriate definitive treatment strategy at this time?

## Options

1. Ankle foot orthosis (AFO)
2. Medial slide calcaneal osteotomy + FDL transfer
3. Medial slide calcaneal osteotomy + FDL transfer + deltoid repair
4. Tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis
5. Triple arthrodesis

## Correct option

**4. Tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis**

## Answer explanation

Tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis utilizing a hindfoot nail is indicated in the setting of stage IV posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) with concomitant rigid ankle valgus deformity and ankle arthritis.

PTTI represents a continuum of disease that begins with failure of the posterior tibial tendon, leading to collapse of the foot's medial arch and progresses to rigid bony deformity at the subtalar joint. This process can eventually lead to rigid ankle valgus deformity as well in cases where the deltoid ligament becomes incompetent. In cases of rigid deformity, tendon transfers are not appropriate and bony management is required. If the ankle joint is not involved, a medial double or triple arthrodesis may be appropriate. However, when there is associated ankle deformity and pre-existing arthritic change, the ankle joint must also be addressed. One way to address the ankle and subtalar joints at the same time is by performing a tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis using a hindfoot nail. In doing so, it is important to ensure the deformities are corrected at the time of surgery.

Deland provides a review of adult-acquired flatfoot deformity, noting that it was initially described as PTTI, but also encompasses other deformities. Early diagnosis and appropriate management is helpful in treating symptoms and progression. If surgery is necessary, the goal is to improve the alignment of the foot, while maintaining as much flexibility as possible.

*Haddad et al. provides an overview of the management and treatment of acquired flatfoot deformity.* **They note** that much of the controversy revolves around stage II deformities, as surgical options are broader with the foot remaining flexible. They go on to describe, at length, the different surgical options and tips/tricks associated with each technique.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Nonoperative management would not be appropriate for this patient as she has a significant deformity which is functionally limiting.
Answer 2: Medial slide calcaneal osteotomy with FDL transfer would be appropriate for a stage II PTTI where there is flexibility to the deformity with arch collapse.
Answer 3: Medial slide calcaneal osteotomy, FDL transfer and deltoid ligament repair would not appropriately address this patient's pathology, as her deformity is rigid.
Answer 5: Triple arthrodesis would not address this patient's ankle deformity or pre-existing arthritic changes.
