## Question

A 23-year-old male presents with an unstable pelvic ring injury requiring both anterior and posterior fixation. In order to stabilize the posterior pelvic ring, the surgeon plans on placing a transversely oriented percutaneous transsacral screw within the S1 body. Which of the following areas serves as the most ideal starting point for this percutaneous screw fixation?

![[q219198-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. A
2. B
3. C
4. D
5. E

## Correct option

**2. B**

## Answer explanation

Posterior pelvic ring injuries and/or sacral fractures often require surgical stabilization, which can be achieved utilizing percutaneous sacroiliac or trans-sacral screw fixation. The starting point for trans-sacral screws should lie posterior to the iliac cortical density (ICD) on true lateral imaging and sufficiently anterior to the neural tunnels where the sacral roots travel from posterior to anterior relative to the sacrum (Label B).

The iliac cortical density (ICD) is the dense area of the anterior ilium located directly lateral to the SI joint and the sacral alar anterior cortical anatomy. It is radiographically denser than the anterior sacral alar cortical bone. Therefore, the ICD is most often utilized as an easy-to-identify radiographic landmark that is coplanar with anterior sacral alar cortical bone (in non-dysmorphic patients) visible on true lateral sacral fluoroscopic images (Illustration A). At the cranial sacrum, the sacral ala and promontory extend more anterior than the sacrum’s central body of bone. This leads to convex areas on both sides of the sacrum’s central body, where the L5 nerve roots are found to drape over the anterior sacrum. This area of bone (Illustration B; asterisk symbol) is generally avoided as a starting point for percutaneous screw fixation due to the risk of L5 nerve injury (i.e. “in-out-in” screw placement). The starting point for percutaneous trans-sacral screws should be posterior to the ICD to avoid L5 nerve injury. At the same time, the starting point should be sufficiently anterior to the neural tunnels where the S1 nerve root travels near the S1/S2 transitional zone (Illustration B; red dotted box), leaving a relatively narrow “safe zone” (Illustration B; blue lined box). Sacroiliac (SI) screws require a slightly more posterior start point to accommodate the inferocaudal to superocephalad positional orientation.

Surgeons should also be aware of aberrant sacral dysmorphism (Illustrations C&D) which often results in a tighter S1 osseous tunnel or may even entirely preclude the safe use of the S1 trans-sacral screws. In these patients, the safe zone in the second sacral segment can typically still accept a trans-sacral screw without major changes to the normal operative technique (Illustration E). Preoperative CT scans can allow for measuring the osseous corridors as part of the operative plan.

Miller and Routt provided a comprehensive review article regarding variations in sacral morphology and implications for posterior pelvic screw fixation. They discuss sacral dysmorphism is present in up to half of the population and, when present, imparts significant adjustments on operative techniques for percutaneous posterior pelvis fixation (primarily tighter osseous tunnels and inability to safely place S1 trans-sacral screws). The authors further provide brief technique guides for both obliquely oriented SI and transversely oriented trans-sacral screws in normal and dysmorphic pelvis patients. They conclude that sacrum anatomic variations are important to understand to avoid iatrogenic nerve root injuries, particularly with iliosacral screw placement.

*Iorio and colleagues provided a technique guide regarding percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation of the posterior pelvic ring.* **The authors highlight** the importance of proper fluoroscopic views to include: (1) an ideal inlet with superimposing the S1/S2 sacral bodies (2) an ideal outlet with pubic symphysis superimposed on the S2 vertebral body and (3) a true lateral sacral view superimposing the greater sciatic notches. **They further highlight** the subtle adjustments required in dysmorphic pelvises. **The authors conclude** that percutaneous posterior pelvic fixation is safe and effective with appropriate surgical techniques.

*Mendel and colleagues performed a CT-based virtual analysis of the iliosacral anatomy in the human pelvis to characterize bone corridors for the safe placement of SI screws within the first sacral segment.* **The authors found** transversely oriented S1 trans-sacral screws had a consistent, predictable trajectory whereas SI screw trajectory was highly variable. **The authors conclude** that their 3-D analysis may serve to aid in the visual thinking of surgeons and can improve pre-operative planning.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The labeled area (“A”) denotes the safe starting point for an S2 trans-sacral screw, not an S1 sacroiliac screw.
Answer 3: The labeled area (“C”) would place the S1 nerve root at risk for injury. The neural tunnels where the sacral nerve roots travel from their posterior position within the spinal cord into the anterior pelvis generally lie at the transitional zones between S1/S2, S2/S3, and so forth (Illustration B, red dotted box).
Answer 4&5: The labeled areas (“D and E”) are anterior to the ICD and lie anterior/outside of the safe zone for screw placement. Either starting point would result in screw placement anterior to the central sacral body and significantly risks resultant L5 nerve root injury.
