## Question

A 32 years male presented to emergency following a fall on outstretched hands and elbow pain and deformity. Orthogonal X ray views of elbow joint are given below. After reduction his elbow examination shows some posterolateral subluxation of the elbow joint with increased ulno-humeral distance. What is the next line of management?

![[TO17137263-1.png]]

## Options

1. LCL reconstruction
2. Splinting for one month
3. Splinting for one week then active motion with the forearm pronated through the full range of motion
4. LCL reconstruction and active motion at two weeks with the forearm supinated through the full range of motion

## Correct option

**3. Splinting for one week then active motion with the forearm pronated through the full range of motion**

## Answer explanation

Simple elbow dislocations are typically the result of a fall on an outstretched hand. O'Driscoll et al. described a valgus, axial, and posterolateral force that results in the typical posterolateral dislocation of the elbow joint. The soft tissue injury is thought to begin on the lateral side of the elbow with disruption of the LCL and then proceeds through the capsule to the medial side with the medial collateral ligament (MCL) being injured last. The MCL may remain intact in some injuries

However some recent studies suggest that the sequence of failure may begin on the medial side of the elbow with acute valgus instability in an extended elbow and may explain why some elbows are very stable after reduction since the LCL complex is sometimes preserved.

Elbow stabilizers: Elbow stability is provided equally by the soft tissue structures surrounding the joint and by the bony articulations of the joint itself. The soft tissue restraints can be divided into static and dynamic stabilizers. The static stabilizers include the joint capsule and the LCLs and MCLs. The normal joint capsule is thin but does contribute to stability with the elbow in full extension and flexion.

The LCL has three components, the radial collateral ligament, annular ligament, and the lateral ulnar collateral ligament
It is the primary varus and posterolateral rotational stabilizer of the elbow

The MCL consists of the anterior and posterior bundles.

The anterior bundle is the key valgus stabilizer of the elbow

The posterior bundle provides a secondary restraint to valgus load and also resists ulnar rotation

The dynamic restraints include the biceps, brachialis, and triceps which provide compressive stability to the elbow due to their joint reactive forces.
The common extensor muscles provide varus stability and the common flexor muscles provide valgus stability.

Treatment protocol:

Patients with simple elbow dislocations routinely have disruption of both the MCL and LCL and the elbow capsule. After reduction, the elbow is taken through an arc of flexion–extension in pronation, neutral, and supination in order to evaluate for residual instability. Since the lateral-sided soft tissue injuries are typically more severe, pronation of the forearm often improves stability. If the elbow redislocates when flexed to less than 30 degrees, operative treatment should be considered. However, in the majority of cases, the elbow will be stable after closed reduction, particularly after muscle tone returns in the arm. Most patients will have varus– valgus instability given the pathoanatomy of elbow dislocation, but this plane of instability alone is not an indication for operative treatment.

The elbow is then immobilized in a light plaster splint with the forearm in pronation, neutral, or supination (depending on the position of maximal stability) and the elbow at 90 degrees of flexion. After 1 week, the splint is removed. Isometric and active ROM exercises are started and light strengthening exercises can be started at 12 weeks. Varus or valgus loading should be avoided until 12 weeks. Collaterals heal in most of the cases with this protocol with full range of motion.

Some patients may have subtle residual mild posterolateral subluxation following a closed reduction. The “drop sign,” or an increase in the static ulnohumeral distance in unstressed postreduction lateral radiographs of dislocated elbows suggests it. In these patients, an active motion protocol should be employed. It consists of avoiding varus stress at all times by exercising with the elbow at the side while sitting or standing. Active motion is performed with the forearm pronated through the full range of motion. Supination is performed with the forearm flexed to 90 degrees or greater.

Operative indications of elbow dislocations:

The main indication for operative management of simple elbow dislocations is an inability to maintain a concentric elbow joint after closed reduction or a recurrent dislocation. Elbows which are so unstable that prolonged immobilization will be required benefit from early surgical management to avoid excessive stiffness. Open dislocations, vascular disruption, and irreducible dislocations are also indications for operative treatment but these are rare injuries. Throwing athletes may benefit from direct repair of the MCL

Answer: 3
