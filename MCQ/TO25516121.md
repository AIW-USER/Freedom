Chapter: Arthritis MCQs — Sexually Acquired Reactive Arthritis

## Question

A 28-year-old male presents with an asymmetrical oligoarthritis affecting his right knee and left ankle, alongside dactylitis of the second toe, lasting for 4 weeks. He reports a history of dysuria and a clear urethral discharge approximately 3 weeks prior to the onset of joint symptoms, which resolved spontaneously. PCR testing of first-catch urine is positive for Chlamydia trachomatis. Arthrocentesis of the right knee reveals a white blood cell count of 25,000/mm³ with a neutrophil predominance, and routine Gram stain and culture are negative. Which of the following statements regarding the pathogenesis and intra-articular environment of this patient's condition is most accurate?

## Options

1. The arthritis is a purely sterile, immune-mediated phenomenon triggered by molecular mimicry, and the synovium is completely devoid of the triggering organism or its components.
2. Intra-articular glucocorticoids are strictly contraindicated in this scenario due to the high risk of fulminant reactivation of a subclinical intra-articular Chlamydial infection.
3. The pathogenesis involves a localized intra-articular immune response to persistent, metabolically active, yet non-culturable bacteria or bacterial antigens harbored within the synovial tissue.
4. A prolonged 3-to-6 month course of combination antibiotics (e.g., doxycycline and rifampin) has been definitively shown to eradicate this synovial reservoir and permanently cure the chronic joint symptoms.

## Correct option

**3. The pathogenesis involves a localized intra-articular immune response to persistent, metabolically active, yet non-culturable bacteria or bacterial antigens harbored within the synovial tissue.**

## Answer explanation

In Chlamydia-associated reactive arthritis, viable but non-culturable organisms (in a persistent state) and bacterial DNA/antigens disseminate to and persist within the joint via monocytes, driving the local inflammatory response. While historically considered a completely 'sterile' arthritis, modern PCR and immunofluorescence techniques have demonstrated that the synovium in Chlamydia-induced SARA is not entirely sterile.

Clinical, Radiological, and Laboratory Features of SARA :

Definition and Overview

Sexually acquired reactive arthritis (SARA) is a form of reactive arthritis (ReA) — a sterile, inflammatory arthropathy that develops in a genetically susceptible individual following a genitourinary tract infection, most commonly caused by Chlamydia trachomatis (the dominant pathogen in SARA, responsible for 40–60% of cases), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Mycoplasma genitalium. The term "reactive" denotes that the arthritis develops at a site remote from the primary infection — the joint itself is not directly infected (unlike septic arthritis), but rather the immune response triggered by the antecedent infection cross-reacts with joint tissues, producing a sterile inflammatory synovitis.

SARA falls within the broader spectrum of seronegative spondyloarthropathies — a family of HLA-B27-associated inflammatory disorders that share axial involvement, enthesitis, asymmetric oligoarthritis, mucocutaneous manifestations, and negative rheumatoid factor. The other members of this family include ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, and enteropathic arthritis (associated with inflammatory bowel disease).

Reactive arthritis can also follow enteric infections (post-dysenteric ReA — triggered by Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Campylobacter), but SARA specifically refers to the form following genitourinary infection. Historically, the triad of arthritis, urethritis, and conjunctivitis was termed Reiter syndrome (described by Hans Reiter in 1916), though this eponym has fallen out of favour due to Reiter's association with Nazi war crimes and because the full triad is present in only one-third of patients — the condition is now termed reactive arthritis or SARA.

SARA predominantly affects young adult males (20–40 years), with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 5–10:1 for the post-venereal form (in contrast, post-enteric reactive arthritis has a more equal sex distribution). The condition develops 1–6 weeks (typically 2–4 weeks) after the genitourinary infection — this latent interval between infection and arthritis onset is a critical diagnostic clue.

Clinical Features

The clinical manifestations of SARA extend far beyond the joints — it is a systemic inflammatory condition affecting the musculoskeletal system, genitourinary tract, eyes, skin, and mucous membranes.

Musculoskeletal Manifestations

Peripheral Arthritis: The hallmark presentation is an acute, asymmetric oligoarthritis predominantly affecting the lower limb large joints — the knee, ankle, and subtalar/midfoot joints are most commonly involved. The joints are swollen, warm, erythematous, and painful, with significant effusion. The pattern is characteristically asymmetric (affecting one side more than the other or involving different joints on each side) and oligoarticular (2–4 joints), though polyarticular involvement (>4 joints) may occur in severe cases. Upper limb joints are less commonly affected but may include the wrists and small joints of the hands. The arthritis may be migratory (moving from one joint to another over days) or additive (sequential involvement of additional joints while previously affected joints remain inflamed).

Dactylitis ("Sausage Digit"): Diffuse, fusiform swelling of an entire digit (toe more commonly than finger) due to combined flexor tenosynovitis, synovitis of the interphalangeal and MTP joints, and periosteal inflammation. Dactylitis is a hallmark of the spondyloarthropathy family and is present in approximately 15–30% of SARA patients. The affected digit appears uniformly swollen from base to tip, resembling a sausage — distinguishing it from the focal joint swelling of rheumatoid arthritis.

Enthesitis: Inflammation at sites where tendons, ligaments, and fascia insert into bone (entheses) is a cardinal feature of spondyloarthropathies and is frequently present in SARA. The most commonly affected enthesis is the Achilles tendon insertion (insertional Achilles tendinitis — pain, tenderness, and swelling at the posterior calcaneus) and the plantar fascia origin (plantar fasciitis — pain at the plantar aspect of the calcaneus, particularly with the first steps in the morning). Other sites include the patellar tendon insertion (inferior pole of the patella), the quadriceps tendon insertion (superior pole of the patella), the tibial tuberosity, and the greater trochanteric attachments. Enthesitis may precede, accompany, or follow the arthritis.

Axial Involvement: Inflammatory low back pain (sacroiliitis and/or spondylitis) occurs in approximately 20–30% of patients with SARA. The back pain is characteristically inflammatory
— insidious onset, worse in the morning and after prolonged rest, improves with activity and exercise, and is associated with prolonged morning stiffness
(>30–60 minutes). Sacroiliitis in reactive arthritis is characteristically asymmetric
(unilateral or asymmetrically bilateral), distinguishing it from the symmetric bilateral sacroiliitis of ankylosing spondylitis. Sacroiliac joint tenderness may be elicited by specific provocation tests (FABER/Patrick test, sacral compression, pelvic distraction).

Genitourinary Manifestations

Urethritis: The antecedent genitourinary infection may have been asymptomatic (particularly chlamydial urethritis, which is asymptomatic in up to 50% of men) or may have produced dysuria, urethral discharge (typically mucopurulent — distinguishing chlamydial urethritis from the frankly purulent discharge of gonococcal urethritis), and urinary frequency. By the time arthritis develops (2–4 weeks later), the urethritis may have resolved or may persist as a low-grade, asymptomatic inflammation. Sterile urethritis (an inflammatory urethritis that is culture-negative and represents the reactive component rather than persistent infection) may develop or recur during the arthritis episode.

Prostatitis: Chronic prostatitis is common in SARA and may produce perineal discomfort, urinary frequency, and pelvic pain. Prostatic tenderness may be elicited on digital rectal examination.

Circinate Balanitis: A characteristic mucocutaneous manifestation — painless, shallow, erythematous erosions with a serpiginous (circinate) border on the glans penis and prepuce. Present in approximately 20–40% of male patients with SARA. In uncircumcised men, the lesions are moist and shallow; in circumcised men, they may become dry, scaly, and psoriasiform (resembling psoriatic plaques). Circinate balanitis is virtually pathognomonic of reactive arthritis when present in the clinical context.

Ocular Manifestations

Conjunctivitis: The most common ocular manifestation, present in approximately 30–50% of patients. Typically bilateral, mild, and self-limiting — producing redness, grittiness, and a watery or mucopurulent discharge lasting days to weeks. It may be transient and missed if not specifically enquired about.

Anterior Uveitis (Iritis): A more serious ocular manifestation occurring in approximately 10–20% of patients, particularly those who are HLA-B27 positive. Presents with unilateral eye pain, photophobia, lacrimation, blurred vision, and a characteristic ciliary injection (circumcorneal redness). Slit-lamp examination reveals anterior chamber cells and flare (protein exudation), keratic precipitates, and posterior synechiae (adhesions between the iris and lens) if inadequately treated. Anterior uveitis requires urgent ophthalmological referral — untreated, it can produce permanent visual impairment from synechiae, glaucoma, cataract, and macular oedema. Unlike conjunctivitis, uveitis tends to recur and may become chronic.

Keratitis: Corneal inflammation may occur in severe cases, producing pain, photophobia, and visual impairment.

Mucocutaneous Manifestations

Keratoderma Blennorrhagicum: A characteristic skin eruption of SARA — raised, hyperkeratotic, papulopustular lesions with a waxy, crusted surface, predominantly affecting the soles of the feet (most common) and palms. The lesions begin as vesicles or pustules on an erythematous base, evolve into brown, keratotic plaques, and may coalesce into large, confluent, psoriasiform patches. Histologically, keratoderma blennorrhagicum is indistinguishable from pustular psoriasis — indeed, the relationship between reactive arthritis and psoriasis/psoriatic arthritis is debated, with some authorities considering them overlapping conditions within the spondyloarthropathy spectrum. Present in approximately 10–20% of SARA patients.

Oral Ulcers: Painless, shallow ulcers on the palate, tongue, buccal mucosa, or lips occur in approximately 10% of patients. They are often asymptomatic and discovered only on examination.

Nail Changes: Onycholysis (separation of the nail plate from the nail bed), subungual hyperkeratosis, nail pitting, and yellowing — again indistinguishable from psoriatic nail changes.

Systemic Features

Constitutional symptoms including fever (low-grade in most cases, occasionally high spiking fever mimicking septic arthritis), malaise, fatigue, and weight loss are common during the acute episode. Lymphadenopathy (inguinal — related to the genitourinary infection) may be present.

Cardiac Involvement (Rare)

In chronic or recurrent reactive arthritis, aortitis and aortic root inflammation can produce aortic regurgitation — analogous to the card\iac involvement in ankylosing spondylitis. Conduction abnormalities (first-degree heart block, bundle branch block) occur in approximately 5–10% of chronic cases.

Laboratory Features

Inflammatory Markers: ESR and CRP
are elevated during acute episodes, reflecting systemic inflammation. The degree of elevation correlates with disease activity and can be used to monitor treatment response. CRP is more responsive to short-term changes in disease activity.

Complete Blood Count: May show a normocytic normochromic anaemia of chronic disease, leukocytosis (neutrophilia during acute flares), thrombocytosis (reactive — as an acute phase reactant), and elevated platelet count.

HLA-B27: Positive in approximately 60–80%
of patients with SARA (compared to 6–8% prevalence in the general Caucasian population). HLA-B27 positivity is a risk factor for developing reactive arthritis following genitourinary or enteric infection, and is associated with more severe disease, a greater likelihood of axial involvement (sacroiliitis, spondylitis), anterior uveitis, and chronicity. However, HLA-B27 is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis — 20–40% of patients are HLA-B27 negative, and the majority of HLA-B27-positive individuals never develop reactive arthritis. It supports the diagnosis in the appropriate clinical context but does not confirm or exclude it.

Rheumatoid Factor (RF) and Anti-CCP Antibodies: Both are negative — this is the defining feature of the "seronegative" spondyloarthropathies. Negative RF and anti-CCP in a young male with asymmetric oligoarthritis strongly argues against rheumatoid arthritis and supports a spondyloarthropathy diagnosis.

Antinuclear Antibody (ANA): Negative — helps exclude SLE.

Microbiological Investigations — Identifying the Triggering Infection:

First-void urine or urethral swab: Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT/PCR) for Chlamydia trachomatis — the most sensitive test for detecting chlamydial infection. PCR can detect chlamydial DNA even after the active urethritis has resolved (up to several weeks after infection). First-void urine is the preferred non-invasive sample in men. PCR for Neisseria gonorrhoeae should be performed concurrently.

Urethral discharge culture: If discharge is present — conventional culture on Thayer-Martin medium for gonococcus and McCoy cell culture for chlamydia (though PCR has largely replaced culture for chlamydia).

Serology: Anti-chlamydial antibodies (IgG and IgA) may be elevated, suggesting recent or past chlamydial infection. However, serology has limited sensitivity and specificity and cannot distinguish current from past infection. It is useful as supportive evidence when direct testing is negative.

Stool culture: If a post-enteric aetiology is considered (recent gastroenteritis) — culture for Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, and Campylobacter.

Synovial Fluid Analysis:

Aspiration of an affected joint is important to exclude septic arthritis (particularly gonococcal septic arthritis, which must be distinguished from SARA — gonococcal arthritis involves direct joint infection rather than a reactive process). The synovial fluid in SARA is characteristically inflammatory — turbid, elevated white cell count (typically 10,000–50,000 cells/mm³, predominantly neutrophils), reduced viscosity, and elevated protein. Crucially, Gram stain and culture are negative (confirming the sterile, reactive nature of the synovitis). Crystal analysis under polarised microscopy is negative (excluding gout and pseudogout). Some studies have detected chlamydial DNA in synovial fluid by PCR in a proportion of reactive arthritis cases — the significance of this finding (whether it represents viable organisms or degraded DNA fragments taken up by macrophages) is debated and does not change the classification of the arthritis as "reactive" rather than "septic."

Serum Uric Acid: Normal — helps exclude gout as a differential diagnosis.

Management of SARA in a Young Adult Male

Management of SARA is multifaceted — it addresses the triggering infection, the inflammatory arthritis and extra-articular manifestations, and the prevention of chronic disease. Treatment is guided by disease severity and follows a stepwise escalation from conservative measures to immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment of the Triggering Infection

Eradication of the inciting genitourinary infection is the essential first step. Even if the urethritis has clinically resolved by the time the arthritis presents, residual chlamydial infection may persist and perpetuate the immune-mediated inflammatory response. Treatment of confirmed or suspected chlamydial infection:

Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7–14 days is the preferred regimen. Some authorities advocate a prolonged course (up to 6 weeks or even 3 months) based on evidence suggesting that extended antibiotic therapy for chlamydia-triggered reactive arthritis may reduce the duration and severity of the arthritis and decrease the rate of progression to chronicity — though this evidence remains controversial and is not universally accepted. The rationale is that chlamydial elementary bodies may persist in the synovium in a metabolically active but non-cultivable state, perpetuating the immune response, and prolonged antibiotics may eliminate this reservoir.

Alternatively, azithromycin 1 g single dose (or 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg days 2–5) may be used if compliance with doxycycline is a concern, though doxycycline is preferred for reactive arthritis due to its potential additional anti-inflammatory properties (tetracyclines have mild MMP-inhibitory and anti-inflammatory effects independent of their antimicrobial activity).

Sexual partner(s) must be tested and treated concurrently to prevent reinfection — this is essential for public health and for preventing recurrent episodes of SARA triggered by re-exposure to the same pathogen. Referral to a genitourinary medicine (GUM) clinic for contact tracing and partner notification is recommended.

If gonococcal infection is identified, appropriate antibiotic therapy per local guidelines is administered (typically ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose + azithromycin 1 g oral single dose).

Anti-Inflammatory and Immunosuppressive Therapy

NSAIDs — First-Line Anti-Inflammatory:

NSAIDs are the first-line pharmacological treatment for the arthritis, enthesitis, and axial symptoms of SARA. They provide symptomatic relief of pain and swelling and reduce the inflammatory response. Full-dose, scheduled (not as-needed) NSAID therapy is recommended during the acute phase. Indomethacin (75–150 mg/day in divided doses) and naproxen (500 mg twice daily) are the most commonly used agents — indomethacin has a long tradition of use in spondyloarthropathies and may have particular efficacy in this group, though evidence for superiority over other NSAIDs is limited. Etoricoxib (60–90 mg/day) — a selective COX-2 inhibitor — is an alternative with a better gastrointestinal safety profile. NSAIDs are continued for the duration of active disease and tapered gradually as symptoms resolve.

Gastroprotection (proton pump inhibitor co-prescription) is recommended for patients with gastrointestinal risk factors. Renal function should be monitored during prolonged NSAID use.

Intra-Articular Corticosteroid Injection:

For individual severely inflamed joints that do not respond adequately to systemic NSAIDs, intra-articular corticosteroid injection (triamcinolone hexacetonide 20–40 mg for large joints, 5–10 mg for small joints) provides rapid, localised anti-inflammatory effect. This is particularly useful for monoarticular or oligoarticular flares and for enthesitis (peritendinous injection at the Achilles insertion or plantar fascia origin, performed with caution to avoid tendon rupture). Septic arthritis must be excluded by aspiration and culture before injection.

Systemic Corticosteroids:

Short courses of oral prednisolone (20–30 mg/day for 1–2 weeks with rapid taper) may be used for severe polyarticular flares or when NSAIDs are contraindicated. However, prolonged systemic corticosteroid use is avoided due to the risk of adverse effects and the availability of more effective disease-modifying agents for persistent disease.

Disease-Modifying Anti-Rheumatic Drugs (DMARDs)

DMARDs are indicated for patients with persistent symptoms beyond 3–6 months
despite adequate NSAID therapy, recurrent episodes, progressive joint damage, or significant extra-articular disease (chronic uveitis, severe skin involvement).

Sulfasalazine (2–3 g/day in divided doses): The first-line DMARD for reactive arthritis — it has the best evidence base among conventional DMARDs for spondyloarthropathies, with randomised controlled trial data supporting its efficacy in reducing peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, and inflammatory markers. It is less effective for axial disease. Onset of action is 4–8 weeks. Monitoring includes complete blood count and liver function tests at baseline and every 2–4 weeks for the first 3 months, then every 3 months. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal intolerance (nausea, abdominal pain), rash, hepatotoxicity, and reversible oligospermia (relevant in young men — counsel about fertility).

Methotrexate (15–25 mg/week): Used as second-line DMARD when sulfasalazine is ineffective or not tolerated. Evidence for methotrexate in reactive arthritis is less robust than for RA, but clinical experience supports its use in persistent peripheral arthritis. Supplementation with folic acid (5 mg weekly, on a different day from methotrexate) reduces side effects. Monitoring as for RA (baseline and serial LFTs, CBC, renal function). Contraindicated with alcohol and requires reliable contraception.

Biologic Therapy (Refractory Disease)

TNF-alpha Inhibitors: For SARA refractory to conventional DMARDs (persistent active disease despite 3–6 months of sulfasalazine or methotrexate), biologic agents targeting TNF-alpha
have demonstrated efficacy. Etanercept (50 mg subcutaneous weekly), infliximab
(5 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 8 weeks), and adalimumab (40 mg subcutaneous every 2 weeks) are used. TNF inhibitors are particularly effective for axial disease and enthesitis (which respond poorly to conventional DMARDs). They also reduce the frequency and severity of anterior uveitis flares. Prior to initiating TNF inhibitors, screening for latent tuberculosis (Mantoux test, IGRA, chest radiograph), hepatitis B and C, and HIV is mandatory.

IL-17 Inhibitors: Secukinumab
and ixekizumab (monoclonal antibodies against IL-17A) are effective in ankylosing spondylitis and psoriatic arthritis and represent emerging options for refractory reactive arthritis, though specific trial data in reactive arthritis are limited.

Management of Extra-Articular Manifestations

Conjunctivitis: Usually self-limiting — managed with topical lubricants (artificial tears) and cold compresses. Topical antibiotic drops if secondary bacterial infection is suspected.

Anterior Uveitis: Requires urgent ophthalmological management — topical corticosteroid drops (prednisolone acetate 1% hourly initially, tapered gradually) and cycloplegic drops (atropine or cyclopentolate to prevent posterior synechiae and relieve ciliary spasm). Recurrent or refractory uveitis may require periocular or systemic corticosteroids, and is a strong indication for TNF inhibitor therapy (adalimumab has the best evidence for preventing uveitis recurrence among TNF inhibitors).

Keratoderma Blennorrhagicum and Circinate Balanitis: Usually self-limiting and resolve as the systemic inflammation is controlled. Mild lesions require only emollients and topical keratolytics. More severe or widespread lesions may respond to topical corticosteroids (moderate potency — betamethasone valerate 0.1% for skin lesions, hydrocortisone 1% for genital lesions) or topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.1% ointment for genital lesions — steroid-sparing). Severe, widespread keratoderma may require systemic treatment with methotrexate or acitretin (a systemic retinoid used for psoriasiform skin disease).

Enthesitis: In addition to systemic NSAIDs and DMARDs, local measures include activity modification, heel cushions and orthotic insoles (for Achilles and plantar fascia enthesitis), eccentric stretching exercises, and local corticosteroid injection (with caution at the Achilles tendon due to rupture risk — peritendinous rather than intratendinous injection). Refractory enthesitis is a strong indication for TNF inhibitor or IL-17 inhibitor therapy.

Answer: 3
