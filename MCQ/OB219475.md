## Question

A 58-year-old female presents to your office with complaints of knee pain for 6 weeks after an a fall while hiking. She reports the pain is localized to her medial joint line and is worse with twisting and pivoting activities. She also experiences symptoms of locking and catching at times. Radiographs demonstrate no significant joint space narrowing. MRI shows a complex posterior horn medial meniscus tear. Unfortunately she fails to improve with physical therapy and opts to undergo partial medial meniscectomy. She is doing well at her initial 2-week post-op visit but presents at 6-weeks with increasing pain over her medial joint line. She is not improving with physical therapy and a new MRI is ordered (Figures A & B). What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Osteomyelitis
2. Osteonecrosis
3. Advanced osteoarthritis
4. Inflammatory arthritis
5. Osteochondral lesion

## Correct option

**2. Osteonecrosis**

## Answer explanation

This patient has post-arthroscopic osteonecrosis of the medial femoral condyle (MFC).

Osteonecrosis of the knee can occur in several variations. This can happen spontaneously (SONK) or post-arthroscopically. In cases where osteonecrosis occurs after an arthroscopic procedure, the reason is unclear. In most situations, it occurs after a meniscectomy has been performed and some think the altered biomechanics and loss of shock absorption from the meniscectomy cause insufficiency fractures to develop that eventually progress into a crescent shaped foci of osteonecrosis. This typically presents as tenderness over the MFC, medial joint line and swelling. MRI is helpful in these scenarios and typically shows increased T2 uptake. If the area of necrosis is small (<3.5 cm), most patients can be managed nonoperatively with unloader bracing, protected weight bearing, NSAIDS, and sometimes bisphosphonates. If the lesion is larger (>5 cm) and doesn't improve with nonoperative management, surgical management may be necessary, usually in the form of arthroplasty.

*Di Caprio et al. published a review on osteonecrosis of the knee after arthroscopy.* **They note** that post-arthroscopy osteonecrosis is the rarest form of knee osteonecrosis and most commonly affects those undergoing meniscectomy who are older than 55 years old. While **they note** the etiology is debated, they postulate that altered biomechanics after meniscectomy may predispose the knee to increased contact forces, insufficiency fractures and subsequent osteonecrosis. **They argue** that most can be treated nonoperatively with protected weight bearing for 4-8 weeks.

*Zaremski et al. provide a generalized overview of knee osteonecrosis,* **noting** that it usually affects women in their middle ages and most commonly is seen in the MFC. It is exacerbated by weight bearing and is attributed to subchondral insufficiency fractures in poor quality bone that eventually lead to osteonecrosis. Treatment is based on the size and signal of the lesion.

Figures A & B show the coronal and sagittal MRI cuts of a knee with spontaneous osteonecrosis of the MFC, as evidenced by the crescent shaped area of increased T2 uptake.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While increased signal on a T2 MRI can also represent changes of osteomyelitis, this patient has no signs of infection.
Answer 3: No significant joint space narrowing was identified on preoperative radiographs and the post-operative MRI does not show significant MFC chondrosis.
Answer 4: Inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid, typically show a reactive synovial irritation and destruction of the joint's soft tissues in addition to the cartilage surfaces. There is no suggestion of these changes in this patient.
Answer 5: No osteochondral lesion is seen on the MRI.
