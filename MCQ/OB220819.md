## Question

A 7-week-old male is referred to your office by a pediatric orthopedist for management of an upper extremity plexopathy, following a traumatic birth in which the patient sustained a clavicle fracture following a prolonged, forceps-assisted delivery. Which of the following clinical findings would most likely require consideration for early surgical intervention?

## Options

1. Posterior humeral head subluxation
2. Rigid wrist extension
3. Absence of biceps function
4. Presence of moro reflex
5. Presence of miosis and anhidrosis

## Correct option

**5. Presence of miosis and anhidrosis**

## Answer explanation

The clinical finding most likely to require urgent referral to a sub-specialist is the presence of pre-ganglionic findings, which can include miosis and anhidrosis (Answer 5).

Brachial plexopathies are uncommonly encountered in practice. Luckily, the vast majority exhibit favorable outcomes, as the most common brachial plexopathy is Erb's palsy (80%), which spans the C5-6 nerve roots and inherently has an excellent prognosis for spontaneous recovery without sequelae (90%). However, certain examination findings, which include Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhydrosis), as well as hemidiaphragm elevation, suggest a pre-ganglionic injury and thus more expansive injury to the brachial plexus. These findings are notorious in the sense that they represent a low likelihood of spontaneous neurologic recovery and require prompt referral to a sub-specialist for operative management, which may include nerve grafting, nerve transfer, or neurotization.

Waters provides a review of obstetric brachial plexus injury, focusing on the evaluation and management of the condition. The author notes spontaneous recovery within two months of birth is a good predictor for complete recovery; however, delays in recovery beginning at three months or longer progressively worsen the prognosis. Moreover, distal brachial plexopathies (C8-T1) or total brachial plexopathies tend to have a poorer prognosis and a higher likelihood of pre-ganglionic injuries.

*Pulos et al. similarly provide a synopsis on brachial plexus birth palsies,* **noting** that their incidence is decreasing. Repeated serial examinations are key to detecting the need for early referral and thus surgical intervention, which often can include interposition grafting or neurotization. Due to the rarity of brachial plexopathies, surgical options are limited, with the existing literature confined to retrospective studies.

Lin and Somora also summarize brachial plexus injuries occurring during birth. The authors similarly report serial examinations as being crucial for detecting pre- versus post-ganglionic nerve injuries, and thus the need for surgical intervention, which is most commonly in the form of neurolysis, neuroma excision, interpositional nerve grafting and conduits, and/or nerve transfers. Direct nerve repair is typically not performed due to the inability to create a tensionless coaptation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: Posterior humeral head subluxation and rigid wrist extension can be seen on examination, particularly in the absence of stretching, and may necessitate surgical intervention. However, their presence does not require urgent referral.
Answer 3: Lack of biceps function is common at this stage. Only after three months does lack of biceps function portend a poor prognosis, and surgical intervention can be considered.
Answer 4: At this age, the Moro reflex is commonly present and not pathological
