## Question

A 23-year-old man presents to the trauma resuscitation unit after being hit by a high-speed motor vehicle while crossing the street. He sustains a closed right tibial fracture, bilateral forearm fractures, and a grade 2 splenic laceration. On presentation, he is hypotensive and tachycardic and is taken emergently to the operating room for an exploratory laparotomy and right tibial external fixation; intra-abdominal hemostasis is achieved, and the patient's abdomen is closed. In which of the following scenarios is the patient most appropriate for external fixator removal and definitive intramedullary nail fixation of his right tibia fracture?

## Options

1. Hospital day 3; Lactic acid 4.8 mmol/L; Base excess -6.0 mmol/L
2. Hospital day 3; Lactic acid 2.0 mmol/L; Base excess -2.5 mmol/L
3. Hospital day 5; Lactic acid 2.0 mmol/L; Base excess -6.0 mmol/L
4. Hospital day 15; Lactic acid 0.4 mmol/L; Base excess -2.0 mmol/L
5. Hospital day 21; Lactic acid 0.4 mmol/L; Base excess -2.0 mmol/L

## Correct option

**2. Hospital day 3; Lactic acid 2.0 mmol/L; Base excess -2.5 mmol/L**

## Answer explanation

The patient should be taken for conversion from external fixation to definitive intramedullary fixation as soon as he is adequately resuscitated (Answer 2).

Damage control orthopedics (DCO) is an approach employed in trauma management that focuses on the initial stabilization of patients with severe skeletal injuries while minimizing the risk of further complications. This technique typically involves performing urgent fixation of long bone fractures and dislocations or temporary external fixation, allowing for definitive surgical interventions to be conducted once the patient's overall condition stabilizes. Lactic acid levels < 4.0 mmol/L and base excess levels > -5.5 mmol/L are considered safe parameters for early appropriate care (EAC). However, long bone fractures should be converted from external fixation to definitive intramedullary fixation as soon as safely possible to reduce the risk of infection. Closed tibial fractures treated in external fixation for periods greater than 14 days are associated with a higher risk of infection than those that are definitively treated within a week.

*Bunzel et al. performed a retrospective analysis of tibial fractures treated with provisional external fixation and subsequent conversion to IMN from 2009 to 2019.* **They revealed** a high infection rate (32%), with a median external fixator duration of 8 days for infected patients versus 4 days for non-infected patients. Open fractures, compartment syndrome, and provisional external fixation were independent risk factors for infection. While controlling for open fractures, the 8-14 day group had RR = 1.81 (p = 0.2), and the 15+ day group had RR = 2.67 (p = 0.003) as compared with the < 7-day group for infection risk. This study suggests that early conversion from external fixation to IMN may reduce infection rates, and the authors advise caution in using external fixation for tibial fractures definitively.

*Pairon et al. discussed strategies for converting from external fixation to intramedullary nailing for fractures of the femur and tibia.* **They suggest** that long-term external fixation is associated with long-term complications like infection and nonunion. The review suggests that conversion from external fixation to nailing should occur as soon as the patient's condition allows, particularly before pin-tract infections develop, and should be performed in a single-stage procedure when possible. Early conversion promotes better outcomes in terms of reduced infection risk. The article emphasizes that while external fixation is useful in the acute phase, especially for critically ill polytraumatized patients, definitive treatment with intramedullary nailing is advantageous for long-term management.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3: The patient's lactic acid and/or base excess indicates inadequate resuscitation for definitive orthopedic treatment.
Answers 4 and 5: Definitive fixation over 8 days after external fixation increases the risk of infection compared to early definitive fixation.
