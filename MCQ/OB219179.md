## Question

A 22-year-old male presents to the emergency department trauma bay with pain in his left leg after a motorcycle accident prior to arrival. He is neurovascularly intact but a deformity is noted. You suspect a proximal third tibial shaft fracture which is confirmed on the radiographs seen in Figure A. You plan for an intramedullary nail with Poller screws proposed in the locations marked by the red dots in Figure A. Assuming these screws are placed appropriately, what deformity are they attempting to prevent?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Valgus and recurvatum
2. Valgus and procurvatum
3. Varus and recurvatum
4. Varus and procurvatum
5. Procurvatum only

## Correct option

**2. Valgus and procurvatum**

## Answer explanation

Placement of Poller screws lateral/posterior in the proximal fragment helps correct valgus and procurvatum deformities.

Proximal third tibial shaft fractures represent a significant subset of tibial shaft fractures and can be challenging to fix. The primary difficulty is the anatomy of the proximal tibia and associated deforming forces. The patellar tendon attachment pulls the proximal fracture fragment into extension with the gastrocnemius pulling the distal fracture fragment into flexion, causing a procurvatum deformity. This is compounded by the anterior compartment musculature creating a valgus deformity. Together, these forces contribute to a high rate of valgus/procurvatum deformity after intramedullary nailing (IMN). Poller screws are one of the primary methods for preventing malreduction as they help keep the nail centered within the shaft. They may also increase construct stability if left in place. Generally, they should be placed on the concave side of the respective displaced fracture fragment.

*Maslow et al. looked at tibial IMN entry points radiographically to determine the most accurate starting point for tibial nailing.* *They talk about the ideal starting point just medial to the lateral tibial plateau and anterior to the articular margin and in their study, noted that the average deviation from this point fluoroscopically was noted to be 4.6 mm medial, 2.9 mm anterior, and 2.7 mm distal.* *They go on to discuss the effects this can have on reduction and fracture fixation.*

*Hiesterman et al. reviewed nailing of proximal third tibia fractures without joint involvement,* **noting** that these account for 5-11% of all tibial shaft fractures. They discuss the common deformity is valgus and procurvatum and offer solutions for minimizing malreduction, including Poller screws, proper starting point, unicortical plating, alternative positioning and nailing systems, and use of the universal distractor. **They report** that with the use of these techniques, the malreduction rate in these fractures is approximately 8.2%.
