## Question

## Options

1. Increased risk of angular deformity requiring future surgery
2. Decreased risk of angular deformity requiring future surgery
3. Increased risk of postoperative fracture displacement
4. Decreased risk of postoperative fracture displacement
5. Increased time to radiographic union

## Correct option

**2. Decreased risk of angular deformity requiring future surgery**

## Answer explanation

The patient in Figure A is more skeletally mature than the patient in Figure B, as evidenced by the comparatively increased ossification and fusion of the tibial tuberosity. The degree of tibial tuberosity ossification is an independent risk factor for the development of clinically significant angular deformities or limb length discrepancies requiring further surgical intervention.

Distal femoral physeal fractures are rare injuries accounting for less than 2% of all physeal injuries. In skeletally immature patients, the relatively weaker physis can act as a plane along which the fracture line may propagate. The distal femoral physis grows at an average of 10 mm per year and provides the majority of longitudinal femoral growth; therefore injuries to the physis make it highly susceptible to partial or complete premature physeal arrest. Up to 50% of distal femoral physeal fractures result in some form of growth disturbance, and that percentage increases with injury severity. Post-traumatic growth disturbances that occur in younger children are at a higher risk of requiring future surgical intervention, which may involve osteotomies or epiphysiodesis to correct angular deformities or limb length discrepancies.

The tibial tuberosity is almost always visible on plain radiographs taken to assess distal femoral physeal fractures either at the initial injury or during the immediate perioperative period, and it matures through a well-established sequence of radiologically progressive stages making it a reliable local surrogate for determining skeletal maturity. This defined progression allows tibial tuberosity ossification staging to be used as an estimation of remaining growth potential at the distal femoral physis, which can predict the risk of developing a clinically significant deformity following a fracture to the physis.

*Vergara-Amador et al. published a 2016 radiographic study assessing the radiologic characteristics of the tibial tuberosity development in a pediatric population stratified by age.* *The authors assessed 210 plain-film X-rays of the knee in patients aged 10 to 17 years and noted the presence of ossification of the tibial tuberosity, the distance between the tuberosity and the metaphysis, and fusion with the epiphysis.* **The authors found** that the tuberosity has a single center of ossification that follows a predictable ossification pattern: ossification starts distally, then the proximal portion fuses with the remainder of the epiphysis, and ends with fusion of the distal portion. In females, the tibial tuberosity was ossified at 11 years, fusion started at 12 years, and fusion was complete by 17 years. In males, the process was delayed by approximately one year.

*Kennedy et al. performed a retrospective review of 82 children over a 17-year period who underwent treatment for distal femoral physeal fractures.* *The authors hypothesized that the tibial tuberosity ossification stage would predict the need for further reoperation.* **The authors found** that the odds of receiving any further surgery if the ossification stage was 3 or 4 (tuberosity fused to metaphysis), compared with 0 to 2 (tuberosity not fused to metaphysis) was 0.12. It should be noted that this study’s population contained patients with a variety of Salter-Harris fractures who underwent different interventions, ranging from closed reduction and casting to operative fixation.

Figure A is a radiograph of a Salter-Harris II fracture of the distal femur with a tibial tuberosity in late Stage 2 of ossification. Figure B is a radiograph of a Salter-Harris II fracture of the distal femur with a tibial tuberosity in Stage 0 of ossification. Illustration A details the tibial tuberosity ossification staging as described by Vergara-Amador.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient in Figure A is more skeletally mature, and at a decreased risk of incurring deformity or limb length discrepancy requiring surgery, not an increased risk.
Answer 3: Increased risk of fracture displacement has not been associated with skeletal maturity.
Answer 4: A decreased risk of fracture displacement has not been associated with skeletal maturity.
Answer 5: Increased time to radiographic union has not been associated with skeletal maturity.
