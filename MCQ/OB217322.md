## Question

## Options

1. Biopsy and initiation of chemotherapy
2. CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis and full body bone scan
3. Curettage
4. Radiation
5. Proximal humerus resection and total shoulder arthroplasty

## Correct option

**3. Curettage**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a symptomatic enchondroma, which is best treated with intralesional resection, curettage, and bone grafting or cement augmentation.

Enchondromas are benign cartilaginous neoplasms often found in the metaphyseal regions of long bones. A vast majority of them can be successfully managed with nonsurgical intervention, and are usually found incidentally on imaging for other reasons. Enchondromas fall on the spectrum of cartilage neoplasms, with chondrosarcoma being their malignant counterpart. Solitary enchondromas have a low risk of malignant transformation (~1%), but those with Ollier's disease and Maffucci syndrome have a significantly higher risk, upwards of 30%. Low-grade cartilaginous neoplasms can be successfully treated with an intralesional excision, high-speed burr, thermal cauterization, and cement versus allograft bone augmentation.

*Mermerkaya et al performed a retrospective study of 21 consecutive patients with intramedullary low-grade chondrosarcoma of long bones treated by intralesional curettage and adjuvant therapy comprising high-speed burring, thermal cauterization, and cementation.* A majority of the lesions were in the proximal humerus and they followed patients for an average of approximately 5 years. At average follow-up (58 months), no patient had developed local recurrence and none developed distant metastases. The average MSTS score was 95%, indicating excellent clinical and functional outcomes.

*Omlor et al performed a retrospective review of 228 enchondromas versus atypical cartilaginous lesions treated conservatively or surgically.* *66% of the lesions were treated conservatively, while 33% were treated with intralesional excision and cement or allograft augmentation.* MSTS scores were nearly equivalent, 96% and 97%, respectively. **They concluded** symptomatic lesions may be treated with surgery, but most should be managed conservatively due to the risk of recurrence with resection and potential for higher-grade transformation.

*Akoh et al performed a retrospective study on the clinical outcome and cost-effectiveness of surveillance of 55 long bone enchondromas.* *3 out of 55 (~5%) displayed growth at an average of 23 months.* **They concluded** that growth of enchondroma-appearing lesions is rare and typically appears at 2 years post detection. **They recommended** that surveillance last approximately 3 years to rule out growth, and radiographs over that time provided a cost-savings of approximately 75% compared to routine MRI surveillance.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Benign enchondromas and low-grade cartilaginous neoplasms have not been shown to respond to systemic chemotherapy.
Answer 2: CT chest, abdomen, pelvis, and full body bone scan is not necessary to work up enchondromas as they are localized, benign entities with low metastatic potential.
Answer 4: Benign enchondromas and low-grade cartilaginous neoplasms have not been shown to respond favorably to radiation. Radiation is also associated with the risk of developing local radiation-induced sarcomas.
Answer 5: Benign enchondromas and low-grade cartilaginous neoplasms can be successfully treated with intralesional resection with local adjuvant therapy. Wide resection and arthroplasty is over-treatment for this patient and may result in inferior outcomes.
