## Question

Which of the following scenarios serves as the strongest indication to utilize a rotating platform hinged implant?

## Options

1. Significant (15 degrees) varus deformity with 1cm medial tibial plateau erosion
2. Significant (15 degrees) valgus deformity with 1cm lateral tibial plateau erosion
3. Aseptic revision TKA with a gross mismatch (>15mm) between flexion and extension gaps despite upsizing the femoral component
4. Stage 2 revision total knee arthroplasty after removing a dynamic spacer
5. Revision TKA for persistent anterior knee pain and stiffness in a patient with patella baja

## Correct option

**3. Aseptic revision TKA with a gross mismatch (>15mm) between flexion and extension gaps despite upsizing the femoral component**

## Answer explanation

Discrepancies between the flexion and extension gaps may be managed with soft tissue releases and/or changing the implants’ sizes depending on the cause and size of the gap mismatch. However, persistent gross gap mismatch despite these techniques should prompt the utilization of a hinged prosthesis (Answer 3).

Rotating hinged implants have been historically reserved as a salvage option for complex total knee arthroplasty (TKA) cases involving complete collateral ligamentous instability. However, indications have expanded to include oncologic reconstruction, periprosthetic fracture management, genu recurvatum (>5 degrees), and gross mismatch between flexion and extension gaps. Significant gap mismatch may be seen in the primary TKA setting with chronic joint contractures seen in polio or rheumatoid arthritis. In the setting of revision total knee arthroplasty, it is exceedingly common to encounter a larger flexion gap compared to the extension gap. The etiology is likely multifactorial in nature (preferential posterior bone loss from prior implant removal, larger exposure compared to the primary TKA, the posterior cruciate ligament being removed, overall ligamentous laxity, etc.). This increased flexion gap can be managed by upsizing the femoral component and utilizing posterior femoral augments if needed. However, if there remains a persistent gross mismatch despite this technique, a hinged implant should be utilized. Otherwise, the patient would develop flexion instability and potentially dislocate over the central post of any unlinked, posterior cruciate stabilizing implant.

*Kowalczewski and colleagues performed a retrospective review of 12 knees in which a rotating hinged platform was placed as the primary implant in the setting of gross joint destruction, significant axial deformities, and contracture with a dysfunctional medial collateral ligament.* **They reported** all patients retained the implants at 10 years, with only 3 requiring a secondary formal surgical wound closure. **The authors conclude** by advocating for rotating hinged implants in primary total knee arthroplasty procedures when appropriately indicated.

*Gehrke and colleagues retrospectively reviewed the long-term outcomes of their institution’s utilization of rotating-hinged components for non-oncologic indications.* **They reported** when used as a primary implant there was a 94% survival rate in patients older than 60 but only a 77% survival rate in patients younger than 60 at 13 years follow-up. **The authors concluded** that rotating hinged implants have good long-term survivorship, however, note that revision can be challenging, especially with long-stemmed implants.

*Cottino and colleagues retrospectively reviewed the long-term outcomes of their institution’s utilization of rotating-hinged components for non-oncologic indications.* **They identified** 408 patients with 18% indicated for complex primary cases and 82% for revision cases. All patients had significantly improved Knee Society scores with a cumulative incidence of all-cause revisions being 9.7% at 2 years and 22.5% at 10 years. **They further found** lower revision rates for aseptic loosening when metaphyseal cones were utilized. **The authors concluded** that contemporary rotating-hinge TKA implants are durable constructs with good long-term outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1 and 2: There are many techniques to manage coronal plane deformities prior to utilizing a hinged prosthesis. Significant varus/valgus deformities with unicompartmental bone loss can be managed via soft tissue releases and tibial tray metal wedge augmentation. More significant coronal plane deformities (>20°) can be treated with osteotomy procedures prior to arthroplasty.
Answer 4: A prior dynamic spacer implies that the patient has retained competent collateral ligaments and a constrained, non-hinged design would be a more appropriate implant option.
Answer 5: Patella baja can be managed by lowering the joint line (i.e. distalizing the femoral component or utilizing a proximal-displacing tibial tubercle osteotomy). However, patella baja in and of itself does not necessitate a rotating hinged implant.
