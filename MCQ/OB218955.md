## Question

A 38-year-old male presents to your clinic with pain and decreased range of motion in his right elbow. He states he sustained a forearm fracture approximately six months prior for which he underwent surgical fixation from an outside surgeon. His radiographs from your clinic today are shown in Figure A. What is the most likely cause of continued symptoms in this patient?

![[q218955-a-monteggia2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Failure to recognize collateral ligament injury
2. Inadequate ulnar reduction
3. Inadequate repair of the annular ligament
4. Failure to perform radial head resection at the index surgery
5. Normal postoperative stiffness

## Correct option

**2. Inadequate ulnar reduction**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with radial head subluxation after previous open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) of the ulna for a Monteggia fracture, most often due to failure to obtain anatomic alignment of the ulna (Answer 2). This patient would likely require ulnar corrective osteotomy and open radial head reduction to achieve stability.

Monteggia fractures are fractures of the proximal ulna with associated radial head instability. Monteggia injuries lead to proximal radioulnar joint (PRUJ) disruption, allowing the radial head to dislocate from the capitellum and proximal ulna. In adults, these injuries typically occur as a result of high-energy mechanisms and almost always necessitate operative intervention. *Bado classified these injuries according to the direction of radial head displacement.* Complications after fixation include radial head dislocation or subluxation, posterior interosseus nerve injury, synostosis, and loss of motion. In the event of radial head dislocation after ulnar fixation, a second surgery is indicated. This typically features correction of the malreduced ulna fracture with an ulnar osteotomy and open reduction of the radial head. Restoring the length, alignment, and rotation of proximal ulna fractures is critical for the long-term stability of the radial head. Most authors would suggest a direct posterior incision over the ulna with direct and fluoroscopic verification of reduction. Once the ulna is anatomically reduced, subluxation or dislocation may still be persistent secondary to soft tissue structures including the interposed annular ligament or joint capsule. These structures are addressed via a separate lateral incision.

*Ring et al. reviewed Monteggia fractures in the adult and pediatric populations.* **The authors state** that the anatomic reduction of the radial head hinges on a stable anatomic reduction of the ulnar fracture. **The authors conclude** that early recognition of PRUJ instability is key to good outcomes after Monteggia fractures.

*Patel et al. also reviewed Monteggia fractures.* *The authors reiterated the importance of anatomic surgical reduction and stabilization of the ulna in these fractures in adults.* **They found** that a less common cause of persistent PRUJ instability may be interposed soft tissue blocking radial head reduction. **The authors conclude** that in the setting of persistent radial head subluxation or dislocation after ulna ORIF, surgical reassessment of the ulnar reduction should be performed.

*Delpont et al. provide a review of Monteggia fractures focusing on missed and chronic injuries.* **The authors note** that even minimal displacement of the ulnar fragment may cause radial head dislocation. Acute treatment involves internal fixation of the ulna to stabilize the radial head. Chronic or missed Monteggia fractures pose a greater challenge and often necessitate open reduction of the radial head and proximal osteotomy of the ulnar shaft. **The authors note** that missed Monteggia fractures can become irreducible at 2-3 weeks as fibrosis of the elbow begins. **The authors conclude** that high suspicion should be used for suspected Monteggia fractures as the outcomes of untreated radial head dislocation can be severe.

Figure A demonstrates a lateral radiograph of the forearm with persistent anterior radial head dislocation in the setting of a proximal ulna ORIF. Illustration A is a table of the Bado classification.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Ligamentous insufficiency can be a cause of continued elbow instability after Monteggia ORIF; however this is not the most common etiology. Ligamentous insufficiency is also not the cause of continued subluxation in this setting of malreduced ulna fracture.
Answer 3: Repair of the annular ligament does not reestablish PRUJ instability in the setting of malreduced ulna fracture. Annular ligament interposition is a rare complication of Monteggia fractures.
Answer 4: Radial head resection or arthroplasty would not be indicated in this patient as they do not have a radial head fracture and the radial head would presumably reduce after the restoration of ulnar length.
Answer 5: Although fibrosis is a complication associated with this fracture pattern and most fractures about the elbow, this patient is presenting with radial head subluxation.
