## Question

You are in the middle of performing a primary total hip arthroplasty on a 75-year-old woman with a medical history significant for HTN, HLD, breast cancer, and GERD. She underwent routine pre-operative clearance and imaging with your medical team. After you make your femoral neck cut, you note abnormal-appearing fatty tissue and erosion about the calcar. Intraoperative histopathology results are obtained and shown in figure A. Her pre-operative imaging is shown in figure B. What is the next appropriate step in the procedure?

## Options

1. Proceed with cementless acetabular and femoral implants
2. Place intramedullary rod to span entire bone
3. Convert from a press fit stem to a cemented stem and cup
4. Proceed with amputation
5. Close the wound and abort the procedure

## Correct option

**3. Convert from a press fit stem to a cemented stem and cup**

## Answer explanation

With the patients history of breast cancer, an abnormal lesion in the proximal femur would be concerning for metastasis. The histopathology slide reveal breast carcinoma. Cemented fixation should be used due to the need for post-operative radiation which would inhibit bony ingrowth in cementless fixation designs.

In this scenario, a concerning bone lesion was not fully appreciated pre-operatively, and the decision was made to proceed with primary total hip arthroplasty. When this occurs, the surgeon must remember their basic oncologic principles. Tissue pathology should be obtained before proceeding with any further surgery, and the entire femur should be imaged intra-operatively for the presence of any skip lesions. In a patient over the age of 40 with a destructive skeletal lesion and history of malignancy, metastatic disease is highest on your differential. Cancers that frequently metastasize to bone include breast, lung, thyroid, prostate and renal (mnemonic: BLT with Pickles on Rye).

*Biermann et al. discuss the evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment of metastatic bone disease.* **They emphasize** that tissue diagnosis is of primary importance prior to treatment determination. This avoids improper management of primary bone malignancies and optimizes oncologic treatment.

*Weber et al. retrospectively reviewed musculoskeletal metastases to the lower extremity at three institutions.* The most common source was breast cancer, and the most common location was the femur. Of 225 lesions, 168 were treated surgically with internal fixation (96) and prosthetic reconstruction (72). Patients who did not have surgery died at a mean of 14.2 months, and those who had surgery died at 11.5 months. Many of the non-operatively treated lesions were not at risk for impending fracture and could be treated with radiation for local control. 60 (35.7%) of the patients treated surgically were for a diagnosis of pathologic fracture, and the rest were for impending fracture.

Figure A is a bone biopsy revealing evidence of metastatic breast carcinoma. Figure B is an AP of the pelvis and lateral left hip which shows a degenerative hip joint with a lytic lesion in the femoral neck.

Incorrect answers
Answer 1: Cementless fixation relies on bony ingrowth which would be inhibited by radiation therapy
Answer 2: The neck cut has already been made in this scenario so intramedullary fixation would not be appropriate
Answer 3: The patient does not require an amputation
Answer 5: It is within the means of the treating orthopaedist to continue the operation. Not completing the operation would subject the patient to significant immobility.
