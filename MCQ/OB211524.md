## Question

A 42-year-old male presents to the ED after injuring his right arm while moving a solid wood dresser. He reports trying to put the dresser down when it slipped forcing his arm into extension. He then heard a “pop” and had pain and weakness in his right arm. On examination, he has mild swelling about the antecubital fossa, prominence of the biceps muscle belly, and a abnormal hook test. Which of the following properly describes the mechanism of contraction that most commonly leads to this patient’s pathology?

## Options

1. Muscle contracts with constant length
2. Muscle contracts with constant speed
3. Muscle contracts with constant force
4. Muscle shortens during contraction
5. Muscle lengthens during contraction

## Correct option

**5. Muscle lengthens during contraction**

## Answer explanation

Distal biceps ruptures most commonly occur following excessive eccentric contraction. Typically, the biceps muscle lengthens during contraction as the arm is forced from a flexed to an extended position.

Ruptures of the distal biceps are relatively rare and account for 10% of all biceps ruptures. Most commonly, these injuries occur during eccentric contraction and in the dominant elbow of male patients in their 40’s. Most patients are able to give a history of a painful “pop” followed by weakness in supination. On exam, patients may have a “reverse Popeye sign” in which the retracted biceps muscle belly is prominent in the upper arm. Additionally, patients may have a positive hook test, in which the examiner is unable to hook the lateral aspect of the biceps tendon hooking from lateral to medial. Several techniques exist for repair including one and two-incision approaches and various fixation methods: bone tunnels, suture anchors, intraosseous screw fixation, intramedullary buttons and suspensory cortical buttons.

*Bisson et al review complications associated with a modified two-incision technique for repair of distal biceps ruptures.* **They report** a complication rate of 27% on 45 consecutive cases including nerve dysfunction (n=7), radioulnar synostosis (n=3), loss of motion unrelated to heterotopic ossification (n=2), early re-rupture (n=1), and reflex sympathetic dystrophy (n=1). **They conclude** that functional synostosis occurred more frequently in their series than previously reported.

*Cusick et al review the incidence of tendon re-rupture following distal biceps repair using a cortical button and interference screw.* **They report** an early failure rate of 1.2% with only 2 patients having failure of fixation. **They conclude** that the use of cortical suspensory fixation in conjunction with an interference screw is an effective method of repairing a distal biceps rupture with a low early failure rate.

*Watson et al review the various repair techniques and complication rates for distal biceps repairs.* **They report** an overall complication rate of 24.5%. This rate did not differ significantly between one or two-incision techniques; however, bone tunnel and cortical button fixation methods have significantly lower complication rates than did suture anchor and interosseous screws. **They conclude** that future studies should be performed to determine the optimal number of incisions.

*O'Driscoll et al review the hook test for distal biceps tendon avulsions.* *They report on the accuracy of the hook test performed by a single examiner on 45 patients who were undergoing surgical exploration of the distal biceps tendon.* *100% of patients (n=33) with a complete avulsion had an abnormal hook test, whereas, it was intact in the 12 remaining patients with only a partial tear.* *MRI diagnosed a complete tear in 11 of 12 patients with partial tears and 11 of 13 with complete avulsions.* **They conclude** that the hook test is both highly sensitive and specific for distal biceps tendon avulsions

*Amarasooriya et al performed a systematic review of the complications following distal biceps tendon repair.* **They report** an overall complication rate of 25% (n=774), with major complications occurring in 4.6% (n=114) including PIN injury in 1.6% of patients. **They conclude** that an extensile single incision was associated with a higher rate of superficial radial nerve injury (minor complication) compared with a limited single incision. Furthermore, synostosis occurred only after double incision techniques.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: An isometric contraction is one in which the muscle contracts with a constant length and is not associated with distal biceps ruptures.
Answer 2: Isokinetic contractions occur with constant speed and are not associated with distal biceps ruptures.
Answer 3: Isotonic contractions occur with constant force and are not associated with distal biceps ruptures.
Answer 4: Concentric contractions occur when the muscle is shortened during contraction as occurs during a biceps curl and are not highly associated with rupture.
