## Question

A 77-year-old male presents to your office with complaints of chronic shoulder pain and difficulty raising his arm overhead. A radiograph obtained in the office is seen in Figure A. He notes he has already had several injections at an outside office and undergone multiple rounds of physical therapy, without much benefit. You discuss surgical intervention with the patient and while obtaining informed consent you note the risks and benefits of the best indicated procedure. Intraoperatively, which of the following could help prevent the most common complication seen after this procedure?

![[q217943-a-rotator-cuff-arthropathy.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Use of four bicortical locking screws in baseplate
2. Use of lateralized baseplate
3. Increase humeral stem angle
4. Placement of drain until day two post-operatively
5. Perform subscapularis tenotomy

## Correct option

**2. Use of lateralized baseplate**

## Answer explanation

This patient would be indicated for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) given his chronic rotator cuff tear. The most common complication after RTSA is dislocation and a lateralized baseplate would decrease this risk.

The utilization of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty is increasing significantly in patients with chronic rotator cuff arthropathy in addition to patients with glenohumeral arthritis and significant glenoid retroversion (B2 glenoid). Modes of failure in RTSA must be considered by surgeons before performing these procedures. The most common mode of failure seen is dislocation/instability, which has been noted, on average, in 2-4% of cases. Established risk factors for dislocation include male sex, previous shoulder surgery, proximal humeral bone loss, axillary nerve injury and subscapularis deficiency. Surgeons can prevent this dreaded complication by lateralizing the glenoid baseplate or glenosphere, increasing the glenosphere size, placing the baseplate eccentrically, lateralizing/distalizing the humeral component or using a constrained implant.

*Bacle et al. published a review of long-term outcomes after Grammont-style RTSA.* *They followed 87 RTSAs for an average of 150 months and compared the results to a prior study that they had done at 40 month follow-up.* **They noted** that Constant scores decreased significantly compared to previous follow-up, with 73% of shoulders showing evidence of scapular notching, and 12% having undergone revision surgery.

*Ascione et al. reviewed long-term humeral complications in 1,035 patients after Grammont-style RTSA.* **They found** a 3.3% rate of overall complication, with complications more common in those cases where the RTSA was implanted for tumor, fracture or revision. **They noted** that these fractures increase with longer follow-up and have a negative effect on functional outcomes.

*Chae et al. wrote a JAAOS review article on instability in RTSA.* **They note** that with the expanding indications in RSTA, complications, including dislocation have increased in prevalence. They cite a dislocation rate of 1.5%-31% and note that surgeons should pay close attention to patient selection, prosthetic design, surgical technique and prosthetic biomechanics in order to prevent this complication.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of a right shoulder demonstrating a high-riding humeral head indicative of a chronic rotator cuff tear with acetabularization of the acromion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Use of four bicortical locking screws would help with securing the glenoid baseplate, but baseplate loosening is not the most common complication after RTSA. There are also some authors who have suggested that unicortical screws directed at the coracoid may decrease the risk of acromial stress fracture and that two screws may provide sufficient baseplate purchase.
Answer 3: A more valgus humeral stem angle would medialize the humeral component, decreasing deltoid wrapping and would not decrease the risk of dislocation.
Answer 4: Drain placement is aimed at decreasing hematoma formation after RTSA, which is less common than dislocation.
Answer 5: Violation of the subscapularis without repair may increase the risk of dislocation, not decrease it.
