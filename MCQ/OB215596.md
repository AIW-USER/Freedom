## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an incomplete spinal cord injury. Her physical examination suggests an anterior cord syndrome with sensory sparing of the posterior column.

Anterior cord syndrome presents with motor dysfunction and dissociated sensory deficit below the spinal injury. Anterior cord syndrome often occurs secondary to direct compression of the anterior spinal cord or from injury to the anterior spinal artery. On examination there will be loss of motor, pain, and temperature. However, proprioception and vibratory sense will be maintained. With incomplete spinal cord injuries, hypotension and bradycardia are often evident secondary to neurogenic shock. This incomplete spinal cord injury carries the worst prognosis and has a low chance of motor recovery.

*Maynard et al. reviews the International Standards for Neurological and Functional Classification of Spinal Cord Injury.* *They report on neurological clinical syndromes including central cord, Brown-Séquard, anterior cord, conus medullaris, and cauda equina syndromes.* **They conclude** that a thorough clinical examination is important to aid in the diagnosis of these syndromes.

*McKinley et al. retrospectively compared functional outcomes for individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) clinical syndromes, including central cord (CCS), Brown-Sequard (BSS), anterior cord (ACS), posterior cord (PCS), cauda equina (CES), and conus medullaris (CMS).* **They report** that CCS was the most common (44.0%), followed by CES (25.1%) and BSS (17.1%). **They concluded** that patients with ACS and PCS show functional gains with inpatient rehabilitation, with patients with ACS displaying the longest length of stay of the SCI syndromes.
