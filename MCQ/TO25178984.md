Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Carpal Instability

## Question

Which of the following is the defining characteristic of Carpal Instability Non-Dissociative (CIND)?

## Options

1. Trans-scaphoid perilunate dislocations
2. Ligamentous disruption within a single carpal row
3. Instability occurring between carpal rows or between the radius and carpus without intrinsic ligament tear
4. Malunion of the distal radius causing carpal tilt

## Correct option

**3. Instability occurring between carpal rows or between the radius and carpus without intrinsic ligament tear**

## Answer explanation

CIND is characterized by global malalignment (like VISI or DISI) without a tear in the interosseous ligaments (SLIL or LTIL).

Carpal Instability Non-Dissociative (CIND)

Definition

CIND refers to instability occurring between carpal rows (radiocarpal or midcarpal joints) with intact intrinsic interosseous ligaments within each row. The proximal and distal rows are individually intact, but the relationship between the rows is abnormal due to extrinsic (capsular) ligament insufficiency. The pathology lies in the extrinsic ligaments that connect the radius to the proximal row (radiocarpal instability) or the proximal row to the distal row (midcarpal instability).

Midcarpal Instability (The Most Common CIND)

This is the most clinically significant form of CIND, characterised by a "catch-up clunk" — during ulnar deviation, the proximal row suddenly shifts from a VISI (flexed) posture to a reduced (neutral or extended) posture with a painful, palpable clunk as the distal row "catches up" with the proximal row. The pathology involves laxity of the extrinsic ligaments that control midcarpal kinematics, particularly the anterior (palmar) arcuate ligament complex (triquetrohamatocapitate ligament — the arcuate ligament or ligament of Poirier) and the dorsal radiocarpal ligaments.

Clinical Features: Patients present with diffuse wrist pain, a sensation of the wrist "giving way," and the characteristic clunk reproduced by the midcarpal shift test — the examiner stabilises the forearm, applies an axial load to the hand, and passively ulnar deviates the wrist; a sudden, painful, clunking reduction of the proximal row is felt as the carpus shifts from VISI to neutral.

Radiographic Findings: Static radiographs may be normal. Dynamic fluoroscopy is the key investigation — it demonstrates the sudden transition (clunk) of the proximal row from VISI to neutral during ulnar deviation, confirming the dynamic midcarpal instability.

Management:

Non-Operative (First Line): Wrist strengthening and proprioceptive rehabilitation targeting the wrist flexors and extensors (particularly flexor carpi ulnaris and extensor carpi ulnaris, which are dynamic stabilisers of the midcarpal joint). Activity modification, wrist splinting during provocative activities, and NSAIDs. Many patients with midcarpal instability improve with a dedicated rehabilitation programme.

Operative (Refractory Cases): Thermal capsulorrhaphy (arthroscopic thermal shrinkage of the lax midcarpal capsule — controversial due to variable outcomes and risk of capsular necrosis). Open capsular plication/reefing of the lax palmar arcuate ligament or dorsal capsule. Soft tissue reconstruction of the deficient extrinsic ligaments. For severe, refractory midcarpal instability, limited midcarpal arthrodesis (four-corner fusion or triquetrohamate fusion) eliminates the pathological midcarpal motion at the cost of reduced wrist mobility. In rare cases, total wrist arthrodesis is required for intractable symptoms.

Radiocarpal Instability

Less common than midcarpal instability. The entire carpus translates abnormally on the distal radius — either dorsally (dorsal radiocarpal instability, associated with malunited distal radius fractures with loss of volar tilt) or ulnarly (ulnar translocation, where the entire carpus migrates ulnarward on the radius, commonly associated with rheumatoid arthritis or disruption of the radioscaphocapitate ligament).

Management: Dorsal radiocarpal instability secondary to malunion is managed by corrective osteotomy of the distal radius restoring normal volar tilt. Ulnar translocation in RA is managed by radiocarpal arthrodesis or total wrist arthrodesis depending on the extent of articular destruction.

Answer: 3
