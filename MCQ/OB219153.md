## Question

Figure A contains the clinical photo and radiographs of a 20-year-old male involved in a motorcycle crash. He undergoes urgent debridement and application of an external fixator, with subsequent debridements until the remaining soft tissue appears healthy and viable. In planning for definitive fixation and eventual flap soft tissue coverage, which of the following is the strongest predictor of postoperative wound infection?

![[q219153-a-opentibia.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Age
2. Flap failure
3. Presence of temporary external fixation
4. Time from initial debridement to flap coverage
5. Time from definitive fixation to flap coverage

## Correct option

**2. Flap failure**

## Answer explanation

Flap failure is the strongest predictor of postoperative wound infection in the setting of open tibial shaft fractures with severe soft tissue loss requiring flap coverage.

Tibial shaft fractures are the most common long bone fracture, and along with the phalanges of the hand, the most common site of open fractures. Open fractures with soft tissue injury are more often the result of high-energy mechanisms and are associated with an increased risk of complications, including infection, postoperative osteomyelitis, and compartment syndrome. Severe soft tissue injury may require flap coverage; this is ideally performed within 7 days of definitive fixation to minimize the risk of postoperative wound infection. The flap of choice depends on the anatomic location of the soft tissue defect: proximal 1/3 injuries are often treated with a rotational gastrocnemius flap, middle 1/3 with a rotational soleus flap, and distal 1/3 with a free flap. Free flaps may also be used in injuries involving the proximal or middle 1/3 when the gastrocnemius or soleus are damaged or compromised, respectively. Major complications of flap coverage include flap failure and donor site morbidity; notably, flap failure has been shown to be the strongest predictor of wound infection, followed by time to definitive fixation to flap coverage.

*Khundkar published a 2019 review of lower extremity flap coverage following trauma.* *The author reviewed the vascular anatomy, types of flaps, and flap options for various regions of the lower extremity.* A thorough debridement of all necrotic tissue, which may necessitate multiple trips to the operating room, is essential for maximizing the chance of a successful flap and infection-free bony union. The size and nature of the defect must be evaluated by both the orthopaedic and plastic surgery teams once the wound bed is clean and viable.

Coles discussed the initial management of open wounds, flap coverage options, and the timing of definitive coverage in a 2020 review article. Negative pressure wound therapy, while not a substitute for soft tissue coverage, may provide a temporary dressing over large open wounds; however, there is no statistically significant reduction in infection rates compared to conventional soft dressings (WOLFF Study, 2018). Fasciocutaneous flaps may be suitable options for smaller defects not requiring large muscle flaps, and evidence shows equivalent rates of bone healing, limb salvage, and infections between fasciocutaneous and muscle flaps. Finally, the current dictum is to achieve soft tissue coverage within 7 days of definitive orthopaedic fixation to minimize the risk of wound infections. Despite this, there have been challenges in meeting this goal, and the UK has implemented a combined “orthoplastic” team approach, where patients with severe injuries are transferred directly to centers with both services, resulting in earlier coverage and improved outcomes.

*Kuripla et al. conducted a 2020 retrospective analysis of a multicenter database of open tibial fractures requiring flap coverage.* *The authors evaluated the clinical courses between patients who developed infections versus those who did not and performed logistic regression to assess which aspects of their clinical course were significantly associated with increased infection risk.* Multivariate analysis found that flap failure was the strongest predictor of postoperative wound infection, followed by the time from definitive fixation to flap coverage. Interestingly, time to initial antibiotic prophylaxis was not found to be statistically significantly associated with an increased infection risk in this study, although the study’s evaluated parameters were separated into those who received antibiotics in less than versus greater than one hour.

Figure A is a clinical photo and accompanying radiographs of a Gustilo Type III open tibial shaft fracture, with an associated fibular shaft fracture more distally.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 3: age and temporary external fixation are not significant predictors of postoperative wound infection following flap coverage of open fractures.
Answer 4: while univariate analysis found that time from initial debridement to flap coverage was significantly associated with increased infection risk, it is not as strong of a predictor as flap failure.
Answer 5: while both univariate and multivariate analyses found that time from definitive fixation to flap coverage was significantly associated with increased infection risk, it is not as strong of a predictor as flap failure.
