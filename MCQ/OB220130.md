## Question

A 19-year-old male with cerebral palsy presents to your clinic with his parents to establish care. He is wheelchair-bound and requires caregiver assistance for his activities of daily living, but is of appropriate intelligence for his age and able to converse normally during the encounter. He previously underwent an operation on his right wrist; X-rays taken in clinic are shown in Figure A. Physical examination reveals a stiff, rigid wrist positioned in neutral alignment with a well-healed, dorsally based surgical scar. Which of the following was the most likely indication for this procedure?

![[q220130-a-220130-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Floppy, hypotonic wrist with poor neuromuscular control
2. Volkmann angle of 15 degrees with passively extendable digits
3. Supple wrist with ulnar deviation deformity
4. Volkmann angle of 90 degrees with palmar wounds
5. GMFCS V disease

## Correct option

**4. Volkmann angle of 90 degrees with palmar wounds**

## Answer explanation

This patient has GMFCS V cerebral palsy and previously underwent a proximal row carpectomy with wrist fusion, which is indicated in the setting of a severe wrist contracture with a fingers-in-palm deformity, which can lead to palmar wounds at risk of infection (Answer 4).

Upper extremity deformities in the setting of spastic neuromuscular disease present with a spectrum of physical exam findings, from supple wrists with mild deformity to stiff, contracted wrists with concomitant digital deformities. Severe finger contractures can present as a fingers-in-palm deformity, making nail trimming difficult and leading to wounds in the palm that are at increased risk of infection. In severe, rigid wrist contractures with palmar wounds, the goal is to get the fingers out of the palm. This is best achieved by shortening the carpus through a proximal row carpectomy, fusing the wrist, and transferring the FDS to the FDP.

*Rayan and Young published a 1999 case series on arthrodesis of the spastic wrist.* *Nine patients (11 wrists) who had wrist arthrodeses for severe spastic flexion contracture were evaluated, where the etiology of the spasticity was due to cerebral palsy, traumatic head injury, or cerebrovascular accident.* A standardized hand function questionnaire was used to determine functional improvement following surgery. The objective evaluation included clinical evidence of fusion, skin condition, wrist position, and radiographic assessment. **The authors found** that improved appearance, hygiene, and a certain degree of upper extremity function, regardless of cognitive abilities, can be expected following arthrodesis for severe spastic wrist deformity.

*Van Heest and Strothman published a 2009 case series on wrist arthrodesis in cerebral palsy.* *They examined 34 patients (41 wrists) with severe spastic wrist flexion deformities who were treated by wrist arthrodesis using a dorsal plating technique.* **The authors reported** a high union rate (98%) and a high rate of satisfaction (94%). Hardware complications were common, and consequently, they routinely recommend hardware removal.

*Peraut et al. published a 2018 case series on the results and complications of FDS-to-FDP tendon transfer, with the intent of evaluating the effectiveness of this technique for correcting these deformities.* *All 26 patients had a class V hand (severe clenched-fist deformity with zero pulp-to-palm distance) preoperatively.* *Postoperatively, 10 patients had a class I hand and 12 had a class II hand with satisfactory appearance.* Three patients had a class III hand and one had a class IV hand. None of the patients had a class V hand postoperatively. Superficialis-to-profundus tendon transfer (STP) provides satisfactory outcomes in terms of hand opening, with some patients also experiencing improved hand function.

Figure A consists of AP and lateral radiographs of a wrist that has previously undergone a proximal row carpectomy and wrist fusion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This is not an indication for proximal row carpectomy and wrist fusion.
Answer 2: A Volkmann angle of 15 degrees is a mild wrist contracture, which may be managed with FCU or FCR lengthening.
Answer 3: A supple wrist with an ulnar deviation deformity may be treated with an FCU to ECRB (Green) tendon transfer, not proximal row carpectomy and wrist fusion.
Answer 5: GMFCS V disease by itself is not an indication for proximal row carpectomy and wrist fusion.
