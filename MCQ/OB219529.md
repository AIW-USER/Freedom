## Question

You are discussing an algorithm for treating shoulder instability during a lecture with a group of orthopedic residents. You discuss different treatment options which would include bankart labral repair, remplissage, and latarjet. In which of the following patients would a latarjet be the most appropriate form of definitive treatment?

## Options

1. 20-year-old wide receiver with bankart lesion, small Hill-Sachs lesion and no glenoid bone loss
2. 33-year-old recreational sand volleyball player with bankart lesion, small Hill-Sachs lesion and 10% glenoid bone loss
3. 18-year-old linebacker with bankart lesion, large Hill-Sachs lesion and 6% glenoid bone loss
4. 23-year old professional rugby player with bankart lesion, small Hill-Sachs lesion and 15% bone loss
5. 16-year-old swimmer with circumferential labral tearing, no Hill-Sachs lesion and no glenoid bone loss

## Correct option

**4. 23-year old professional rugby player with bankart lesion, small Hill-Sachs lesion and 15% bone loss**

## Answer explanation

A relatively young, contact athlete with 15% bone loss is an indication to perform a bone block procedure such as a latarjet as the risk for recurrence is high and the risk for failure is high with a soft-tissue-only procedure.

Traumatic shoulder instability is incredibly common, especially in a young and athletic population. The topic has been multiply studied with the main risk factors including young age (<20) and involvement in competitive, contact sports. Once a shoulder dislocates, younger patients who return to these activities are at very high risk of sustaining another dislocation. Other factors to consider are the degree of glenoid bone loss on imaging (CT/MRI), as well as the size of the Hill-Sachs lesion, and the severity of the labral/capsular injury. The degree of bone loss can be quantified on advanced imaging but should also be appreciated on the Grashey and axillary radiographs. Typically, if bone loss is seen on the axillary view, it is fairly significant already. There is still some argument about what is considered "critical," bone loss with some defining this as little as 13.5% of the glenoid diameter. Ultimately, the whole clinical picture must be considered before making a treatment plan. Bone block procedures such as the latarjet should be considered in the setting of critical bone loss or in patients with multiple risk factors for dislocation in addition to borderline glenoid bone loss. Enlarging the glenoid track allows the distance it takes for the humeral head to engage to increase, thus reducing the risk of recurrent instability.

*Schrouff and Verlaan compared bankart repair with remplissage to the latarjet procedure with regards to outcomes, range of motion, and recurrence rates.* **They noted** that more of the patients undergoing revision surgery were allocated to the latarjet group. The risk for recurrent instability was similar between the two groups in Hill-Sachs lesions with depth <10mm but the latarjet had better external rotation than the remplissage group.

*Gouveia et al. compared bankart repair + remplissage to bone block augmentation for bipolar bone loss lesions.* **They note** that in cases with subcritical bone loss, recurrent rates were similar between the two groups (5-13%), while in the group with 10-15% bone loss, there was an increased rate of instability with soft tissue repair (6-13%) compared to bone block augmentation (0-8%). This said, complication rates were lower overall in the soft-tissue-only group. Their takeaway message was that in patients with glenoid bone loss >10%, a bone block procedure should at least be considered.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Bankart repair +/- remplissage would be the most appropriate line of treatment for a young, contact athlete with a Hill-Sachs lesion and no glenoid bone loss.
Answer 2: For a recreational athlete at 33 years old, the most appropriate treatment would be bankart repair +/- remplissage. While 10% bone loss could be considered a relative indication for a bone block procedure in a young contact athlete, this patient would not meet those indications.
Answer 3: Despite being a young football player who is a linebacker, this patient has a low degree of glenoid bone loss and a large Hill-Sachs lesion which would likely benefit from remplissage or a humeral head allograft depending on the exact size rather than a bony glenoid procedure.
Answer 5: The high school swimmer with a circumferential labral tear and no bone injury on the humeral or glenoid side would be indicated for repair rather than bone block transfer.
