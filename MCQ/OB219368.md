## Question

A 32-year-old male presents two weeks after a tackle-related injury while playing recreational flag football. He states he has felt pain in this thumb since, particularly with performing pinching maneuvers and grasping objects. On physical examination, there is a palpable nodule along the ulnar aspect of the thumb. An MRI demonstrates that his thumb ulnar collateral ligament is torn from its distal insertion and retracted proximally (Figure 1). Which of the following structures is preventing the ulnar collateral ligament from reducing back to its native insertion?

![[q219368-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Anterior oblique ligament
2. Adductor aponeurosis
3. Volar plate
4. Flexor pollicis longus tendon
5. Lumbrical musculature

## Correct option

**2. Adductor aponeurosis**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an acute thumb ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injury with a palpable mass consistent with a Stener lesion. This occurs secondary to UCL entrapment by the adductor aponeurosis (Answer 2), preventing the reduction of the UCL’s insertion along the proximal phalanx.

Thumb UCL injuries may be acute (i.e. “Skiers thumb”) or chronic (i.e. “Gamekeeper’s thumb) and vary in severity from mild sprains to complete avulsions. In uninjured thumbs, the adductor aponeurosis blankets dorsally over the UCL. In high-energy acute injuries, the UCL may avulse from its insertion on the proximal phalanx and may retract far enough proximally to slip out from under the adductor aponeurosis and reorient superficially (Illustration A). This creates the classic “Stener lesion” which is the balled-up, retracted UCL along the proximal edge of the adductor aponeurosis that is often palpable on physical exam. It is highly associated with persistent instability and poor outcomes when treated with conservative management, and therefore Stener lesions should be treated with operative open reduction and UCL repair to its native insertion.

*Tang and colleagues provided a review article regarding the management of thumb metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint UCL injuries.* **The authors emphasize** that the diagnosis can be made clinically via physical exam with instability and increased laxity appreciated when the proximal phalanx is radially deviated. They mention that radiographs may be obtained to evaluate for any osseous avulsion injuries, however, MRI serves as the optimal imaging modality for detecting ligamentous injuries. Given that diagnostic imaging results do not always correlate well with physical examination findings, **the authors conclude** that treatment ultimately depends on the clinical degree of stability, presence of osseous displacement, patient activity level, and/or presence of a Stener lesion.

*Lark and colleagues provided a review article with a focus on Stener lesions, historical perspectives, and appropriate diagnostic imaging.* *They describe that early surgeons believed a Stener lesion could only be diagnosed intraoperatively with direct visualization.* However, with the widespread use of ultrasound and MRI, Stener lesions can now be established with high diagnostic accuracy without the need for intraoperative assessment. **The authors conclude** that the general contemporary consensus mandates operative intervention for patients presenting with Stener lesions.

Figure A demonstrates a coronal MRI imaging of a thumb MCP joint in which the UCL has retracted and balled up proximally (i.e. Stener lesion). Illustration A demonstrates the anatomic etiology of Stener lesions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The anterior oblique (“beak”) ligament spans the carpometacarpal (CMC) joint between the volar aspect of the trapezium and 1st metacarpal. Bennett fractures of the thumb base often demonstrate a volar fragment of the metacarpal that remains anchored in place by the anterior oblique ligament of the thumb. Further, attenuation of the anterior oblique ligament is implicated in basilar thumb arthritis or thumb CMC joint instability after dislocation.
Answer 3&4: In the setting of thumb MCP joint dorsal dislocations, the volar plate, sesamoid bones, or flexor pollicis longus tendons can become blocks to reduction. Due to their more volar/central location along the joint surface, the volar plate and flexor pollicis longus have not been implicated as potential blocks to UCL reduction.
Answer 5: In the setting of MCP joint dorsal dislocations involving the 2-5 digits, the lumbricals can wrap dorsally around the metacarpal head and serve as a block to reduction. However, given the lack of lumbrical musculature attachments to the thumb, they have not been implicated as potential blocks to reducing the UCL.
