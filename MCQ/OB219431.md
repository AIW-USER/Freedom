## Question

A 32-year-old orthopedic surgery resident presents to your office complaining of ulnar-sided wrist pain. It bothers him when he is operating, specifically when he is suturing. He does not recall any specific injuries. After examining him, you diagnose him with a subluxating extensor carpi ulnaris. Which of the following physical examination findings was most likely seen to come to this diagnosis?

## Options

1. Pain with movement of the wrist from ulnar to radial deviation with the wrist in slight extension
2. Pain with flexion and supination of the wrist
3. Pain with axial loading with the wrist in maximum ulnar deviation
4. Pain to palpation in the soft spot between ulnar styloid and flexor carpi ulnaris
5. Instability with piano key testing

## Correct option

**2. Pain with flexion and supination of the wrist**

## Answer explanation

Extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) subluxation occurs in the setting of ECU subsheath injury where the tendon has no restraint with supination of the forearm.

The ECU tendon functions within the 6th dorsal compartment and ECU subsheath. In cases where attenuation or rupture of this subsheath occurs, there is no restraint preventing movement of the tendon with flexion and supination of the wrist and patients may describe a snapping sensation as the tendon subluxates over the dorsal wrist. It will typically reduce if the forearm is brought back into a pronated position. This can eventually lead to tendonitis of the ECU tendon. First-line management is conservative with the arm immobilized in slight pronation to allow for healing of the soft tissues. If this is unsuccessful, ECU subsheath reconstruction can be performed to address the instability component.

*MacLennan et al. reviewed the diagnosis and anatomic reconstruction of ECU subluxation.* *They discuss a newer method of ECU sheath reconstruction using suture anchors which was performed on 21 patients with persistent subluxation of the ECU tendon.* **They noted** a significant improvement in wrist range of motion, grip strength, pain, patient satisfaction, and outcome scores using this technique.

Garcia-Elias describes the anatomy of the ECU and the 6th dorsal compartment which is made of a fibro-osseous tunnel and ECU subsheath. In some settings, ECU tendinopathy can occur with thickening of the tendon due to overuse and in other cases, the subsheath can rupture which allows the ECU to sublux in a volar direction. They go on to discuss nonoperative and operative treatment strategies for both of these issues.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes Watson's test, which, when positive is indicative of an injury to the scapholunate ligament.
Answer 3: Axial loading and maximum ulnar deviation would cause pain in cases of ulnar styloid impingement or ulnocarpal abutment syndrome but is typically not as symptomatic in cases of snapping ECU syndrome.
Answer 4: Tenderness to palpation over the ulnar soft spot is indicative of injury to the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC). The ECU is located just dorsal to this location and may not be as tender to direct palpation.
Answer 5: Piano key testing involves shucking the distal radius and ulna to evaluate for instability that would be indicative of injury to the distal radial ulnar joint (DRUJ)
