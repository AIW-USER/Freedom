## Question

A 16-year-old male competitive soccer player presents with acute anterior hip pain after forcefully kicking a ball during a game. He felt a sudden “pop” and immediate pain, followed by difficulty walking. Physical exam reveals localized tenderness over the anterior pelvic crest and pain with resisted hip flexion. Radiographs demonstrate a minimally displaced avulsion fracture of the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS). What is the next best step in treatment?

## Options

1. Corticosteroid injection at the avulsion site
2. Early aggressive strengthening and return-to-play program
3. MRI of the pelvis to further characterize the injury before treatment
4. Open reduction and internal fixation of the avulsed fragment
5. Rest, activity modification, and physical therapy

## Correct option

**5. Rest, activity modification, and physical therapy**

## Answer explanation

The first line of treatment for an AIIS avulsion, which is the origin of the rectus femoris, is rest with activity modification and physical therapy.

Rectus femoris avulsion injuries at the anterior inferior iliac spine most commonly occur in adolescents due to the relative weakness of the apophysis compared with the musculotendinous unit during forceful contraction. In cases with minimal displacement, the standard of care is nonoperative management consisting of rest, protected weight–bearing as needed, NSAIDs for pain control, and a gradual, supervised physical therapy program. The majority of patients treated conservatively experience excellent outcomes with predictable healing and full return to sport.

Surgical intervention is generally reserved for specific indications such as significantly displaced fragments (commonly >2 cm), painful nonunion, symptomatic heterotopic ossification, or high-level athletes with persistent functional limitations despite adequate nonoperative treatment. In an acute, minimally displaced injury in a young patient, conservative management minimizes surgical risk while providing reliable recovery.

*Knapik et al. reviewed the diagnosis, treatment options, and outcomes of proximal rectus femoris avulsion injuries and ruptures in skeletally mature, high-level athletes.* **The authors found** that both nonoperative and operative treatments can yield good outcomes, but surgical management was associated with faster return to play and lower recurrence rates in elite athletes with complete avulsions or retracted tears. **They concluded** that treatment should be individualized, with operative intervention favored in high-demand athletes or those with significant tendon retraction.

*Zini and Panasci conducted a study to assess clinical outcomes following arthroscopic excision of post-traumatic heterotopic ossification of the rectus femoris.* **The authors found** significant improvements in pain, hip range of motion, and functional scores at 2-year follow-up, with no major complications or recurrences reported. **They concluded** that arthroscopic treatment is a safe and effective option for symptomatic post-traumatic rectus femoris ossifications refractory to conservative management.

*Ryan et al. conducted an anatomic study to define the precise origin and footprint of the direct and reflected heads of the rectus femoris.* **The authors found** that the direct head originates from the anterior inferior iliac spine, while the reflected head originates from the supra-acetabular groove and anterior hip capsule, with a consistent and reproducible anatomic relationship. **They concluded** that understanding this dual-origin anatomy is critical for accurate diagnosis, surgical repair, and arthroscopic management of rectus femoris injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Corticosteroid injections are contraindicated in acute avulsion fractures as they may impair healing and do not address the underlying bony injury.
Answer 2: Early aggressive strengthening is not appropriate, as this risks fragment displacement, delayed union, and prolonged symptoms.
Answer 3: Plain radiographs are sufficient to diagnose and dictate treatment strategies for the vast majority of acute avulsion fractures.
Answer 4: Open reduction and internal fixation of the avulsed fragment is not indicated for minimally displaced rectus femoris avulsion injuries in adolescents without functional compromise or nonunion.
