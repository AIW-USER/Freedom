## Question

A 27-year-old male presents to the trauma bay at your institution after a dirt bike accident in which he was thrown off and landed on his left side. He initially reports left-sided chest and ipsilateral shoulder pain. The trauma team sends him to the CT scanner before you can evaluate him. On his initial scans, you diagnose a left glenoid neck fracture. Which of the following concomitant injuries, if present, would be the strongest indication for orthopedic intervention in this patient?

## Options

1. Left distal clavicle fracture
2. Left brachial plexus injury
3. Right pubic rami and sacral ala fractures
4. Pneumothorax
5. Left 5th and 6th rib fractures

## Correct option

**1. Left distal clavicle fracture**

## Answer explanation

Concomitant ipsilateral fractures of the glenoid neck and clavicle (Answer 1) are termed a "floating shoulder," and represent a relative indication for operative stabilization.

Scapular fractures are often caused by high-energy trauma and are commonly associated with other bony, pulmonary, or neurologic injuries. The shoulder girdle is a complex articulation of bones and ligaments which is best defined as the superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC). The SSSC is composed of the distal clavicle, acromion, coracoid, glenoid and their associated ligamentous connections. With a disruption in two of these structures, the SSSC is said to be disrupted which some have termed a "floating shoulder." A floating shoulder is a relative indication for operative intervention in order to stabilize the shoulder girdle and allow for improved shoulder function. In most cases, the clavicle is chosen for stabilization as the approach and fixation are easier. However, either or both bones may be stabilized if other indications are met, such as excessive scapular medialization or alterations to the glenopolar angle.

*Cole et al. reviewed the management of scapular fractures,* **noting** that most scapular fractures can be treated nonoperatively with early motion and pain control. However, a better understanding of specific scapular fractures that may benefit from surgery is helping to shape operative indications. **They note** that accurate physical examination and imaging, including a 3D-CT, can help to best identify those who might benefit from operative intervention.

*DeFranco and Patterson reviewed the floating shoulder,* **noting** that it is a combination of bony and ligamentous disruption. **They note** that the degree of ligamentous injury and displacement drive the need for fixation. In the setting of a concomitant clavicle and scapular neck fracture, **they recommend** clavicular fixation, as this helps restore the contour of the shoulder and may even indirectly reduce the scapular fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Brachial plexus injury can occur in the setting of a scapula fracture but resolve about 75% of the time and usually does not require operative intervention.
Answer 3: An LC-1 pelvic fracture would not represent an indication for surgery in the setting of a concomitant glenoid neck fracture, as this is a stable pelvic ring injury through which the patient can bear weight.
Answer 4: Pneumothorax can occur with scapular fractures and may require chest tube placement, but does not dictate bony fixation.
Answer 5: Rib fractures at two levels with a concomitant glenoid neck fracture would not meet the criteria for operative intervention. Flail segments, noted by three or more consecutive rib fractures anteriorly and posteriorly, may be indicated for fixation.
