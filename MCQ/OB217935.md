## Question

Figure A is the AP and lateral radiograph, respectively, of a 45-year-old farmer who presented to your clinic after a fall onto an outstretched hand five days ago. He notes "a bit" of elbow pain, and has had difficulty performing his work duties. The patient has a positive gravity-assisted varus stress test, and the patient states that his elbow "sounds like crispy rice cereal" when he moves it. You bring the patient to the operating room and perform open reduction and internal fixation of the fracture shown. If the elbow remains unstable, which of the following is the insertion of the ligament you should next address surgically?

![[q217935-a-amf-coronoid.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Anteroinferior aspect of medial epicondyle
2. Crista supinatoris of proximal ulna
3. Radial tuberosity
4. Sublime tubercle of the ulna
5. Tip of the coronoid process

## Correct option

**2. Crista supinatoris of proximal ulna**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a fracture of the anteromedial facet of the coronoid process and has a physical exam concerning for posteromedial rotatory instability (PMRI). If the elbow is still unstable following fixation of the anteromedial facet of the coronoid process, the next best step is to repair the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL), which inserts on the crista supinatoris of the proximal ulna (Answer 2).

Fractures of the coronoid process can occur secondary to a wide range of elbow trauma. These injuries can be classified through two classification schema: The Regan and Morrey (Illustration A) and the O'Driscoll (Illustration B). Regan and Morrey classify these injuries based on the anteroposterior height of the fracture, whereas the O'Driscoll system subdivides these injuries based on the location and number of coronoid fragments. Coronoid tip fractures classically occur in the setting of a terrible triad injury; failure to properly treat this injury pattern can lead to the development of posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI). If not treated properly, anteromedial facet fractures can lead to the development of posteromedial rotatory instability (PMRI). Patients with these injury patterns typically complain of crepitus with range of motion, secondary to joint incongruity. Continued instability following ORIF of anteromedial coronoid facet fractures is typically due to lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) injury. Treatment of LUCL injury is typically repair in the acute setting, and reconstruction or internal-joint stabilizer (IJS) in the chronic setting.

Doornberg and Ring provide a case series of patients with an anteromedial facet coronoid fracture. Their study reported on 18 patients; 12 with acute injuries and 6 with chronic injuries. The authors note that this injury pattern is distinct from a terrible triad type injury and that patients with this injury pattern are at risk for developing PMRI. Furthermore, the authors note that 15/18 (83.33%) of patients had an associated LUCL injury. Treatment for these patients is ORIF of the coronoid with repair versus reconstruction of the LUCL.

*Wyrick et al. provide a comprehensive review of complex elbow dislocations (i.e. those with an associated fracture).* **The authors note** the importance of fully identifying all injured structures, and highlight that these injuries are fraught with chronic instability, posttraumatic arthrosis, and poor functional outcomes. **They also emphasize** the importance of elucidating a clear clinical history, as the various injury patterns are associated with unique patterns of injury, which can help to drive treatment.

Karbach and Elfar provide a comprehensive review of elbow instability. They note that PMRI usually occurs secondary to an axial and valgus load through a pronated forearm, and that injury to the LUCL is common. They also note that isolated injury to the MUCL is rare, as the medial-sided failure occurs at the coronoid. They note that the gravity-assisted varus stress test (In which the patient’s arm is abducted to 90° in neutral rotation as the elbow is flexed and extended) is the most sensitive and specific maneuver in diagnosing PMRI.
