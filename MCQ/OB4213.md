## Question

A 22-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by ambulance after a motor vehicle collision. He denies any subjective weakness in his arms or legs, and only complains of back pain. Examination reveals no palpable spinal step-offs, posterior spinal pain, and normal neurologic function in the lower extremities. Normal perineal sensation and normal rectal tone are present. A CT scan is performed and is shown in Figures A and B. An MRI is performed and shows no signal intensity in the posterior ligamentous complex on T2 weighted images. What is the most appropriate next step in treatment.

## Options

1. External orthosis
2. Percutaneous vertebroplasty
3. Anterior corpectomy and arthrodesis
4. Posterior instrumented arthrodesis
5. Posterior decompression and instrumented arthrodesis

## Correct option

**1. External orthosis**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a L2 burst fracture with a Thoracolumbar Injury Classification System (TLICS) score of 2 (morphology = 2, neurologic status = 0, PLC = 0). Therefore, nonoperative treatment with ambulation and an external orthosis is the most appropriate treatment.

Indications for surgical treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures are controversial. Historical indications of local kyphosis of > 30 degrees and loss of vertebral height of > 50% are no longer considered indications for surgery. The TLICS focuses on the morphology of the fracture, the neurologic status of the patient, and the integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) as the primary variable to dictate treatment. In this scoring system, if the PLC is scored as intact, suspect/indeterminant, or disrupted. The TLICS has been found to contribute to improved interobserver agreement on management of thoracolumar spinal trauma.

*Wood et al. performed the first randomized control trial comparing operative versus nonoperative treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures in patients with no neurological deficits.* **They found** no major long-term advantages with operative treatment, and increased disability and complications with operative treatment.

*Wood et al. reviewed the same cohort from their 2003 study at long-term follow-up (16-22 years).* **They found** those with a stable burst fracture who were treated nonoperatively reported actually less pain and better function compared with those who were treated surgically.

*Agus et al.* **found** that there was not correlation between clinical outcome and the radiographic parameters of local kyphosis angle, vertebral body height, and canal encroachment (CE). **They conclude** that these parameters should not be used for an indication of surgery.

*Patel et al. report the TLICS was developed to define injury based on three clinical characteristics: injury morphology, integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex, and neurologic status of the patient.* Point values are assigned to each major category based on injury severity. The sum of these points represents the TLICS severity score, which may be used to guide treatment. The injury scores are totaled to produce a management grade that is, in turn, used to guide treatment. A score of >4 suggests the need for surgical treatment because of significant instability, whereas a score of <4 suggests nonsurgical management.

Figures A and B are axial and sagittal CT scans showing a burst fracture with some central canal stenosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Vertebroplasty is not recommended for management of thoracolumbar burst fractures.
*Answers 3, 4, and 5: Surgical treatment, either through an anterior or posterior approach, has been shown by Wood and associates to result in increased pain and disability and is therefore not indicated in this setting.* Additionally, there is no need for decompression in the setting of a neurologically intact patient.
