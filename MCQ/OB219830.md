## Question

A 21-year-old professional rock climber and free soloist presents with complaints of intense volar forearm pain, tightness, and paresthesias for the last two weeks after climbing only 20 meters. After resting for 30 minutes, he feels completely back to normal but is concerned about his grip strength during climbing and no longer feels safe free-soloing. Which of the following is the best next step in management?

## Options

1. CT angiogram of the upper extremity
2. EMG and nerve conduction studies of the upper extremity
3. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the upper extremity
4. Referral to vascular surgery for formal angiography of the upper extremity
5. Simulated grip strength activity followed by forearm compartment pressure measurements

## Correct option

**5. Simulated grip strength activity followed by forearm compartment pressure measurements**

## Answer explanation

The patient is exhibiting signs and symptoms of exertional forearm compartment syndrome, which is best evaluated with dynamic forearm compartment pressure measurements.

Though the forearm is less commonly involved than the lower leg, sports and activities that involve repetitive gripping are often involved. The diagnosis is typically made based on the clinical history, which demonstrates exercise-induced pain, cramping, and tightness of the affected extremity, which improves with rest and refraining from inciting activity. Chronic exertional forearm compartment syndrome can be confirmed using intracompartmental pressure measurements before, during, and after the activity. If non-operative treatment fails, including rest/refraining from the inciting activity, fasciotomy can be considered.

*García-Mata et al. review chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) of the forearm in adolescents.* **The authors note** that CECS is a well-known process but rarely occurs in the forearm. **They conclude** that a high index of suspicion is necessary to diagnose it, that diagnosis is based on symptoms and ICP measurements, and that surgical treatment involving isolated decompression of the superficial volar compartment is safe and effective.

*Liu et al. reviewed chronic exertional compartment syndrome in athletes.* **The authors note** that in the upper extremity, this condition most commonly affects athletes during sports requiring repetitive and vigorous gripping, such as rowers. In addition to clinical history and examination, common methods used to aid diagnosis include compartment pressure measurements, magnetic resonance imaging, and near-infrared spectroscopy. **They conclude** that when symptoms persist despite conservative treatment, multiple operative techniques have been described as treatment, including open, mini-open, and endoscopic release of involved compartments.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: Though MRI, vascular workup, and EMG/NCS may be indicated if additional pathology, including vascular aneurysm or nerve entrapment, is suspected, dynamic compartment pressure measurements are more appropriate workup for suspected chronic exertional compartment syndrome.
