## Options

1. Physeal bridge resection and fat interposition through a metaphyseal window
2. Corrective osteotomy, with physeal bridge resection and fat interposition through the osteotomy site
3. Transarticular arthroscopically-assisted physeal bridge resection and fat interposition through the ankle joint
4. Completion of epiphysiodesis
5. Non-operative treatment

## Correct option

**2. Corrective osteotomy, with physeal bridge resection and fat interposition through the osteotomy site**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a central physeal bridge with significant angular deformity. Presence of a physeal bridge <50% of physeal area in a patient with at least 2 years or 2cm of growth is an indication for physeal bridge resection. In addition, angular deformity >10-20degrees is an indication for osteotomy as the deformity will not correct spontaneously after bridge resection.

Physeal bridges commonly occur after Salter-Harris (SH) physeal fractures in early adolescence when the physis is thickest and the cartilage is weakest. Small (<25% of physeal area), central bridges have a better prognosis. There are 3 types of physeal bridges: (1) Peripheral bridges cause angular deformity and may be amendable to excision. (2) Elongated bridges involve involve the middle of the physis and are most commonly caused by SH III or IV fractures. (3) Central bridges have a perimeter of healthy physis but act as a central tether and can tent/distort the articular surface. When bar resection is performed, interposition options includes fat, cranioplast, bone wax, cartilage, muscle and silicone.

*Khoshhal et al. reviewed physeal bridge resection.* Most arise from SH II fractures, although unreduced SH IV fractures have the greatest potential to form a physeal bridge. Complete physeal arrest is addressed with contralateral epiphysiodesis or ipsilateral limb lengthening. Partial bridges are addressed with bridge resection where possible (<50%), or completion of epiphysiodesis (with/without contralateral epiphysiodesis) if not (>50%, complicated bridge).

*Kang et al. described the resection of a physeal bridge under computer-assisted guidance and* **showed** that it facilitated precise bar resection through a minimal incision.

*Marsh et al. described the use of an arthroscope in central physeal bridge excision.* The arthroscope is introduced through a metaphyeal bone window. They achieved excellent results in 70%, good results in 17%, and poor results in 13% of the patients.

Figures A through C demonstrate a central physeal bridge occupying 14.4% of physeal area. Illustration A demonstrates the types of partial physeal bridges. Illustrations B through D are various approaches to central bridges (B, a metaphyseal window; C, metaphyseal osteotomy; D, pre-drilled metaphyseal wedge).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Physeal bridge resection alone is insufficient as there is significant (>20deg) angular deformity.
Answer 3: Arthroscopically-assisted resection is possible, but the arthroscope is introduced through a metaphyseal window. The physeal bridge is not visible from inside the joint.
Answer 4: Completion of epiphyseodesis is indicated if bridge resection is not possible (>50%, complicated bridge). He has a small (<25%) central bridge which is easily resected.
Answer 5: Non-operative treatment will lead to unsatisfactory results as he has >2yrs of growth left (up to 16yrs in boys, 14yrs in girls).
