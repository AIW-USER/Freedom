## Question

## Options

1. The procedure shown in Figure E provides a superior complication profile compared to the procedure shown in Figure F.
2. The procedure shown in Figure F is indicated given the rotator cuff tearing seen on the pre-operative CT scan.
3. The procedure shown in Figure E provides better post-operative range of motion than the procedure shown in Figure F.
4. The procedure shown in Figure E is not indicated given the degree of retroversion on her pre-operative CT scan.
5. The procedure shown in Figure F provides better functional and patient-reported outcomes than the procedure shown in Figure E.

## Correct option

**3. The procedure shown in Figure E provides better post-operative range of motion than the procedure shown in Figure F.**

## Answer explanation

The patient has evidence of primary glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis, for which anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) has been shown to provide better postoperative range of motion (Figure E, Answer 3).

Patients with primary glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis have a very low rate (<5%) of full-thickness rotator cuff tears. Because of this, many can be treated with an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), as compared to a reverse (RSA) total shoulder arthroplasty, allowing for preservation of range of motion with intact rotator cuff force couples. This trade-off, however, results in a less constrained articulation that depends on proper functioning of the soft tissues, especially the subscapularis, and leads to greater shear stresses across the glenoid, making it more susceptible to mechanical loosening over time. In general, both RSA and TSA are indicated for osteoarthritis, but TSA has been shown to provide better postoperative range of motion with revision rates and functional outcomes being similar. Contraindications to performing TSA include insufficient glenoid bone stock (excessive retroversion), rotator cuff arthropathy, deltoid dysfunction, and irreparable rotator cuff tearing.

*Kim et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis looking at whether reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) is more advantageous than anatomic TSA for osteoarthritis with intact cuff tendons.* *The authors reviewed 1,061 studies, six of which met the inclusion criteria, and found that range of motion, especially external rotation, was better for aTSA than for RSA, with functional scores and overall revision rates showing no difference between aTSA and RSA at mid-term follow-up.* **They concluded** that better ROM was achieved after aTSA than after RSA, while there was no difference in the revision rates at mid-term follow-up between aTSA and rTSA.

*Schaller et al. published a retrospective cohort study comparing the range of movement and patient-reported outcomes between anatomical total shoulder arthroplasty and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty in matched cohorts over the age of 70 years at a single institution between 2011 and 2015.* *The authors included 75 patients and found at one year that range of movement metrics in flexion, abduction, and external rotation all demonstrated greater improvements in the anatomical total shoulder arthroplasty cohort in comparison to the reverse total shoulder arthroplasty cohort.* **They concluded** that anatomical total shoulder arthroplasty demonstrates a significant improvement in range of movement in comparison to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, while no statistical difference in functional assessment scores between reverse shoulder replacement and anatomical shoulder replacement exists between matched elderly patient cohorts.

Figures A-D are AP radiographs, and coronal, axial, and 3D reconstruction CT scan images, respectively, of a shoulder with primary glenohumeral joint arthritis showing subchondral sclerosis and cyst formation within the glenoid with minimal retroversion, large inferior humeral "goat's beard" osteophyte, and evidence of well-centered glenohumeral articulation with an intact rotator cuff. Figures E and F are post-operative images of an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty and a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, respectively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Complication profiles are similar between anatomical TSA and RSA.
Answer 2: Though some may consider performing primary RSA for primary glenohumeral arthritis, this is controversial; there is also no evidence of rotator cuff tear on the CT scan.
Answer 4: There is minimal retroversion evident on the CT scan that would not preclude performing an anatomical TSA.
Answer 5: Anatomical and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty show similar functional and patient-reported outcomes in matched cohort studies, as above.
