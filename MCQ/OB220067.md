## Question

A 37-year-old oil field worker presents to the clinic for a follow-up of his right lateral epicondylitis. He has a history of left lateral epicondylitis that failed nonoperative treatment, including steroid injection, and underwent left open extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) debridement and release. He is nervous that the right side will require surgical intervention as well. Given his persistent symptoms despite activity modification, he undergoes a platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection at the lateral epicondyle through the common extensor origin. What is the expected outcome of this patient compared to his prior contralateral injection?

## Options

1. Equivalent patient-reported outcomes at one year compared to the steroid injection
2. Quicker return to work with the PRP injection
3. Decreased pain scores 6 weeks post-injection compared to the prior steroid injection
4. Decreased pain scores at 12 months with the PRP injection
5. Improved strength and functional outcomes at 12 months compared to the steroid injection

## Correct option

**4. Decreased pain scores at 12 months with the PRP injection**

## Answer explanation

PRP injections for lateral epicondylitis are proposed to enhance tendon healing. Several high-quality studies have shown that they improve patient-reported outcomes and decrease pain at 12 months compared to steroid injections (answer choice 4).

Lateral epicondylitis is an overuse injury that results in tendinosis at the origin over the extensor carpi radialis brevis. Initial management involves activity modification, bracing, and NSAIDs. PRP is an autologous blood-derived product that is proposed to enhance and promote a healing response within the tendon origin. There are various ways to prepare PRP; however, the two general categories are leukocyte-enriched and leukocyte-depleted. Steroid injections have been used in the past to treat lateral epicondylitis; however, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) between steroids and placebo found equivalent pain, grip strength, and patient-reported outcomes.

*Peerbooms et al. published the one-year results of a randomized double-blind study comparing corticosteroid and PRP injections for the treatment of lateral epicondylitis.* *A total of 100 patients (PRP=51, steroid=49) were randomized and followed for 1 year.* Although the steroid group showed early benefits, these results declined. At one year, 49% of steroid patients and 73% of PRP patients had a 25% reduction in pain scores.

*Gosens et al published the 2-year results of the Peerbooms et al trial referenced in the preceding paragraph.* At 2 years 21% of steroid patients and 65% of PRP patients had a 25% reduction in pain scores. The patient-reported outcomes measures (PROM) scores of the PRP group significantly improved while the steroid group returned to baseline.

*Niemiec et al published a meta-analysis of 26 studies that involved PRP injections for lateral epicondylitis.* After PRP injection, all PROM scores improved with time. The minimally clinically important difference was exceeded in each of the reported PROM scores at nearly all time points. **The authors recommend** PRP as an effective treatment for lateral epicondylitis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: PRP injections show a statistically significant decrease in pain scores and PROMS when compared to corticosteroid injections.
Answer 2: There is no high-level evidence that shows a quicker return to work with either injection.
Answer 3: PRP does not show a decrease in pain scores when compared to steroid injection at the 6-week time frame. In fact, some studies show steroids’ pain control outperforms PRP in the short term.
Answer 5: PRP injections show a statistically significant decrease in pain scores and PROMS when compared to corticosteroid injections, not strength or functional outcomes.
