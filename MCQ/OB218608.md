## Question

A 20-year-old male football player is playing defensive back when he makes a tackle which leads to his head subsequently striking the ground. You are the sole physician on the field and you are asked by the coach whether the player can return to the game or if he needs to be further evaluated. Which of the following signs/symptoms would warrant emergent transport to the emergency department for further imaging, physical examination and monitoring?

## Options

1. Transient numbness in left upper extremity that resolves after several minutes
2. No loss of consciousness and normal cognitive examination with residual headache
3. Mild headache, shoulder pain and history of prior concussion
4. Mastoid ecchymosis and eyelid hematoma but no confusion or loss of consciousness
5. Light sensitivity and dizziness lasting 10 minutes after collision

## Correct option

**4. Mastoid ecchymosis and eyelid hematoma but no confusion or loss of consciousness**

## Answer explanation

Mastoid ecchymosis and/or eyelid hematoma are signs of skull fracture and blepharohematoma, respectively, and require further evaluation in the emergency department with detailed physical examination and advanced imaging.

Concussion represents a continuum of traumatic brain injuries (TBI) which are caused when a trauma is transmitted to the head. Concussions are quite common, especially in contact sports and cause a complex neurometabolic cascade which results in abnormal neurotransmitter release and energy depletion in the brain. There are certain signs/symptoms that require a more detailed evaluation in the emergency department with regards to physical examination and advanced imaging. These include loss of consciousness, GCS score <15, abnormal mental status, signs of skull fracture (mastoid ecchymosis), bloody otorrhea, eyelid hematoma, focal neurologic defects, severe neck pain and amnesia/confusion. CT scan of the head and MRI of the brain may ultimately be required for further evaluation upon arrival to the emergency department.

*Kerrigan et al. wrote an update on treatment of pediatric concussions,* **noting** that rates are increasing through all ages, but most in pediatric age ranges. **They note** that concussions affect each child differently and the metabolic consequences of these injuries can manifest differently. They go on to review, in detail, the current research and guidelines regarding best management practices.

*Shirley et al. published a JAAOS article discussing management of sports related concussions and return to play guidelines.* **They note** that risk factors for concussions include age <18, female sex and prior concussion. **They go on to note** that all suspected concussions should lead to removal from play and a stepwise return to play protocol using neuropsychologic testing and other rehabilitation/medication strategies.

*Almeida et al. reviewed recent advances of pediatric concussion and mild TBIs.* *They discuss clinical signs relevant for diagnosis as well as neuropsychologic testing, advanced imaging, and lab markers.* *They discuss signs/symptoms which should lead to more detailed evaluation and note that a multidisciplinary approach should be undertaken to reduce recovery time.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Transient numbness in the left upper extremity that resolves quickly is indicative of a stinger, with the player able to return to the game.
Answer 2: Headache itself is not a symptom that requires emergent transport to the hospital for more detailed concussion evaluation.
Answer 3: Headache and shoulder pain are not reasons for emergent transport, nor is the history of a prior concussion.
Answer 5: Light sensitivity and dizziness are common after concussion and as long as they do not last longer than 15 minutes or occur with focal neurologic conditions or cognitive abnormalities, do not require emergent evaluation in the ED.
