## Question

A 7-year-old patient presents with right elbow swelling and deformity after falling off of a trampoline. Figures A and B demonstrate the injury radiographs. In the emergency department, the patient has a warm pink hand with a strong radial pulse and intact AIN motor function. The patient is taken to the OR the next morning for closed reduction and percutaneous pinning. After the fracture is reduced and the pins are placed, the patient's hand appears pale and cool with absent radial pulses. What is the next appropriate step?

## Options

1. Apply a splint and reassess pulses in the PACU
2. Warm the extremity and reassess pulses
3. Perform a doppler examination
4. Remove the pins, re-displacement of the fracture, and reassess pulses
5. Perform an anterior exploration

## Correct option

**4. Remove the pins, re-displacement of the fracture, and reassess pulses**

## Answer explanation

The patient has developed a poorly perfused hand with absent pulses after a closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) of the fracture. This may be due to the entrapment of the artery within the fracture. The next step should consist of removing the pins, re-displacement of the fracture, and reevaluating the vascular status of the extremity.

Concomitant vascular injuries with supracondylar humerus fractures are rare and present as a pulseless or cool and pale hand, which necessitates emergent treatment. Initially, fractures should be reduced and pinned followed by repeat vascular examination. If a previously well-perfused hand becomes dysvascular during CRPP the brachial artery may have become entrapped in the fracture site. The pins should be removed, re-displacement of the fracture, followed by a repeat vascular examination. If there is no improvement after pin removal then anterior exploration should be performed.

*Franklin et al. reviewed the treatment of pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures with vascular compromise.* **The authors stated** that patients that initially present with absent distal pulses should be treated with an immediate closed reduction and pinning followed by reassessment of vascular status. Additionally, **the authors stated** that patients presenting with neurapraxias alone can be observed.

*Abzug et al. reviewed the management of supracondylar humerus fractures in pediatric patients.* **The authors stated** that pulseless extremities that are poorly perfused should be treated with immediate fracture reduction and pinning. If the hand remains dysvascular then anterior exploration should be performed with possible arterial repair and fasciotomies.

*Mooney et al. performed a case study of appropriate use criteria for pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures with vascular compromise.* **The authors proposed** five assumptions that can assist in clinical decision-making comprised of the positioning of fractured limb, recognition of urgent surgical treatment, and possible transfer to a facility with vascular services.

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of the elbow demonstrating a displaced Gartland type III supracondylar humerus fracture. Illustration A is a treatment algorithm for a poorly perfused hand after a supracondylar humerus fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A patient with a newly dysvascular hand after a CRPP should be addressed immediately in the OR prior to transfer to the PACU.
Answer 2: Given that the patient previously had good perfusion prior to the CRPP cold-induced vasospasm is unlikely the cause of the patient's symptoms.
Answer 3: A Doppler examination is not unreasonable in this situation, but the etiology of the vascular change is due to the brachial artery entrapped in the fracture.
Answer 5: An anterior exploration is necessary when there is no improvement in vascular status after the pins are removed. This should not be done initially.
