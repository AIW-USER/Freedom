## Answer explanation

The histology slide in Figure A is most consistent with chondroblastoma, as evidenced by the benign-appearing chondroid matrix background, mature chondroblasts, and classic "chicken-wire" calcifications seen in the bottom left corner of the slide. Figure E is an AP radiograph of an adolescent knee showing a lytic epiphyseal lesion, consistent with chondroblastoma.

Chondroblastomas are rare, benign cartilagenous tumors primarily diagnosed in the second and third decades of life. They are almost exclusively epiphyseal in location, but occasionally cross the physis and progress into the metaphysis. These lesions are typically painful and show extensive perilesional edema on MRI. Similar to giant cell tumors of bone, they can metastasize to the lungs, so a chest radiograph is commonly ordered as part of the diagnostic and surveillance workup. Treatment is extended intralesional curettage and bone graft with a recurrence rate of approximately 10-15%. Occasionally the recurrences can develop malignant transformation and metastasize.

*Chen et al provide an updated review on chondroblastomas.* **They note** that chondroblastomas make up less than 1% of bone tumors, are diagnosed in the second and third decades of life, and are characterized as solitary well-circumscribed lesions affecting the epiphyseal regions of long bones. Radiation was previously used as adjunctive therapy to saucerization and bone grafting but resulted in an unacceptably high secondary sarcoma rate and has since been abandoned as a primary treatment for these lesions.

*Santiago et al completed a case series of 12 patients with 13 chondroblastomas treated with radiofrequency thermal ablation (RFA) under CT guidance.* Five of the 13 lesions had percutaneous cementation once the RFA was completed as these lesions involved weight-bearing areas of bone. **They reported** an 83% (11/13 cases) success rate in their measurable endpoints, which were resolution of pain, absence of edema on follow-up MRI, and necrosis extending beyond the margins of the original lesion. Despite this apparent success, they did have one patient with a 4cm shortening of the involved humerus compared to the contralateral limb, and one development of early hip arthritis (15 years after initial treatment). RFA appears to be an effective treatment modality, but caution must be had to avoid permanent damage to the physis and subchondral bone that can lead to early cartilage wear and arthritis.
