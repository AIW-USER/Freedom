## Question

A 23-year-old stuntman falls from a 4-story building while on set and sustains the injury shown in Figure A. Which of the following has been shown to have a significant effect on the odds of displacement of this fracture if it is not fixed surgically?

![[q219210-a-screenshot-2024-01-21-at-1-27-58-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Brachial plexus palsy
2. Contralateral scapular body fracture
3. Deltoid atony
4. Ipsilateral first through fourth rib fractures
5. Presence of a closed head injury

## Correct option

**4. Ipsilateral first through fourth rib fractures**

## Answer explanation

Associated fractures of the upper one-third of the rib cage significantly increase the odds of non-operatively treated clavicle fractures being >100% displaced on early follow-up at 1-4 weeks from the injury.

Clavicle fractures typically occur in the mid-shaft region at the junction of the outer and middle-third, given that this is the thinnest portion of the bone and is also the only area not protected by or reinforced with muscular or ligamentous attachments. Because of this, the medial fragment is usually pulled posterosuperiorly by the attached sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle, while the lateral fragment is pulled inferomedially by the pectoralis major/deltoid insertions and the weight of the arm. Fractures displaced less than 100% with < 2cm of shortening have been traditionally treated non-operatively based on relative criteria. However, it is important to assess the effects of additional insults to the surrounding superior suspensory shoulder complex (SSSC) shown in Illustration A, as they may lead to instability and further displacement that would benefit from operative fixation.

*Stahl et al. published the association of ipsilateral rib fractures with the displacement of midshaft clavicle fractures.* *The authors retrospectively reviewed 243 midshaft clavicle fractures from a single Level I trauma center.* *Of the 116 that had associated ipsilateral rib fractures and < 100% initial displacement, 72% demonstrated progression to 100% displacement at follow-up.* **The authors concluded** that the odds ratio for progression of the clavicle fracture to >100% displacement was 4.08 (P= 0.000194) when ribs 1–4 were fractured and not significant when ribs 5–8 or 9–12 were fractured.

*Fokin et al. published specifically on first rib fractures as both a culprit and a sign of injury severity in the multiply-injured patient.* **The authors note** that isolated first rib fractures, alone, are associated with a higher incidence of injuries to subclavian structures and the first thoracic vertebrae. **They concluded** that when they are combined with fractures of other ribs, the overall severity of trauma expands significantly.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the left shoulder showing a midshaft clavicle fracture with butterfly comminution and minimal shortening. Illustration A demonstrates the components of the superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3: Brachial plexus palsy and deltoid atony would likely lead more to a flail extremity with less muscular imbalance across the fracture fragments, lending less to the development of late displacement.
Answer 2: Ipsilateral disruptions of the superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC) would likely contribute to increased displacement and the need for surgical fixation, but a contralateral scapular body fracture would not likely cause late displacement.
Answer 5: Clavicle fractures are often found in patients with closed head injuries due to high energy mechanisms, but a head injury is not an independent predictor for late displacement of an initially minimally displaced fracture.
