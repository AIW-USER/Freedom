## Question

A 30-year-old male sustains a posterior elbow dislocation with associated radial head and coronoid fractures after a fall from a one-story balcony. Following initial reduction and immobilization in the emergency department, he presents for definitive surgical management. Intraoperatively, a radial head arthroplasty and lateral ligamentous reconstruction are first performed. The surgeon and resident then range the elbow and discuss potential next steps should instability persist, paying particular attention to the coronoid. Which of the following fracture patterns would most likely necessitate coronoid fixation as part of the next step in treatment?

## Options

1. Regan-Morrey Type I fracture
2. Regan-Morrey Type II fracture
3. Fracture displacement greater than 5 mm
4. Regan-Morrey Type III fracture
5. Answers 2 and 4

## Correct option

**4. Regan-Morrey Type III fracture**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a terrible triad elbow injury after a fall from height. Surgical management of these injuries involves radial head fracture fixation versus arthroplasty and LCL repair/reconstruction +/- MCL repair/reconstruction and coronoid fracture fixation. Coronoid fractures involving greater than 50% of the coronoid height (Regan-Morrey Type III) are most likely to require direct reduction and stabilization.

The surgical treatment of terrible triad injuries involves a stepwise approach. If instability persists after addressing both the radial head and lateral ligamentous injuries, attention is turned to the medial-sided ligamentous structures and/or coronoid fracture. In Type I and Type II fractures (isolated coronoid tip fractures or fractures involving less than 50% of the coronoid height), the MCL complex can first be repaired or reconstructed, as successful outcomes have been reported in the literature without coronoid fracture fixation. Type III fractures are likely to require fixation.

*Kim et al. performed a retrospective study examining the surgical management of terrible triad injuries in 27 patients.* In patients with Type I and Type II coronoid fractures with resultant instability after radial head and LCL restoration, torn MCL complexes were repaired without fracture fixation. All Type III fractures were fixed with either plate and screw or screw constructs. There were no significant correlations between outcome measures tested, including recurrent instability, between patients with and without coronoid fracture fixation. **The authors concluded** that fixation of Type I and II coronoid fractures is not necessary if stability is conferred following radial head and ligamentous restoration.

*Papatheodorou et al. also performed a retrospective review of 14 patients with terrible triad injuries that included Type I or II coronoid fractures.* *All patients were treated with radial head arthroplasty or open-reduction internal fixation and lateral ligamentous complex repair/reconstruction.* Intraoperative stability was conferred in all cases without directly addressing the coronoid fracture or MCL complex. No patients experienced recurrent instability at a minimum follow-up of two years. **They concluded** that terrible triad injuries with Type I or II coronoid fractures can be successfully treated without coronoid fracture fixation when stability is restored following radial head and LCL restoration.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Isolated coronoid tip fractures involving less than 10% of the coronoid height do not confer elbow instability in cadaveric studies and are unlikely to require direct fixation after addressing the radial head and ligamentous structures in terrible triad injuries. If instability persists after radial head and LCL restoration, attention can first be turned to the MCL complex.
Answers 2&5: In the setting of Type II coronoid fractures, if instability persists after radial head and LCL restoration, the next most appropriate step as described in the literature is likely to involve repair or reconstruction of the MCL complex. Successful outcomes have been reported following Type II fractures treated without fixation.
Answer 3: The severity of coronoid fracture displacement has not been a well-described consideration in the management of terrible triad injuries. Rather, the percent involvement of the coronoid height, as described by the Regan-Morrey classification system, is more appropriately used in prognosticating when fracture fixation may be necessary.
