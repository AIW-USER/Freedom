## Question

Which type of fracture healing occurs in locked compression plating? [General Ortho.-Fracture Healing]

## Options

1. primary
2. secondary
3. mixed
4. contact healing

## Correct option

**2. secondary**

## Answer explanation

Two types of fracture healing are primary and secondary.

Primary Fracture Healing or Direct Fracture Healing (Healing without Callus Formation): It is a less common mode of fracture healing. It is seen in rigid internal fixation of fractures (compression plating) and in unicortical fractures (Greenstick fractures). It is a direct attempt of bone to restore its continuity without forming fracture callus. It is of two types: (1) gap healing and (2) contact healing. Gap healing occurs when there is a minimal gap in between rigidly fixed fracture ends. Woven bone (immature bone having a random arrangement of collagen fibers) is initially formed in transverse orientation between fracture gaps which is later replaced by lamellar bone (regular parallel alignment of collagen into sheets/lamellae). Contact healing is seen when fracture ends are closely approximated to each other without any gap. Osteoclasts at one end of fracture cause bone resorption to form so called “cutting cones” such that the fracture gap widens initially. In these cones, osteoblasts lay down new bone.

Secondary Fracture Healing or Indirect Fracture Healing (Healing by Callus Formation): This is the more common method of fracture healing. It is seen in the absence of rigid fixation (e.g. in cast immobilization, intramedullary nailing and bridge plating for comminuted metaphyseal fractures).

Conventional plates (Compression plates) require anatomical reduction and provide absolute rigidity thereby promoting Primary healing.

In contrast, locking plates are mostly meant to be used in comminuted fractures, segmentally deficient or Porotic bone where bone quality is poor. Here absolute stability does not exist. Here the plate is used in bridge mode and functions as “internal fixator” with multiple anchor points. Here anatomic reduction is not required as the fixed angle device converts axial load to compressive force across fracture site minimising gap length and strain. Now as per strain theory, tolerable strain 2-10% promotes secondary healing, even in absence of anatomic reduction. The fact that these plates are applied also using biologically friendly (minimally invasive) approaches further promotes callus formation encouraging secondary healing.

Answer : b
