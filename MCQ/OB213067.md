## Question

A 17-year-old girl sustained a twisting injury to her knee during a basketball tournament 2 weeks ago. She presents to clinic with significant knee pain and swelling. On examination, her knee range of motion (ROM) is limited to 10-75°. A dial test is performed and reveals a 5-degree external rotation asymmetry compared to the contralateral knee. Her radiographs are shown in Figures A and B. What is the likely diagnosis and the best next step in management in order to optimize her outcomes?

## Options

1. Arcuate complex injury; ligament complex repair
2. Anterior cruciate ligament injury; ligament reconstruction
3. Anterior cruciate ligament injury; physical therapy to optimize ROM
4. Posterolateral corner injury; ligament complex repair
5. Posterolateral corner injury; physical therapy to optimize ROM

## Correct option

**3. Anterior cruciate ligament injury; physical therapy to optimize ROM**

## Answer explanation

This patient's radiographs depict a Segond fracture (avulsion of the proximal lateral tibia), which is pathognomonic for an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear. A dial test with <10-15-degree asymmetry is unremarkable for posterolateral corner injury. Given her knee stiffness, physical therapy to optimize her ROM pre-operatively ("pre-hab") would be the next best step in management.

Acute ACL reconstruction in patients with limited knee ROM has been shown to lead to increased incidence of postoperative stiffness. Preoperative ROM and quadriceps strength is an important predictor of knee functional outcomes after ACL reconstruction; as such, enhancing quadriceps strength and function of the knee preoperatively improves final outcomes.

*Eitzen et al. investigated preoperative predictive factors for knee function after ACL reconstruction.* **They reported** that quadriceps muscle strength, presence of a meniscal injury, and SF-36 pain scores were the most significant predictive factors 2 years after ACL reconstruction. **They recommended** that ACL reconstruction should not be performed if quadriceps muscle strength is less than 20% or more of the uninjured limb.

*Shaarani et al. performed a randomized controlled clinical trial on the effect of a 6-week prehabilitation program on outcomes after ACL reconstruction.* **They reported** that patients undergoing prehabilitation before ACL reconstruction experienced improved knee function based on single-legged hop test and self-reported assessments at both 6 weeks and 12-weeks postoperatively. **They suggested** prehabilitation as a consideration for patients awaiting ACL reconstruction.

*Logerstedt et al. performed a study to examine the relationship between preoperative quadriceps strength and postoperative knee function at 6 months after ACL reconstruction.* **They reported** that preoperative quadriceps strength was a significant predictor for IKDC2000 score (a joint-specific outcome score for assessing symptoms, function, and activity) 6 months after ACL reconstruction. **They emphasized** the importance of good quadriceps strength prior to ACL reconstruction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The arcuate complex is part of the posterolateral corner. Injury to the arcuate complex may present as an avulsion fracture of the proximal fibula, known as the arcuate sign.
Answer 2: Acute ACL reconstruction in patients with limited knee ROM has been shown to lead to postoperative stiffness.
Answer 4: The radiographs depict an avulsion of the proximal lateral tibia (Segond fracture), suggestive of an ACL tear, not a PLC injury which would be demonstrated by an avulsion of the proximal fibula (arcuate sign). Furthermore, the dial test revealed less than 15 degree asymmetry, which would make a PLC less likely.
