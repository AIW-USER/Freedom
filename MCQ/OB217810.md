## Question

A 56-year-old male presents to the clinic with progressive radiating low back pain and subjective right lower extremity weakness. On examination, you find a positive straight leg raise with weakness of foot dorsiflexion and numbness along the medial ankle. You explain to the patient that his symptoms are likely due to which of the following?

## Options

1. L4-5 Far Lateral Disc Herniation
2. L4-5 Posterolateral Disc Herniation
3. L5-S1 Far Lateral Disc Herniation
4. L5-S1 Posterolateral Lateral Disc Herniation
5. L4-5 Central Facet Cyst

## Correct option

**1. L4-5 Far Lateral Disc Herniation**

## Answer explanation

The patient demonstrates symptoms of an L4 radiculopathy affecting the tibialis anterior (foot dorsiflexion) and sensation over the medial malleolus, which can be caused by a far lateral disc herniation at the L4-5 level compressing the exiting nerve (L4).

Lumbar disc herniation can occur through various processes but can often lead to compression of nearby neural structures and cause pain, weakness, or numbness. The location of the herniation plays a critical role in symptom manifestation and treatment planning. When the compression occurs in the paracentral location, also called posterolateral, the traversing nerve root is affected. When the herniation is foraminal (far lateral or extraforaminal), the exiting nerve root is affected. In this way, a central herniation at L4-5 will affect L5 (the traversing root), and a foraminal herniation will affect L4 (the exiting root). Paracentral herniations are much more common (~90-95%) than far lateral herniations (5-10%). MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing lumbar disc herniations, showing disc pathology and nerve root compression. Treatment begins with conservative management but may require a surgical intervention with failure.

*Gregory et al. reviewed lumbar disc herniations with an emphasis on diagnosis and management.* *The authors describe the common symptoms, including sciatica, but report that sensory and motor symptoms provide greater diagnostic yield.* Regarding imaging, **the authors emphasize** that not every patient will require MRI, and in the absence of red flag symptoms (cauda equina), imaging should be delayed until after 6 weeks of conservative management.

*Marquardt et al. reviewed their series of patients with foraminal herniations treated with a lateral approach.* **The authors report** that while major and moderate leg pain and low back pain decreased, sensory and motor deficits were still present in most patients. Despite residual symptoms, the vast majority of patients expressed satisfaction with the result of surgery.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 2: A disc herniation at this location (central) would affect the TRAVERSING nerve root L5, manifesting as weakness of toe dorsiflexion and numbness of first web space.
Answer 3: A disc herniation at this location (lateral) would affect the EXITING nerve root L5, manifesting as weakness of toe dorsiflexion and numbness of first web space.
Answer 4: A disc herniation at this location (central) would affect the TRAVERSING nerve root S1, which would manifest as weakness of plantar flexion and numbness along the lateral foot
Answer 5: A facet cyst is another possible cause of nerve root compression. A facet cyst in this location (central) would affect the TRAVERSING nerve root L5, which would manifest as weakness in toe dorsiflexion and numbness in the first web space.
