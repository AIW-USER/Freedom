## Question

A 25-year-old female presents to your clinic with several years of hip pain that is now worsening. She describes the pain as deep within the hip and states that it occurs with weight bearing or excessive rotation of the hip. She previously saw an outside provider who ordered an MRI of her hip which was read as a labral tear. You obtain radiographs which are seen in Figure A. Which of the following, if seen on this radiograph, would represent a contraindication to isolated hip arthroscopy and labral repair in this patient?

![[q218928-a-adult-hip-dysplasia.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Lateral center edge angle = 28°
2. Patient age
3. Anterosuperior location of labral tear
4. Tonnis angle = 15°
5. Alpha angle = 40°

## Correct option

**4. Tonnis angle = 15°**

## Answer explanation

Hip dysplasia (Tonnis angle >10°) is a contraindication to isolated arthroscopic labral repair because the dysplasia has not been addressed at the time of surgery.

Hip arthroscopy is a valuable technique in many cases of intra- and extra-articular hip pathology. One of the most common pathologies addressed are hip labral tears which can be caused by a number of reasons and can be arthroscopically debrided or repaired. While the presence of femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is common with labral tears, hip dysplasia can also contribute to similar pathology. However, surgeons must understand that these two contributors are treated differently. The presence of hip dysplasia is generally a contraindication to standalone arthroscopic labral repair because the cause of the labral tear (dyplasia) is not being addressed. There has been interest in combined arthroscopic labral repair and periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), as this may help correct the bony deformity as well.

*Vahedi et al. reviewed outcomes in the presence of combined FAI and hip dysplasia.* **They noted** that the modified Harris Hip Scores and SF-36 scores were significantly lower in the dysplastic group compared to the FAI only group. **They also found** a much higher rate of failure (29%) in the dysplastic group compared to the FAI only group (2.5%).

*Larson et al. retrospectively looked at 88 hips with dysplastic radiographic features an average of 26 months after hip arthroscopy and matched them to an FAI cohort without dysplasia.* **They noted** a much lower rate of good/excellent outcomes in the dysplasia group and a 32.2% rate of failure. **They ultimately concluded** that hip arthroscopy should be avoided in patients with even mild to moderate acetabular dysplasia.

Figure A demonstrates an AP pelvis radiograph which shows bilateral acetabular dysplasia with increased Tonnis angle.

Illustration A demonstrates a radiograph demonstrating how to measure the Tonnis angle. Illustration B demonstrates a radiograph measuring the lateral center edge angle (LCEA). Illustration C demonstrates the alpha angle, which is measured by the intersection between a line through the center of the femoral neck and a second line that exits the anterolateral femoral head/neck junction where cam deformity prominence begins. Illustration D demonstrates the Tonnis angle of 15°, as measured in Figure A.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The LCEA of 28° is within normal limits (>25°).
Answer 2: This patient's age (25) is not a contraindication to hip arthroscopy and labral repair.
Answer 3: Anterosuperior labral tears are the most common anatomic location and do not represent a contraindication to repair.
Answer 5: An alpha angle of 40° is within normal limits, but if it were increased it would be representative of cam-type femoroacetabular impingement, which can be addressed with hip arthroscopy.
