## Question

A 2-year-old male with Down syndrome presents with worsening neurologic deficits due to occipitocervical instability. Surgical planning consists of occiput-C2 posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation with plans for post-operative halo immobilization. Which of the following is recommended with regards to halo immobilization to limit treatment complications?

## Options

1. Preoperative skull radiographs
2. Preoperative skull CT
3. Application of four pins tightened to 8 inch-pounds of torque
4. Hand tightening of pins with maximal force
5. Placement of pins into the temporalis muscle

## Correct option

**2. Preoperative skull CT**

## Answer explanation

In skeletally immature patients, preoperative skull CT is recommended to identify safe regions for pin placement and to avoid placement of pins into skull sutures.

Halo immobilization is a relatively new treatment technique that was initially designed to protect cervical arthrodesis in polio patients in the 1950s. It has been utilized as an adjunctive treatment for cervical spine stabilization following cervical spine surgery or as definitive treatment in pediatric cervical spine trauma. Complications include pin loosening, pin site infections, pin penetration in the skull, and cranial nerve palsy. The use of more pins with lower torques in pediatric patients compared to adults can reduce these complications, with eight pins tightened to 2-4 inch-pounds is recommended. Preoperative CT of the skull is recommended to identify safe areas for pin placement and avoidance of cranial sutures in skeletally immature patients.

*Wong and Haynes performed a retrospective computed tomography (CT) study of 48 normal children to determine the average skull thickness in regions of routine halo pin placement.* **The authors found** that wide variability in skull thickness in regions of routine pin placement with increasing skull thickness with age and the average thinnest area measured 1.9 mm. **The authors concluded** that there is no "safe area" for pin placement in the pediatric skull and limited preoperative skull CTs are recommended to determine safe areas for pin placement.

*Arkader et al. performed a retrospective study of 10 patients under the age of 3 years that were treated with halo immobilization for cervical spine conditions.* **The authors reported** two complications related to halo immobilization, which included pin loosening in one patient that was successfully treated with pin tightening and one transient pin site infection that resolved with antibiotics. **The authors concluded** that halo immobilization is a safe treatment in this age group, but a good understanding of the developmental anatomy is critical and recommends preoperative CT for pin placement and the use of multiple, perpendicular, short tipped, and wide flanged pins with precise tightening.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Preoperative skull radiographs would not provide adequate information with regards to sufficient skull thickness for pin placement and location of suture lines.
Answer 3: In skeletally immature patients, six to eight pins tightened to 2-4 inch-pounds of torque is ideal to avoid skull penetration and subsequent epidural abscess.
Answer 4: Hand tightening pins with maximal force would penetrate the skull, which is not the goal of pin placement. Pins should be placed with 2-4 inch-pounds of torque or "finger-tight."
Answer 5: Pin placement into the temporalis muscle could result in pain and difficulty eating as this muscle is involved with mastication. Further, the temporal bone is relatively thin compared to other regions of the skull, which can result in skull penetration.
