## Question

A 35-year-old male presents to your clinic with right knee pain after sustaining a twisting injury while playing basketball 3 weeks ago. He is able to walk but is now experiencing severe knee pain and limited flexion. Figures 1 and 2 are representative MRI slices of his right knee. The patient undergoes the appropriate surgical treatment. Which of the following is a positive prognostic indicator for his outcome following surgery?

## Options

1. Unaddressed ACL insufficiency
2. Large rim width
3. Tear at red-white junction
4. Radial tear
5. Vertical and longitudinal tear

## Correct option

**5. Vertical and longitudinal tear**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a locked bucket-handle medial meniscal tear. Given the patient's age as well as the location and size of the tear, the appropriate treatment would be meniscal repair. Good prognostic indicators for successful meniscus repair include: vertical and longitudinal tears, peripheral tears in the "red-red" zone and those with smaller rim width.

Bucket-handle meniscal tears are vertical and longitudinal tears that are long enough to permit displacement of the inner rim into the femoral intercondlar notch. This may result in significant pain accompanied by locking and catching of the knee, specially with knee flexion. The diagnosis of a buckle handle tear can be made by MRI, where the characteristic "double PCL" is often seen on a sagittal cut through the notch. In this scenario, the anterior band in the "double PCL" sign is the flipped meniscal tear. A bucket-handle meniscal tear generally requires operative intervention given the mechanical symptoms it causes when engaged. Given that these tears are usually vertical and longitudinal, they are amenable to repair as long as they are not significantly degenerated or located in the "white-white" avascular zone.

*Espejo-Reina et al. performed a retrospective review of the clinical outcomes of a series of 24 patients who underwent repair of chronic bucket-handle medial meniscus tears.* The mean time from injury to surgery was 10 months and 16 of the patients underwent concomitant ACL reconstruction. At a mean follow-up for 48 months, **the authors found** that 83% of the patients (20 of 24) achieved complete healing of the tears, with an International Knee Documentation Committee rating of A or B. However the other four patients (17%) required revision surgery.

*Burns et al. discussed the repair techniques for meniscal tears.* *They upheld that the inside-out repair remained the "gold standard" for bucket-handle tears, but that new generation all-inside repairs were appropriate for smaller tears.* The outside-in repair was supported for anterior horn tears. **The authors concluded** that meniscal debridement should be reserved for degenerative tears or patterns that are not amenable to repair.

Stärke et al. reviewed indications and techniques for meniscal repair. The authors strongly advocated for meniscal repair in the setting of longitudinal peripheral tears in young patients and especially in the setting of concomitant ACL reconstruction given the increased healing rates. They noted the probability of healing is decreased in complex or degenerative tears, central tears, and tears in unstable knees, and concluded that biologic factors might be of greater importance to the success of meniscal repair than surgical technique.

Figure A and B show coronal and sagittal T2 weighted MRI images showing a displaced medial meniscus tear into the notch with the characteristic "double PCL" sign seen on the sagittal image indicating a bucket handle meniscus tear.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: If a torn ACL is not addressed at the time of surgery, it would lead to increased strain on the meniscus as a secondary stabilizer and decrease the likelihood of healing a meniscus repair.
Answer 2: Rim width is the distance from the tear to the peripheral meniscocapsular junction and correlates with the ability of a meniscal repair to heal. A high rim width tear has decreased blood supply compared to a low rim width tear and is less likely to heal.
Answer 3: The optimal location of healing for meniscus tears are peripheral tears in the "red-red" zone given the superior vascularity of this region compared to the red-white and white-white zones.
Answer 4: Radial tears disrupt the circumferential collagen fibers and affect the ability of the meniscus to absorb tibiofemoral loading. These tears are generally not amenable to repair.
