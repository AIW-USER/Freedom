## Question

Which of the following can be considered as an individualized approach to attaining the desired knee alignment after TKR.

## Options

1. Mechanical knee alignment
2. Anatomical knee alignment
3. Kinematic knee alignment
4. Both B and C

## Correct option

**3. Kinematic knee alignment**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C
EXPLANATION: -

COMPONENT ALIGNMENT IN TKR

Alignment of native knee

![[TO21733208-1.jpg]]

In native knee coronal relationship between distal femur and proximal tibia is achieved by having a slight valgus alignment of the distal femur (0° to 4°) and a slight varus alignment of the proximal tibia (1° to 5°). The resultant overall limb alignment (mechanical lower limb axis) is on average in slight varus (around 3o) as compared to a vertical axis. And also note the femur specifically is in valgus of around 9o compared to vertical axis and angle between mechanical and vertical axis of femur is 6o.
The knee joint itself also tends to have a peculiar alignment. Proximal tibia tends to have a posterior slope (1° to 9°), while the joint line of knee tends to be in 3o of varus (see pics below); may it be flexion or extension position (as the medial femoral condyle extends down relatively more than the lateral condyle).

ALIGNMENT GOALS AFTER TKR
Restoration of knee alignment is considered one of the mainstays of a successful total knee arthroplasty (TKA). For achieving this, various methods have evolved in parallel over time. These include: -
A) Fixed alignment approaches: Mechanical and Anatomical alignments
B) Individualized alignment approaches: Kinematic and Functional alignments

Mechanical alignment
The method was introduced in 1973 by Michael Freeman and Swanson. Mechanical alignment can be defined as implanting both tibial and femoral components perpendicular to the limb’s mechanical axis, thus achieving a neutral overall alignment. The aim of creating a neutral lower limb alignment is to achieve a balanced load distribution between the medial and lateral compartments.
This is in contrast from natural where the overall limb alignment is 3o varus from vertical axis and the knee joint line also is in 3o varus. The proponents of this alignment feel that the varus alignment of native knee after TKR will lead to asymmetric poly wear so post TKR one should aim neutral alignment.
The coronal targets in this method are achieved by referencing the bone resections (measured resection) to the femoral and tibial anatomical axes and by inference, their mechanical axes. Sagittal targets are dictated by the patient’s anatomy and vary with implant design and the level of constraint used. Femoral rotational targets can be defined by use of epicondyles as references. Once bone is cut, to ensure cuts remain absolutely perpendicular to vertical axis, ligament releases are performed to balance the flexion extension gaps.
Considering a varus knee, the positioning of a typical component will remove more bone from the lateral tibia than the medial tibia, more bone from the distal medial femoral condyle (MFC) than lateral femoral condyle (LFC), and more bone from the posterior MFC than the posterior LFC. This results in a horizontal joint line; medial collateral ligament (MCL) tightness in flexion and extension usually requiring release in the patient with constitutional varus; and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) tightness in flexion usually accommodated by natural or acquired LCL laxity.
The main setbacks have been as follows:
- How can one size fit all: different knees may have different alignments which is not respected by this method. Kinematic method described below acknowledges the significant phenotypic variability of knee alignment and positions the prostheses relative to each patient’s unique constitutional anatomy.
- Soft tissue releases are required that can be source of continued pain and some unsatisfactory results seen after this method as almost 15-20% people still continue to be symptomatic despite satisfactory surgery.
- 3 D anatomy of the knee is not focussed which is actually far more complex (read kinematic alignment below)

Anatomical (classical) alignment
The anatomical method was developed by Hungerford et al.(1982) and is used mostly for the CR type knee. As the knee joint line has a 3° varus, this method consists of varus cutting of the tibial plateau at 3° and an additional valgus cutting at 3° from the mechanical axis of the femur, resulting in a total valgus cutting of femur at 7–10°.
The proponents of this concept believe that this method reflects the ground force more biomechanically as it generates the same varus at knee (oblique joint line) as natural.

But as mentioned, it could lead to asymmetric poly wear with loading and it lowers reproducibility to result in more than 3° of varus tibial osteotomy and may damage the lateral collateral ligament and/or popliteus tendon due to excessive valgus osteotomy on the femoral side (see above pic).

Kinematic alignment (considered true resurfacing)
The concept of Kinematic Alignment (KA) was introduced by Howell and Hull (2008) as they described their “custom fit positioning technique”. Despite good surgeries, about 20 % of patients would not get satisfied with the conventional mechanical alignment method and would have continued pain and poor functioning in activities of daily living. That’s what made the developers think of restoring natural kinematics of the knee to better these results.
Kinematics refers to the relative relationship of the femur, patella, and tibia at any flexion angle of knee; restoring the same by controlling the three-dimensional placement of the femoral and tibial components so that the orientation of the three kinematic axes is unchanged from the normal knee joint is the target of Kinematic alignment. Initially this was done with calipers (for measuring cut bone) and patient specific guides but now with advent of navigation, robotics and AI, the job has become much easier.
In this technique the surgeon seeks to recreate the anatomy of the native knee by cutting bone such that the natural tension of the ligaments is maintained as it was prior to surgery. So conventional ligament releases are not part of this method rather in non-balanced gaps, bone cuts are made to make gaps acceptable (this is in contrast to mechanical alignment where ligament release on tighter side is done to balance gaps). In essence, it is a pure prosthetic resurfacing, with resections based on the primary femoral transverse axis, about which the tibia flexes and extends. Equal measured resections of distal femoral, posterior femoral, and proximal tibial surfaces are performed, with adjustment for assumed osteochondral loss.
When aiming Kinematic alignment, the surgeon first gets knee imaging done (CT/ MRI) and feeds into computer and defines the three kinematic axes of the patient’s knee. These are the functional axes of movement about which the knee flexes and rotates: -
1- the flexion extension axis (same as TEA) aka cylindrical axis: this is the transverse axis around which the tibia flexes and extends across distal femur,
2- the patellar tracking axis: The axis along which patella travels through the femoral trochlea. It’s an isometric rotation axis located across the medial femoral condyle (analogous to Schottle’s point on femur used in MPFL reconstruction),
3- the external internal rotation axis: longitudinal axis located in medial compartment around which the tibia rotates internally and externally.

![[TO21733208-4.jpg]]

These axes are superimposed on normal and arthritis knee models of patient made via computer software to define bone cuts. The osteotomy planes corresponding to the positions of the femoral and tibial components in the normal knee model are transferred onto the arthritic knee model. The surgeon carries out symmetrical resections of the medial and lateral femoral condyles, and medial and lateral tibial plateaus, having compensated for wear. The tibial slope is matched to the patient’s native slope. The axial rotation of the femur is set according to the Posterior Condylar Axis, having compensated for wear. Axial rotation of the tibia is set perpendicular to a line drawn from the centre of the medial and lateral tibial plateaus. In this manner, minimal soft tissue releases are required rather if a resection gap is not balanced, a compensatory bone cut is made to align the gap as desired rather than ligament release to balance gap as in mechanical alignment. This technique in a way has elements of both measured resection (mainly) and gap-balancing techniques.
After osteotomy is done and shape matching of the femoral component (single radius femoral component is an optimal design) to the restored articular surface of the femur is done, there are just four subsequent steps to restore motion: osteophyte removal, adjusting the tibial cutting plane, releasing the posterior capsule off the femur, and medialization or lateralization of the tibial component. So here basically, provided an accurate femoral resurfacing has been achieved, balancing of a KA TKR is achieved through osteophyte removal and “tibial bone resection” only as described by the decision tree of Howell et al.

![[TO21733208-5.jpg]]

Note: Only tibial cutting is performed in KA to achieve gap balancing

The limb alignment is assessed intraoperatively once osteophytes have been removed, allowing coronal correction to be carried out through a manually-applied varus or valgus force to correct the deformity. This allows the software to generate the size of the potential gaps, both in extension and in 90° of flexion. Once all signals are acceptable surgeon proceeds with prosthesis implantation.
In summary, the key principal of the KA approach is that restoration of the three axes, the joint line orientation angle (JLOA) and physiological soft tissue balancing will improve gait, feel of the knee and range of motion (ROM); and thus, result in superior patient outcomes.
There are some controversies regarding this concept also. First, this method cannot be used without the patient-specific template system. Second, kinematic alignment is used despite of the fact that normal kinematics is altered due to the sacrifice of the ACL and both the menisci. Third, the kinematics without weight bearing is different from that with weight bearing. Fourth, There are reports that the mechanical axis in kinematically aligned TKA is similar to that in conventional and computer-assisted TKA. Fifth, KA, places no restrictions on final implant alignment angles: it pursues an individualized approach for each patient regardless of potentially significant alignment outliers. At times, KA and its derivatives could result in components aligned in tibial varus and femoral valgus far more than MA; hence its use is to be restricted only to mild deformed knees. Finally, still there are not many reports of clinically established long-term results of this method.

Functional alignment
In the pursuit of an ideal combination of restoring 3D constitutional knee alignment, preserving native constitutional laxities, and achieving stability through a range of motion, the concept of functional alignment (FA) was developed. It aims to place components in the positions that least compromise the soft-tissue envelope, and hence to restore the plane and obliquity of the joint back to that dictated by the soft-tissues.
This technique has elements of both measured resection and gap-balancing techniques. The limb alignment is assessed intraoperatively once osteophytes have been removed, allowing coronal correction to be carried out through a manually-applied varus or valgus force to correct the deformity. This allows the software to generate the size of the potential gaps, both in extension and in 90° of flexion. With a traditional gap-balancing technique, the tibia would be cut at 90° to the tibial axis and the femur at 90° to the femoral axis in the coronal plane. Gaps could then be assessed to allow soft tissue release, balancing the knee in extension and flexion. With functional alignment, the gaps can be balanced by changing the implant targets in all three planes. Thus, a smaller medial extension gap can be balanced by placing the tibial component in up to 3° of varus. A tighter lateral flexion gap can be balanced by internally rotating the femoral component. These targets are individualized to the patient’s knee and gaps. They can be kept within currently accepted safe limits and allow the overall limb alignment to be kept within the 0° ± 3° safe zone of coronal alignment.
