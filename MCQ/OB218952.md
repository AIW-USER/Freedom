## Question

A 52-year-old male is referred to the clinic by a sports surgeon in the community. The patient was being worked up for a meniscal tear in the left knee and was found to have a concerning mass in his proximal tibia based on MRI findings. He denies having pain specific to the area but has been having popping, clicking, and medial joint line pain for the past year. He does not have a CD of his images, but he does have the radiology report, which describes a “chondroid mass, possible chondrosarcoma” with a single image shown in Figure A. Which of the following is the best next step in management?

## Options

1. Obtain complete weight bearing radiographs of the knee
2. Have the patient get a chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT scan with contrast
3. Reorder an MRI with and without contrast of the entire left lower extremity
4. Perform core needle biopsy to establish a tissue diagnosis
5. Schedule patient to undergo extended intralesional curettage and bone-void filling

## Correct option

**1. Obtain complete weight bearing radiographs of the knee**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with potential concerns for chondrosarcoma based on MRI chondroid tumor findings alone. The next best diagnostic workup step is obtaining standard orthogonal radiographs to characterize the lesion better.

Patients are commonly referred to a musculoskeletal oncologist to work up incidental findings on advanced imaging. It is pragmatic to ensure these patients’ images are available for review by the musculoskeletal oncologist to determine if any additional workup is needed. In general, the workup of all unknown lesions should begin with non-invasive imaging modalities and progress to invasive procedures if further concerns arise. Particularly in the setting of chondroid tumors, plain orthogonal radiographic images can provide a great deal of information regarding tumor size, appearance, and overall behavior. MRIs alone are insufficient to diagnose chondrosarcoma and should be used in conjunction with patient history, other imaging modalities, and, if needed, a biopsy. In this patient, plain radiographic imaging revealed a simple enchondroma (Illustration A), and the patient did not require any intervention for the lesion.

*Deckers and colleagues performed a systematic review to determine if MRI could differentiate between atypical cartilaginous tumors (ACT) and high-grade chondrosarcomas.* *There was substantial data heterogeneity which limited their ability to perform a meta-analysis.* However, they did report that loss of entrapped fatty marrow, cortical breakthrough, and extraosseous soft tissue expansion was far more common in high-grade chondrosarcomas than their low metastatic potential ACT counterparts. **The authors concluded** that more studies are needed to develop and assess reliable imaging modalities to differentiate between these two diagnoses.

*Gulia and colleagues performed a retrospective review of established chondrosarcoma patients at their institution to determine if they required additional imaging for skeletal-based metastatic lesions.* *All patients, despite tumor grade, underwent a thoracic CT scan and bone scan or PET-CT.* **They found** that very few patients had any skeletal metastases, even if they had present pulmonary metastases. **The authors concluded** that skeletal scanning is unnecessary overall in chondrosarcoma patients; however, advanced imaging of the chest for pulmonary metastases remains imperative for staging.

*Wells and colleagues provided an overview of conventional cartilaginous tumors and their respective diagnostic workup and treatment.* The report that the first step in evaluation is obtaining standard orthogonal multiplanar radiographs and pursuing further imaging if there are concerning findings. They further delineate between different imaging modalities, establish a clinical spectrum of chondroid tumors, and discuss appropriate treatment options. **They conclude** that differentiating between chondroid tumors may be difficult at times, and any ambiguity warrants evaluation by a musculoskeletal oncology multidisciplinary team.

Figure A shows sagittal plane MRI imaging of a proximal tibial lesion with a heterogenous intensity of stippled calcifications on T2-weighted imaging. Illustrations A and B show plain radiographic imaging of the same lesion with the classic “rings and arcs” calcification pattern consistent with a benign enchondroma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Obtaining a chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT scan with contrast is part of the standard evaluation workup for an unknown lytic lesion in an adult (>40 years) patient. The concern in that setting is an undiagnosed primary carcinoma. A standalone chest CT would be appropriate if this patient had a true chondrosarcoma to evaluate for any pulmonary metastasis.
Answer 3: Obtaining an MRI with and without contrast of the entire left lower extremity would be appropriate in the setting of a soft tissue sarcoma. The MRI allows for full characterization of the soft tissue sarcoma and looks for any obvious lymphadenopathy or associated localized spread with specific tumor subtypes.
Answer 4: Performing a core needle biopsy to establish a tissue diagnosis would be considered appropriate if there is a genuine concern for chondrosarcoma. However, obtaining plain radiographic imaging would be considered the most prudent next step in management for better characterization of this tumor.
Answer 5: An extended intralesional curettage and bone-void filling is appropriate for symptomatic enchondromas and atypical cartilaginous tumors; however, a standard radiographic workup should be performed first.
