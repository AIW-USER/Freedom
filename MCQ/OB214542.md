## Options

1. Observation until lesion heals, then curretage and bone grafting
2. Biopsy of the lesion
3. Admission to hospital for IV antibiotics
4. Open reduction, internal fixation and bone grafting
5. Referral to an orthopaedic oncologist

## Correct option

**5. Referral to an orthopaedic oncologist**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with a malignant bone lesion (likely osteosarcoma). Referral to an orthopedic oncologist is the most appropriate next step in management.

Biopsy of soft-tissue and bone lesions is a difficult procedure that is fraught with complications if obtained improperly. Occasionally, improperly performed biopsies may preclude limb-salvage, thus greatly altering the outcome of the disease process. As such, it is considered standard-of-care to refer patients with possible musculoskeletal tumors to treatment centers capable of proper diagnosis and management of the condition. While a community orthopedic surgeon may know how to properly biopsy a lesion, it is in the patient's best interest to be referred to a treatment center prior to biopsy for biopsy and subsequent definitive management.

*Mankin et al. reviewed 597 musculoskeletal oncology cases to determine the complications associated with biopsies.* *Of the 597 patients reviewed, 282 (47%) had the procedure at a referring institution.* *Out of all the biopsies,* **they found** an 18% rate of diagnostic error, a 19% chance of an incorrectly performed biopsy causing a change in management, and a 3% incidence (18 patients) of an unnecessary amputation being performed. **The authors concluded** that complications and changes in the course and outcome were up to 12 times greater (p < 0.001) when the biopsy was done in a referring institution instead of in an oncology treatment center.

*Enneking et al. presents a surgical staging system for musculoskeletal sarcomas.* *They describe that their staging system, which stratifies lesions on the basis of the risk factors associated with various surgical procedures, enhances outcomes by properly weighing the risk of recurrence with the benefit of retained function.*

Figure A is a radiograph that shows an ill-defined large destructive lytic lesion of the left proximal humerus. Figures B and C are the T1-weighted and post-contrast T2-weighted coronal MRI studies revealing a multi-lobulated destructive bone lesion with cortical breakthrough, extensive soft-tissue involvement, and contrast enhancement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation until healing followed by subsequent curettage and bone grafting would be appropriate for a unicameral bone cyst but this is a destructive bone lesion consistent with osteosarcoma.
Answer 2: A biopsy should be postponed until the patient is referred to an oncology center for the reasons described above.
Answer 3: While infection should always be in the differential for lytic bone lesions, this lesion is highly suspicious for osteosarcoma and the patient should be referred to an orthopedic oncologist.
Answer 4: The MRI studies show a complex multilobulated bone neoplasm with soft tissue extension consistent with osteosarcoma. There is no indication for open reduction internal fixation of this lesion at this time.
