## Question

Nine months ago, you performed a latarjet procedure on a 22-year-old professional hockey player after his third traumatic shoulder dislocation. He was compliant with post-operative instructions and had been doing well until recently when he began having more pain and feelings of apprehension and subjective instability. A CT scan is performed to evaluate for healing and positioning of the graft and is shown in Figures A & B. What is the most likely explanation for this patient's complication?

## Options

1. Axillary nerve injury
2. Graft placement too lateral on glenoid face
3. Coracoid graft resorption
4. Nonunion of bony transfer
5. Overly aggressive rehabilitation protocol

## Correct option

**3. Coracoid graft resorption**

## Answer explanation

Both the patient's history and CT findings are concerning for latarjet graft resorption.

Anterior shoulder instability is one of the most common shoulder injuries that orthopedic surgeons are tasked with managing. Almost all cases have an associated anterior labral tear. Importantly, recent studies have demonstrated the need to quantify the degree of anterior glenoid bone loss in these injuries. As glenoid bone loss increases, so too does the need for bone augmenting surgery. The latarjet procedure is a classic treatment option, however, is associated with a higher rate of complications than soft tissue procedures. Graft resorption occurs rather frequently and is more commonly recognized to affect the superior aspect of the graft. It is unclear if graft resorption negatively affects outcomes or increases rates of recurrent dislocation. If revision is ultimately necessary, hardware removal with iliac crest autograft or distal tibial allograft may be utilized.

*Cartucho et al. reviewed the evaluation and management of failed shoulder instability surgery for all-comers, including latarjet.* *They start by noting that 80% of failures are a result of performing a soft-tissue procedure in the setting of unrecognized bone loss.* *They discuss indications for bone block procedures including latarjet and iliac crest grafts, and further discuss management options in cases where these surgeries fail.*

*Willemot et al. published a JAAOS review article on bony anterior glenoid reconstruction in the setting of recurrent anterior shoulder instability.* *They discuss the surgical technique and pearls of latarjet transfer, but note that complications have created increased interest in other auto- and allograft options like iliac crest and distal tibia.* *They also discuss the use of allogenic osteochondral grafts but note that challenges with availability, cost, and disease transmission have slowed their acceptance among surgeons.*

Figures A & B demonstrate CT findings of coracoid graft resorption about the superior half of the graft.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While axillary nerve injury can occur iatrogenically during latarjet transfer and can cause deltoid incompetence, it would not explain the CT findings of graft resorption and the delay in symptoms up to nine months.
Answer 2: On the CT, the coracoid graft is appropriately placed on the surface of the glenoid. If if placement were too lateral, the complication would be glenohumeral arthritis rather than feelings of instability.
Answer 4: CT findings demonstrate bony union of the coracoid transfer but show the bony resorption taking place superiorly.
Answer 5: While overly aggressive rehabilitation can be detrimental to latarjet transfer, this would not explain the new finding of graft resorption nine months post-operatively.
