## Question

A 24-year-old female presents with lateral ankle swelling, pain, and subjective instability for the past 3 months. She states that she rolled her ankle on a hike and could not walk for a week, but gradually returned to normal activities. Before her injury, she denies any ankle/leg pain. Her left ankle has excessive laxity with anterior drawer testing in both plantarflexion and dorsiflexion. Figures A and B show her radiographs obtained during a recent urgent care clinic visit. The patient denies any history of cancer and denies knowing of any bone disorders. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Functional rehabilitation; primary focus on proprioception
2. Referral to an orthopaedic oncologist for evaluation and lesion biopsy
3. Core needle biopsy of the lesion within the distal tibia under sedation
4. Brostrom procedure; primary repair and imbrication of torn ligaments
5. Extended intralesional curettage with bone void filling as needed

## Correct option

**1. Functional rehabilitation; primary focus on proprioception**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with chronic lateral ankle instability, which should be treated with a trial of nonoperative management to include functional rehabilitation focusing on proprioception (Answer A). The incidentally found enchondroma is a benign tumor that does not require any intervention in asymptomatic individuals.

Enchondromas are non-metastatic intramedullary tumors that usually present during the third or fourth decade of life as an asymptomatic, incidental finding on radiographs. They are the most common primary bone tumor of the hand but are also encountered in long bones, including the femur, humerus, or tibia. Regional pain around an enchondroma is most often caused by another underlying pathology rather than the enchondroma itself. In this case, the patient denied any antecedent pain before her injury, and all her symptoms are secondary to her ankle sprain rather than the enchondroma. Standard non-operative treatment of her lateral ankle sprain is the most appropriate management. The majority of enchondromas do not require any management. In rare cases, larger enchondromas may incur persistent symptoms despite appropriate management of regional pathology, involve night pain, or demonstrate radiographic progression. In such cases, the true diagnosis may be low-grade chondrosarcoma. Therefore, unremitting or increasing pain warrants close follow-up with advanced diagnostic imaging and a referral to an orthopaedic oncologist as indicated.

*Wells and colleagues provided an overview of conventional cartilaginous tumors and their respective diagnostic workup and treatment.* *They review the spectrum of cartilage tumors, including enchondromas, atypical cartilaginous tumors (ACTs; grade 1 chondrosarcoma of the extremities), high-grade chondrosarcomas, and their respective treatment options.* **They conclude** that differentiating between chondroid tumors may be difficult, and any ambiguity warrants evaluation by a musculoskeletal oncology multidisciplinary team.

*Levy and colleagues provided a retrospective review regarding the causes of pain in benign solitary enchondromas of the proximal humerus.* *Among their cohort of 57 patients, 82% were found to have regional causes for the pain identified on MRI alone, including shoulder impingement, rotator cuff tears, bicipital tendinitis, or other relevant pathology.* **The authors conclude** that solitary enchondromas of the proximal humerus are often incidental findings and pain in the area is most often caused by adjacent regional pathology rather than enchondromas.

*Akoh and colleagues provided a similar retrospective review regarding long bone enchondromas referred to their institution.* *Among their cohort of 55 patients, only 3 exhibited continued growth on radiographic follow-up, and only 1 tumor was found to be a grade 1 chondrosarcoma.* **The authors conclude** that given the low risk for malignant transformation, surveillance with plain radiographic follow-up every 3-6 months for the first year and then annually for at least 3 years of total follow-up is appropriate.

Figures A and B demonstrate this patient’s left ankle with a solitary enchondroma contained within the intramedullary bone of the distal tibia.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: As with all acute and chronic lateral ankle instability cases, conservative management is the first step in treatment. This includes early functional rehabilitation with a focus on proprioceptive training. Patients who fail this nonoperative management are indicated for the Brostrom procedure, which only involves a small subset of patients (<10%).
Answers 3&4: If a patient has radiographs suggestive of primary bone malignancy, they should be promptly referred to an orthopaedic oncologist for biopsy and treatment. Multiple studies have shown that patients should undergo a biopsy by the surgeon who will be performing the tumor resection. This avoids the hazards of improper biopsy technique, which is associated with an increased morbidity (i.e. increased dissection and need for flap coverage).
Answer 5: Atypical cartilaginous tumors (ACTs; low-grade chondrosarcomas in the extremities) appear as large enchondromas that may be sizable (>5cm) and display endosteal scalloping on MRI. However, they are NOT accompanied by aggressive features such as a large extraosseous soft tissue mass, which would be considered diagnostic for high-grade chondrosarcoma. ACTs can be initially treated with observation and serial follow-up in asymptomatic cases. However, symptomatic cases may require extended intralesional curettage with bone void filling as needed.
