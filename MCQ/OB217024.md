## Question

A 20-year-old collegiate soccer player sustains a non-contact twisting injury to her knee during the conference championship. MRI was obtained confirming an isolated complete disruption of her anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). Upon presentation to the clinic, the patient, who is majoring in biomechanical engineering, inquires on how the kinematics have changed in her knee now that it is ACL deficient. What changes would be expected in her ACL deficient knee compared to her unaffected knee?

## Options

1. Smaller range of anteroposterior translation of the medial femoral condyle
2. Lateral femoral condyle located significantly more anterior during full extension
3. Increased paradoxical anterior femoral translation during progressive knee flexion
4. Different axial rotation pattern during the first 30 degrees of flexion
5. No change in kinematics

## Correct option

**3. Increased paradoxical anterior femoral translation during progressive knee flexion**

## Answer explanation

ACL deficient knees have increased paradoxical anterior femoral translation during progressive knee flexion when compared to normal knees.

The kinematics of the tibiofemoral articulation change dramatically after an ACL disruption. The ACL functions as the primary restraint to anterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur. It also acts as a secondary restraint to tibial rotation and varus/valgus rotation. These alterations in kinematics cause aberrant joint loading and are contributing factors to the joint degeneration observed in ACL-deficient(ACLD) patients. These changes also lead to symptoms of instability, which may cause meniscal injury and/or osteochondral lesions. ACL reconstruction is the mainstay of treatment to prevent these sequelae.

*Murayama et al. investigated tibiofemoral motion in the medial and lateral compartments of both ACL-deficient (ACLD) knees and uninjured knees using in vivo three-dimensional (3D) dynamic motion.* **They found** that ACLD knees had a significantly more posteriorly located lateral femoral condyle during full extension than in contralateral uninjured knees. **They also found** a greater range of anteroposterior translation of the medial femoral condyle when compared to contralateral uninjured knees.

*Dennis et al. used dynamic fluoroscopic surveillance to accurately determine the three-dimensional (3D), in vivo, weight-bearing kinematics of normal and ACLD knees.* **They found** a considerable difference between the center of rotation locations of the normal and ACLD subjects, with ACLD subjects exhibiting substantially higher variance in kinematic patterns. Particularly the ACLD knees had more paradoxical anterior femoral translation and a different axial rotation pattern after 30 degrees of knee flexion.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: There is a larger range of anteroposterior translation of the medial femoral condyle in the ACLD knee.
Answer 2: The lateral femoral condyle is located more posteriorly during full extension in the ACLD knee.
Answer 4: The axial rotation pattern during the first 30 degrees of flexion is similar in both the ACLD knee and the unaffected knee. After 30 degrees of flexion, the axial rotation pattern exhibits different behaviors in the ACLD knee.
Answer 5: There are various kinematic changes in the ACL deficient knee.
