## Question

A 51-year-old male presents to the clinic after his masseuse pointed out a palpable mass along his right volar forearm. He notes that it is not particularly painful when palpated, but he has had progressively worsening paresthesias about the thumb, index, and middle fingers over the last year. On exam, there is a positive Tinel's sign along the same distribution. ## Options

1. Chemotherapy alone given degree of nerve involvement
2. Wide resection with reconstruction of the affected nerve
3. Marginal resection with excision parallel to the nerve fascicles
4. Marginal resection with adjuvant radiation
5. Marginal resection with adjuvant chemotherapy

## Correct option

**3. Marginal resection with excision parallel to the nerve fascicles**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a neurilemmoma (or Schwannoma) of the volar forearm involving the median nerve, the treatment for which is marginal resection with careful lesional excision parallel to the involved nerve fascicles.

Neurilemmomas are benign, encapsulated nerve sheath tumors composed of Schwann cells that typically occur on the surface of peripheral nerves, most commonly along the flexor surfaces of the extremities or along the head and neck. They present in the third to sixth decade of life, with the most common symptom being paresthesias along the distribution of the affected nerve. Diagnosis is primarily made by physical exam and imaging, while confirmatory biopsy shows histologic evidence of palisading Verocay bodies (Figure B), which is pathognomonic for the disease, and immunostaining, which is positive for S100. Treatment is observation when asymptomatic, but marginal resection with preservation of the involved nerve is recommended when progressive symptoms are present.

*Lee et al. reviewed the prevalence of classic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings of intramuscular peripheral nerve sheath tumors from a cohort of 30 patients.* **The authors found** that all intramuscular Schwannomas revealed two or more classic MRI findings, which include the split fat sign, fascicular sign, target sign, entering and exiting nerve, and thin hyperintense rim. **They concluded** that intramuscular Schwannomas can be easily diagnosed based on MRI.

*Kwee et al. reviewed calcified or ossified benign soft tissue lesions that may simulate malignancy.* In terms of a variant of Schwannoma, the Ancient Schwannoma, **the authors note** that the lesions are usually long-standing, slow-growing, and usually located deep in the head and neck, chest, retroperitoneum, pelvis, and extremities of elderly patients, making them diagnostic challenges. **They also note** that Ancient Schwannomas typically show cystic necrosis and calcifications, which can lead them to be mistaken for soft tissue sarcomas. **They conclude** that in these cases, biopsy and pathological analysis are warranted.

Figure A is a sagittal, enhanced fluid-sensitive sequence demonstrating the typical features of a Schwannoma with T2 hyperintensity and contrast enhancement. Figure B is a representative histological slide showing the pathognomonic palisading Verocay bodies.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Chemotherapy is not indicated in the treatment of Schwannomas.
Answer 2: En-bloc resection that includes the entirety of the involved median nerve segment is typically not indicated in benign lesions and would be excessively morbid, likely leading to permanent deficit.
Answer 4: Marginal resection with primary repair/nerve conduit can be done if part of the nerve is resected, but post-operative radiation is not indicated for Schwannomas.
Answer 5: Wide resection is excessively morbid and not indicated for benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors.
