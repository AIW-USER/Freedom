## Question

A 33-year-old male presents to your clinic with complaints of shoulder pain and stiffness that have been present for the last 6-9 months. He works in construction and is frequently required to lift >50 pounds. Radiographs in the office demonstrate moderate glenohumeral osteoarthritis. In an effort to avoid shoulder arthroplasty in this active, high-demand patient, you recommend shoulder arthroscopy with extensive debridement and capsular release. Which of the following is true regarding the use of shoulder arthroscopy to treat shoulder osteoarthritis?

## Options

1. There is a 45% rate of conversion to total shoulder arthroplasty within 1 year
2. Higher rates of failure are associated with increasing posterior glenoid wear
3. Patients >50 years of age have better patient reported outcomes
4. Presence of bipolar osteoarthrits does not affect failure rates
5. Best results are noted with reaming of glenoid and avoidance of aggressive humeral debridement

## Correct option

**2. Higher rates of failure are associated with increasing posterior glenoid wear**

## Answer explanation

In patients with glenohumeral joint arthritis, posterior glenoid wear is a negative prognostic factor for improvement when treated with shoulder arthroscopy debridement.

Arthritis of the glenohumeral joint in young patients presents a difficult problem for orthopedic surgeons as, traditionally, arthroplasty is a more reliable result but may not be feasible in younger, active patients. These patients tend to complain of pain and stiffness with difficulty performing their daily duties. Arthroscopic debridement has been advocated by some as a temporizing measure to improve pain and range of motion in younger patients with glenohumeral arthritis and has recently been further expanded to include capsular releases, osteoplasty, nerve decompression and other associated procedures. Studies have shown that younger patients with less severe arthritis and little joint space narrowing have better outcomes after arthroscopic debridement. A higher degree of glenoid retroversion and posterior glenoid wear also represents a significant risk factor for failure of arthroscopic management and contributes to further arthritic progression. While for some patients this is a reasonable treatment strategy, there is a high (20%) rate of conversion to shoulder arthroplasty within 1 year due to treatment failure.

*Mitchell et al. performed a case-control study evaluating risk factors for treatment failure of arthroscopic shoulder debridement for osteoarthritis.* *In 107 shoulders, with 2 year follow-up,* **they noted** that 16% of shoulders progressed to shoulder arthroplasty and risk factors for failure included less preoperative joint space, higher Kellgren-Lawrence OA grades, age >50, and B2/C glenoid morphology. **They concluded** that while comprehensive arthroscopic management of osteoarthritic shoulders can provide improvement in pain and function, there are also limitations that can cause early failure and should be appreciated pre-operatively.

*Van Thiel et al. performed a retrospective review of arthroscopic management of glenohumeral arthritis.* *They evaluated 81 patients and 16/71 (22%) patients who followed up progressed to arthroplasty at an average of 10 months.* **They noted** that patients with less pre-operative joint space and bipolar lesions had a higher rate of failure, although those patients who did not progress to shoulder arthroplasty were doing quite well at follow-up.

*Skelley et al. evaluated the use of arthroscopic debridement and capsular release in treating shoulder OA.* *In 33 patients, they noticed initial improvements in pain and range of motion, which quickly deteriorated about 4 months after surgery.* Over 60% of patients reported being unsatisfied with the outcome of their procedures, therefore, **they concluded** that they could not justify arthroscopic debridement and capsular release in this patient population.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Rate of conversion to total shoulder arthroplasty is approximately 20% at 1 year.
Answer 3: Younger patients <50 years of age tend to have better results with arthroscopic debridement.
Answer 4: Bipolar osteoarthritis (humeral head and glenoid) is a negative prognostic indicator for improvement after arthroscopic debridement.
Answer 5: With extensive arthroscopic debridement, both the glenoid and the humeral head should be aggressively treated in order to prevent progression and improve function as much as possible.
