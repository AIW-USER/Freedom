## Question

## Options

1. A 23-year-old pitcher with pain in the overhead position, loss of external rotation and radiographic findings shown in Figure A
2. A 45-year-old manual laborer with shoulder pain, loss of external rotation and radiographic findings shown in Figure B, which are found to be irreparable in the operating room
3. A 47-year-old manual laborer with shoulder pain, loss of internal rotation and radiographic findings shown in Figure C, which are found to be irreparable in the operating room
4. A 65-year-old patient with pain and loss of range of motion and radiographic findings shown in Figure D
5. A 66-year-old patient with pain and loss of range of motion and radiographic findings shown in Figure E

## Correct option

**2. A 45-year-old manual laborer with shoulder pain, loss of external rotation and radiographic findings shown in Figure B, which are found to be irreparable in the operating room**

## Answer explanation

A lower trapezius tendon transfer is best used in the treatment of an irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tear in middle-aged patients who engage in manual labor (Answer 3). The other patients are best treated with alternative surgical interventions.

Rotator cuff tears are a common source of shoulder pain and dysfunction. The etiology of this pathology ranges from acute injury to chronic degeneration, and there is a direct relationship between prevalence and age; in fact, over 65% of individuals over the age of 70 have evidence of a full-thickness rotator cuff tear! The risk factors for rotator cuff tears include age, tobacco use and hypercholesterolemia. The best treatment is dependent on a variety of factors including, but not limited to, patient age, chronicity of tear, the presence/absence of fatty degeneration and the presence/absence of glenohumeral arthritis. Recently, tendon transfer has gained acceptance as a treatment option for middle-aged individuals with irreparable rotator cuff tears without evidence of glenohumeral arthritis. In patients with chronic subscapularis tears, a pectoralis major transfer has demonstrated the ability to restore internal rotation motion and strength. In patients with large, irreparable posterosuperior (supraspinatus +/- infraspinatus) tears, lower trapezius or latissimus dorsi tendon transfer can be used to restore external rotation motion and strength.

Omid and Lee provide a review of tendon transfers for irreparable rotator cuff tears. The authors note that for irreparable rotator cuff tears in patients with an intact subscapularis and no signs of glenohumeral arthritis, lower trapezius or latissimus tendon transfer has the ability to both relieve pain and improve functional outcomes. The authors note that patient selection is critical and that patients must be counseled that functional benefit is a less reliable outcome than pain relief. They note that pectoralis major tendon transfers are best reserved for patients with irreparable subscapularis tears, and are not indicated for irreparable postero-superior rotator cuff tears.

*Elhassan et al. performed a scoping review on the management of failed rotator cuff repairs.* **The authors note** that optimal management depends on a variety of patient-specific factors. **The authors highlight** the lower trapezius and latissimus dorsi transfers as options for patients who failed attempted repair of posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, and that these options are particularly attractive in higher-demand patients who are too young for arthroplasty.

*Broida et al. performed a systematic review of the literature regarding latissimus dorsi tendon transfer for irreparable rotator cuff tears.* *Their study ultimately included 10 studies for synthesis.* **They note** that subscapularis deficiency, advanced teres minor atrophy and the need for revision surgery were correlated with poor functional outcomes. Overall, while the results were promising, **the authors highlight** the importance of counseling patients that full pain relief and return to function should not be expected.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: This patient with a SLAP tear would be best treated with either a SLAP repair or a biceps tenodesis.
Answer 3: This middle-aged laborer with an irreparable subscapularis tear and intact supraspinatus would be best treated with a pectoralis major tendon transfer.
Answer 4: This geriatric patient with glenohumeral arthritis would best be treated with an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA).
Answer 5: This geriatric patient with cuff tear arthropathy would best be treated with a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).
