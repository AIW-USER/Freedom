## Question

A 12-year-old male presents to your clinic with complaints of right shoulder pain. He is a right-handed pitcher for his little league team. He localizes the pain over the lateral aspect of his throwing shoulder and says it has been slowly worsening for several months now to the point where he can no longer pitch without pain. His dad stresses that he needs to get back to pitching because so he can get participate in college showcases. His radiograph is seen in Figure A. Which of the following is true regarding this patient's most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Most commonly affects proliferative zone of physis
2. Deficit in shoulder internal rotation increases risk of recurrence
3. Stress to physis greatest in early cocking phase of throwing cycle
4. Associated with partial articular rotator cuff tears
5. Percentage of curveballs thrown is most important risk factor

## Correct option

**2. Deficit in shoulder internal rotation increases risk of recurrence**

## Answer explanation

Little league shoulder is common in young overhead throwers. Those with glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) are at higher risk of physeal injury and recurrence.

Little league shoulder is thought to be due to injury to the hypertrophic zone of the proximal humerus physis from the excessive torque during overhead throwing. This injury occurs in the late cocking and deceleration phases of the throwing cycle. At the extremes of external rotation during the late cocking phase, significant torque is placed through the physis which can be exaggerated with an imbalance in shoulder flexibility and a decrease in internal rotation. Patients complain of pain over the lateral shoulder and the diagnosis can often be confirmed radiographically with lateral widening of the proximal humerus physis compared to the contralateral shoulder. The most important risk factor is the overall number of pitches the athlete is throwing. Treatment includes rest and physical therapy with immediate and complete cessation of throwing.

*Harada et al. reviewed conservative management of little league shoulder and the factors that related to recurrence of pain and inability to return to baseball in 87 youth baseball players.* **They noted** that 95% of players fully returned to sport at an average of 2.8 months, with pain recurring in 25% around 6 months. Factors associated with a slow return to play and recurrence included poor shoulder flexibility and longer time from diagnosis to complete cessation of throwing.

*Heyworth et al. reviewed the diagnosis and management of little league shoulder by evaluating 95 patients who were diagnosed at their institution (avg age 13.1).* Treatment included rest and physical therapy. The average time to symptom improvement was 2.6 months, and the return to competition averaged 4.2 months. GIRD was recognized in 30% of the study population and 14% of the group with GIRD had a later recurrence, making the likelihood of recurrence 3.6x greater in those with associated internal rotation deficit.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of a skeletally immature right shoulder showing widening of lateral physis representative of little league shoulder.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The hypertrophic zone of the physis is affected in little league shoulder.
Answer 3: Physeal stress is greatest to the proximal humerus in the late cocking and deceleration phases of throwing given the rotatory forces and eccentric stresses, respectively.
Answer 4: Given the patient's age and presentation, there is a very low likelihood of a concomitant rotator cuff tear.
Answer 5: The overall number of pitches thrown is the greatest risk factor for developing little league shoulder while breaking pitches have not been shown to be worse on the shoulder compared to fastballs.
