## Question

A 54-year-old male presents to the orthopedic clinic for follow-up regarding a 10-month history of right shoulder pain. The patient’s surgical history includes a left shoulder arthroscopic debridement with an open subpectoral biceps tenodesis 5 years prior. His physical exam and shoulder MRI are consistent with biceps tendonitis. A prior ultrasound-guided steroid injection in the bicipital groove gave 90% pain relief for approximately 4 weeks. He is indicated for a right shoulder diagnostic arthroscopy with arthroscopic suprapectoral biceps tenodesis. How should the patient be counseled regarding his expected outcome after arthroscopic biceps tenodesis compared to his prior open biceps tenodesis?

## Options

1. Higher rates of anterior shoulder pain after arthroscopic tenodesis
2. Lower rate of Popeye deformity after arthroscopic tenodesis
3. Higher measured biceps strength after arthroscopic tenodesis
4. Equivalent patient-reported outcomes after arthroscopic tenodesis
5. Higher rate of neurologic complication after arthroscopic tenodesis

## Correct option

**4. Equivalent patient-reported outcomes after arthroscopic tenodesis**

## Answer explanation

The long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) is a common pain generator in the shoulder. Several studies have shown no difference in re-operation or patient-reported outcomes when comparing open subpectoral tenodesis to arthroscopic suprapectoral tenodesis.

The LHBT can be symptomatic secondary to inflammatory, traumatic, or degenerative etiologies. Patients present with anterior shoulder pain and positive LHBT exam findings (bicipital groove pain, Speed, and Yergason’s Tests). Initial management includes activity modification, physical therapy, and steroid injections. Biceps tenodesis involves detaching the LHBT from the superior labrum and re-attaching it to the humerus. The open subpectoral technique attaches the tendon distal in the bicipital groove while arthroscopic techniques typically insert the tendon proximally in the groove. Several studies have compared these operative techniques. These studies consistently show no difference in pain, Popeye deformity, strength, range of motion, or patient-reported outcomes.

*Forsythe et al. published the results of 73 patients randomized to subpectoral or suprapectoral tenodesis.* *The authors had previously published the one-year data showing no difference in pain or strength.* *The mean follow-up in this updated study was 2.9 years.* **The authors reported** no statistically significant differences in patient-reported outcomes, complications, or revision surgery. They do not make a formal recommendation for either surgical approach.

*Hughes et al. published the results of a retrospective review of 100 patients who underwent either isolated supraspinatus repair (57/100), supraspinatus repair with biceps tenotomy (16/100), supraspinatus repair and arthroscopic biceps tenodesis (18/100), or supraspinatus repair and open biceps tenodesis (9/100).* There were no significant differences between the groups for complications, patient-reported outcomes, range of motion, or strength.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is no consistent difference found in anterior shoulder pain between the two techniques.
Answer 2: There is no consistent difference found in Popeye deformity between the two techniques.
Answer 3: There is no consistent difference found in biceps or arm strength between the two techniques.
Answer 5: There is no increase in neurologic complications with a suprapectoral technique.
