## Question

You are evaluating a 27-year-old male who sustained a left femoral shaft fracture (Figure A) in a motor vehicle collision. As a part of his workup, you have obtained plain film images of the left femur, pelvis, and ipsilateral hip. The patient is now undergoing computed tomography (CT) examinations to include his pelvis. What finding on CT indicates the presence of an occult femoral neck fracture?

![[q217332-a-injury-femur-ap.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Gull Wing sign
2. CT can only identify occult femoral neck fractures if a fracture line is present
3. Greater than 1 mm capsular distension
4. Alpha angle greater than 60 degrees
5. Subtrochanteric lateral cortical flaring

## Correct option

**3. Greater than 1 mm capsular distension**

## Answer explanation

A difference of >1mm capsular distension between injured and uninjured sides indicates the presence of an occult femoral neck fracture in the setting of a high-energy, ipsilateral femoral shaft fracture.

Ipsilateral femoral neck fractures should be considered in all high-energy femoral shaft fractures, as they have a reported incidence of 0-15% and are missed 19-31% of the time. These associated injuries are often basicervical, vertical, and nondisplaced in nature. The lack of displacement is due to the majority of energy being dissipated through the femoral shaft. One method of detecting an occult femoral neck fracture is the CT Capsular Sign: a difference of >1mm capsular distension between injured and uninjured sides on the axial soft tissue window.

*Park et al. performed a retrospective study in which* **they identified** 13 occult femoral neck fractures among 79 patients who sustained high-energy femoral shaft fractures. Using the CT Capsular Sign on soft tissue window (a difference of >1mm capsular distension between injured and uninjured sides), 12 of 13 occult femoral neck fractures had the Capsular Sign present while a false positive was present in 4 of 66 patients who did not have an occult femoral neck fracture. **The authors concluded** that the CT Capsular Sign is a reliable way to identify occult femoral neck fractures in the setting of high-energy femoral shaft fractures.

*Rogers et al. reviewed whether the preoperative diagnosis of ipsilateral femoral neck fractures in patients with high-energy femoral shaft fractures can be improved with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) compared with radiographic and CT imaging.* **They reported** that rapid limited-sequence MRI of the pelvis for patients with femoral shaft fractures identified femoral neck fractures that were not diagnosed on thin-cut high-resolution CT in 12% of patients. **They concluded** that the frequency of femoral neck fractures may be underrepresented on CT imaging in polytraumatized patients. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Gull-wing sign represents the impaction of the superior acetabular roof in the presence of a posterior wall acetabulum fracture.
Answer 2: In addition to visualizing a non-displaced femoral neck fracture line, capsular distension can also indicate the presence of an occult femoral neck fracture.
Answer 4: This finding indicates a cam lesion.
Answer 5: Subtrochanteric lateral cortical flaring can be seen on plain films – no CT needed – and is present in the setting of atypical subtrochanteric femur fractures associated with bisphosphonate use.
