## Question

A sixty-year-old patient presents with long-standing left-sided knee pain that has not improved despite a trial of conservative measures including NSAIDs, corticosteroid injections, physical therapy, and activity modifications. On physical exam, he does not display any ligamentous laxity and has an intact extensor mechanism. His presenting radiographs are shown in Figure A. Which of the following implant designs would be most appropriate for this patient undergoing a total knee replacement?

## Answer explanation

This patient has a history of patellectomy without any other signs of ligamentous instability. Although both cruciate retaining (CR) and posterior stabilizing (PS) implants have been used as primary total knee implants in this cohort of patients, PS implants remain the gold standard.

It has been proposed that the anterior-posterior intrinsic stability of the knee is part of a dynamic system consisting of the extensor mechanism (patellar tendon, patella, and quadriceps tendon) anterior cruciate ligament, and posterior cruciate ligament. It believed that patients who have undergone prior patellectomy have increased AP instability compared to a standard arthritic knee. Therefore, these patients have been classically treated with increasing constrained implants, PS in particular. However, some newer studies have shown similar functional outcome scores and implant survival rates utilizing CR implants. While there remains a continued debate, either implant may serve as an appropriate option for this patient with an intact extensor mechanism and otherwise ligamentous stable knee. If the patient had additional ligamentous instability, further constrained implants would be warranted.

*Dodds and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their cohort of patients undergoing total knee replacement with a history of prior patellectomy.* **They found** that patients treated with CR implants had higher rates of early revision compared to PS knees due to instability. While they acknowledged their very low sample size, **they concluded** that posterior stabilized knees serve as a more appropriate implant option.

*Reinhardt and colleagues presented their cohort of 23 patients treated with CR implants in the setting of prior patellectomy.* **They reported** 84% 10-year survivorship with similar functional outcomes compared to other studies using PS implants. **They conclude** that CR implants serve as a viable option for this patient cohort.

Figure A shows AP and lateral imaging of a left knee with a history of prior patellectomy. Figure B displays a posterior stabilized (PS) total knee arthroplasty. Figure C displays a posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasty with an additional nonhinged constrained design with high post polyethylene. Figure D displays a constrained, rotating hinged total knee arthroplasty implant. Figure E displays a proximal tibial replacement implant. Figure F displays a distal femoral replacement implant.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Figure C displays a posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasty with an additional nonhinged constrained design. This can be appreciated by the metal post seen between the femoral and tibial components. This metal post is held within a "high/large/tall" tibial post held within a deeper femoral box. This high tibial post provides additional varus/valgus stability in patients with laxity in the medial or lateral cruciate ligaments.
Answer 3: Figure D displays a constrained, rotating hinged total knee arthroplasty implant. This is often utilized for patients with global ligamentous instability and most often serves as a salvage revision arthroplasty implant. However, some primary indications include patients with hyperextension instability (polio patients) or bone loss in a neuropathic joint.
Answer 4: Figure E displays a proximal tibial replacement implant. This is a constrained, rotating hinged type construct with a proximal tibial replacement tibial component. This is most often utilized in tumor surgery and requires patellar tendon repair to the prosthesis. Unfortunately, this implant is fraught with long-term complications including aseptic loosening, extensor tendon weakness/deficiencies, and infection. It is not indicated in an uncomplicated prior patellectomy patient.
Answer 5: Figure F displays a distal femoral replacement implant. This is a constrained, rotating hinged type construct with a distal femur replacement component. It is most often utilized in the setting of excessive bone loss within the distal femur after multiple TKA revision attempts or highly comminuted distal femur fractures in the elderly not amenable to fixation.
