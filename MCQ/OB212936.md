## Question

Lightly brushing each abdominal quadrant does not cause the umbilicus to move in any direction. Radiographs from 1 year ago are shown in Figures A and B. ## Options

1. Curve progression; correction with growing rods
2. Pulmonary compromise; posterior spinal fusion
3. Curve progression; 24-hour per day bracing
4. Spinal cord abnormalities; MRI of the entire spine
5. Progressive pelvic tilt; anterior and posterior spinal fusion

## Correct option

**4. Spinal cord abnormalities; MRI of the entire spine**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with progressive juvenile idiopathic scoliosis and absence of the abdominal reflex. The best next step would be to perform a whole spine MRI to identify any spinal cord abnormalities.

Juvenile idiopathic scoliosis is an early onset spinal deformity occurring between ages 3 to 10 years. There is a high risk of curve progression as well as cord abnormalities such as Arnold-Chiari malformation, syringomyelia, tethered cord, and spinal cord tumors. Full spine MRI should be performed in patients with any neurologic abnormalities or rapid curve progression. Abdominal reflexes are elicited by gently stroking each quadrant of the abdomen and observing for a contraction of the abdominal muscles; in other words, the umbilicus should move towards the quadrant being tested. While absent abdominal reflexes can be a normal finding in patients, asymmetric reflexes are more concerning for spinal cord abnormalities. Treatment should consist of growth preserving constructs or spinal fusion when the curve exceeds 50 degrees.

*Pahys et al.* *2009 performed a retrospective review of 54 patients presenting with infantile idiopathic scoliosis.* **The authors found** a 13% prevalence of spinal cord abnormalities on MRI with 71.4% of these patients requiring neurosurgical intervention. Additionally, no difference was found between patients with normal and abnormal MRIs with regards to the age at diagnosis, curve magnitude, and direction or location of the main curve. **The authors concluded** that routine MRI screening in this patient population is not necessary and should be done based on rapid curve progression or neurologic changes.

*Pereira et al.* *2017 performed a retrospective review of 71 patients with idiopathic scoliosis under the age of 11 years.* **The authors found** that 5.6% of patients had abnormal spine MRIs with abnormal abdominal reflexes being the only associated finding. **The authors recommended** routine MRI screening in idiopathic scoliosis patients under the age of 11, especially in the presence of neurologic deficits, due to the incidence of cord anomalies and the relatively low cost of the study.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The curve in this patient has progressed and would likely require surgical correction. However, given the neurologic deficits, assessment of the spinal cord for any abnormalities must be performed first.
Answer 2: Progressive scoliosis has been associated with a progressive decline in pulmonary function, but correcting the deformity with fusion is undesirable given the growth potential in this patient. Furthermore, posterior spinal fusion places this patient at risk for crankshaft phenomenon.
Answer 3: The curve has progressed significantly over the span of 1 year and will continue to progress until puberty. Ideally, this curve should be corrected with a growing rod construct but addressing spinal cord abnormalities takes a higher priority.
Answer 5: Pelvic tilt can be exacerbated by the worsening curve, and in many cases can be corrected by addressing the coronal deformity of the spine. The lack of abdominal reflexes warrants investigation into whether a spinal cord abnormality is present. Anterior and posterior spinal fusion would avoid crankshaft phenomenon in patients with greater growth potential.
