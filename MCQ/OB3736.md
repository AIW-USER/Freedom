## Question

A 32-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with two days of worsening back and leg pain after feeling a sharp pop in her low back after an episode of coughing. She complains of numbness in her perianal region and bilateral buttocks that she notices when wiping herself with toilet paper after urinating. She also reports she is having difficulty urinating and had some episodes of incontinence. She complains of bilateral leg pain that is worse on the left in the region of the anterolateral calf. On physical exam she has weakness to big toe extension of 3/5 and is unable to walk on her heels with the toes elevated. MRI of her spine is shown in figures A through C. After surgical intervention, which of her symptoms is least likely to improve?

## Options

1. Big toe extension weakness
2. Bladder dysfuction
3. Loss of perianal sensation
4. Numbness in the left foot
5. Burning pain in left leg

## Correct option

**2. Bladder dysfuction**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with clinical symptoms suggestive of cauda equina syndrome secondary to a large lumbar disc herniation. After decompression, bladder dysfunction is the symptom that is least likely to improve.

Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is most commonly secondary to a large disc herniation and typically presents with saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg pain, weakness in the lower extremities, and bowel and bladder dysfunction. The most common bladder symptom is that of urinary retention. Disruption of bladder contraction and sensation leads to urinary retention and eventually to overflow incontinence. Studies suggest that decompression within 48 hours leads to improved outcomes and return of function. Bladder dysfunction is least likely to improve, as it may represent a more severe and longer duration of neural compression.

*Korse et al. retrospectively reviewed patients with CES who underwent surgery prospectively evaluated bladder, bowel, and sexual function with questionnaires at long term follow-up at mean of 13.8 years after surgery.* **They found** a 38% rate of bladder dysfunction, 43% of defecation dysfunction, and 54% of sexual dysfunction.

*Spector et al. reviewed the clinical presentation and treatment of cauda equina syndrome.* *They explain that bladder control is mediated by the parasympathetic, sympathetic, and somatic nervous system.* The parasympathetic system typically promotes bladder emptying, the sympathetic system bladder storage, the somatic system the external sphincter. CES leads to a lower motor neuron disturbance causes both sensory and motor loss to the bladder leading overflow incontinence and retention.

*McCarthy et al. retrospectively evaluated factors affecting long term outcomes after cauda equina syndrome.* *In their series, symptom duration before intervention and speed of onset did not affect two-year outcome.* Thirteen patients who failed their post operative void test had worse outcomes.

Figure A is a sagittal T2 MRI showing a large disc herniation at L4-L5 and a smaller herniation at L5-S1. Figure B and C are axial cuts at L4-L5 and L5-S1 respectively, with left paracentral disc herniations displacing the cauda equina.
