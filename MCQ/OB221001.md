## Question

A 28-year-old recreational basketball player presents with persistent medial ankle pain and instability for 18 months following an inversion injury. Despite physical therapy and activity modification, he continues to experience deep ankle pain with cutting maneuvers and a sensation of "giving way." Physical examination reveals positive anterior drawer and talar tilt tests. MRI demonstrates a 1.8 centimeter medial talar dome osteochondral lesion with an intact overlying cartilage layer, but a 12 millimeter subchondral cyst; the lesion is located in the posteromedial aspect of the talar dome. Which of the following represents the most appropriate surgical approach and treatment strategy?

## Options

1. Arthroscopic microfracture of the lesion with concomitant modified Broström lateral ligament repair
2. Osteochondral autograft transplantation via medial malleolar osteotomy with staged lateral ligament reconstruction 6 months later
3. Osteochondral autograft transplantation via medial malleolar osteotomy with concomitant lateral ligament repair
4. Retrograde drilling of the subchondral cyst alone
5. Autologous chondrocyte implantation via anterior arthrotomy alone

## Correct option

**3. Osteochondral autograft transplantation via medial malleolar osteotomy with concomitant lateral ligament repair**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with chronic lateral ankle instability and a 1.8 cm posteromedial talar dome osteochondral lesion with a subchondral cyst. The most appropriate management is osteochondral autograft transplantation through a medial malleolar osteotomy with concomitant lateral ligament repair and subchondral bone grafting (Answer 3).

Chronic ankle instability contributes to osteochondral defect formation through repetitive microtrauma caused by abnormal joint mechanics. Recurrent episodes of pathologic talar motion and altered loading lead to cumulative injury of the articular cartilage and underlying subchondral bone, which helps explain why 32 to 56% of patients with chronic lateral ankle instability develop osteochondral lesions. Medial talar dome lesions, accounting for approximately 68% of cases, are particularly common despite lateral ligament injury, as repetitive talar tilt and translation during inversion create impaction forces along the medial dome. Osteochondral autograft or other restorative techniques are preferred over microfracture for larger lesions greater than 10 mm in diameter, 100 mm² in area, or 5 mm in depth, cystic or uncontained defects, failed prior bone marrow stimulation, lesions larger than 50 to 60 mm² in patients with chronic ankle instability, and typically deeper medial talar lesions.

*Paget et al. published a multicenter, double-blind, randomized trial of 100 patients with ankle osteoarthritis, comparing two intra-articular PRP injections with a saline placebo and following patients for 26 weeks.* *Both groups improved similarly, with no significant between-group difference and no differences in secondary outcomes, and the effect size was well below the minimal clinically important difference.* *PRP provided no benefit over placebo, a finding that persisted at one-year follow-up.*

*Massri-Pugin et al. performed a cadaveric arthroscopic study on sequentially sectioned syndesmotic ligaments and applied a 100 N lateral hook test to determine the threshold for coronal plane instability and the optimal measurement site within the incisura.* Significant posterior incisura diastasis occurred only after complete disruption of the AITFL, IOL, and PITFL, whereas anterior diastasis required additional deltoid ligament injury. Complete syndesmotic disruption is therefore necessary for arthroscopically detectable coronal instability, best assessed at the posterior third of the incisura, and anterior widening suggests concomitant deltoid injury.

*Hurley et al. reported on the International Consensus Meeting on Cartilage Repair of the Ankle issuing 14 statements supporting osteochondral autograft transplantation for talar lesions larger than 1.0 to 1.5 cm², cystic or uncontained defects, and failed bone marrow stimulation,* **recommending** the ipsilateral knee as the preferred donor site with graft depth of 12 to 15 mm and perpendicular access with flush placement to optimize outcomes. Failure rates increase for lesions greater than 2.25 cm², though short to mid-term results are good to excellent in approximately 80 to 87% of patients, with donor site morbidity up to 15%. While OAT provides superior structural restoration compared with marrow stimulation, graft cartilage may not fully replicate native hyaline cartilage long term.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Microfracture is contraindicated for lesions >1.5 cm² and cystic lesions with subchondral bone loss.
Answer 2: Staging the ligament reconstruction is inappropriate; consensus supports simultaneous treatment of instability to prevent continued pathologic loading.
Answer 4: Retrograde drilling alone is insufficient for this large lesion with an intact cartilage cap and a significant cystic component.
Answer 5: Autologous chondrocyte implantation requires two-stage surgery and is not the primary recommendation for this lesion size.
