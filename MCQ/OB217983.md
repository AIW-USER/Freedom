## Question

Figure A is the MRI of a 13-year-old baseball pitcher that presents with complaints of posterolateral elbow pain that is aggravated with throwing. On examination, he has full range of motion and strength. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment for this patient?

![[q217983-a-ocd-1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Immediate initiation of a throwing program for the next 6-12 weeks focusing on proper throwing mechanics
2. Cessation of activity for 3-6 weeks followed by slow progression back to activities over next 6-12 weeks
3. Casting of the elbow for 6-8 weeks followed by slow progression back to activities over next 6-12 weeks
4. Arthroscopic microfracture or drilling
5. Osteochondral allograft transplantation

## Correct option

**2. Cessation of activity for 3-6 weeks followed by slow progression back to activities over next 6-12 weeks**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a Type I Osteochondritis Dissecans (OCD) lesion of the elbow. Initial treatment is nonoperative with cessation of activity for 3-6 weeks followed by slow progression back to activities over next 6-12 weeks.

OCD of the elbow is a localized injury and subsequent separation of articular cartilage and subchondral bone of the capitellum, most commonly seen in gymnasts and pitchers. Diagnosis can be made with plain radiographs (generally with a slightly flexed elbow view) but MRI studies can be helpful to evaluate for the size of lesion and extent of bony edema. Treatment can be nonoperative or operative depending on size of the lesion, stability of the lesion, age and skeletal maturity of the patient and presence of loose bodies. Nonoperative management is recommended for type I lesions (stable fragments). Arthroscopic microfracture or drilling of capitellum is indicated in unstable type I lesions and in stable type II lesions. Fixation of the lesion is indicated in large lesions that are incompletely displaced. Arthroscopic debridement and loose body excision is indicated in unstable type II lesion and in type III lesions. Osteochondral autograft or allograft transplantation surgery (OATS) is indicated in large type II and III capitellar lesions which engage the radial head, or in uncontained lesions may require size-matched fresh allograft.

*Takahara et al. reviewed the outcomes and to determine the most useful classification for the choice of treatment.* **They reported** that OCDs of the capitellum can be classified as stable or unstable. Stable lesions that healed completely with elbow rest had all of the following findings at the time of the initial presentation: an open capitellar growth plate, localized flattening or radiolucency of the subchondral bone, and good elbow motion. **They noted** that unstable lesions, for which surgery provided significantly better results, had one of the following findings: a capitellum with a closed growth plate, fragmentation, or restriction of elbow motion of >/=20 degrees. **They concluded** that for large unstable lesions, fragment fixation or reconstruction of the articular surface leads to better results than simple excision.

*Ruchelsman et al. reviewed OCDs of the capitellum.* **They reported** that lesion size, location, and grade determine management, which should attempt to address subchondral bone loss and articular cartilage damage. **They noted** that early, stable lesions are managed with rest, while surgery should be considered for unstable lesions. **They concluded** that longer-term follow-up studies are necessary to fully assess surgical management, and patients must be counseled appropriately.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Nonoperative management for a type I OCD lesion most commonly involves cessation of activity for 3-6 weeks followed by slow progression back to activities over next 6-12 weeks.
Answer 3: Casting for 6-8 weeks would be too long of immobilization and lead to elbow stiffness.
Answer 4: Arthroscopic microfracture or drilling of capitellum is indicated in unstable type I lesions and in stable type II lesions.
