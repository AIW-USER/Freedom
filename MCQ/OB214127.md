## Question

A 37-year-old intravenous drug user presents with an 8-day history of fevers, chills, back pain, and difficulty with ambulation. On examination, he is febrile to 102.7 F, exhibits full strength and normal sensation in the lower extremity but has a positive Hoffman's sign. Laboratory markers reveal an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 100 mm/h and C-reactive protein of 55 mg/L. Lumbar spine radiographs are obtained and are unrevealing. A lumbar MRI reveals a spinal epidural abscess from L3-L5. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Observation for deterioration of neurological function
2. Biopsy of L3-L5 spinal epidural abscess
3. Urgent irrigation and debridement of lumbar spine
4. XR of cervical spine
5. MRI of cervical and thoracic spine

## Correct option

**5. MRI of cervical and thoracic spine**

## Answer explanation

In this patient with a delayed diagnosis of a lumbar spinal epidural abscess in the setting of fevers, high inflammatory markers, and myelopathic signs, the most appropriate next step is an MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine to look for skipped lesions above the lumbar spine.

Simultaneous noncontiguous spinal epidural abscesses outside of the lumbar spine (also known as skip lesions) in cases of lumbar spinal epidural abscesses pose a great diagnostic challenge, especially if the patient demonstrates symptoms that are concordant with lumbar MRI findings. Failing to detect a skip lesion can lead to continued sepsis or neurologic decline. Thus, MRI or CT imaging (when MRI contraindicated) of the entire spine is warranted. A concomitant cervical or thoracic spinal epidural abscess requires urgent decompression to prevent sepsis and/or neurologic decline.

*Kim et al. investigated independent predictors of failure of nonoperative management of spinal epidural abscesses.* **They reported** that age older than 65 years, diabetes, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) were independent risk factors for failure. **They concluded** that when these risk factors are absent, nonoperative management of spinal epidural abscesses may be considered as the initial line of treatment with close monitoring.

*Ju et al. formulated a set of clinical and laboratory predictors for identifying patients with concurrent noncontiguous spinal epidural abscesses.* **They reported** that a delay in presentation (i.e. symptoms ≥7 days), a concomitant area of infection outside the spine and paraspinal region, and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of >95 mm/h at presentation were significant predictors for noncontiguous spread. **They concluded** that these three predictors may be a useful tool in predicting the risk of a skip lesion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation without assessing for skip lesions would be inappropriate.
Answer 2 and 3: While a lumbar spinal epidural abscess without neurological symptoms may be treated nonoperatively with IV antibiotics, it is imperative to assess for skipped lesions which may need urgent decompression.
Answer 4: A cervical spine XR is not the modality of choice to assess for skipped lesions.
