## Question

When utilizing the Joshi's External Stabilization System (JESS) for a neglected clubfoot in a 2-year-old, which biomechanical principle is prioritized to prevent the development of a 'rock-bottom' deformity during the distraction phase?

## Options

1. Rapid distraction of the posterior ankle capsule at a rate of 2 mm per day.
2. Maintaining the foot in a plantigrade position throughout the entire frame duration
3. Simultaneous distraction of the calcaneocuboid and talonavicular joints before addressing equinus.
4. Rigid fixation of the L-rod to the tibial component to prevent any ankle motion.

## Correct option

**3. Simultaneous distraction of the calcaneocuboid and talonavicular joints before addressing equinus.**

## Answer explanation

Correcting the midfoot components (adduction and varus) before attempting to correct equinus ensures the midfoot is stabilized as a single unit, preventing a breach at the midtarsal joint.

Preventing the "Rock-Bottom" Deformity in JESS

Rationale and Surgical Principles

The "Rock-Bottom" (or reverse clubfoot) deformity is a catastrophic complication where the foot breaks at the midtarsal joint (the midfoot) instead of the ankle joint.

The Mechanical Failure:

If one attempts to correct equinus
(pulling the heel down) while the midfoot is still in varus and adduction, the force of distraction is transmitted through the flexible midfoot. The tight Achilles tendon acts as a fulcrum, causing the midfoot to "hinge" open while the heel stays stuck in equinus.

The Surgical Principle (The "Unit" Concept):

 - Forefoot-Hindfoot Alignment: You must first correct the adduction and varus to align the forefoot with the hindfoot. This effectively turns the foot into a single, rigid lever arm.

 - Stabilizing the Midfoot: By distracting the medial side (JESS Distractor between the calcaneus and first metatarsal) first, you stretch the medial soft tissues and lock the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints into a reduced position.

 - Correcting Equinus Last: Once the foot is a "single block," the force applied to correct equinus is transmitted directly to the posterior ankle capsule and the Achilles tendon. The entire "block" moves as one, ensuring the heel comes down without the midfoot collapsing.

Answer: 3
