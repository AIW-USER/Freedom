## Question

A 24-year-old male is using a waterjet cutting tool when he gets distracted, and the waterjet hits his ungloved right hand. He is brought to the ER, where he is diagnosed with a high-pressure injection injury. The patient complains of pain and paresthesias in his hand but is able to weakly flex and extend all of his fingers. Antibiotic and tetanus prophylaxis is administered, and the patient is admitted for observation. Four hours later, he begins complaining of increasingly severe hand pain that is not relieved with increasing doses of pain medication. Which of the following is the next best step?

## Options

1. Cold compresses and continued observation
2. Blood and wound cultures with stat Gram stain
3. Immediate surgical debridement of the entry site only
4. Immediate surgical debridement of the entire zone of injury
5. Amputation

## Correct option

**4. Immediate surgical debridement of the entire zone of injury**

## Answer explanation

In the setting of a high-pressure water injection injury complicated by the development of compartment syndrome, surgical debridement of the entire zone of injury is indicated (Answer 4).

High-pressure injection injuries may appear innocuous given the small wound size; however, the astute clinician must be aware that the zone of injury often involves a wider area than initially observed. The force delivered through injection pressure is the precipitant cause of injury, ranging from 3,000 to 10,000 psi. High pressures cause the injected material to spread along planes of least resistance, which tend to follow neurovascular bundles. Injury to vascular structures can precipitate a local inflammatory reaction similar to a typical compartment syndrome, and the nature of the injected material delivers a second hit by inducing further inflammation, potentially leading to thrombosis and ischemia. While most injection injuries require immediate wide surgical debridement of the entire zone of injury, injection of water and air are the only substances where nonsurgical management with antibiotic and tetanus prophylaxis may be a reasonable initial treatment option. However, the development of compartment syndrome is an indication for emergent fasciotomy and debridement.

*Amsdell and Hammert published a 2013 review detailing the treatment options for high-pressure injection injuries.* *The authors performed a retrospective literature review and detailed the presentation, pathophysiology, and treatment methods.* **The authors state** that historically, there has been some success in treating injections of water or air nonoperatively - although air and water can still cause dissection of tissue planes, these two substances are not toxic and cause less tissue inflammation and damage compared to other injected materials. However, clinical suspicion should remain high for compartment syndrome, and fasciotomies may still ultimately be required.

*Rosenwasser and Wei published a 2014 JAAOS review on high-pressure injection injuries.* This publication emphasized similar points as the Amsdell and Hammert article, **highlighting** the nature of the injected substance as the most significant prognostic factor. Many commonly injected fluids are intrinsically cytotoxic; however, organic solvents such as paint thinner, diesel fuel, gasoline, and oil are particularly pro-inflammatory, leading some authors to recommend primary amputation as the initial treatment. Nonsurgical treatment is a reasonable initial treatment plan reserved only for injections of air and water; these injuries may be managed expectantly, but compartment syndrome is an absolute indication for surgical decompression.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has developed compartment syndrome. Continued observation is not an appropriate treatment.
Answer 3: This patient has developed compartment syndrome. An infection workup is not the next best step.
Answer 3: In the setting of compartment syndrome, the entire zone of injury must be explored and debrided, not just the entry site.
Answer 5: Amputation is reserved only as a last resort when wide surgical debridement has failed to save the appendage.
