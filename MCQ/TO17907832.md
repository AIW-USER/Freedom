## Question

Commonest complication you expect 5 years after a Total Shoulder Arthroplasty

## Options

1. Loosening of glenoid component
2. Loosening of humeral component
3. Acromion stress fracture
4. Infection

## Correct option

**1. Loosening of glenoid component**

## Answer explanation

Answer is a) Loosening of glenoid component

Total Shoulder Arthroplasty (TSA) involves replacement of the humeral head coupled with a glenoid resurfacing usually performed via the deltopectoral approach (no significant differences in outcomes between subscapularis peel, lesser tuberosity osteotomy, subscapularis tenotomy).

Glenoid being very minimally restrained stability greatly rests on proper functioning of soft tissues. Any dysfunction is particularly liable to place greater shearing stress on glenoid making it more susceptible to loosening.

Indications for the procedure:

- Glenohumeral chondral wear from Primary Osteoarthritis or Inflammatory arthritis (particularly RA)
- Patients with primary or recurrent shoulder dislocation who are at an increased risk for the development of glenohumeral osteoarthritis
- Complicated proximal humerus fractures unamenable for osteosynthesis

Contraindications: -

Precisely speaking, the only absolute contraindications to TSA is active infection

Relative contraindications

- neuropathic arthropathy (Charcot arthropathy)
- severe brachial plexopathy
- intractable shoulder instability
- severe glenoid bone loss (in an erosion of the glenoid down till the coracoid, glenoid resurfacing is almost impossible).
- patients whose medical comorbidities make them too great a risk for surgery.
- concomitant rotator cuff and deltoid dysfunction

Although an intact and functional rotator cuff is imperative for proper functioning of a TSA, deficiency has to be dealt in following manner:-

- if rotator cuff is deficient and proximal migration of humerus is seen on x-rays (rotator cuff arthropathy) then glenoid resurfacing is contraindicated
- if there is an irreparable rotator cuff deficiency then proceed with hemiarthroplasty or a reverse shoulder arthroplasty
- in an isolated supraspinatus tear without retraction, one may proceed with TSA

Component positioning:-

GLENOID COMPONENT

Polyethylene backed components are preferred as glenoid is not thick enough for supporting metal backed.

The component is best positioned in Neutral version (acceptable 5o anteversion to 10o retroversion).

Accurate glenoid positioning is the most critical factor in success of TSA and is most challenging. Firstly, glenoid bone stock is very limited and then further reduced because of wear from arthritis. Secondly, anatomical position is poorly understood and there is high patient to patient variability. And then very few accurate intra op landmarks/ guides are available. The blade of scapula that could be deterministic is often difficult to define in many patients.

Glenoid deficiency due to posterior glenoid wear from arthritis significantly adds to problem of component placement. Options like eccentric glenoid reaming or building posterior glenoid with grafts etc. need to considered to ensure correct version.

“Rocking-horse phenomenon” occurs in total shoulder arthroplasty when there is an abnormal glenoid version angle that causes glenoid component failure. Here anterior or posterior translation of the humeral head causes eccentric glenoid loading and lifting of the opposite, unloaded glenoid rim.

HUMERAL COMPONENT

Both cemented and porous coated uncemented stems are available. Preferable positioning is 20-40o retroversion. Valgus positioning of the stem and overstuffing of the joint is to be avoided as it puts the cuff under tension.

Final goal is to have the top of the humeral head 5 to 8 mm superior to the top of the greater tuberosity.

Complications of the procedure: -

- Stiffness (66%)
- Glenoid loosening is the most common significant complication and is the commonest cause of TSA failure (39%).
- Periprosthetic fracture involving greater tuberosity and humeral shaft (11%)
- Iatrogenic rotator cuff tears (7.7%) resulting from improper humeral neck osteotomy or overstuffing of joint
- Humeral loosening (6.5%), relatively rarer than glenoid and seen mostly in infection cases or RA or osteonecrosis patients
- Posterior instability (6%)
- Anterior instability (5%) due to subscapularis repair failure
- Neuro-vascular injury (4.8%); Arcuate artery, branch off the anterior humeral circumflex artery, can be damaged during biceps tendon elevation
- Infection (4.6%)

Management of Periprosthetic fractures: Best guided by Wright and Cofield classification of Periprosthetic fractures: -

Type A: Fracture is proximal to stem tip (usually tuberosities involved). Mostly managed conservatively unless there is significant overlap among fragments to suggest stem is loose which requires revision to long stem prosthesis.

Type B: Fracture is centered at tip of stem but may extend distally. Revise to long stem prosthesis.

Type C: Fracture located well distal to tip of stem. ORIF (plate overlap prosthesis by 2 cortical diameters to avoid stress riser)
