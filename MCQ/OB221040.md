## Question

An 18-year-old male is evaluated in the emergency department following a high-speed motorcycle collision. The secondary survey reveals a leg injury, and radiographs are shown in Figures A and B. He has received opioid analgesia for pain control, but is alert and oriented, and his pain appears to be stable. Neurovascular exam does not demonstrate any abnormalities. His blood pressure is 142/98 mmHg, and compartment pressure measurements are performed, with the highest value being in the anterior compartment at 35 mmHg. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Emergent (<6 hrs) fasciotomy of all four leg compartments
2. Closed reduction and splint application with frequent serial clinical exams
3. Administration of additional analgesia with continued clinical monitoring alone
4. Emergent (<6 hrs) operative fixation open reduction internal fixation with intramedullary nail
5. Urgent (<24 hrs) operative stabilization with external fixator application

## Correct option

**2. Closed reduction and splint application with frequent serial clinical exams**

## Answer explanation

In this patient with a diastolic blood pressure of 98 mmHg and a measured anterior compartment pressure of 35 mmHg, the delta pressure (ΔP = diastolic BP − compartment pressure) is 63 mmHg, which exceeds the 30 mmHg threshold for fasciotomy. Although an absolute compartment pressure above 30 mmHg has been cited in some historic studies as a threshold for fasciotomy, this is poorly specific for acute compartment syndrome and potentially leads to unnecessary fasciotomies. In a patient who can participate reliably in the examination, has stable pain, and has an intact neurovascular exam, the most appropriate next step is closed reduction and splint application, with serial clinical reassessments (Answer 2). Repeat compartment pressure measurements may be indicated if the patient is unable to provide a reliable exam (e.g., due to altered mentation), or in cases of equivocal exam findings.

Acute compartment syndrome of the leg is a devastating lower extremity condition in which osseofascial compartment pressure rises to a level that decreases perfusion and may lead to irreversible muscle and neurovascular damage. Diagnosis relies on severe, progressive leg pain that worsens with passive ankle motion, along with firmness and decreased compressibility of the compartments; needle compartment pressure measurement is the diagnostic standard in cases with inconclusive physical examination findings or in sedated or unreliable patients. The threshold for operative decompression is a delta pressure of 30 mmHg or less between the compartment pressures and diastolic blood pressure, and observation is appropriate when the delta pressure exceeds this threshold and the clinical presentation is not unequivocally consistent with compartment syndrome. Fractures account for the majority of cases of acute compartment syndrome, and young patients are at the highest risk, with the highest prevalence in those aged 12 to 19 years.

*McMillan et al. reviewed the available literature on diagnostic modalities for acute compartment syndrome, examining options including compartment pressure monitoring, near-infrared spectroscopy, pulsed phased-locked loop ultrasound, intramuscular pH and glucose monitoring, and serum biomarkers.* **They found** that intracompartmental pressure monitoring remains the gold standard objective diagnostic method but lacks diagnostic specificity and could lead to unnecessary fasciotomy when used as the sole criterion for diagnosis, with single-measurement approaches associated with a significant false-positive rate; continuous pressure monitoring, which captures trends over time, reduces this risk and demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity. **They concluded** that pressure monitoring is ideally used when clinical suspicion is raised, rather than as a routine screening tool, and that the trend in compartment pressure over time is much more useful than a single pressure reading.

*Frink et al. reviewed the anatomy, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of compartment syndrome of the lower leg and foot, including surgical approaches for decompression.* **They found** that fasciotomy is indicated when the difference between compartment pressure and diastolic blood pressure is less than 30 mmHg or when clinical symptoms are unambiguous, and that in uncertain cases repeated clinical examinations at least every hour are essential, given that irreversible damage may occur within 3 hours. **They concluded** that the most important step in diagnosing compartment syndrome is clinician awareness, combined with appropriate repeated examination, and that fasciotomy of the lower leg may be performed via a single lateral incision or combined anterolateral and posteromedial incisions, depending on the clinical situation.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs, respectively, of a displaced distal tibia shaft fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Emergent fasciotomy is indicated when the delta pressure is 30 mmHg or less, or when clinical findings are unambiguously consistent with compartment syndrome. In this patient, the delta pressure is 63 mmHg, and the clinical examination is reassuring, making fasciotomy premature and potentially subjecting the patient to unnecessary operative morbidity, including wound complications, the need for delayed closure, and skin grafting.
Answer 3: Administration of additional analgesia is not appropriate as a primary management strategy in a patient being monitored for acute compartment syndrome, as it may mask the cardinal symptom of progressive pain and obscure clinical deterioration that would otherwise prompt reassessment and repeat pressure measurement.
Answer 4: Emergent operative fixation with an intramedullary nail is not indicated at this stage. While this may be an appropriate definitive treatment, the immediate priority in this patient is serial monitoring to ensure the compartment pressure threshold for fasciotomy is not crossed.
Answer 5: Urgent stabilization with external fixator application is similarly not the appropriate next step in a hemodynamically stable patient without a met operative threshold for compartment syndrome. External fixation may be appropriate in the context of severe soft tissue injury, vascular compromise, or damage control scenarios, none of which are present in this patient.
