## Question

While on call at the local rural community hospital, you're called by an emergency medicine colleague. He states he has a 30-year-old lumberjack who earlier today was playing softball in the county championship when he slid into home plate in the bottom of the 9th inning. While celebrating the historic victory, he noticed his finger was deformed and painful. He came to the ER at that point to be evaluated. On exam, he is neurovascularly intact. Which of the following acute fracture patterns would best be treated with open reduction and internal fixation?

## Answer explanation

Figure D demonstrates a displaced unicondylar phalanx fracture, which is unstable and would best be treated with open reduction and internal fixation.

Phalanx fractures account for 10% of all fractures and are more common in males than females. The treatment of the fracture can be with buddy taping, splinting, CRPP, or ORIF. Often treatment is dictated by the stability of the fracture with stable fractures allowing for nonoperative treatment. Unicondylar and bicondylar fractures are often unstable and require operative fixation. Extra-articular fractures with <10° angulation, <2mm shortening, and no rotational deformity can be treated with conservative measures.

*Pun et al. treated 52 unstable fractures of the proximal or middle phalanx with A.O. mini-screws and plates.* *61.5% of fractures were open, 40% had concomitant significant soft tissue injury.* *20% were treated with screws only, while the remaining were treated with plate and screw fixation.* **They found** that only 26.9% of fractures had a good result. Fractures without significant soft tissue injury had significantly better results. Complications included intra-articular penetration with fixation, hardware failure, wound dehiscence, nonunion, unsatisfactory reduction, and loss of range of motion. **The authors conclude** that plate and screw fixation leads to unsatisfactory outcomes and frequent complications.

*Kawamura and Chung reviewed fixation choices for closed unstable oblique phalangeal and metacarpal fractures.* *They included percutaneous K-wires, external fixation, and open reduction and internal fixation.* **They recommend** the use of percutaneous K-wire fixation for oblique phalangeal fractures when an adequate reduction can be obtained, despite being less biomechanically rigid than other strategies. Immobilization of 3 weeks results in good functional outcomes.

Figure A shows an intra-articular fracture of the dorsal aspect of the distal phalanx, consistent with likely bony mallet finger. Figure B shows a fracture-dislocation of the distal interphalangeal joint with a small volar base fracture. Figure C is a nondisplaced extra-articular fracture of the distal phalanx. Figure D is a displaced unicondylar fracture of the proximal phalanx. Figure E is an extra-articular fracture of the proximal phalanx.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer A: This bony mallet finger should be treated with extension splinting until healed, likely 6-8 weeks.
Answer B: DIPJ fracture-dislocations with minimal articular involvement are best treated with closed reduction +/- splinting.
Answer C: This nondisplaced fracture of the distal phalanx does not require any treatment and will likely heal without issue. An alumifoam splint could be used to protect the phalanx and provide symptomatic relief for the patient.
Answer E: This fracture likely could be treated with buddy taping and splinting after an appropriate reduction is performed. This pattern is not an absolute indication for open reduction internal fixation.
