## Question

A 74-year-old female presents with complaints of persistent back pain for the past 2 months. She reports sustaining a fall down the stairs 10 months prior and was initially seen in the Emergency Department where imaging was obtained (Figure A). She was managed with rest and physical therapy. Following initial conservative management, she reported improvement in symptoms until the past 2 months. She denies any new injury. Figures B-D represent the MRI images obtained one week ago. What is the most likely etiology for her persistent back pain?

## Options

1. Infection in the disk or the vertebral body
2. Intravertebral disc herniations
3. Metastatic disease
4. Post-traumatic vertebral body osteonecrosis
5. Vertebral body nonunion

## Correct option

**4. Post-traumatic vertebral body osteonecrosis**

## Answer explanation

This patient initially sustained a vertebral body compression fracture. Ten months later, MRI imaging suggests avascular necrosis, also known as Kummell disease.

Vertebral compression fractures are the most common fragility fracture. A history of two prior vertebral compression fractures is the strongest predictor of future vertebral fractures in postmenopausal women. Avascular necrosis of the vertebral body may occur following compression fractures at the thoracolumbar junction. Classically, a patient will present with a symptomatic vertebral compression fracture that heals with a pain-free interval of months to years, followed by pain and increasing deformity and the pathognomonic intravertebral cleft. Treatment is based on the individual and no definitive recommendations exist.

*Formica et al. reviewed vertebral body osteonecrosis.* *In their literature review, they report on 81 articles and found that there were 45 studies based on the pathophysiology, 52 on diagnosis, and 38 on the treatment options.* **They concluded** that the literature on vertebral body osteonecrosis was limited and mainly focused on post-traumatic cases with considerable overlap with nonunion and pseudoarthrosis.

*Ma et al. reviewed post-traumatic osteonecrosis, also known as Kummell's disease, in a 75-year-old female who presented with significant thoracolumbar pain and delayed T12 collapse after a ground-level fall 4 months prior.* **They report** that a trial of medical management and therapy was attempted but unsuccessful and T12 vertebroplasty was performed. **They concluded** that surgical intervention may maintain vertebral height and sagittal alignment in patients refractory to conservative management.

Figure A is the sagittal CT image demonstrating a T11 vertebral body compression fracture. Figures B-C are the sagittal and coronal MRI images demonstrating a fluid-filled cavity with partial vertebral body collapse suggestive of vertebral body osteonecrosis, also known as Kummell disease. Illustration A is an example of an intravertebral cleft.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has no concerning symptoms for infectious etiology making this less likely
Answer 2: Intravertebral disc herniation, also known as Schmorl nodes, are often asymptomatic and unlikely to manifest as seen on this patient's MRI
Answer 3: Metastatic disease should always be considered, but in a patient with a previous compression fracture following a fall and MRI changes to the same vertebral body, AVN is more likely
Answer 5: Improvement in symptoms following the initial injury followed by new-onset pain makes vertebral body nonunion less likely
