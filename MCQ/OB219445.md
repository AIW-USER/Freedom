## Question

A 36-year-old male jiu-jitsu practitioner presents to your clinic with complaints of numbness in his thumb, index, and middle fingers. He says that the pain is worse after intense training sessions, an activity that requires repetitive forearm pronation and supination. On physical examination, the patient has a negative Tinel's sign at the wrist. Which of the following is the innervation of the structure that is the most likely source of this patient's symptoms?

## Options

1. Anterior interosseous nerve proper
2. Median nerve proper
3. Posterior interosseous nerve proper
4. Radial recurrent branch of the median nerve
5. Ulnar nerve proper

## Correct option

**2. Median nerve proper**

## Answer explanation

This middle-aged martial artist has symptoms consistent with pronator syndrome, in which the median nerve is compressed at the elbow (Answer 2).

Pronator syndrome is a compressive neuropathy of the median nerve at the elbow. It is a rare compressive neuropathy (<1 per 100,000 annually) and is common in individuals with well-developed forearm muscles (weightlifters, climbers, mixed martial artists). There are, overall, five potential sites of entrapment including:

1.) Supracondylar process, a residual osseous structure on the distal humerus present in 1% of the population (Illustration A).
2.) Ligament of Struthers, which travels from the tip of the supracondylar process to the medial epicondyle (Illustration B).
3.) Bicipital aponeurosis, also known as the lacertus fibrosus (Illustration C).
4.) Between the ulnar and humeral heads of the pronator teres, considered by many to be the most common site of entrapment (Illustration D).
5.) The FDS aponeurotic arch, is a rare site of entrapment (Illustration D).

Patients will often present with paresthesias in the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring fingers as seen in carpal tunnel syndrome. Despite the similarities in presentation, patients with pronator syndrome typically experience symptom exacerbation with repetitive prono-supination and also will have a negative Tinel's sign at the wrist. Patients will also typically complain of an aching pain over the proximal volar forearm, and sensory disturbances over the distribution of the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve and will deny nocturnal symptoms. The treatment of pronator syndrome almost always begins with a trial of nonoperative management including rest, splinting, and NSAIDs. Patients with symptoms recalcitrant to nonoperative management often undergo surgical decompression of the median nerve at all five possible sites of compression.

Strohl and Zelouf provide a review of compressive neuropathies of the upper extremity. The authors note that while carpal and cubital tunnel represents the most common compressive neuropathies, clinicians must remain vigilant of less common syndromes including Guyon's canal syndrome, PIN syndrome, or pronator syndrome. The authors note that patients with pronator syndrome often do not experience nocturnal symptoms (which help to differentiate it from Carpal Tunnel Syndrome), and will also have symptoms that worsen with forearm prono-supination.

Adler and Wolf provide an overview of pronator syndrome. The authors note that symptoms of pronator syndrome often mimic those of carpal tunnel syndrome, and note that patients often have negative electrodiagnostic studies. The authors conclude that all patients should undergo initial nonoperative management and state that although there is a lack of high-quality prospective data, retrospective studies support surgical decompression in patients with recalcitrant symptoms despite appropriate nonoperative management.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The AIN proper innervates the deep forearm muscle including the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus, flexor pollicis longus and pronator quadratus. It does not provide sensory function.
Answer 3: The PIN proper innervates the common and deep extensors of the forearm, none of which can cause pronator syndrome. further, it does not provide sensory function.
Answer 4: The radial recurrent branch of the median nerve innervates opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, and the superficial aspect of flexor pollicis brevis.
Answer 5: The ulnar nerve innervates flexor carpi ulnaris, flexor digitorum profundus to the ring and small fingers, adductor pollicis, the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis, and numerous interossei and hypothenar muscles. A common site of ulnar nerve entrapment is between the two heads of FCU.
