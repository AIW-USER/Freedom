## Question

A 68-year-old female, approximately 3 months after total knee arthroplasty presents to your clinic with knee pain and a large anterior wound after missing her first follow-up appointments. On examination, she has a full-thickness soft tissue defect of about 5 cm over the anterior knee with drainage. Upon closer examination, the medial aspect of the tibial component is exposed in the wound. Based on the obvious defect, you decide that soft tissue reconstruction is required in this patient along with implant removal and antibiotic spacer insertion. Which of the following is true regarding the indicated soft tissue reconstruction?

## Options

1. This patient will need a free muscle flap at the stage one procedure
2. A lateral gastrocnemius flap is indicated in this patient
3. The medial sural artery provides arterial supply to the indicated flap
4. A soleus flap is indicated in this patient
5. The defect should be covered with a fasciocutaneous flap

## Correct option

**3. The medial sural artery provides arterial supply to the indicated flap**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a large soft tissue defect associated with a total knee arthroplasty prosthetic joint infection. In this case, the patient would need a medial gastrocnemius flap, which is supplied by the medial sural artery (Answer 3).

Prosthetic joint infections (PJI) with soft tissue defects provide a particular set of challenges. The soft tissue reconstructive ladder must be considered in all patients presenting with this issue. For large defects with exposed hardware, as in this case, flap reconstruction is necessary to provide the best opportunity to eradicate the infection and avoid limb salvage procedures. The medial gastrocnemius flap is the workhorse flap for large anteromedial knee wounds. The local pedicled rotational medial gastrocnemius muscle flap provides several advantages, including a large arc of rotation, significant size, solid blood supply, and minimal risk of nerve injury during harvest. Because the medial gastrocnemius flap is pedicled by the medial sural artery, reliable delivery of antibiotics and humoral defense factors can be expected. Given that this patient has a draining wound with exposed hardware 3 months after arthroplasty implantation, a diagnosis of prosthetic joint infection (PJI) must be made. Therefore, a two-stage revision is indicated in this patient with implant removal, antibiotic spacer insertion, and soft tissue reconstruction at stage one. The patient will receive tailored intravenous antibiotics and eventual second-stage implantation after ensuring the eradication of infection.

*Corten et al. published a retrospective study on the outcomes and complications of gastrocnemius flap in stage one of two-stage revisions for infected TKAs.* *The study featured 24 patients undergoing medical gastrocnemius flap at explantation with an average 4.5-year follow-up.* **The authors found** that 22 of the 24 patients (92%) had successful flap healing and eventual successful second-stage reconstruction. **The authors concluded** that the medial gastrocnemius flap reliably provided adequate vascularization and wound healing in the setting of revision arthroplasty for PJI.

*Parvizi et al. provided updated criteria for periprosthetic joint infection in 2018.* The evidence-based criteria were designed to facilitate the diagnosis and treatment of PJIs. The new criteria feature several major criteria, minor criteria, and inconclusive criteria. With the presence of any of the two major criteria, two positive cultures of the same organism or a sinus tract with evidence of communication with the joint or visualization of the prosthesis, a prosthetic joint infection diagnosis can be made. The updated criteria have a 98% sensitivity and specificity of 99.5%.

*Theil et al. published a retrospective study evaluating the medial and lateral gastrocnemius flap for soft tissue deficiencies in TKA infections.* *43 patients were followed for an average of 53 months, evaluating survival of the flap and knee function after coverage.* **The authors reported** 71% and 63% infection-free survival at 2 and 5 years, respectively. **The authors concluded** that gastrocnemius flap coverage is a valid option for soft tissue coverage in the setting of large defects in PJI, although complications are not uncommon.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Free muscle flaps make up the highest rung on the reconstructive ladder. These require harvesting of muscle and vasculature removed from the injured knee and transplanting the muscle and vasculature into the recipient site. These complicated procedures should be reserved for situations where less complex reconstruction has failed or local tissue is inadequate for local flaps. Most authors would recommend this procedure be reserved for trained plastic and reconstructive surgeons. A free flap is not indicated in this patient as local pedicled flaps have not been trialed.
Answer 2: A lateral gastrocnemius flap can be used for reconstruction around the lateral aspect of the knee. The medial gastrocnemius flap provides better coverage for more anterior and medial wounds, as it offers a greater arc of rotation and surface area. Harvesting a lateral gastrocnemius flap poses an inherent risk to the common peroneal and tibial nerves. The lateral gastrocnemius flap is incorrect in this patient as the wound is anteromedial.
Answer 4: Soleus flaps are used for middle or distal third leg wounds. Because this patient has a defect in the proximal leg and anterior knee, a soleus flap would not provide the excursion for adequate coverage in this area.
Answer 5: Fasciocutaneous flaps consist of skin, fascia, and subcutaneous tissues. These flaps are used for larger skin defects (4-10cm) without exposed bone or implants. A fasciocutaneous flap is not the correct treatment in this scenario as this patient has a full-thickness defect with exposed bone and hardware.
