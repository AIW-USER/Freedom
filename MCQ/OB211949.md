## Question

A 75-year-old diabetic male patient underwent an anterior cervical decompression and fusion 2 years ago for cervical myeloradiculopathy. During his follow-up visit, new radiographs are obtained and are shown in Figure A. The patient currently denies any pain or clinical findings consistent with radiculopathy or myelopathy. What factors are most likely associated with these findings?

![[q211949-a-post-operative-ossification.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Diabetes
2. Placement of the cervical plate within 5 mm of the adjacent disc
3. Use static screws rather than variable angle screws in the anterior plate
4. Male sex
5. Age greater than 60 years at the time of surgery

## Correct option

**2. Placement of the cervical plate within 5 mm of the adjacent disc**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with adjacent segment ossification, which has been associated with the placement of the anterior cervical plate within 5 mm of the adjacent disc space.

Adjacent segment degeneration has a reported reoperation rate of 2.4% per year, however, there is speculation that this is in part due to the natural course of cervical degenerative disc disease rather than iatrogenic degeneration. Adjacent segment ossification is a potential complication of anterior cervical plating and fusion with unknown clinical significance. *Kim et al. described this as a separate entity from adjacent segment disease in that there is the preservation of disc height and lack of herniated disc material resulting in clinical radiculopathy of myelopathy.* Placement of the anterior plate greater than 5 mm from the adjacent disc space can reduce the risk of developing anterior ossification.

*Duan et al. performed a meta-analysis of 11 studies comparing zero-profile cervical cages to anterior cervical plating for cervical degenerative disc disease.* **They reported** that there was a significant decrease in postoperative dysphagia both at long-term and short-term follow-up, decreased intraoperative blood loss, increased improvement of Cobb angle in multi-level surgery, and greater improvement in Japanese Orthopedic Association scores compared to anterior cervical plating. However, **they observed** significantly higher subsidence rates with the zero-profile constructs, which the authors did not think was clinically significant. **They concluded** that the use of zero-profile cages appears to be a safer and more effective construct than anterior cervical plating with decreased postoperative dysphagia and increased subsidence rate.

*Lawrence et al. performed a systematic review of five studies examining factors associated with adjacent segment pathology following cervical spine arthrodesis.* From the included studies in the analysis, the authors deduced an annual incidence of clinically relevant adjacent segment disease (ASD) of 1.6% to 4.2% with a reoperation rate of 0.8% per year. **They reported** risk factors associated with the development of ASD being age <60 years at the time of surgery, fusion constructs that include 3-levels or less, and excluding C5-6 and C6-7 levels at the time of the index procedure. **The authors concluded** that ASD is a multifactorial process that spine surgeons must balance when considering treatment approaches for cervical degenerative disc disease.

*Lee et al. performed a retrospective study of 1038 patients undergoing cervical spine surgery for cervical degenerative disc disease to determine reoperation risk factors for adjacent segment disease.* **They reported** an annual reoperation incidence of 2.4% with the C5-6 segment having the highest risk. Multivariate analysis revealed female gender, smoking, and 2 or fewer segments included in the index procedure as significant risk factors adjacent segment disease. **The authors concluded** that 22% of patients may require reoperation at 10 years for adjacent segment disease, which appears to be a multifactorial process needing careful consideration of the risk factors preoperatively by the treating surgeon.

*Kim et al. performed a systematic review of 5 articles examining adjacent segment ossification development following anterior cervical plating, total disc arthroplasty, and noninstrumented fusion.* **They reported** an adjacent segment ossification rate of 41% - 64% following anterior plating and fusion, 24% following total disc arthroplasty, and 6% following noninstrumented fusion. For anterior plating, there was a significantly increased risk of ossification if the plat was positioned less than 5 mm from the adjacent endplate. **They concluded** that there is not enough evidence to make recommendations for the surgical treatment of adjacent segment ossification.

Figure A is the lateral cervical spine radiograph demonstrating a large anterior osteophyte from the C5 vertebral body making contact with the anterior cervical plate.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Diabetes has been linked to the development of Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH), but not to adjacent segment ossification.
Answer 3: Use of static screws would not permit the plate to settle closer to the adjacent segment postoperatively, thus would be protective of adjacent segment ossification. Variable angle screws allow for controlled subsidence for a theoretical benefit of improved fusion rates, but with the potential of settling within 5 mm of the adjacent disc space.
Answer 4: Male sex has not been associated with adjacent segment ossification or ASD. Female sex has been linked to ASD.
Answer 5: Patients that are younger, < 60 years, are at higher risk for ASD. However, age has not been correlated with adjacent segment ossification.
