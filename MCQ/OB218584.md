## Question

A 16-month-old male is being evaluated for leg pain in the emergency department. He is accompanied by his younger brother, his mother and her boyfriend. The patient's family states that 2 hours before presentation, the patient was outside chasing a butterfly when he slipped off a 10-inch high deck. Following his fall, he was inconsolable and unable to bear weight. On examination, the patient is tender to palpation along the entirety of his left lower extremity, and diffuse ecchymosis and swelling are present. His radiographs are available for review in Figures A and B. Which of the following is the next best step in the management of this patient?

## Options

1. Admission to the hospital with plans for next-day flexible nailing of the fracture
2. Admission to the hospital with plans for next-day open reduction and plate fixation of the fracture
3. Conscious sedation followed by reduction and long leg cast application
4. Conscious sedation followed by reduction and short leg cast application
5. Consultation to child protective services

## Correct option

**5. Consultation to child protective services**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a sub-acute diaphysial tibia fracture and a proximal metaphyseal corner fracture, findings highly suggestive of nonaccidental trauma (NAT). Early involvement of child protective services (CPS) is essential when managing patients with these injuries (Answer 5).

Pediatric Abuse/NAT is the second most common cause of death in children, and 50% of fractures in children under the age of 1 are directly attributable to abuse. 90% of fractures due to abuse occur in children younger than 5 years of age. There are a number of fracture "red flags" which, if present, should prompt an immediate child protective services workup. One such red flag is the presence of a classic metaphyseal lesion (CML), which occurs at the junction of the metaphysis and physis. Two subtypes of CMLs include corner fractures (Illustration A) and bucket handle fractures (Illustration B). If NAT is suspected, the immediate involvement of both pediatrics and CPS is essential; it is important to remember that all physicians are mandated reporters of child abuse.

*Flaherty et al. produced a practice guideline for evaluating children with fractures to ensure that the risk of missing child abuse is minimized.* **The authors report** that long bone fractures in non-ambulatory children, posterior rib fractures and the presence of CMLs should raise suspicion for a nefarious etiology. The authors, in this document, provide multiple guidelines and checklists to aid clinicians in performing a thorough and thoughtful survey in pediatric patients with fractures.

*Ranade et al. provide an overview of the role for the orthopaedic surgeon in the identification and management of NAT.* As mentioned above, **they note** that all physicians are mandated reporters of child abuse. *Similar to Flaherty et al., the authors provide a scoping review of the evaluation of children with suspected NAT and* **note** that clear and accurate documentation is paramount.

Figures A and B demonstrate a sub-acute distal tibia diaphyseal fracture with a concomitant proximal tibial CML. Illustration A demonstrates a classic corner fracture and B a bucket-handle fracture, two CMLs highly suggestive of NAT.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: while admission for surgery and/or conscious sedation may eventually be required, the next best and most crucial step is an immediate consultation to child protective services.
