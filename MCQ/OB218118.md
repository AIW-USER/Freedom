## Question

A 17-year-old high school quarterback is tackled behind the line of scrimmage for a loss. A facemask penalty was called as the defender was noted to forcefully pull the quarterback to the ground, bending his head toward his right shoulder, which forcefully recoiled when he hit the ground. Upon standing, the quarterback notes weakness in the right shoulder accompanied by a non-dermatomal pattern of paresthesias and tingling. Both sensations and the weakness resolve within minutes, the player has full cervical spine range of motion without tenderness on palpation, and he is permitted to go back into the game without restriction. Just before halftime he is tackled again and runs to the sideline with similar symptoms involving the right upper extremity, with shoulder and biceps weakness that resolve within five minutes. If he has a known history of one similar event just prior to scoring the game-winning touchdown last week, what is the next best step when determining the player's ability to return to the game?

## Options

1. Allow to return to play immediately
2. Allow to return to play without restrictions after halftime
3. Allow to return to play during this game once asymptomatic
4. Withhold from the remainder of the game and pursue further off-field workup
5. Withhold for two weeks and perform electrodiagnositc evaluation

## Correct option

**4. Withhold from the remainder of the game and pursue further off-field workup**

## Answer explanation

The patient is exhibiting a recent history of three stingers. Though transient with complete resolution of symptoms, multiple stingers warrant further neurologic workup, and the patient should be withheld from the remainder of this game until advanced cervical spine imaging can be performed.

Traumatic upper trunk brachial plexopathy, also known as a stinger or burner, is the most common upper extremity neurologic injury among athletes and most commonly involves the upper trunk. When a stinger is suspected, the physical examination should include an emphasis on testing the shoulder external rotator muscles, deltoid, and biceps, all of which are innervated by the upper trunk of the brachial plexus. Cervical spine hyperextension with or without concomitant lateral flexion is thought to compress or pinch the nerve root in the neuroforamen, thereby causing transient symptoms. It is generally accepted that the athlete may return to immediate play if complete symptomatic resolution is achieved with full motor strength and full painless cervical range of motion. However, if the player has two or more stingers in one game, it is recommended that he or she be withheld from the current sporting contest and re-revaluated at the end of the game. Symptoms persisting beyond this point warrant a full neurologic workup with imaging (Illustration A).

Ahearn, et al. provide a current concepts review article on the diagnosis and management of brachial plexopathy in athletes. The authors note that recent studies have shown the incidence of both acute and recurrent injuries to be higher in patients with certain anatomic changes in the cervical spine and that despite modern awareness, tackling techniques, and protective equipment, the incidence may be slowly on the rise in contact athletes. They conclude that though the severity of neurologic injury varies widely, stingers and burners usually do not result in significant loss of playing time or permanent neurologic deficits if appropriate management is undertaken.

Paulus, et al. provide a review of return-to-play considerations for cervical spine injuries in athletes. The authors propose a return-to-play algorithm (Illustration A) in which a second stinger during the same season requires being withheld for the remainder of the game, re-examined, and then allowed for a return to the subsequent game if no persistent deficits. while a third stinger, regardless of symptom duration, necessitates radiological evaluation of the cervical spine to assess for neuroforaminal or uncovertebral stenosis that may predispose the player to repeat injuries, or disc herniation with atypical radicular presentation.

Rosenthal, et al. published a review on return-to-play for sports-related trauma. In regard to stingers, the authors note that because the dorsal root ganglion occupies the largest proportion of space within the neural foramen, it often takes the brunt of the injury, which is why purely sensory findings may be the result of a stinger. They conclude that although the long-term natural history of athletes who have recurrent stingers is not well described in the scientific literature, some believe that recurrent episodes may lead to long-term proximal arm weakness and persistent pain.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3: Given that this player has sustained multiple stingers, despite the resolution of symptoms, allowing them to return to this game would be inappropriate prior to obtaining further neurologic workup.
Answer 5: Withholding the player for two weeks and performing an EMG/NCS would only be indicated in the setting of persistent weakness on exam.
