## Question

An infant first presents holding his limb in the position shown in Figure A. Birth history reveals that he was a large-for-gestational age infant and labor was prolonged. His orthopedist recommends passive stretching including shoulder abduction and external rotation. For which of the following scenarios is this an appropriate line of treatment?

![[q4732-a-fig-a-pic.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. One-month-old infant with partial antigravity biceps strength.
2. Four-month-old infant with scapula winging and elevated hemidiaphragm.
3. Seven-month-old infant with posterior glenoid dysplasia.
4. Eight-month-old infant with recent anterior Z-lengthening of the subscapularis tendon.
5. Five-month-old infant with recent ipsilateral latissimus dorsi and teres major transfer.

## Correct option

**1. One-month-old infant with partial antigravity biceps strength.**

## Answer explanation

This infant has obstetric brachial plexus palsy. If there is partial antigravity upper trunk muscle strength return in the first 2 months of life, there is good prognosis for complete recovery by the first 2 years. Surgery is not indicated.

Obstetric brachial plexus palsy is usually the result of a traction injury to the brachial plexus during vaginal delivery. Indications for surgery include absence of biceps function return associated with an upper trunk lesion, total plexopathy, and Horner's syndrome. Reconstruction for POSTGANGLIONIC (extraforaminal) lesions is done at 3-9 months and involves neuroma excision and sural nerve grafting. Reconstruction for PREGANGLIONIC (root avulsion) lesions is performed at 3 months and involves nerve transfers.

*Waters reviewed the management of pediatric brachial plexus palsy.* **He suggests** that partial antigravity biceps recovery by 2 months is a good prognostic indicator for complete recovery.

Figure A shows the classic "waiter's tip" posture of the hand (shoulder adduction, internal rotation, elbow extension, wrist and digit flexion). Illustration A shows brachial plexus nerve injuries of varying severity. Illustration B shows the 2 types of microsurgical reconstruction performed in brachial plexus lesions: (1) Cranial nerve XI nerve transfer to suprascapular nerve, and (2)sural nerve grafts from C5 and C6 to the anterior division (musculocutaneous nerve) and posterior division (radial and axillary nerves) of the upper trunk.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The presence of presence of winged scapula (long thoracic nerve) and elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic nerve) suggest a PRE-GANGLIONIC (ROOT AVULSION) lesion. Nerve transfer is indicated. Other signs of a pre-ganglionic lesion include Horner's syndrome (sympathetic chain), absence of rhomboid (dorsal scapular nerve), rotator cuff (suprascapular nerve), and latissimus dorsi (thoracodorsal nerve) function.
Answer 3: Surgery for Type III glenohumeral deformity involves anterior release of contractures, and posterior latissimus dorsi and teres tendon transfer.
Answer 4: Subscapularis lengthening is performed when external rotation cannot be achieved at 6-12months. Stretching a recently lengthened subscapularis can lead to failure.
Answer 5: This surgery is performed for Type III glenohumeral deformity.
