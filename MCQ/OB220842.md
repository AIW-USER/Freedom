## Question

A 48-year-old woman presents with a closed, unstable bimalleolar ankle fracture-dislocation and develops fracture blisters diffusely over the ankle. Relevant past medical history is significant for well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus without peripheral neuropathy. Which initial management strategy is LEAST likely to lead to secondary loss of reduction and skin necrosis in this scenario?

![[q220842-a-fracture-blisters.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Closed reduction with external fixation
2. Total contact casting with local wound care
3. Local wound care without immobilization
4. Closed reduction with application of splint
5. Percutaneous reduction and pinning of distal fibula

## Correct option

**1. Closed reduction with external fixation**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with an ankle fracture-dislocation complicated by fracture blisters. The management option least likely to lead to secondary loss of reduction and skin necrosis is closed reduction with external fixation (Answer 1).

Fracture blisters are lesions that develop over areas of significant trauma following high-energy injuries, such as ankle fracture-dislocations. They typically appear within 6–72 hours post-injury due to rapid swelling. The recommended management is to delay definitive surgical intervention until the blisters have resolved or re-epithelialized, as incisions through compromised skin substantially increase the risk of wound breakdown and infection. Temporary stabilization, such as external fixation, is preferred if surgery must be delayed. For blister care, intact blisters should be left undisturbed while unroofed blisters may be treated with topical antimicrobial agents like silver sulfadiazine to promote healing and reduce infection risk.

*Wawrose et al. performed a retrospective comparative study assessing splint immobilization and temporizing external fixation in patients with ankle fracture-dislocations.* *The authors excluded patients undergoing immediate definitive fixation.* External fixation was associated with a significantly lower risk of loss of reduction and skin necrosis compared to splint immobilization in patients with ankle fracture-dislocations. Among those managed with splinting, 50% experienced loss of reduction and 17.6% developed anteromedial skin necrosis, whereas no such complications occurred in the external fixation group. **The authors concluded** that external fixation provides superior maintenance of reduction and soft tissue protection compared to splinting in unstable ankle fracture-dislocations.

*Liskutin et al. describe a systematic surgical technique for achieving anatomic alignment with temporizing, ankle-spanning external fixation in the setting of severe ankle injuries with significant soft tissue compromise or polytrauma, where immediate definitive fixation is contraindicated.* **The authors recommend** first restoring limb length, then correcting sagittal and coronal plane alignment to optimize anatomic reduction and minimize complications during the temporizing phase. **They conclude** that this technique is important for the initial management of complex ankle fractures and dislocations, facilitating soft tissue recovery.

*Taylor et al. discuss modifications to the classic delta-frame external fixator for high-energy pilon fractures, including the addition of a "steering wheel" transverse bar and "kickstand" rods.* The transverse bar facilitates fine-tuning of alignment and reduction, and the supplemental rods counteract deforming forces and prevent loss of reduction, especially in cases with significant comminution or soft tissue compromise. **They conclude** that this approach is efficient, reproducible, and minimizes the risk of decubitus lesions and soft tissue complications during the temporizing phase before definitive fixation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Total contact casting with local wound care may reduce wound complications compared to open surgery in select older adults. It is associated with higher rates of malunion and difficulty maintaining reduction in complex, unstable fractures with significant soft tissue injury, especially in diabetic patients.
Answer 3: Local wound care without immobilization is inappropriate for an unstable bimalleolar fracture-dislocation due to persistent gross instability, displacement, and an extremely high risk of malunion, nonunion, and soft tissue breakdown.
Answer 4: Closed reduction with application of a splint is associated with a high risk of secondary loss of reduction and skin necrosis due to inadequate stabilization and persistent pressure on vulnerable skin.
Answer 5: Isolated pinning of the fibula does not address bimalleolar instability or provide sufficient stabilization for a fracture-dislocation, risking loss of reduction and further soft tissue injury.
