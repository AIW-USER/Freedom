## Question

A 4-year-old patient is brought to the clinic by his mother after complaints of frequent tripping and appearing "bow-legged." The patient has a history of walking at 9 months of age and is in the 90th percentile for weight. On examination, he has localized bowing at the proximal tibia and a lateral thrust during gait bilaterally. Full-length weight-bearing radiographs are obtained and shown in Figure A. This is the first time the mother has sought any treatment for the deformity. In regard to treatment, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. Surgical corrective osteotomy should be performed, with the goal of undercorrecting varus to prevent a valgus deformity.
2. Given the metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle of 17°, this patient should be managed with close monitoring alone, as the deformity will likely resolve.
3. Guided-growth, via a medial tibial hemiepiphysiodesis, will likely correct this patient's genu varum.
4. This patient's weight, age, and bilateral nature predispose him to failing nonoperative management.
5. Given his age and lack of conservative treatment thus far, bracing with a knee-ankle-foot orthosis is indicated.

## Correct option

**4. This patient's weight, age, and bilateral nature predispose him to failing nonoperative management.**

## Answer explanation

Risk factors for failure of conservative management in patients with Infantile Blount's disease include obesity (weight >90th percentile), varus thrust, age (3 years old at initiation of treatment), bilateral involvement, and severe disease (Langenskiöld Stage III) (answer choice 4).

Infantile Blount’s disease is a growth disorder characterized by progressive genu varum due to abnormal development of the medial proximal tibial physis. Excessive medial tibial pressure (as a result of early walking or obesity with a varus alignment) produces an osteochondrosis of the physis. The most common classification used is the Langenskiöld classification, which provides prognosis guidelines. Early diagnosis and treatment are essential to prevent worsening deformity and permanent growth disturbances. Initial management in young children with mild deformity often involves bracing (e.g., KAFO), though results are mixed and less effective in patients over age 3 or with more severe deformity. For patients who fail bracing or present with moderate to advanced disease (Langenskiöld III or above), surgical intervention becomes necessary. Growth modulation, particularly lateral proximal tibial hemiepiphysiodesis using tension-band plates, has become increasingly favored for its minimally invasive approach and reliable correction, especially in early Langenskiöld stages. In cases with more severe deformity, or in older children with diminished growth potential, proximal tibial valgus osteotomy (acute or gradual) is often required. Long-term follow-up is critical due to the risk of recurrence, asymmetric growth, and rebound deformity, particularly in younger children or those with delayed intervention.

*Danino et al. published a multicenter retrospective study to evaluate the efficacy of guided growth via lateral proximal tibial hemiepiphysiodesis as a first-line treatment for Blount's Disease.* *The authors defined success as a normalization of the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle after an average 24-month follow-up in 45 patients (55 knees).* The overall correction rate was approximately 1° per month, and success rates were highest in adolescent-group patients (>10 years; 87.5% corrected), compared to juvenile (4–10 years; 67%) and infantile (<4 years; 64%) subgroups. **The authors concluded** that hemiepiphysiodesis is an effective, minimally invasive primary treatment for Blount’s disease, although outcomes vary by age, with better results in older children.

*Assan et al. published a retrospective analysis of 17 children (24 knees) with infantile Blount's disease undergoing either guided growth or tibial osteotomy.* *Both groups were similar in age (median age of 6.5 years).* *At approximately two years follow-up, the guided growth group achieved a mean mechanical axis correction of about 1.03° per month, with median mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA) increasing from 78° to 87°.* In contrast, the osteotomy group showed initial improvement but significant loss of correction over time, with only modest overall alignment changes and a 60% recurrence rate. **The authors concluded** that guided growth is the preferred first-line treatment in skeletally immature patients with early-stage Blount’s disease, offering better durability and less recurrence than osteotomy.

*Sabharwal et al., on behalf of the Pediatric Orthopedic Society of North America (POSNA), published a comprehensive review of the management principles for infantile Blount's Disease.* **The authors highlight** ongoing debate around bracing efficacy and emphasize that many children exhibit advanced bone age, influencing correction strategies. Growth modulation using tension-band guided growth has gained traction as a minimally invasive first-line option. For older children (over age 7) with advanced deformity, combining medial hemiplateau elevation with lateral proximal tibial hemiepiphysiodesis may be necessary. Both acute and gradual proximal tibial osteotomies remain viable in appropriate clinical settings. Due to variable and sometimes unpredictable physeal behavior, **the authors advocate** for long-term monitoring of all affected children until skeletal maturity, and call for standardized outcome reporting to facilitate evidence-based guidelines.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: When performing a corrective osteotomy, it is recommended to fix the distal fragment in slight valgus (about 10°) to avoid recurrence.
Answer choice 2: A metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle of greater than 16° has a 95% likelihood of bowing progression. Serial monitoring would not be recommended for this patient.
Answer choice 3: A lateral tibial hemiepiphysiodesis is used to guide the growth of skeletally immature patients with tibia vara. A medial tibial hemiepiphysiodesis will only exacerbate the problem.
Answer choice 5: This patient has multiple risk factors for failing conservative management, including his weight, varus thrust, age, bilateral involvement, and severity of disease. Furthermore, conservative management is bypassed in patients over 4 years of age.
