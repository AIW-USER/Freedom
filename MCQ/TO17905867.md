## Question

Shown below is a graph with three curves A-C. The curves are showing glycogen depletion with exercise. Exercise pattern in the three curves is as under

![[TO17905867-1.png]]

## Options

1. A- Light, B- Moderate, C- Heavy
2. A- Heavy, B- Moderate, C- Light
3. A- Moderate, B- Light , C- Heavy
4. A- Moderate, B- Heavy, C- Ligh

## Correct option

**1. A- Light, B- Moderate, C- Heavy**

## Answer explanation

Ans: A

![[TO17905867-2.png]]

THE CARBOHYDRATE UTILIZATION CURVE

Carbohydrates are a very limited source of energy, accounting for only about 1-2% of total

bodily energy stores. About 80% of total carbohydrate is stored in skeletal muscle, about

14% is stored in the liver, and about 6% in the blood in the form of glucose. Now, Glycogen is

the storage form of glucose and carbohydrates (CHO), so this would represent about

300-400g of glycogen stored in muscle and about 70-100g stored in the liver.

Now, Glycogen (Glucose) is one of the fuels to generate ATP mainly via the Glycolysis

pathway; substantial amount of energy can also be generated in body from fats that get

metabolized via Krebs cycle (which needs oxygen to function). And one last system that

provides immediate ATP for a sudden burst of activity is the Creatine Phosphate system

(PCr). Basically, Creatine phosphate is a compound in muscles that is directly storing ATP

molecules.

Understanding the curve: -

![[TO17905867-3.png]]

1. Light Exercise

During light exercise (walk or warm-up), the bulk of energy required by the muscle is

provided by oxidation of free fatty acids (via Kerbs cycle) that are predominantly derived

from the plasma. Here, body relies on a low-level steady use of glycogen, in addition. So

here, aerobic metabolism is going on (as oxygen is being used), utilizing fats and just some

carbohydrates. Creatin Phosphate (PCr) system is not heavily involved at this stage since it’s

more focused on short bursts of high-intensity efforts.

This scenario is being depicted by curve A, where the glycogen content (%age glycogen) is

relatively remaining constant as mostly fatty acids are getting metabolized to generate

energy.

2. Moderate Exercise

As the intensity ramps up into moderate exercise, like jogging or a moderate-paced game,

the glycogen utilization accelerates.

This leads to the increased contribution from the anaerobic system, specifically the glycolytic

pathway. The aerobic system is still active but is supplemented by anaerobic processes to

meet the growing energy demands. PCr system might play a minor role in providing quick

bursts of energy during transitions or short accelerations.

This scenario is being depicted in curve B, where the glycogen content (%age glycogen) of

muscles is relatively depleting as exercise is being continued for an incremental time.

Basically, this reflects the shift to a more glycolytic energy system, common in activities

requiring sustained effort, where less of ATP is generated for the amount of glucose used.

3. Heavy Exercise:

Heavy exercise is characterized by activities like sprinting or high-intensity interval training.

The graph here shows a sharp decline, indicating rapid glycogen depletion as is evident in

curve C.

Here there is predominant reliance on anaerobic glycolysis. This phase involves rapid

glycogen breakdown to fuel high-intensity efforts. The aerobic system is still contributing,

but anaerobic metabolism takes center stage to meet the immediate energy needs. The PCr

system is now definitely activated for rapid ATP regeneration during intense, short bursts of

activity.
