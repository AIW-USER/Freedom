## Question

A 77-year-old female sustains a fall on the ice while walking to her car, landing on her right shoulder. Given her pain level and the complexity of her fracture pattern, you recommend reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. You discuss with her that this procedure should improve her pain and function but may decrease her shoulder external rotation compared to before the injury. What is the most important intraoperative consideration to maintain this patient's external rotation postoperatively?

## Options

1. Increased medialization of glenoid component
2. Extensive inferior capsular release
3. Fixation and healing of greater tuberosity
4. Greater degree of humeral anteversion
5. Repair of subscapularis

## Correct option

**3. Fixation and healing of greater tuberosity**

## Answer explanation

Healing of the greater tuberosity is the most important intraoperative consideration for maintaining shoulder external rotation after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) for a proximal humerus fracture.

Indications for RTSA in treating proximal humerus fractures are growing, particularly in patients over 70 with complex 3- and 4-part fractures or head-split components. This construct relies on the deltoid to power the shoulder, rather than the rotator cuff muscles. Therefore, after RTSA, rotational movements are often diminished, as the external rotators attach to the greater tuberosity which is usually affected by the fracture. In order to restore some degree of external rotation, the greater tuberosity should be repaired and allowed to heal. Positioning of the greater tuberosity can be judged off of the pectoralis major tendon which is 5.6 cm below the top of the humeral head. Many modern implant systems provide a means for helping stabilize this fragment in an appropriate position.

*Sabesan et al. performed a biomechanical study to address the effects of greater tuberosity healing on RTSA.* **They noted** that greater tuberosity healing did not impact abduction or forward flexion but did increase external rotation forces and decrease joint reactive forces. **They concluded** that greater tuberosity healing improved external rotation after RTSA but patients could likely expect good results if the fragment were to not heal.

*Jain et al. evaluated greater tuberosity healing in RTSA after proximal humerus fractures in a systematic review of 382 shoulders.* The rate of tuberosity healing was 70.5% and patients with a healed tuberosity had much better forward flexion, abduction, and external rotation as well as better outcome scores. It was their final opinion that healing of the greater tuberosity had a positive impact on improving overall shoulder function and outcomes in this population.

*Boileau et al. investigated whether or not reattaching the tuberosities was worthwhile in RTSA surgery.* **They noted** an 84% rate of tuberosity healing and lower subjective outcomes plus decreased forward elevation and external rotation in those who went on to nonunion. **They concluded** that tuberosity healing after RTSA for fracture was important in improving range of motion and patient satisfaction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While RTSA functions to medialize the shoulder center of rotation compared to the native shoulder, further medialization may decrease the tension on the rotator cuff muscles, decreasing rotational range of motion.
Answer 2: Capsular releases are important for exposure during RTSA but do not significantly affect external rotation postoperatively.
Answer 4: Adding humeral retroversion, not anteversion, is thought to potentially improve the degree of external rotation postoperatively after RTSA.
Answer 5: Subscapularis repair improves internal rotation, not external rotation, and may provide further limitations on maintaining external rotation postoperatively.
