## Question

A 72-year-old female is seen in office for her preoperative visit. She is scheduled to undergo a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) this week for rotator cuff tear arthropathy with pseudoparalysis. Her radiographs reveal Hamada stage 4a changes. While discussing rotator cuff arthropathy, which of the following statements is true?

## Options

1. Centering of the humeral head on the glenoid is not possible with an absent supraspinatus
2. Concavity compression is the primary mechanism of glenohumeral centering to resist upward pull of the deltoid
3. Glenohumeral stability can be completely restored with rotator cuff repair after effective glenoid concavity is lost
4. Intact biceps tendon will prevent superior humeral head migration with cuff tear arthropathy
5. Net force vector of the rotator cuff muscles are oriented optimally to depress the humeral head against the upward pull of the deltoid

## Correct option

**2. Concavity compression is the primary mechanism of glenohumeral centering to resist upward pull of the deltoid**

## Answer explanation

Concavity compression is the primary mechanism by which the head of the humerus is centered and stabilized in the glenoid fossa to resist the upward pull of the deltoid.

Rotator cuff tear arthropathy (CTA) represents a spectrum of pathology encompassing three key features: rotator cuff insufficiency, degenerative changes of the glenohumeral joint, and superior migration of the humeral head. The rotator cuff muscles help compress the humeral head against the glenoid, allowing for concentric rotation of the head (concavity-compression). Alterations in this concavity-compressive result in glenohumeral instability and superior translation of the humeral head.

*Walker et al. reviewed the biomechanical challenges associated with the rotator cuff-deficient shoulder and the use of an RSA.* **They reported** that various implant design factors and surgical implantation techniques affect the stability of an RSA. **They concluded** that several basic science and clinical studies improve our understanding of various design factors in RSA.

*Drake et al. reviewed RSA use in patients with rotator cuff arthropathy.* **They reported** that modern RSA designs restore deltoid tension and a functional fulcrum to the rotator cuff deficient shoulder, which allows recovery of active shoulder elevation and effectively restores function. **They concluded** that RSA relieves symptoms and restores function for patients with cuff tear arthropathy and irreparable rotator cuff tears with pseudoparalysis (preserved deltoid contraction but loss of active elevation).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Even when a substantial supraspinatus defect is present, compression from the subscapularis and infraspinatus can hold the humeral head centered on the glenoid away from the coracoacromial arch
Answer 3: Once the effective glenoid concavity is lost, repair of the rotator cuff tendons or complex capsular reconstructions cannot completely restore the glenohumeral stability provided by compression into an intact concavity
Answer 4: The biceps tendon does not serve as a humeral head depressor and does not prevent proximal migration of the shoulder when it is present
Answer 5: The net force vector of the supraspinatus muscle and other cuff muscles are not oriented optimally to depress the head of the humerus against the upward pull of the deltoid
