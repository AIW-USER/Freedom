Chapter: Orthopedics Oncology MCQs — Simple Unicameral Bone Cyst

## Question

For an Eccentric lytic lesion, least likely possibility would be [FNB 2022]

## Options

1. SBC
2. ABC
3. GCT
4. Fibrous cortical defect

## Correct option

**1. SBC**

## Answer explanation

Ans: A

IMPORTANT RADIOGRAPHIC FEATURES: -

SIMPLE/ UNICAMERAL BONE CYST [SBC/ UBC]

UBC mainly involves the metaphysis long bones, and especially the proximal humerus, followed by the proximal femur; these two locations account for more than 80% of cases. On plain X-ray, UBCs are mainly found (90–95%) on the metaphysis of the long bones, and are juxtaphyseal, radiotransparent, moderately expansive, well-contoured, central in location, oblong along the longitudinal axis of the bone, usually unicameral, with fine boney margins and thinning of the facing cortical bone . Exceptionally, simple bone cysts may be found on the diaphysis, where they are large, multicameral and only slightly expansile. In fractures, there may be a small “fallen fragment” which is considered to be pathognomic for SBC.

![[TO17143746-1.png]]

Computed tomography (CT) finds a thin-walled lesion, often with pseudo-septum, and may show a fissure in the cyst. This examination serves mainly to assess cyst wall thickness and fracture risk. It can be used to assess lesion extension in complex regions such as the spine or pelvis. Bone scintigraphy and positron emission tomography are non-contributive

ANEURYSMAL BONE CYST [ABC]

Aneurysmal bone cysts are benign, osteolytic, expansile and haemorrhagic lesions, comprising 1% to 6% of primary osseous tumours. The majority of patients diagnosed with an aneurysmal bone cyst are children and adolescents less than 20 years old. There is a slightly increased incidence rate seen in females.

Aneurysmal bone cysts present in the metaphysis of long bones (femur > tibia is most common bone involved). They also occur in the spine, posterior elements, and pelvis. Less frequently, they can appear in the craniofacial bones and epiphyses.

There are various forms of aneurysmal cysts: the usual one is primary or “classical” ABC; others are secondary (associated with another lesion), solid ABC or giant cell reparative granuloma, and soft-tissue aneurysmal cyst.

On plain X-ray, ABC shows as an eccentric, osteolytic, expansive and sometimes trabeculated lesion containing fine-walled cystic cavities. Internal contours are well defined, with or without an osseous sclerotic ring, and the cortical bone bulges. Loss of cortical contours or extension into soft tissue may mimic a malignant lesion, indicating an aggressive form

![[TO17143746-2.png]]

CT is less sensitive than MRI, and shows fluid levels in only a third of cases. In complex regions such as the spine or pelvis, CT can provide a lesion map, determine fracture risk and assess filling after treatment.

MRI is the examination of choice to complement X-ray. The typical aspect is an expansile lesion, lobular or with septa. Multiple fluid levels may be detected on T2-weighted axial sequences at rest; although not specific, they are highly suggestive. Gadolinium injection shows enhancement of the cyst walls and internal septa. Bone scintigraphy, is usually non-contributive. It can help locate a painful lesion, particularly spinal lesions in children.

FIBROUS CORTIAL DEFECT [FCD]

Fibrous cortical defects typically occur in children (usually 2-15 years), and are the most common benign bone lesions, which combined with non-ossifying fibromas are seen in up to 40% of skeletally immature children/ adolescents. There is a male predilection by a ratio of 2:1.

A classic X-ray picture of a fibrous cortical defect shows a cystic lesion located in the cortical layer of a bone (eccentric lesion), usually oval, surrounded with a thin sclerotic rim, with a long axis parallel to the axis of the bone. The external outline of the cortical layer at the level of the lesion may be poorly visible or completely invisible.

![[TO17143746-3.png]]

Anteroposterior radiograph of the distal femur shows a solitary corticate lucent lesion within the posteromedial distal femoral cortex; this finding is consistent with a fibrous cortical defect.

GIANT CELL TUMOR [GCT]

Giant cell tumors are locally aggressive and rarely metastasizing neoplasms often found eccentrically located at the end of long bones (most common site is distal femur) affecting the epiphysis extending into metaphysis.

Radiographs and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the standard imaging modalities for the diagnosis of GCT. Computed tomography (CT) can be used to assess cortical thinning and pathologic fractures, and to monitor fracture consolidation. The typical radiographic features of GCT include a purely osteolytic lesion with a geographic type of bone destruction, a well-defined nonsclerotic margin, eccentric location, extension to the subchondral bone, closed physes. On MRI, GCT usually shows a low to intermediate signal intensity on T1 and a high signal on T2-weighted images. The intramedullary portion of the tumor is best seen on T1, whereas its extraosseous component is more clearly observed on T2-weighted images. After intravenous injection of gadolinium, heterogeneous enhancement of the tumor is observed. Fluid levels in the tumor have been reported in 10–14% of patients and are considered to be secondary to an aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) component. Radionuclide bone scan rarely provides additional information, because the degree of tracer uptake does not correlate with the histologic grade of the tumor. However, bone scan may help to detect multiple foci, if multicentric disease is clinically suspected.
