## Question

A 68-year-old man undergoes a revision total hip arthroplasty (THA) for persistent groin pain and elevated serum cobalt and chromium levels. He previously had a primary THA with a modular femoral stem and a large-diameter cobalt-chrome head on a titanium alloy neck. Intraoperatively, the surgeon notes black debris and significant "pitting" at the modular head-neck taper interface. The metal surfaces appear to have lost their protective oxide layer due to repetitive, small-amplitude oscillations during the gait cycle. Which of the following mechanisms best describes the degradation of the modular interface, characterized by the disruption of the passive oxide layer due to micromotion?

## Options

1. Adhesive wear
2. Crevice corrosion
3. Fretting corrosion
4. Galvanic corrosion
5. Pitting corrosion

## Correct option

**3. Fretting corrosion**

## Answer explanation

Fretting corrosion is specifically defined as the mechanical disruption of a metal’s protective passivation layer due to micromotion at a modular interface, leading to continuous oxidation and material loss (answer choice 3).

Modular implants in orthopaedic surgery rely on the stability of the metal-on-metal interface; however, these junctions are susceptible to mechanically assisted crevice corrosion. Metals like titanium and cobalt-chrome rely on a "passivation" layer—a stable oxide film—to prevent degradation. When micromotion occurs at the taper interface (fretting), this thin layer is mechanically scraped away. The exposed metal reacts with the surrounding fluid to reform the oxide layer, but subsequent micromotion wipes it away again. This repetitive cycle of "re-passivation" and "de-passivation" leads to significant ion release, metal debris, and eventual implant failure.

*Dyrkacz et al. evaluated the influence of femoral head size on the corrosion and fretting behavior at the head-neck interface using a laboratory hip simulator.* **They noted** that larger head sizes increased the frictional torque at the taper, which significantly accelerated the rate of material loss. **They concluded** that larger head diameters are associated with increased fretting and corrosion damage in modular total hip designs.

*Srinivasan et al. evaluated the clinical utility and risks associated with modular femoral components in total hip arthroplasty.* **They noted** that while modularity allows for better restoration of hip biomechanics, it introduces new interfaces susceptible to corrosion and fatigue failure. **They concluded** that surgeons must weigh the benefits of intraoperative flexibility against the potential for adverse local tissue reactions caused by modularity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This occurs when two surfaces rub together with enough force to bond and pull material from the softer surface, rather than the electrochemical degradation of a protective layer.
Answer 2 & 5: While often seen alongside fretting, pitting/crevice is a localized form of corrosion that creates small holes (pits) in the metal in stagnant areas, rather than the mechanical disruption caused by movement.
Answer 4: This requires the contact of two dissimilar metals to create an electrochemical potential difference, whereas the specific mechanism of micromotion-induced layer disruption is defined as fretting.
