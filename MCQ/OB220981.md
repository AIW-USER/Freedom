## Question

A 14-year-old female tennis player presents with a 6-month history of lower back pain and radicular pain down her left leg. MRI imaging of her lumbar spine is shown in Figure A. Which of the following physical exam findings, if present, is most consistent with the pathology seen in her imaging?

## Options

1. Weakness with ankle plantarflexion, diminished sensation over the lateral aspect of the foot
2. Weakness with hip flexion, diminished sensation over the medial aspect of the thigh
3. Weakness with ankle dorsiflexion, diminished sensation over the medial aspect of the foot
4. Weakness with knee extension diminished sensation over the medial aspect of the knee
5. Weakness with extension of the great toe, diminished sensation over the dorsum of the foot

## Correct option

**1. Weakness with ankle plantarflexion, diminished sensation over the lateral aspect of the foot**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an L5/S1 paracentral disc herniation with resultant left-sided paracenteral stenosis affecting the S1 nerve root. Patients with S1 radiculopathy present with ankle plantar flexion weakness and altered sensation over the lateral foot (Answer 1).

Pediatric disc herniation is an uncommon condition (incidence of 0.1-0.2%) that presents similarly to adult disc herniations but is often diagnosed late due to its rarity. This condition is typically associated with inciting trauma or activity (e.g., sports). Patients present with radiculopathy in the distribution of the affected nerve root. Management of patients is initially conservative. However, pediatric patients are more likely to fail this mode of treatment compared to their adult counterparts. In those that fail conservative treatment, surgical intervention in the form of a discectomy is typically pursued.

*Lavelle et al. published a review on the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric disk herniations.* *They review the rarity of this condition, risk factors such as heavy lifting/high impact activities/family history, the propensity for herniations to occur at L4/L5 and L5/S1, and differential diagnoses (apophyses injuries/tumor/infection/piriformis syndrome).* **They discuss** that surgery is typically reserved for those who failed conservative management (often the case in adolescents) or progressive neurological deficit. **They conclude** that patients indicated for discectomy have excellent outcomes, though 20-30% may require additional lumbar surgeries in the long run.

*Liu et al. performed a retrospective analysis using the PearlDiver database to determine the rate of reoperation in pediatric patients who underwent lumbar discectomy.* **They found** a 12% 5-year reoperation rate, with higher rates seen in older patients. Nearly half of the reoperations occurred within 1 year of surgery as a result of repeat herniation. Higher BMI and comorbidity index were associated with a greater risk of reoperation. **They concluded** that their findings parallel prior literature reporting an approximately 12% rate of reoperation and that similar reoperation rates are seen after revision surgery as well. However, risk factors for revision surgery have not yet been well elucidated.

Figures A and B are sagittal and axial MRI slices with evidence of a paracentral L5/S1 disc herniation with resultant left paracentral canal stenosis (L > R).
