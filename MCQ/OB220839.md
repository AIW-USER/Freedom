## Question

A 54-year-old man with a history of a bimalleolar ankle fracture eight years ago presents with chronic, worsening right ankle pain and progressive difficulty ambulating. On examination, he has a markedly antalgic gait and limited ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. Radiographs reveal severe joint space narrowing and varus deformity at the tibiotalar joint, with mild degenerative changes at the subtalar joint. His medical history is notable for well-controlled type 2 diabetes without neuropathy. He elects to undergo an arthrodesis procedure. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Isolated tibiotalar arthrodesis in >5° plantarflexion
2. Isolated tibiotalar arthrodesis with slight internal rotation
3. Combined tibiotalar and subtalar arthrodesis
4. Isolated tibiotalar fusion with slight posterior translation of the talus
5. Combined tibiotalar, subtalar, and calcaneocuboid arthrodesis

## Correct option

**4. Isolated tibiotalar fusion with slight posterior translation of the talus**

## Answer explanation

The patient has end-stage post-traumatic ankle arthritis and a fixed varus deformity who has failed conservative management. The most appropriate next step is isolated tibiotalar fusion with slight posterior translation of the talus to restore the weight-bearing axis, optimize sagittal plane alignment, and achieve a plantigrade foot while preserving motion at the subtalar joint (Answer 4).

Tibiotalar arthrodesis is indicated for patients with end-stage tibiotalar osteoarthritis, posttraumatic arthritis, avascular necrosis of the talus, failed total ankle arthroplasty, or severe deformity unresponsive to nonoperative management, provided the subtalar joint is relatively preserved. Key steps include thorough debridement of the joint surfaces, correction of deformity, optimal positioning of the foot (neutral dorsiflexion, slight external rotation, and 5–10° valgus), and rigid internal fixation using screws and/or plates, with or without bone grafting as dictated by bone quality and defect size. Most patients achieve solid fusion and report good to excellent clinical results; however, loss of ankle motion and the potential for adjacent joint arthritis should be discussed preoperatively.

*Buck et al. conducted a gait analysis study to determine the optimum position for ankle arthrodesis, focusing on how different fusion angles affect knee and ankle biomechanics during ambulation.* **They found** that fusing the ankle in a neutral position (0° dorsiflexion and plantarflexion) resulted in the most normal gait pattern, minimizing compensatory changes at the knee and optimizing limb function. **They conclude** that ankle arthrodesis should be performed in a neutral position to achieve the best functional outcome and gait mechanics.

AlSayel and Valderrabano discussed that the primary goal in surgical management of varus ankle deformity is to achieve a pain-free, stable, and plantigrade foot and ankle with correct alignment, typically aiming for neutral to slight valgus (0–5°) in the coronal plane to optimize function and minimize complications. The article emphasizes that corrective ankle arthrodesis is a technically challenging procedure and often requires individualized surgical approaches and fixation methods tailored to the degree and location of the deformity. Residual varus malalignment is associated with poor outcomes and increased risk of adjacent joint degeneration, making precise intraoperative assessment and correction of alignment critical for successful long-term results.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Fusing in plantarflexion causes equinus gait and difficulty with foot clearance during the swing phase of gait.
Answer 2: Internal rotation malalignment leads to abnormal foot progression angle and inefficient gait.
Answer 3: The Subtalar joint only has mild changes and should be preserved to maintain hindfoot motion.
Answer 5: Triple fusion is unnecessarily extensive and sacrifices remaining hindfoot and midfoot motion.
