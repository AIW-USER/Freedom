## Question

Figures A&B are the radiographs of a 44-year-old man who previously underwent arthroscopic shoulder surgery 6 months prior. He continues to have pain despite a prolonged course of physical therapy. Figure C is a recent axial MRI image. The remainder of the MRI illustrates a well-healed rotator cuff repair. On examination, he is noted to have tenderness to the superior aspect of the shoulder and evidence of impingement. Which treatment is most indicated at this time?

## Options

1. Observation
2. Continued physical therapy
3. Arthroscopic subacromial decompression and acromioplasty
4. Open fragment excision only
5. Two-stage fusion

## Correct option

**5. Two-stage fusion**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a symptomatic os acromiale with evidence of impingement. He has failed a prolonged course of nonoperative management, and a two-stage fusion would be indicated at this time.

An Os Acromiale is an unfused secondary ossification center of the acromion that can be associated with shoulder impingement and rotator cuff tendonitis. Pain is thought to be caused by either motion at the site or downward displacement of the anterior aspect of the acromion onto the rotator cuff, causing impingement. Diagnosis is made with axillary lateral radiographs of the shoulder or advanced imaging. Treatment is usually observation in the majority of cases with operative management being reserved for patients who have persistent shoulder impingement or rotator cuff disease that have failed nonoperative management. Surgical management may include two-stage fusion, arthroscopic subacromial decompression, and acromioplasty, or open or arthroscopic fragment excision. Two-stage fusion is indicated with a symptomatic os acromiale with impingement and consists of rigid internal fixation and bone grafting followed by an acromioplasty, which has yielded satisfactory results.

*Warner et al. reviewed 15 shoulders with painful unstable os acromiale.* **They reported** that 8 patients had an associated rotator cuff tear. **They report** that patients that underwent ORIF with the use of cannulated screws and a tension-band construct obtained a solid osseous union in all but one of them. **They concluded** that symptomatic unstable os acromiale can be effectively treated with the use of autogenous bone-grafting and internal fixation with a rigid tension-band construct and cannulated screws.

*Sammarco et al. reviewed the frequency and anatomy of os acromiale in the general population.* **They report** that the condition was bilateral in 33% of the skeletons, was more frequent in blacks than in whites 13% compared with 6%, more frequent in men than in women 9% compared with 5%. **They concluded** that a fused os acromiale, which has not been described previously, might be mistaken for a free ossicle in the clinical setting.

Figures A and B are radiographs of the right shoulder demonstrating an os acromiale. Figure C is an axial MRI image re-demonstrating an os acromiale. Illustration A depicts the anatomic locations of an os acromiale.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: This patient has failed a prolonged course of nonoperative management
Answer 3: Arthroscopic subacromial decompression and acromioplasty is indicated with impingement with or without rotator cuff tear where the os acromiale is only incidental and not symptomatic
Answer 4: Open fragment excision (indicated for symptomatic pre-acromion with small fragment or as salvage after failed ORIF). Excision may be a viable treatment option for the preacromion type.
