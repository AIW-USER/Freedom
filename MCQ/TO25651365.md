Chapter: Metabolic Bone Disorder MCQs — Osteoporosis and Fragility Fractures

## Question

In a patient with very high fracture risk, what is the most appropriate therapeutic sequence based on the results of the DATA and DATA-Switch studies?

## Options

1. Simultaneous initiation of Alendronate and Teriparatide.
2. Anabolic therapy followed by an antiresorptive agent.
3. Antiresorptive therapy followed by an anabolic agent.
4. Continuous Teriparatide for five years without transition.

## Correct option

**2. Anabolic therapy followed by an antiresorptive agent.**

## Answer explanation

The 'anabolic window' is best utilized first; following anabolic therapy with an antiresorptive (like Zoledronic acid or Denosumab) consolidates and further increases BMD gains.

The traditional approach of initiating osteoporosis treatment with an antiresorptive agent and reserving anabolic agents for refractory cases is being challenged by emerging evidence supporting an "anabolic-first" strategy — starting with an anabolic agent (teriparatide, abaloparatide, or romosozumab) to build bone rapidly, then transitioning to an antiresorptive agent (denosumab or bisphosphonate) to maintain the gains.

The rationale is that starting with a bisphosphonate suppresses the bone turnover that anabolic agents subsequently need to stimulate — prior bisphosphonate use blunts the anabolic response to teriparatide (the "blunting effect"), reducing its efficacy. Conversely, starting with an anabolic agent in treatment-naïve bone produces the maximum anabolic response, and subsequent antiresorptive therapy consolidates the gains.

Optimal Sequences (by decreasing BMD gain):

Romosozumab (12 months) → Denosumab (ongoing) — produces the greatest cumulative BMD gain

Teriparatide/Abaloparatide (24 months) → Denosumab (ongoing) — second greatest cumulative gain

Romosozumab (12 months) → Bisphosphonate (ongoing)

Denosumab (ongoing) — continuous antiresorptive, excellent sustained efficacy

Bisphosphonate (3–5 years, then drug holiday) — first-line for moderate risk

Monitoring Treatment Response

DXA:
Repeated at 2-year intervals during treatment (or at 1 year for anabolic agents). A stable or increasing BMD indicates adequate response. A decline in BMD of >5% at the spine or >4% at the hip (beyond the precision error of the DXA machine — typically 1–2%) suggests treatment failure and should prompt investigation for secondary causes and consideration of treatment change.

Bone Turnover Markers (BTMs): Serum CTX (resorption marker) and P1NP (formation marker) can be measured at 3–6 months after initiating therapy to assess early response — a >30–40% reduction in CTX with antiresorptive therapy, or a >30% increase in P1NP with anabolic therapy, indicates a therapeutic response before BMD changes become detectable on DXA.

Fracture Occurrence: The ultimate outcome measure — any new fragility fracture during treatment indicates treatment failure regardless of BMD changes.

Drug Holidays — Bisphosphonate-Specific

Because bisphosphonates accumulate in the bone mineral and continue to exert antiresorptive effects for months to years after discontinuation (due to their long skeletal half-life — estimated at 10 years for alendronate), temporary discontinuation ("drug holiday") after an adequate treatment period is recommended to reduce the risk of rare long-term complications (AFF, ONJ) while maintaining residual anti-fracture benefit.

Recommended approach: After 3–5 years of oral bisphosphonate or 3 years of IV zoledronic acid, reassess fracture risk. Moderate risk patients — drug holiday for 2–3 years (oral) or 3 years (after zoledronic acid), with DXA and BTM monitoring; reinitiate if BMD declines significantly or a fracture occurs. High risk patients — continue treatment (or switch to an alternative agent) without a holiday.

Drug holidays are NOT appropriate for denosumab (rebound effect), anabolic agents (BMD is lost without sequential antiresorptive), or SERMs (no residual effect after discontinuation).

Answer: 2
