## Question

Figure A shows radiographs of an 8-year-old boy with no radial pulse after a fall. Following emergent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning, the radial pulse remains unpalpable. His hand is pink, warm to touch and the capillary refill is brisk (<2 seconds). The neurological examination is normal. The surgeon decides to maintain fixation and observe the patient overnight with serial vascular examinations. What would be the expected outcome?

![[q5533-a-gartland-iii.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Compartment syndrome of the forearm
2. Acute limb ischemia
3. Late-presenting limb ischemia
4. Return of radial pulse in 24-48 hours post-operatively
5. No return of radial pulse, but maintained collateral blood flow

## Correct option

**4. Return of radial pulse in 24-48 hours post-operatively**

## Answer explanation

The lack of a palpable radial pulse after closed reduction and percutaneous pinning is not an absolute indication for fixation removal and vascular exploration if clinical findings (i.e., Doppler signal and brisk capillary refill) suggest that the limb is well perfused. Most patients will regain a palpable pulse either prior to discharge or by the time of the first postoperative visit.

Immediate vascular exploration is indicated when a limb displays signs of ischemia (coolness of the hand, paleness of the limb, and no radial pulse detected on Doppler ultrasonography). However, the management of a warm, clinically well-perfused limb that lacks a palpable pulse has been debated. Treatment options include, immediate vascular exploration, emergent angiography, color doppler ultrasound, and serial vascular examinations. When no radial pulse is detected with Doppler ultrasound after closed reduction and percutaneous pinning, this should raise a high degree of suspicion for arterial injury.

*Weller et al. reviewed 1297 consecutive, operatively treated supracondylar humeral fractures in patients presenting to a Level-1 pediatric trauma center.* *Twenty (37%) of the 54 patients who lacked a palpable radial pulse had a pulse documented with use of Doppler ultrasound.* *These patients were observed in the hospital for signs of ischemia and 19/20 patients regained a palpable pulse either prior to discharge or by the time of the first postoperative visit.*

*Scannell et al. reviewed a cohort of 20 children with a perfused, pulseless hand after closed reduction, percutaneous pinning of a supracondylar humeral fracture.* All were treated with observation only. At final follow-up, 15/20 children demonstrated a palpable distal radial pulse, normal growth of the arm, and good/excellent functional outcomes. 5/20 children had an occluded brachial artery, but normal growth and outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The incidence of compartment syndrome is estimated to be <1%. Risk factors include splinting the elbow >90 degrees of flexion, Gartland Type III, neurological deficit, acute vascular injury and delayed treatment.
Answer 2, 3: A clinically well-perfused limb, which is pulseless pre- and post-operatively, will rarely progress to limb ischemia. However, patients with a pulseless limb and poorly perfused hand (pale, negative doppler) are at the highest risk for limb ischemia.
Answer 5: Most large population studies will show that in the presence of a pulseless, well-perfused hand, the majority of patients will have a return of the radial pulse. Of note, patients who had a radial pulse pre-operatively and have no pulse post-operatively should have fixation removed and revised either closed or open.
