## Question

A 35-year-old active woman who participates in recreational softball leagues presents to the office with lateral ankle pain and recurrent ankle sprains. She has sprained her ankle over five times, during both sports and leisurely walking, despite multiple rounds of physical therapy. On exam, she has laxity with the anterior drawer test, but no pain with ankle eversion or the squeeze test. The examiner can evert and invert her subtalar joint without difficulty. ## Options

1. 6 weeks of walking cast followed by proprioceptive training
2. Lateral ankle ligamentous reconstruction
3. Ankle syndesmosis repair with anterior inferior tibiofibular ligamentous reconstruction
4. Lateral ankle ligamentous reconstruction, plantar fascia release, and 1st metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy
5. Lateral ankle ligamentous reconstruction, FDL transfer, and medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy

## Correct option

**4. Lateral ankle ligamentous reconstruction, plantar fascia release, and 1st metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has chronic lateral ankle instability coupled with flexible cavovarus foot deformity. Lateral ligamentous reconstruction with cavovarus foot deformity correction will provide the highest chance of treatment success. (Answer 4).

Ankle sprains are common injuries that are typically managed nonoperatively. However, recurrent injury can lead to lateral ankle ligamentous insufficiency and chronic lateral instability. Patients with cavovarus foot deformity are at a significantly increased risk of chronic lateral ankle instability. Treatment for lateral ankle instability typically requires ligamentous reconstruction (i.e. modified Brostrom), and the treatment of cavovarus deformity is varied depending on the flexibility of the hindfoot (Coleman block test) and severity of the deformity. In feet with a flexible hindfoot varus deformity, a 1st metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy can be utilized to correct the forefoot pronation that drives the hindfoot deformity.

*Chang et al. provide a comprehensive review of the biomechanical evidence concerning the diagnosis and treatment of chronic lateral ankle instability.* *They review the physical examination maneuvers and imaging modalities required to diagnose chronic instability.* *They also compare open and arthroscopic techniques of ligament repair, as well as biomechanical considerations of each.* *They review risk factors of chronic lateral ankle instability, including cavovarus foot deformity, which can be diagnosed on physical exam and a lateral foot radiograph with a convex upward Meary angle.*

Chilvers and Manoli discuss the implications of subtle cavus foot alignment in athletic injuries, emphasizing its role in recurrent ankle instability and lateral foot overload. They point out that while treating the primary injury may address symptoms, it does not correct the underlying alignment issue, leading to potential recurrence. The article also describes the pathomechanics of the cavus foot, noting that it can be forefoot-driven, due to a plantar-flexed first metatarsal, or hindfoot-driven, resulting from conditions like tarsal coalition. Surgical options include osteotomies and, when necessary, tendon transfers.

Figure A is the lateral radiograph of a foot demonstrating cavovarus deformity. Figures B and C are the T2-weighted coronal and axial images of an ankle, demonstrating chronic anterior talofibular ligamentous insufficiency and intact syndesmosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has failed nonoperative management and is thus indicated for surgical intervention
Answer 2: Lateral ligamentous reconstruction alone will not address the cavovarus deformity and thus carries the risk of recurrent instability
Answer 3: This patient does not have an ankle syndesmotic injury
Answer 5: FDL transfer and medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy are both indicated in the treatment of adult planovalgus foot deformity, not cavovarus deformity
