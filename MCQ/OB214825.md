## Question

A 20-year-old right-handed collegiate baseball pitcher presents with progressively worsening right shoulder pain. His pitch velocity has steadily decreased over the past three months and he lacks command. There has otherwise been no inciting trauma. Physical examination reveals abduction and internal rotation of the right and left shoulder to be 5° and 15°, respectively. Abduction and external rotation of the right and the left shoulder are 115° and 90°, respectively. Rotator cuff muscle strength is symmetric and there is no scapular dyskinesia. Current radiographs are unremarkable. What would be the next best step in treatment for this patient?

## Options

1. Cessation from pitching for 3-6 months
2. MRI of the right shoulder
3. Anterior capsular stretches
4. Posterior capsular stretches
5. Arthroscopic capsular release

## Correct option

**4. Posterior capsular stretches**

## Answer explanation

The patient is an overhead throwing athlete who is presenting with shoulder pain due to a glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD). The best initial treatment for this condition consists of posterior capsular stretches, also known as sleeper stretches.

Throwing athletes develop multiple adaptive changes to the glenohumeral joint that enable mobility and stability simultaneously. Often, this adaptation leads to anterior capsular laxity coupled with posterior capsular contractures. Unfortunately, this can manifest a spectrum of disease which usually presents initially with a lack of internal rotation of the affected side, referred as GIRD. Progression can lead to internal impingement of the articular-side of the rotator cuff against the glenoid, evolving into rotator cuff tears and SLAP lesions. Initial treatment aims at addressing the contracture of the posterior capsule with sleeper stretches.

*Wilk et al. reviewed rehabilitation concepts for overhead throwing athletes.* *The authors describe the "thrower's paradox," which is a delicate balance between mobility and functional stability.* *They describe rehabilitation following a structured protocol to control inflammation, restore muscle balance, improve flexibility, enhance proprioception, and restore neuromuscular control.*

*Wilk et al. also reviewed rehabilitation concepts of the athletic shoulder.* *The authors described adaptive changes that commonly occur in the shoulder, including anterior shoulder hypermobility, excessive external rotation, posterior capsular contractures, limited internal rotation, and generalized ligamentous laxity.* **They stated** that the rehabilitation process focused on improving neuromuscular control, proprioception, force couple efficiency, plyometrics, eccentrics, and scapular stability.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Complete cessation of throwing for 3-6 months may improve his shoulder pain, but would not address the posterior capsular contracture and would likely lead to deconditioning of his shoulder muscles.
Answer 2: An MRI of the shoulder is not initially indicated for isolated posterior capsular tightness in the absence of any injury or internal derangement of the shoulder on the physical exam.
Answer 4: Often, there is anterior capsular laxity in the affected shoulder of throwing athletes. A program focusing on anterior capsular stretching may not improve and would instead possibly exacerbate his condition.
Answer 5: An arthroscopic capsular release is not the next best treatment option without exhausting conservative management. Often, posterior capsular contractures can be addressed with sleeper stretches.
