## Options

1. Repeat biopsy
2. Radiation therapy
3. Wide resection and Winkelmann type BII rotationplasty
4. Wide resection and total knee arthroplasty with customized implants
5. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

## Correct option

**5. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has conventional intramedullary osteosarcoma. The most appropriate next step in treatment is chemotherapy, then followed by surgery, and more chemotherapy.

Treatment for osteosarcoma includes preoperative chemotherapy, surgery, followed by additional postoperative chemotherapy. The 4 most commonly used drugs are doxorubicin (limited by potential for cardiotoxicity), cisplatin, high dose methotrexate (with leucovorin rescue) and ifosfamide. While preop chemo is what is typically given in osteosarcoma, the effectiveness of chemotherapy is the same whether given preop or postop.

*Luetke et al. reviewed the treatment of OS.* Most OS was high grade (80-90%). Only 10% arose in the axial skeleton (most commonly, the pelvis). Axial tumors tended to occur in older patients, and had worse outcome than appendicular lesions. **They found** that multimodality treatment achieved 5 year survival of 60-70% in extremity-localized, non-metastatic disease. Local recurrence was 2-3% after amputation and 5-7% after limb-salvage.

*Carrle et al. reviewed chemotherapy in OS.* **They state** that chemotherapy is used to eradicate subclinical micrometastases. While it can be used before or after surgery, preoperative chemotherapy has the following theoretical advantages: gain of time to prepare for limb-salvage surgery, creation of a collagenous capsule to aid delineation of tumor, allows histological evaluation of response to treatment.

Figure A is an AP and lateral radiograph showing a destructive lesion with both lytic and sclerotic areas, periosteal elevation (Codman triangle), calcification of the tumor matrix and a soft tissue mass. The osteoid matrix produced by the tumor cells creates a "fluffy" or "cloud-like" appearance. Figure B is a delayed phase bone scan demonstrating intense uptake in the distal femur. This finding is non specific and is typical in all lesions with increased bone turnover. Bone scans help exclude multicentric osteosarcoma. Figures C and D are STIR and T1 weighted coronal MRI images through the lesion showing a soft tissue mass completely replacing the medullary cavity and extending into adjacent soft tissues. It demonstrates low to intermediate signal on T1 and high on T2 weighted and STIR images. Figure E is a high-powered photomicrograph showing osteoblasts with a small amount of lacy malignant osteoid between them. Illustration A shows reconstruction with a hinged tumor prosthesis. Illustration B shows the Winkelmann classification of rotationplasty.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Repeat biopsy is unnecessary as the current histopathology is diagnostic.
Answer 2: OS is radioresistant. Radiotherapy is an option for local treatment of unresectable tumors, following intralesional resection, or as palliation of symptomatic metastases.
Answer 3: Wide resection and salvage rotationplasty is an option AFTER preadjuvant chemotherapy. A Winkelmann type AI (not BII) rotationplasty is indicated. In Type A rotationplasty (tibial rotationplasty), the ankle acts as the knee joint (AI for distal femur lesions, AII for proximal tibia). In Type B rotationplasty (hip rotationplasty), the knee acts as the hip joint and the ankle acts as the knee joint (BI for proximal femur lesions not involving the hip, BII for proximal femur lesions involving the hip, and BIII for lesions requiring total femur resections).
Answer 4: Total knee arthroplasty, or more appropriately, salvage knee reconstruction, with a hinged tumor megaprosthesis is indicated AFTER neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Customized implants are unnecessary.
