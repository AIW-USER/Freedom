## Question

A 22-year-old swimmer presents to the clinic with generalized hyperlaxity and complaints of numerous self-reduced right shoulder dislocations. She has a Beighton score of 6 and significant apprehension on physical exam. She ultimately fails conservative treatment and undergoes an arthroscopic capsular shift in the Beach Chair position with the arm in relative adduction and internal rotation. If she presents to the clinic post-operatively with neuropraxia, at which of the following locations was the most likely involved nerve injury?

## Options

1. At the 6 o'clock position inferior to the infraglenoid tubercle
2. At the level of the spinoglenoid notch
3. At the 9 o'clock position when viewed with a 30 degree scope from the posterior viewing portal
4. At the level of the suprascapular notch
5. At the 12 o'clock position at the level of the supraglenoid tubercle

## Correct option

**1. At the 6 o'clock position inferior to the infraglenoid tubercle**

## Answer explanation

The axillary nerve is most often injured during surgeries involving the anteroinferior labrum at the 6 o'clock position inferior to the infraglenoid tubercle (Answer 1)

Though arthroscopic labral repairs and surgeries for shoulder instability are well-established and relatively safe procedures, when the inferior capsule is involved at the 6 o'clock position, the axillary nerve is particularly at risk of injury. The course of the axillary nerve proceeds anterior to the subscapularis muscle, draping over the inferolateral border of the subscapularis tendon, before passing posteriorly through the quadrilateral space (Illustration A). Along this path, it passes within 12-16mm of the inferior glenoid rim, making it susceptible to injury when a large capsular shift/plication is performed. Although performing capsular shifts within and up to 1 cm from the glenoid rim is generally considered safe, taking large amounts of capsule (> 1 cm) to tighten the capsule in this region risks inadvertently damaging the axillary nerve.

*Matsuki et al. provide a review of complications after arthroscopic labral repair for shoulder instability.* **The authors note** that the axillary nerve can be injured when placing sutures at the anteroinferior and inferior positions (the 4:30- and 6-o’clock positions, respectively, in the right shoulder) or when repairing capsular lesions, such as a capsular tear or humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament. **They conclude** that once a patient is suspected to have nerve injury after surgery, electromyography (EMG) can be performed, followed by a "wait and see" period of 3–6 months (even if EMG suggests denervation), followed by surgical treatment should the symptoms fail to improve after that period.

*Moen et al. provide a comprehensive JAAOS review article on complications of shoulder arthroscopy.* In terms of nerve injury, **the authors note** that the axillary, suprascapular, and musculocutaneous nerves are most vulnerable to direct injury. Compared with posterior portals, anterior portals, particularly anteroinferior portals, are associated with a greater risk of injury to neurovascular structures during arthroscopic surgery, and given the proximity of the axillary nerve to the inferior glenoid and joint capsule, the margin of safety diminishes as the portal is placed in progressively inferior positions. **They conclude** that sutures placed within the glenohumeral capsule no further than 1 cm from the glenoid rim are at a relatively safe distance from the axillary nerve.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: A capsular shift is not performed at the spinoglenoid notch, and the suprascapular nerve (not the axillary nerve) passes through this location.
Answer 3: The 9 o'clock position here would be the posterior labrum, which would not be involved in the capsular shift and would not affect the axillary nerve, which has passed to the quadrilateral space.
Answer 4: A capsular shift is not performed at the level of the suprascapular notch, and the suprascapular nerve (not the axillary nerve) passes through this location.
Answer 5: The 12 o'clock position represents the proximal biceps/superior labral complex and would not be involved in the capsular shift.
