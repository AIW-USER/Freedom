## Question

A 15-year-old male with trisomy 21 patient presents requesting orthopedic clearance to compete as a gymnast in the Special Olympics. He has already been cleared by both his cardiologist and endocrinologist. He has been treated in the past for an elbow dislocation as well as bilateral recurrent patellar dislocations but presently has no complaints. He denies any pain, difficulty ambulating, and reports that his training has been progressing well. Which of the following is recommended prior to participation?

## Options

1. AP pelvic radiograph
2. Imaging of both previously injured knees and elbow
3. Imaging of the cervical spine
4. Referral to a neurologist
5. The patient may be cleared for participation without further work-up

## Correct option

**3. Imaging of the cervical spine**

## Answer explanation

Cervical spine clearance of athletes with Down's syndrome is strongly recommended by most governing athletic bodies (and formally required by the Special Olympics) prior to participation in activities resulting in hyperextension, radical flexion, or direct pressure on the neck or cervical spine. Clearance must include a full physical examination and full flexion-extension cervical spine films to rule out atlantoaxial instability.

Patients with Down's Syndrome typically present with generalized ligamentous laxity and decreased tone. Joint subluxation and dislocation are common. Asymptomatic cervical instability is also common, and due to purported increased risk of spinal cord injury with hyperextension, radical flexion, or direct pressure in the setting of hypermobility, cervical spine clearance is recommended and often mandated. Within the Special Olympics, athletes are temporarily restricted from activities resulting in said stresses (including gymnastics, swimming, high jump, etc.) until formally evaluated, after which the athlete may be either cleared in the absence of any instability or permanently restricted if atlantoaxial instability is present, regardless of symptomaticity.

*McKay et al. performed a systematic review of congenital causes of cervical instability.* **The authors noted** that atlantoaxial instability was the most common variety of cervical instability in patients with Down's syndrome. **They recommended** that asymptomatic patients with an ADI < 4.5mm be allowed to participate in unrestricted activities, that asymptomatic patients with an ADI between 4.5-10mm not be allowed to participate in contact sports, and that patients with an ADI >10mm and either symptoms and/or cord changes on MRI should undergo decompression and fusion.

*Dedlow et al. reviewed guidelines for evaluation and management of cervical instability in patients with Down's syndrome.* **The authors highlighted** an increasing emphasis on radiographic re-evaluation, regardless of prior clearance. **They concluded** that re-examination should occur prior to participation in sports and/or the onset of new symptoms.

Illustrations A, B and C highlight the C1-2 instability on flexion-extension lateral radiographs. Careful attention can be placed on the relationship of the anterior arch of C1 and the dens (Illustration C). This allows for the measurements of ADI and the space available for the cord (SAC), which is highlighted in Illustration D.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: While hip instability is common in patients with Down's syndrome, an AP pelvis radiograph is not warranted in the absence of symptoms.
Answer 2: In the absence of symptoms, additional imaging of the knees and elbow are not formally required.
Answer 4: A referral to a neurologist is not required in the absence of any neurologic symptoms.
Answer 5: Even in the absence of symptoms, the Special Olympics formally requires flexion-extension cervical spine radiographs prior to participation in activities requiring hyperextension, radical flexion, or direct pressure on the neck.
