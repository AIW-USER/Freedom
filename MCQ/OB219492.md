## Question

A 75-year-old male comes into the clinic with a painful metal-on-polyethylene total hip arthroplasty that was performed nearly seven years ago. His erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and c-reactive protein (CRP) levels are within normal limits, his cobalt ion levels are 2 ng/mL, and his chromium ion levels are 1 ng/mL. He undergoes a revision total hip arthroplasty, and his recovered femoral stem is demonstrated in Figure A. Which of the following implant design features has been shown to contribute most to the type of corrosion that necessitated this patient's revision surgery?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Reduced offset stems
2. Shorter trunion mating surface
3. Smaller diameter femoral heads
4. Use of a similar alloy titanium couple
5. Wider taper trunnion design

## Correct option

**2. Shorter trunion mating surface**

## Answer explanation

The retrieved stem shows evidence of mechanically-assisted crevice corrosion (MACC), or trunnionosis, a risk factor for which is shorter trunnion geometry with a thinner taper, creating an increasingly flexible construct that allows more fluid ingress and disruption of the passivation layer, generating higher torsional forces, micromotion, and subsequent trunnion wear.

Corrosion represents the dissolution of metal in a solution. This can occur by numerous mechanisms, either mechanical or chemical in nature, with the loss of metal ions through the corrosive process resulting in reduced structural integrity of modular implant components such as the trunnion in a total hip arthroplasty (THA). The most commonly used metals in THA include titanium and cobalt-chrome, which form a surface oxide layer known as the passivation layer that when disrupted by corrosive mechanisms generates crevices allowing for re-oxidation of the underlying metal substrate. Over time, oxygen-rich fluid continues to enter these crevices, generating more ions from the underlying metal components to neutralize the acidic corrosive layer, a process termed mechanically assisted crevice corrosion (MACC). As this process continues, the construct weakens and can result in catastrophic implant failure. The factors that have been associated with an increased risk of trunnionosis include shorter, thinner trunnion geometry (which was designed to reduce the risk of impingement but comes at the cost of increasing edge loading), as well as the use of high-offset stems, larger femoral heads, and mixed-alloy implant couples.

*Wylde et al. reviewed catastrophic failures of the Accolade I hip arthroplasty stem in a retrieval analysis study.* **The authors found** that a characteristic destructive wear pattern of the femoral taper (trunnion) resembling a “bird beak” appearance was present in all stems, with this wear pattern creating excessive movement and loosening resulting in a trunnion/head dislocation or brittle fracture of the trunnion. **They concluded** that catastrophic femoral neck fracture was likely due to a combination of material composition mismatch and mechanically assisted fretting corrosion at the head-neck junction leading to gross metallosis and failure.

*Patel et al. published a case report of head-neck taper corrosion following total hip arthroplasty with the Stryker Meridian stem.* *The authors report on a case of a 76-year-old woman with hip pain, abductor weakness, and sciatic nerve symptoms 2 years following metal-on-polyethylene THA with elevated serum cobalt levels and normal chromium levels.* **They found** evidence of locally destructive adverse local tissue reaction (ALTR) on MRI, leading them to conclude that the titanium-molybdenum-zirconium-iron (TMZF) alloy stem and V40 trunnion taper geometry may predispose to destructive head-neck taper corrosion.

*Baratz et al. provide a case report of a fractured, tapered femoral neck associated with a cementless femoral component that had been coupled with a skirted femoral head with a high neck length.* **The authors note** that this case illustrates the potential for catastrophic failure of a femoral component in a previously well-functioning total hip arthroplasty and conclude with a recommendation against the use of skirted femoral head components with a high neck length, particularly in obese patients, due to risk of trunnion failure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Increased offset stems have been theorized to increase the risk of trunnionosis due to increased fretting motion, generating higher levels of metal ions at the level of the trunnion.
Answer 3: Larger diameter femoral heads, though they allow a greater range of impingement-free movement, have been shown to increase wear at the head-neck junction, likely due to greater torque acting on the interface with the larger diameter head.
Answer 4: The use of mixed-metal pairings rather than similar alloy implant couples has been shown to give rise to greater rates of trunnionosis through galvanic corrosion of dissimilar metals.
Answer 5: A wider taper trunnion design increases flexural rigidity which has been associated with less micromotion and reduced corrosion.
