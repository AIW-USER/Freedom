## Answer explanation

The radiograph in Figure A shows an oblique, axially unstable fourth metacarpal shaft fracture with mild shortening. This fracture pattern is the least amenable to intramedullary screw fixation of the figures provided.

Metacarpal fractures are among the most common hand injuries sustained. Many times, these fractures can be treated with nonoperative management (with debated indications). When surgery is indicated, multiple modalities exist, including closed reduction percutaneous pinning, plate fixation, trans-metarcarpal Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation, and intramedullary screw fixation. For metacarpal shaft and neck fractures that exhibit axial stability, intramedullary screw fixation can be used even with large amounts of angulation and rotation. Intramedullary screws offer the advantage of relatively quick insertion while protecting structures with a minimally invasive approach, providing stability for early range of motion, and eliminates the risk of hardware irritation. Retrograde headless compression intramedullary screws rely on adequate axial stability. Large amounts of angulation and rotation can be corrected with closed reduction prior to screw implantation. The most stable fracture pattern would be a transverse shaft fracture. Less stable fracture patterns are characterized by shortening, comminution, or a fracture length that is twice as long as the diameter of the shaft. The transverse metacarpal ligaments can prevent shortening in isolated metacarpal fractures. Due to the compressive nature of the screw and method of implantation, axial instability is not easily corrected and can often worsen with intramedullary screw fixation.

*Ruchelsman et al. published a retrospective review of patients treated with retrograde intramedullary headless compression screws for metacarpal neck and shaft fractures out of their institution.* *The authors assessed 39 patients (13 shaft fractures, 26 neck/subcapital fractures) with axially stable metacarpal fractures for clinical and radiographic outcomes after intramedullary screw fixation.* **The authors found** all patients achieved full active metacarpophalangeal joint extension and full composite fist flexion. Grip strength returned to an average 105% (range 58% to 230%) of the contralateral hand and all patients achieved radiographic union at 6 weeks time with no patients requiring secondary surgery. **The authors concluded** that retrograde intramedullary headless screw fixation is safe and reliable for metacarpal neck and axially stable shaft fractures.

*Avery et al. performed a biomechanical study of metacarpal neck fractures treated with single headless compression screws versus two retrograde K-Wires.* *Thirty-one total metacarpals were fractured near the neck, randomized into the two treatment groups, and subjected to either 3-point bending or axial loading.* **The results of the study showed** higher peak load to failure, 3-point bending, and axial loading in the headless compression screw group. **The authors concluded** that headless compression screws may allow for an earlier postoperative range of motion and therefore earlier return to activity than K-wire fixation.

Figure A demonstrates an oblique, axially unstable fourth metacarpal shaft fracture with mild shortening. Figure B demonstrates transverse shaft fractures of the fourth and fifth metacarpal shafts. Figure C demonstrates a metacarpal neck fracture of the fifth metacarpal with moderate angulation.
Figure D demonstrates a transverse shaft fracture of the fourth metacarpal shaft. Figure E demonstrates multiple transverse metacarpal shaft fractures with shortening and significant displacement. Illustration A is a postoperative radiograph of three intramedullary headless compression screws in the second, third, and fourth metacarpals.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Figure B demonstrates transverse shaft fractures of the fourth and fifth metacarpal shafts. Due to the transverse nature, these fractures would be inherently axially stable and therefore amenable to intramedullary screw fixation.
Answer 3: Figure C demonstrates a metacarpal neck fracture of the fifth metacarpal with moderate angulation. Although this fracture can be treated non-operatively, it would still be amenable to intramedullary screw fixation based on the more transverse nature of the fracture.
Answer 4: Figure D demonstrates a transverse shaft fracture of the fourth metacarpal shaft. This fracture is transverse, axially stable, and amenable to intramedullary screw fixation.
Answer 5: Figure E demonstrates multiple transverse metacarpal shaft fractures with shortening and significant displacement. Multiple metacarpal fractures require operative fixation for stabilization and these fractures would be axially stable once adequately reduced.
