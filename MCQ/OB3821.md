## Question

A 47-year-old male presents to your office 4 weeks following a traumatic laceration to his anterior ankle. His wound was irrigated and closed in the ED. He complains that when he walks his foot slaps to the ground, which has significantly limited his activities since the injury. His foot is well perfused, has a palpable DP pulse, and sensation over the dorsum of the foot is intact. A representative MRI image is seen in Figure A. ## Options

1. Provide no significant improvement over closed treament
2. Restore active dorsiflexion to pre-injury strength only if direct repair is achieved
3. Restore active dorsiflexion to pre-injury strength if soft tissue augmentation is used
4. Restore active dorsiflexion with some residual weakness and improve activity level
5. Restore active dorsiflexion with some residual weakness without significant improvement in activity level

## Correct option

**4. Restore active dorsiflexion with some residual weakness and improve activity level**

## Answer explanation

This patients history and the image in Figure A is consistent with a tibialis anterior rupture. For acute traumatic anterior tibial tendon injuries surgical repair is recommended. It is expected to restore active dorsiflexion and improve the patient's activity level despite some residual weakness.

Anterior tibial tendon ruptures are uncommon injuries. Diagnosis can be delayed due to the action of secondary ankle dorsiflexors (EHL and EDL). In the acute setting (defined as < 6 weeks), surgical repair is recommended. This can be done via direct repair, or with soft tissue augmentation. Although full strength of dorsiflexion is not reliably returned, the gain in dorsiflexion provides a significant increase in activity level and high patient satisfaction.

In a very low demand patient, these injuries can be treated conservatively with an ankle-foot orthosis (AFO). For chronic ruptures, debridement and direct repair can be attempted, but often augmentation with a free tendon graft or EHL tenodesis may be needed.

*Kopp et al. present a retrospective review of 11 patients with traumatic anterior tibial tendon ruptures treated acutely.* **They found** increased AOFAS scores, decreased pain, improved activity levels, and high patient satisfaction after surgery. Kinetic testing during follow-up showed that despite the subjective increase in activity level, there was objective weakness with measured peak torque about the ankle of 72%-84% of the uninjured side.

Figure A is a sagittal MR image demonstrating a tibialis anterior rupture. The "empty sheath" can be seen just deep to the gel tab placed over the skin.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Surgical repair has shown improved outcomes over closed treatment of anterior tibial tendon ruptures in the acute setting.
Answers 2 and 3: Restoration of active dorsiflexion to pre-injury strength is typically not achieved regardless of repair technique.
Answer 5: Despite the residual weakness, activity levels improve after repair.
