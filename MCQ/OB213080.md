## Question

A 75-year-old patient presents with severe back pain after sustaining an osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture 4 months ago. The patient continues to have debilitating pain that prevents normal activities of daily living despite the use of bisphosphonates, anti-inflammatory pain medications, and bracing. ## Options

1. Continued conservative management
2. Vertebroplasty
3. Kyphoplasty
4. Anterior corpectomy and fusion
5. Posterior spinal fusion

## Correct option

**3. Kyphoplasty**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with an old vertebral compression fracture that has developed avascular necrosis with nonunion and collapse. A reasonable next treatment option for this would be kyphoplasty.

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures are one of the most common fragility fractures and have a high concordance with medical frailty. The 2-year mortality rate is approximately 20%, which is comparable to that of hip fractures. Treatment of vertebral compression fractures initially involves conservative measures, including pain medications, activity modifications, brief bed rest, and TLSO bracing. Persistent symptoms beyond 6-8 weeks warrant further treatment measures including percutaneous cement augmentation, preferably kyphoplasty due to lower complication rates and improved sagittal correction. However, even with kyphoplasty, there is a risk the patient may get a limited benefit and is usually reserved after exhaustive conservative measures have been attempted. Open surgical treatment, such as anterior or posterior spinal fusion, is limited to patients presenting with neurological deficits and retropulsion of the vertebral body fragments, which would make cement augmentation at high risk for spinal cord compression.

*Mattie et al. performed a meta-analysis of studies comparing percutaneous vertebral cement augmentation to conservative treatment of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures.* At all time points, there were significantly improved pain scores with cement augmentation compared to conservative management, with the greatest difference at 1-2 weeks. However, each time point failed to achieve the minimal clinically important difference.

*Kim et al. reviewed the literature regarding the treatment of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures.* *The authors cited literature that reported patients treated with early cement augmentation were found to have improved pain score outcomes and that complications in these patients were rare but included cement extrusion, pulmonary embolism, and adjacent segment fracture due to increased vertebral body stiffness.* However, they could not conclude that cement augmentation is a justified first-line therapy and therefore stated that a reasonable approach would include 6-8 weeks of conservative management.

Figures A and B are sagittal and coronal CT images of the lumbar spine with an L2 compression fracture complicated by nonunion and the presence of gas in the vertebral body, consistent with Kümmell disease.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Continued conservative treatment is inappropriate at this time due to the degree of symptoms and the lack of healing potential.
Answer 2: Vertebroplasty is not an appropriate treatment option due to the high likelihood of complications from cement extrusion and the lack of sagittal correction. Vertebroplasty involves the percutaneous injection of low viscous cement at high pressures in the vertebral body compared to kyphoplasty, where high viscous cement is placed at low pressures following ballon reduction of the fracture.
Answer 4: An anterior corpectomy and fusion is not necessary as there is no neurologic symptoms or retropulsion fracture fragments.
Answer 5: Posterior spinal fusion is not necessary as there are no neurologic deficits or spinal cord compression.
