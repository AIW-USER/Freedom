5. Calcium oxalate crystals; rotator cuff debridement, possible repair and administer intravenous colchicine

## Correct option

**2. Calcium hydroxyapatite crystals; rotator cuff debridement, possible repair**

## Answer explanation

This patient has calcific tendinitis of the rotator cuff (CTRC). Pathology is likely to show calcium hydroxyapatite crystal deposits surrounded by fibroblasts, macrophages, giant cells and lymphocytes. The most appropriate surgical treatment is debridement of the rotator cuff with removal of the calcium deposits.

Calcific tendinitis of the rotator cuff (CTRC) results from deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite within the substance of the rotator cuff tissue. Nonoperative treatment includes NSAIDs, PT, corticosteroid injections, extracorporeal shock wave therapy and ultrasound-guided needle lavage. Most cases spontaneously improve over time; however, symptoms that persist despite conservative management can be treated with arthroscopic debridement. Subsequent RCR is controversial and is based on integrity of the remaining tissue and extent of the defect.

*El Shewy et al. reported a case series of 56 patients with CTRC who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff debridement without RCR.* *All patients were able to return to previous level of activity and had significant improvement in functional outcome scores.* Only 3.7% of cases developed rotator cuff tears over the 7 year follow up period. **The authors concluded** that arthroscopic debridement of symptomatic calcium deposits without RCR is a safe and effective treatment for CTRC.

*Suzuki et al. reviewed CTRC.* Though the exact etiology is unclear, CTRC is thought to be an active, cell-mediated process, composed of three distinct phases: formative, resting and resorptive, with the resorptive phase causing the greatest pain. Surgical treatment involves arthroscopic removal of calcific deposits. Further research is required to determine the utility of subacromial decompression and/or RCR in these patients.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the right shoulder notable for a large calcific deposit. Figures B and C are the T1 and STIR MRI sequences of the right shoulder, respectively, showing large hypointense lesions within the supraspinatus tendon, consistent with CTRC. Deposits tend to occur 1-2cm from the tendon insertion, an area of decreased vascularity, and can be multifocal. Figure D is an arthroscopic picture of the subacromial space showing paste-like calcium material being expressed from the rotator cuff (Barber et al.).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Intravenous colchicine does not play a role in treatment of CTRC.
Answer 3 + 4: Calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals do not play a role in CTRC. CPP appear as positively birefringent rhomboid and rod-like shaped crystals and lead to CPP deposition disease or pseudogout. Intravenous colchicine does not play a role in treatment of CTRC.
Answer 5: Calcium oxalate (CO) crystals do not play a role in CTRC. CO crystals are a major constituent of kidney stones and can be found in the urine. In cases of ethylene glycol toxicity (antifreeze poisoning), the formation of excess CO crystals causes renal failure. Intravenous colchicine does not play a role in treatment of CTRC.
