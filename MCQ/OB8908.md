## Options

1. Increased shoulder external rotation
2. Superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tear
3. Bursal-sided partial rotator cuff tear
4. Humeral head retroversion
5. Glenoid retroversion

## Correct option

**3. Bursal-sided partial rotator cuff tear**

## Answer explanation

The sleeper stretch (A) and cross-body stretch (B) are commonly part of the rehabilitation for Glenohumeral Internal Rotation Deficit (GIRD), which is associated with soft tissue adaptations (increased sulcus sign), osseous adaptations (humeral head and glenoid retroversion) and pathology including superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tears and articular-sided partial rotator cuff tears (RCT), not bursal-sided.

GIRD is commonly seen in overhead throwing athletes. It is characterized by increased shoulder external rotation (ER) with a corresponding loss of internal rotation (IR deficit >25° difference compared to contralateral shoulder). The majority of athletes respond to physical therapy that focuses on stretching the posterior shoulder structures. It is associated with internal impingement. Uncommonly, the few patients who fail conservative management may be treated with selective arthroscopic posteroinferior capsulotomy in the region of the posterior band of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (PIGHL).

*Braun et al. reviewed shoulder injuries in the throwing athlete.* GIRD results from stretching of the anterior soft tissues that resist ER (coracohumeral ligament, rotator interval) and contracture of the posterior soft tissues (PIGHL), pectoralis minor and short head of the biceps, with subsequent posterior shift in the shoulder center of rotation. Excessive ER strains the biceps anchor, which is “peeled back” under tension, ultimately culminating in injury to the superior labrum and tensile failure of the rotator cuff with development of partial articular-sided tears. Articular-sided partial RCT may also result from internal impingement, as the greater tuberosity contacts the posterosuperior glenoid in abduction and ER, pinching the rotator cuff.

*Crockett et al. evaluated glenohumeral (GH) joint motion and osseous anatomy of the dominant and nondominant shoulders of 25 professional pitchers and 25 nonthrowing subjects.* Professional pitchers demonstrated significantly increased ER, humeral head and glenoid retroversion, and decreased IR in their dominant shoulder compared to nondominant shoulder. There was no difference in total range of motion and anterior or posterior laxity between the shoulders. Nonthrowing subjects had no significant difference between the dominant and nondominant shoulders.

Figures A and B are images from Braun et al. demonstrating the sleeper stretch and cross-body stretch, respectively. The sleeper stretch is performed by having the patient lie in the lateral decubitus position on the affected side with the shoulder in 90° of forward flexion and internally rotating the shoulder. The cross-body stretch is performed by having the patient stand against a wall to prevent the scapula from rotating and using the other arm to pulled the involved arm across the body.

Incorrect Responses:
Answer 1: Increased shoulder external rotation is often seen in patients with GIRD due to stretching of anterior structures that resist external rotation (coracohumeral ligament, rotator interval).
Answer 2: SLAP tears are commonly associated with GIRD and are thought to result from “peeling back” of the biceps anchor under excessive tension in ER with subsequent injury to the superior labrum.
Answers 4 + 5: Humeral head and glenoid retroversion are seen in patients who began pitching prior to skeletal maturity. These adaptive osseous changes develop in response to a posterior shift in the GH joint center of rotation.
