## Options

1. He should not put any weight on his wrist until the radiocarpal joint fuses
2. He should perform daily hand/finger ROM and should use a wheelchair as his wrist should be non-weight bearing
3. He should perform daily hand/finger ROM and may use a platform walker immediately to assist with ambulation
4. He should perform daily hand/finger ROM and may use crutches immediately to assist with ambulation
5. The patient should not require durable medical equipment and should walk without any assistive devices

## Correct option

**3. He should perform daily hand/finger ROM and may use a platform walker immediately to assist with ambulation**

## Answer explanation

While dorsal bridge plating allows for immediate upper extremity weight bearing after surgery, there are limitations in the construct’s axial loading tolerance ultimately required for the use of ambulatory assistive devices. The current, safest recommendation is to allow for front-wheel walker assistance (Answer 3) as crutch assistance requires higher axial load demands than tolerable for these bridge plate constructs.

Dorsal bridge plate (DBP) fixation was designed with the intent of treating comminuted distal radius fractures via ligamentotaxis-assisted reduction. Given the plate’s ability to initially serve as a load-bearing device, it can allow for upper extremity weight-bearing in the immediate postoperative period. For this reason, it serves as a useful fixation modality in two settings: (1) high-energy polytrauma to avoid the numerous complications of wrist external fixators and (2) comminuted fractures in elderly patients dependent on assistive devices for ambulation. In both circumstances, DBP allows for the earlier use of ambulatory assistive devices and purported avoidance of fracture-related disease seen with prolonged bed rest. However, immediate DBP axial stability (i.e. before any osseous healing in the immediate postoperative period) has been questioned given that approximately 50% of body weight load is transmitted to the wrist during crutch-assisted ambulation. Biomechanical cadaveric studies have demonstrated that DBP can safely brunt the axial loads demonstrated with front-wheel walker assistance (i.e. ~22% body weight with tripod positioning), but cannot safely brunt the loads demonstrated with crutch assistance.

*Huang and colleagues performed a cadaveric study to compare the biomechanical stability of a low-profile 2.4mm titanium DBP to a standard volar-locked plate (VLP) during simulated crutch weight-bearing.* In their model, DBP failed at lower axial loads compared to VLP (i.e. the plate would bend through a central screw hole into flexion posturing). **The authors concluded** that while DBP is effective as an internal spanning fixator to prevent axial collapse at the radiocarpal joint, it may not offer the purported weight-bearing advantage over volar-locked plating. **They recommended** a stiffer 3.5mm DPB or the use of a DBP construct without central holes to better maximize the plate’s biomechanical stiffness compared to their study’s model.

*Raducha and colleagues performed a cadaveric study to demonstrate the biomechanical stiffness of a 3.5mm stainless steel DPB without central holes and their resistance to axial loads.* In a multi-armed, pragmatic load-to-failure model, **they demonstrated** that the DPB could safely resist over 22% body weight (i.e. required to front wheel walker tripod-based ambulation), but had an unacceptable failure rate with axial forces near 50% body weight (i.e. required for crutch assistance). **The authors concluded** that elderly, walker-dependent patients may be able to use their walker as tolerated immediately after DPB fixation, however, should not be allowed to bear full weight using crutches in the immediate postoperative period.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient underwent DPB of their distal radius fracture, not fusion plating.
Answer 2: In this elderly patient, avoidance of complete immobilization is advisable to avoid fracture-related disease complications (i.e. pneumonia, pressure sores, etc.).
Answer 4: As discussed, the axial load requirements for crutch-assisted ambulation are generally higher than DBP constructs can safely handle.
Answer 5: Octogenarians with lower extremity fractures are at high risk for falls during the rehabilitation process. They should be utilizing ambulatory assistive devices (especially with therapist assistance) during the immediate postoperative period.
