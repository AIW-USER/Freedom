## Question

A large retrospective database study evaluates 90-day postoperative outcomes in patients with a history of venous thromboembolism (VTE) undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA). Patients receiving aspirin for VTE prophylaxis are compared to those receiving non-aspirin anticoagulants. The forest plot shown in Figure A displays odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for various complications. Which of the following statements is most accurate?

![[q220572-a-image001-2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Odds ratio for DVT favors aspirin, but the 95% CI crosses 1, indicating no significant difference between groups
2. Aspirin has a larger effect size on post-operative PE than pneumonia
3. Compared to the odds of postoperative hematoma, the outcome of a DVT has less uncertainty
4. Although MI favors aspirin, the CI includes 1, indicating significance with a small effect size
5. OR interpretation does not depend on whether values reflect adjusted logistic regression or crude proportions

## Correct option

**3. Compared to the odds of postoperative hematoma, the outcome of a DVT has less uncertainty**

## Answer explanation

The confidence interval (CI) for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is narrower than that for hematoma, indicating a more precise estimate (Answer 3). The narrower CI range for DVT suggests less uncertainty, making it a more statistically stable result.

In general, odds ratios (OR) reflect the strength and direction of an association between an exposure (e.g., aspirin use) and an outcome (e.g., postoperative complication). An OR less than 1 indicates decreased odds in the treatment group, while an OR greater than 1 indicates increased odds. However, statistical significance is determined by the 95% confidence interval: if the CI includes 1, the result is not statistically significant at the 0.05 level. Importantly, the size of the OR does not necessarily equate to clinical importance; a statistically significant OR of 0.95 may have little clinical value, whereas an OR of 0.40 with a wide CI might reflect a strong potential effect but with high uncertainty. Confidence intervals provide critical context—they reflect the precision of the estimate, with narrower intervals indicating greater confidence in the observed effect and wider intervals signaling more variability and less certainty.

Szumilas completed a topical review on ORs, emphasizing that an OR > 1 suggests increased odds of an outcome, while an OR < 1 suggests a protective effect. It stresses that statistical significance depends on whether the 95% confidence interval (CI) excludes 1, and that narrow CIs indicate greater precision. The author highlights that a non-significant result does not imply no effect, and cautions against interpreting ORs without considering CI width and context.

Tenny & Hoffman discuss how ORs are calculated and interpreted, especially in logistic regression. It notes that ORs may exaggerate risk compared to relative risk when outcomes are common. The 95% CI is essential for determining statistical significance and estimating precision. The article also stresses the importance of distinguishing adjusted vs. crude ORs, as confounding can significantly alter interpretation.

Figure A demonstrates a forest plot comparing postoperative outcomes between aspirin and non-aspirin VTE prophylaxis after total hip arthroplasty. Aspirin was associated with significantly lower odds of DVT, PE, hematoma, GI bleed, MI, pneumonia, and AKI, while other outcomes showed no significant difference between groups.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The CI for postoperative DVT does not include 1, indicating a statistically significant result.
Answer 2: While OR may indicate statistical significance of an intervention if the CI does not include 1, it does not capture the effect size of a treatment.
Answer 4: Aspirin was associated with significantly lower odds of postoperative MI, as the CI does not include 1.
Answer 5: The interpretation of the odds ratio differs substantially depending on which method is used, as unadjusted ORs reflect raw group differences and are susceptible to confounding. Adjusted ORs aim to isolate the association between treatment and outcome by controlling for other variables via matching or logistic regression.
