## Question

A 20-year-old collegiate tennis player slips while playing on an unfamiliar clay court and catches himself with his non-dominant hand. Over the next week, he notes continued pain about his volar palm and has reproducible tenderness at his anatomic snuffbox. If plain radiographs are negative and a follow-up "scaphoid view" is ordered in the clinic, which of the following would be the best way to direct an unfamiliar radiology tech to shoot this image?

## Options

1. Perform a postero-anterior (PA) view in maximal radial deviation
2. Perform a postero-anterior (PA) view in 20° ulnar deviation
3. Perform a 30° supinated lateral view
4. Perform a semi-pronated 45° oblique view
5. Perform an axial view in hyperextension

## Correct option

**2. Perform a postero-anterior (PA) view in 20° ulnar deviation**

## Answer explanation

A scaphoid view adds 20° of ulnar deviation to a neutral PA view of the wrist in order to free the profile of the scaphoid from any bony superimposition.

Scaphoid fractures represent the most common carpal bone fractures and can generally be diagnosed on dedicated radiographs of the wrist; however, radiographs can be negative in up to 27% of cases on initial presentation and should be repeated in 14-21 days if suspicion remains high. Dedicated views, such as the "scaphoid view," are particularly adept at showing scaphoid waist fractures, but more subtle fractures may require advanced imaging such as CT or MRI, especially in the acute period, with MRI being the most sensitive method of detection for acute fractures occurring < 24 hours from the time of injury.

*Compson et al. published the radiological anatomy of the scaphoid in a multi-part series.* *The authors studied 50 dry cadaver scaphoids and noted that the "radiological waist" is rarely the same as the true anatomic waist due to the foreshortening of the bone that normally lies in flexion.* **They concluded** that the position of this "apparent" waist is highly dependent on the angle of the X-ray beam, which changes with carpal alignment.

*Malik et al. provide a review of scaphoid views, as well as a plug for the need for standardization of radiographs used in England at the time of publication, given that* **the authors believe** that asking for scaphoid views from the radiology department often leads to a wide variety and number of radiographic views being taken. **They concluded** that not only is there a complete lack of agreement on what 'scaphoid views' should be, but that there was little agreement on the nomenclature of the individual views themselves.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This is not a standard view.
Answer 3: This view can be obtained to provide a better view of the pisiform and piso-triquetral joint.
Answer 4: This view represents a standard oblique radiograph that would have been obtained in the initial non-diagnostic series.
Answer 5: This represents a carpal tunnel view, which is not used to visualize the scaphoid.
