## Question

A 19-year-old male collegiate football player sustains a non-contact knee injury when he is attempting to spin away from a defender. Three years prior he had undergone trans-tibial ACL reconstruction with a hamstring autograft and had been doing well up until this point. Based on this athlete's desire to return to high-level sports, you recommend revision ACL reconstruction. For his revision ACL reconstruction, which of the following factors most increases the risk for re-rupture?

## Options

1. Utilization of new femoral tunnel
2. Posterior tibial slope = 7°
3. Use of semitendinosus allograft
4. Addition of lateral extra-articular tenodesis
5. Two-stage revision with initial bone grafting

## Correct option

**3. Use of semitendinosus allograft**

## Answer explanation

Utilization of allograft for revision anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction places patients at higher risk for re-rupture compared to the use of autograft tissue.

Rupture of the ACL is very common and >400,000 ACL reconstructions are performed on a yearly basis. Unfortunately, re-rupture is a known complication, occurring in approximately 5% of cases. In cases of ACL revision reconstruction, there are several important considerations, one of which is graft selection. The surgeon must consider what graft was used for the prior ACL reconstruction and what autograft options may still be available. Additionally, an allograft may be used, although there is literature available to suggest that the re-revision rate is higher with the use of allograft compared to autograft. Other considerations include coronal and sagittal plane alignment, tunnel widening, methods of fixation and use of supplemental procedures (lateral extra-articular tenodesis, anterolateral ligament reconstruction, etc).

*Nissen et al. compared allograft and autograft use in ACL revision from a Danish knee ligament reconstruction registry.* **They noted** that the re-revision rate was significantly higher after allograft use (12.7%) versus autograft use (5.4%), leading to a 2.2x higher rate of revision with the use of allograft. **They also noted** that objective knee stability was improved with the use of autograft, although outcome scores were not significantly different between the two groups.

*Grassi et al. performed a meta-analysis of 32 studies evaluating whether graft choice affected the outcome of revision ACL reconstruction.* *1,192 patients received an autograft, while 269 received an allograft; of the allograft group, two studies used irradiated grafts, while seven did not.* **They noted** that autografts had better outcome scores and decreased laxity overall, but when irradiated allografts were excluded, outcomes were similar.

*Wright et al. evaluated the effect of graft choice on outcomes of revision ACL reconstruction as part of the MARS group.* **They noted** that autograft use improved IKDC and KOOS sports/quality of life outcome scores at 2-year follow-up. Perhaps most importantly, **they noted** 24 re-ruptures in allografts and 12 in autografts, leading them to conclude that the use of an autograft decreased the risk for re-rupture by 2.78x compared to allograft.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Utilization of a new femoral tunnel in a more, "anatomic," position in the setting of prior transtibial reconstruction is not a risk factor for re-revision.
Answer 2: Excessive posterior tibial slope should be carefully evaluated in the setting of ACL re-rupture, but a posterior tibial slope of 7° is not excessive and would not place this patient at higher risk for re-revision.
Answer 4: The addition of a lateral extra-articular tenodesis is thought to provide additional rotational stability to the ACL reconstruction, and may be particularly useful in decreasing the rate of re-rupture in revision ACL reconstruction.
Answer 5: Two-stage ACL revision with bone grafting may be necessary in cases of significant tunnel widening (>15mm) and its utilization would not, on its own, increase the likelihood of re-rupture.
