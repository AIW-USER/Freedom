## Answer explanation

Maisonneuve fractures are diagnosed by ankle and dedicated tibia/fibula radiographs, which demonstrate an unstable ankle injury and proximal fibula fracture, respectively (Answer 2).

Maisonneuve fractures are uncommon lower extremity injuries. They are predominantly caused by a pronation-external rotation mechanism of injury, resulting first in a medial malleolus fracture or deltoid ligament injury, followed by disruption of the tibiofibular syndesmosis, and finally, a spiral or oblique fracture of the proximal fibula. A posterior tibial fracture or posterior syndesmotic injury can additionally be present in particularly severe injuries. Vigilant work-up of patients with ankle injuries should include a detailed examination of the fibula and dedicated full-length tibia/fibula films to avoid missing the presence of this injury.

*Duchesneau et al. reviewed the etiology, classification, and treatment of Maisonneuve fractures.* **They discuss** that the mechanism underlying this injury is a pronation-external rotation mechanism that progresses from an insult to the deltoid ligament/medial malleolus to involve the syndesmosis, and then the proximal fibula. **They concluded** that while the optimal technique is unclear, surgical intervention should be undertaken to mitigate the risk of consequent ankle arthrosis.

*Sproule et al. performed a retrospective review to describe the outcomes of 14 patients who underwent surgical intervention for Maisonneuve fractures.* **They noted** that 12 of the 14 patients had satisfactory results (able to resume pre-injury activity without symptoms), and patients with unsatisfactory outcomes had more severe injuries and significant co-morbidities. **They concluded** that patients with this particular fracture pattern should undergo surgical stabilization to ensure adequate reduction of the fibula and restoration of the ankle mortise.

Figure A is an AP x-ray of the ankle demonstrating an unstable ankle fracture with evidence of a medial malleolus fracture and gross widening of the tibiofibular clear space. Figure B is an AP x-ray of the foot demonstrating widening between the first and second rays, suggestive of a Lisfranc injury. Figure C is an AP x-ray of the tibia/fibula with a proximal fibula fracture suggestive of a Maisonneuve fracture. Figure D is an AP tibia/fibula x-ray without osseous abnormalities. Figure E is an AP X-ray of the knee with evidence of a Segond fracture. Figure F is a hindfoot alignment view x-ray.

Answer 1: Figure B is a radiograph of the foot demonstrating evidence of a Lisfranc injury, which is not characteristic of a Maisonneuve injury.
Answer 3: Figure D is a tibia/fibula radiograph without any osseous abnormality and does not demonstrate evidence of a proximal fibula fracture suggestive of a Maisonneuve fracture
Answer 4: Figure E is a radiograph of the knee demonstrating a Segond fracture, a finding more commonly associated with an anterior cruciate ligament injury.
Answer 5: Figure F is a hindfoot alignment view x-ray, which does not have a role in the diagnosis of a Maisonneuve fracture.
