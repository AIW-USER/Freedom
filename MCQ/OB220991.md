## Question

A 22-year-old collegiate sprinter presents with acute posterior thigh pain after forcefully accelerating out of the starting blocks. He felt a sudden “pop” and was unable to continue running. Physical examination reveals bruising over the posterior thigh, weakness with knee flexion, and pain with resisted hip extension. MRI confirms a proximal hamstring avulsion injury. Which of the following muscles is a part of the hamstring musculature but has a separate attachment to the ischial tuberosity?

## Options

1. Gracilis
2. Long head of the biceps femoris
3. Semimembranosus
4. Semitendinosus
5. Short head of the biceps femoris

## Correct option

**3. Semimembranosus**

## Answer explanation

The semimembranosus originates from a separate, more anterolateral footprint on the ischial tuberosity. The semitendinosus and long head of the biceps femoris share a common conjoint tendon arising from a more medial and posterior origin. This distinction is clinically important because proximal hamstring avulsions often involve variable patterns of tendon detachment—sometimes affecting only the conjoint tendon, sometimes the semimembranosus alone, and sometimes all three tendons. On MRI, identifying which tendon(s) are avulsed influences surgical decision-making, prognosis, and rehabilitation timelines.

During surgical repair, understanding the separate insertion of the semimembranosus is critical for accurate anatomic reattachment and restoration of normal hamstring biomechanics. Failure to recognize the distinct footprint may compromise knee flexion strength, endurance, and return-to-sport performance. Additionally, the semimembranosus lies closer to the sciatic nerve, making careful dissection essential to avoid iatrogenic nerve injury.

*Arner and colleagues aimed to review the current evidence on the diagnosis, classification, treatment, and rehabilitation of hamstring injuries in athletes.* **They found** that injury severity, mechanism (sprinting vs stretching), prior injury, and imaging features such as tendon involvement and retraction strongly influence prognosis. Progressive loading and eccentric strengthening form the cornerstone of nonoperative management, while surgery is indicated for select proximal avulsions. **They concluded** that evidence-based, injury-specific treatment protocols and structured return-to-play criteria improve outcomes and reduce reinjury rates.

*Stepien and colleagues sought to provide a detailed anatomical and imaging-based description of the proximal hamstring origin, tendon footprints, fiber orientation, and innervation patterns.* **They demonstrated** that the semimembranosus has a distinct and separate ischial footprint, while the semitendinosus and long head of the biceps femoris share a conjoint tendon, with important spatial relationships to the sciatic nerve and surrounding structures. **They concluded** that precise knowledge of proximal hamstring anatomy is essential for accurate MRI interpretation, injury classification, and safe anatomic surgical repair.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The gracilis originates from the pubis and is not one of the muscles that form the hamstrings.
Answer 2: The biceps femoris (long head) shares a common origin with the semitendinosus.
Answer 4: The semitendinosus is part of the conjoint tendon, not a separate insertion.
Answer 5: The biceps femoris (short head) arises from the linea aspera.
