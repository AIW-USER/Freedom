## Options

1. Revision total ankle arthroplasty
2. Revision total ankle arthroplasty with structural allograft
3. Ankle arthrodesis in situ
4. Ankle arthrodesis with structural allograft
5. Ankle and hindfoot arthrodesis with structural allograft

## Correct option

**5. Ankle and hindfoot arthrodesis with structural allograft**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an infected total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) with varus instability. The 1st stage entails explantation of the prosthesis, antibiotic cement spacer placement and IV antibiotics, with the objective being eradication of infection and sterilization of the joint. Because of extensive bone loss (including talar bone loss) and preexisting instability, the 2nd stage will then involve ankle and hindfoot fusion with structural allograft.

TAA behave differently from TKA and THA. There is a high failure rate owing to component loosening, malalignment, mis-sizing of components, ligamentous instability, polyethylene component dislocation, syndesmotic nonunion, malleolar impingement, persistent pain, soft tissue compromise and deep infection. The relative lack of revision implants, presence of compromised soft tissue and loss of bone stock may preclude successful retention or revision. Salvage is then possible through conversion of the TAA to fusion or transtibial amputation.

*Berkowitz et al. retrospectively reviewed the salvage of failed TAA with fusion using allograft.* There were 12 isolated ankle fusions with plate/screws, and 10/12 used structural bone graft. Theere were 12 ankle-hindfoot fusions with plate/screws, nail, or nail-plate combinations and all used structural allograft. Nonunion of the subtalar joint was the most common complication in the latter. **They recommend** a separate approach for subtalar joint preparation.

*Rand et al. reviewed the management of infected joint arthroplasties.* **They note** that 2.44% of TAA performed between 1974-1983 developed deep infection. These were treated with compression arthrodesis. In this historic paper, **they recommend** against bone grafting, and accepting shortening of 15mm or more.

Figure A shows a failed TAA with varus instability. Figure B is intraoperative fluoroscopy after explantation showing extensive tibial and talar bone loss. Illustration A shows ankle fusion with a plate (left), ankle and hindfoot fusion with a nail (center) and with a nail-plate combination (right). All 3 examples used structural femoral head allograft.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Revision TAA in the setting of extensive tibial/talar bone loss and varus instability is destined to fail. Remodeling of large structural allograft is incomplete at best, and will not tolerate shear stresses of a new TAA implant.
Answers 3 and 4: Because of talar bone loss, there will be inadequate purchase on the talar side, leading to early loosening and implant failure. Because of its shape and vascularity, talar side bone loss cannot be adequately remedied with structural allograft.
