## Question

A skeletally-mature 14-year-old girl presents with her parents to your clinic with a "lump" near her knee. She is very bothered by the appearance of her knee and it is very painful when she bumps the palpable prominence. An AP x-ray and axial MRI slice are shown in figures A and B, respectively. She has no other similar lesions elsewhere in her body, and her parents are unaware of any relevant family history. What is the next best step in management?

## Options

1. MRI and biopsy from a orthopaedic oncologist
2. Marginal resection with complete removal of cartilage cap
3. Educate the family about the benign nature of the condition and prescribe NSAIDs until the pain diminishes
4. Referral to genetics for screening for potential associated malignancies
5. Intralesional curretage and adjuvant therapy

## Correct option

**2. Marginal resection with complete removal of cartilage cap**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an isolated osteochondroma and given the painful prominence and cosmetic deformity, excision is the next best step.

In isolation, osteochondromas are benign bony tumors that can cause local tenderness or pain due to prominence. Indications for removal include mechanical pain from the prominences or limitation in joint motion. They have a near-pathognomoic appearance on x-ray with a bony stalk growing out of the metaphysis and away from the joint line. Histologically, there is a notable cartilage cap on the end of the stalk from which continued growth occurs, and it is important to perform a complete resection of the cartilage cap to limit the chance of regrowth (although margins are not routinely examined by a pathologist) when resecting these lesions. Patients with multiple osteochondromas may have a syndromic form of disease, such as Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (MHE), and genetic counseling may be helpful for families.

Stieber and Dormans' review article discusses the common manifestations of MHE. When treating these children, providers should assess for limb-length discrepancies, synostoses of the radius and ulna or tibia and fibula that can cause deformity and limitation in range of motion with or without joint incongruity. There is a slight risk of sarcomatous transformation in MHE patients, although this is not seen with isolated osteochondromas.

*Temple et al. review osteochondromas in their book chapter on benign bony tumors.* When excising these tumors, **they recommend** an adequate incision to clearly visualize and protect essential nerves and arteries that often abut these exostoses.

Figure A shows a characteristic appearance of a distal femur osteochondroma. Note the exostosis growing away from the joint. The histologic appearance of the cartilage cap is seen in Illustration A, where a "cap" of cartilage seems to be growing from a central stalk of woven bone.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Osteochondromas can be diagnosed by history, physical and x-rays
Answer 3: While osteochondromas are usually benign, this patient has pain from the prominence and a cosmetic deformity, and the stem implies operative management is preferred by the patient and family
Answer 4: Genetic screening is only helpful in assessing for familial forms in patients with multiple osteochondromas
Answer 5: This treatment would be appropriate for benign bony cysts, rather than exostoses
