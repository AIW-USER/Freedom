## Question

A 4-year-old previously healthy boy presents with acute onset of limp, and refusal to bear weight on his left leg. His temperature is 38.5°C and his vital signs are otherwise normal. His parents report minor trauma to the leg one week ago, but no chronic illnesses. On examination, he has swelling, warmth, and limited range of motion of the left hip. Laboratory studies reveal leukocytosis (WBC 15,000/mm³), elevated ESR (65 mm/hr), and CRP (8.5 mg/dL). Plain radiographs of the pelvis are normal. Ultrasound demonstrates a moderate left hip effusion. MRI reveals metaphyseal signal abnormality in the proximal femur adjacent to the hip joint. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Immediate empiric antibiotics before cultures
2. Arthrocentesis and blood cultures prior to antibiotics
3. Observation and repeat imaging in 24 hours
4. Intra-articular drain placement
5. Surgical debridement with culture acquisition

## Correct option

**5. Surgical debridement with culture acquisition**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with suspected septic arthritis of the hip with adjacent osteomyelitis (MRI showing metaphyseal signal abnormality). This child meets all four Kocher criteria (fever, elevated ESR, leukocytosis, inability to bear weight), has an elevated CRP, and has an ultrasound-confirmed hip effusion. Urgent surgical debridement with irrigation is the appropriate next step (Answer 5).

Septic arthritis of the hip is an orthopedic emergency, as bacterial proliferation can rapidly cause irreversible cartilage damage. For the hip joint specifically, arthrotomy is often the best initial approach rather than bedside arthrocentesis, as the hip is a deep joint that is difficult to aspirate reliably and requires procedural sedation regardless. The concurrent osteomyelitis further supports proceeding directly to the OR, as these cases carry a complication rate of 38% (vs. <1% for isolated septic arthritis) and higher rates of bacteremia. Synovial fluid and tissue cultures are obtained intraoperatively as part of the surgical procedure, so microbiologic diagnosis is not sacrificed.

*Arkader et al. provide a comprehensive review of the evolving epidemiology, diagnostic challenges, and management strategies for pediatric musculoskeletal infections, including osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, and pyomyositis.* It emphasizes that increased pathogen virulence and antimicrobial resistance have complicated both diagnosis and treatment, necessitating prompt differentiation from mimicking conditions (such as transient synovitis, autoimmune arthritis, and tumors) and multidisciplinary collaboration for optimal outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Antibiotics should not be started before cultures are obtained unless the patient is septic or hemodynamically unstable. Premature antibiotic administration compromises pathogen identification, which is critical given the rising prevalence of MRSA in pediatric musculoskeletal infections.
Answer 2: While arthrocentesis may be a reasonable first step for more superficial joints, performing a separate bedside hip aspiration in this case delays definitive surgical management. With high-probability septic arthritis and adjacent osteomyelitis already confirmed on MRI, proceeding to the OR (where cultures are obtained at the time of arthrotomy) is more efficient and avoids a redundant procedure.
Answer 3: This patient has clear clinical, laboratory, and imaging evidence of septic arthritis with osteomyelitis. Observation carries a risk of progressive joint destruction and is inappropriate.
Answer 4: Drain placement alone is insufficient when adjacent osteomyelitis is present. Formal arthrotomy allows thorough irrigation, debridement, and assessment of the adjacent metaphysis, which may require additional bone debridement.
