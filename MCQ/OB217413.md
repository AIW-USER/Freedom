## Question

A 34-year-old female presents to your office with heel pain. She states he has been training for a marathon but now has problems running any distance. Her pain is worse after periods of inactivity. On exam, pain is located at the medial, plantar heel and worse with forced dorsiflexion of the toes. She is frustrated and in order to get back to running, she inquires about a corticosteroid injection. What should you tell her about the mechanism and efficacy of these injections when used to treat her condition?

## Options

1. Inhibit heel pad fibroblast proliferation
2. Decrease pain by decreasing plantar fascia inflammation
3. No association with plantar fascia rupture
4. Result in good medium and long-term pain relief compared to placebo
5. Longer duration of pain relief when performed with ultrasound guidance

## Correct option

**1. Inhibit heel pad fibroblast proliferation**

## Answer explanation

Corticosteroid injections used to treat plantar fasciitis (PF) have been shown to inhibit fibroblast proliferation in the heel, leading to heel pad atrophy.

Plantar fasciitis is a condition prevalent in both younger athletes and older patients. History and physical examination are important as patients normally describe pain over the medial calcaneal tuberosity after they rise from a period of rest. Management consists mostly of nonoperative management. This includes rest, NSAIDS, stretching of the PF and gastrocnemius, and orthoses. Minimally invasive treatments like corticosteroid injections and shockwave therapy can also be employed. Corticosteroids have shown to decrease short-term pain levels, but their use is not supported by high quality evidence. Given the fact that PF is a degenerative condition, the anti-inflammatory effect of steroid injections is not thought to play a significant role in management. However, these injections may cause complications including heel pad atrophy, local site reaction and PF rupture.

*David et al. performed a review evaluating the usefulness of corticosteroid injections in patients with chronic plantar heel pain.* **They note** the absence of many high-quality placebo controlled trials, but were able to decide that there was no strong evidence that corticosteroid injections improved medium or long-term pain relief. They had difficulty recommending the use of steroid injections in PF treatment with this weak body of evidence in mind.

*Neufeld et al. described the evaluation and treatment of PF,* **noting** it is the most common cause of heel pain. They discuss the various nonoperative modalities, which are often successful in treating PF. If symptoms remain refractory from 6-12 months, they do note that surgical intervention can be an acceptable alternative.

*Latt et al. reviewed the evaluation and treatment of chronic PF.* **They note** that nonoperative management leads to pain resolution in 90% of patients, but can take 3-6 months. Other options for improvement include steroid injections, shockwave therapy, botulinum toxin and operative intervention.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Corticosteroid injections do not improve PF pain by treating inflammation, because PF is a degenerative condition.
Answer 3: While the risk is relatively low, there have been a number of reports of plantar fascia rupture after PF corticosteroid injections.
Answer 4: There is no high quality evidence to support that steroid injections lead to medium- or long-term pain relief for PF.
Answer 5: Ultrasound guidance of steroid injections for PF is not necessary and does not alter outcomes.
