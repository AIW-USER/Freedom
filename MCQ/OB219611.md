## Question

You are covering a college football game when a 21-year-old wide receiver sustains a hit to the chest with another player's helmet. He is down on the field, and when you run out to check on him, you note that he is having difficulty breathing and appears anxious. You also notice his trachea seems to be deviated and the chest wall is hypertympanic to percussion. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Spontaneous pneumothorax
2. Tension pneumothorax
3. Hemothorax
4. Exercise induced bronchospasm
5. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## Correct option

**2. Tension pneumothorax**

## Answer explanation

Tension pneumothorax is diagnosed in the setting of difficulty breathing with decreased breath sounds, hypertympanic chest wall percussion, and tracheal deviation.

A pneumothorax can occur in athletic populations, most frequently in the setting of significant chest wall trauma. The two primary types of pneumothorax are spontaneous and tension, and the difference lies in the degree of communication between the pleural cavity and the outside world. In a tension pneumothorax, air enters the pleural cavity during inspiration but cannot exit, resulting in elevation of pleural pressure that eventually exceeds atmospheric pressure. At this point, the pleural pressure begins to compress the lung and mediastinal structures, leading to tracheal deviation and cardiopulmonary compromise. On physical exam, patients are anxious, have difficulty breathing and a hypertympanic chest wall with decreased breath sounds. Prompt identification of this pathology is vital to quick treatment with needle decompression. Needle decompression can be performed in the 2nd or 5th intercostal spaces, and after successful decompression, the patient should be immediately transported to the hospital. Return to play after a pneumothorax is allowed after radiographic resolution and is typically around 4 weeks.

*Osterman et al. looked at changes in protocols to prehospital decompression of tension pneumothoraces.* **They noted** that over a period from 2015-2020, prehospital needle thoracotomy had increased from 5.1% to 38.9% and proper patient selection was similar at 23.1% and 27.8%, respectively. Iatrogenic injury slightly decreased from 28.2% to 16.7%. **They concluded** that education regarding the use of needle thoracotomy in the field has fallen short thus far.

*Azizi et al. reviewed the optimal location for needle chest decompression in the setting of a tension pneumothorax.* *They compared chest wall thickness at the 2nd intercostal space-mid clavicular line and the 4/5th intercostal space-anterior axillary line and noted no significant difference in chest wall thickness at either location in those with normal BMI but did find increased chest wall thickness in obese patients.* **They concluded** that either location was a reasonable option for decompression but the chances for successful decompression were slightly higher at the 2nd intercostal space-mid clavicular line, especially in obese patients.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A spontaneous pneumothorax is an example of a closed pneumothorax where there is no communication between the pleural cavity and the outside. Therefore, the pleural pressure stays lower than atmospheric pressure and does not cause tracheal deviation.
Answer 3: A hemothorax can occur with blunt chest trauma and have a similar appearance to a pneumothorax. However, in the setting of a hemothorax, the chest wall is usually dull to percussion.
Answer 4: Exercise-induced bronchospasm is a transient lower airway obstruction after exertion that causes shortness of breath, wheezing, and cough and resolves within 15-20 minutes of exercise cessation. There is no tracheal deviation or change in the quality of percussion to the chest wall.
Answer 5: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes but would not lead to hypertympanic percussion or tracheal deviation on exam.
