## Question

## Options

1. 40-year-old male with the imaging in Figure A; firm endpoint and 4mm of gapping on stress exam
2. 18-year-old female with the imaging in Figure B; pain with deep flexion after landing awkwardly off a jump
3. 25-year-old male with the imaging in Figure C; symmetric tibial external rotation at 30 and 90 degrees of knee flexion
4. 10-year-old male with the imaging in Figure D and a 1 month history of painful limp
5. 12-year-old male with the imaging in Figure E following a hyperextension injury during soccer game

## Correct option

**2. 18-year-old female with the imaging in Figure B; pain with deep flexion after landing awkwardly off a jump**

## Answer explanation

The MRI shown in Figure B depicts a medial meniscal root tear. In a young patient, surgical intervention is the first-line treatment to mitigate early-onset degenerative changes.

Meniscal root tears result in a functionally meniscectomized compartment, destabilizing the tibiofemoral articulation and making it prone to early degenerative changes. In young patients without evidence of arthritis, surgical intervention is indicated to prevent early arthritis. Root repair through a transosseous tunnel is the generally favored technique. Medial root tears are associated with chondral injuries, and lateral root tears with ACL injuries. Concomitant injuries should be addressed to optimize outcomes.

*Bernard et al. published a 2020 matched cohort study comparing the outcomes of root tears treated nonoperatively, with partial meniscectomy, or with repair.* Progression to arthroplasty rates and radiographic outcomes were analyzed between groups. **The authors found** a significantly higher association with progression to arthroplasty in the nonoperative and partial meniscectomy groups compared to the meniscal repair groups (p=0.0003). None of the patients undergoing meniscal repair progressed to arthroplasty within the mean follow-up period of 74 months. **The authors concluded** that meniscus root repair leads to significantly less arthritic progression and subsequent knee arthroplasty than nonoperative management and partial meniscectomy.

*Krych et al. published a 2020 JAAOS review on the current concepts of diagnosis and treatment of meniscal root tears.* Lateral root tears have a 10-times higher association with ACL tears. In contrast, patients with medial root tears have a 6-times higher risk of articular cartilage defects with an Outerbridge grade of 2 or higher. The natural history is poor, with up to 28% of patients undergoing TKA at a mean of 3.2 years after initial diagnosis. Outcomes of root repair are promising, with one group demonstrating arthritic progression by ≥2 Kellgren-Lawrence grades in 14% of patients six years after repair and a 1% conversion rate to TKA at seven years. The authors preferred the transtibial pull-out repair technique, which is most commonly used and provides a biomechanically strong suture construct with standard and familiar knee arthroscopy portals.

Figure A is a coronal MRI depicting a tear of the MCL. Figure B is a sagittal MRI depicting a medial meniscal root tear. Figure C is a sagittal MRI depicting a tear of the PCL. Figure D is a sagittal MRI depicting Osgood-Schlatter disease in a pediatric patient. Figure E shows a sagittal knee CT showing a nondisplaced tibial eminence fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The first-line treatment in a 40-year-old patient with a stable MCL tear and minimal gapping on stress exam is a trial of nonoperative management.
Answer 3: A young patient with an isolated PCL tear and a negative dial test would benefit from a trial of nonoperative management involving quadriceps strengthening.
Answer 4: Osgood-Schlatter disease is best treated nonoperatively with rest, ice, NSAIDs, and activity modification.
Answer 5: A skeletally immature patient with a nondisplaced Meyers & McKeever Type 1 tibial eminence fracture can be managed nonoperatively with a period of non-weight bearing in a long leg cast.
