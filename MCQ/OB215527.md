## Question

A 27-year-old male sustains a femur fracture in an MVA and is taken by ambulance to the nearest trauma center for orthopedic evaluation. He is seen and scheduled for intramedullary nailing the next day. He has no known drug allergies. There is no previous history of MRSA infection. What preoperative antibiotic should be ordered for this patient?

## Options

1. No preoperative antibiotics are necessary
2. Cefazolin
3. Ciprofloxacin
4. Vancomycin
5. Metronidazole

## Correct option

**2. Cefazolin**

## Answer explanation

Cefazolin, given intravenously, would be indicated in this patient preoperatively given the fact he has no drug allergies and no history of MRSA infection.

The most common pathogens encountered are skin flora, including staphylococcus aureus and staphylococcus epidermidis, which are both gram positive organisms. Cephalosporins provide adequate coverage against these organisms and are typically well tolerated, making them an ideal choice for preoperative prophylaxis. In patients with previous MRSA infection or those found to be colonized with MRSA, vancomycin or other glycopeptide based antibiotics are more appropriate, as it is believed this may help prevent postoperative MRSA surgical site infection (SSI). This being said, the rate of MRSA colonization in acute orthopedic trauma patients is low, ranging from 1-4%. Given the low rate of MRSA colonization in this population and the risk for vancomycin resistant organisms, prophylactic vancomycin use preoperatively should be reserved for specific indications.

*Shaw et al prospectively reviewed the carrier rate of MRSA in orthopedic trauma patients to determine whether MRSA screening was a worthwhile tool to prevent infection.* **They found** a low rate (1.4%) of MRSA colonization in acute fracture patients compared to those patients with known fracture related complications (ie. nonunion, implant failure). **They concluded** routine vancomycin administration for acute fracture patients is not recommended.

*Shukla et al. looked at the incidence of MRSA colonization in a trauma unit and* **found** 3.2% were carriers at the time of admission. Those who were carriers were more likely to develop a MRSA SSI after surgery (8.8% vs. 2.2%). They did not discuss whether **they recommend** routine MRSA surveillance, but did note that if patients were found to be colonized, a glycopeptide antibiotic should be given preoperatively.

*Iqbal et al reviewed a large cohort of orthopedic trauma patients and* **noted** that 1.8% were colonized. **They noted** that it was difficult to process the results of the MRSA swab in time to make decisions on preoperative antibiotics and 81% of patients who were MRSA positive did not receive a glycopeptide antibiotic, which did lead to a higher risk of MRSA SSI in this population. **They recommended** that in a population who is known to be colonized with MRSA, glycopeptide antibiotic use prophylactically does decrease SSI risk.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Preoperative antibiotics should be given before orthopedic surgery where implants or hardware are being placed.
Answer 3: Ciprofloxacin is more commonly used for prophylaxis in genitourinary cases and can be used for wounds sustained in brackish, salt and, freshwater. It would not be first line in this case.
Answer 4: Vancomycin, a glycopeptide, is not indicated in this patient as there is no evidence of MRSA colonization.
Answer 5: Metronidazole is more commonly used as prophylaxis against anaerobes in gastrointestinal surgery. It may also be used for wounds contaminated with soil or fecal material. It would not be indicated in this particular case.
