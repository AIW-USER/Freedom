## Question

An 11-year-old boy complains of dull, aching pain over the anterior aspect of his right knee. His mother states that he does not complain when walking, but his football coaches have stated that this knee has been bothering him during practices for about a month. The patient’s mother also states that he has grown about 2 inches in the past 2 months. The patient is mildly tender to palpation over the anterior aspect of the tibial tubercle but is most painful on active extension of the knee against resistance. The remainder of his exam is nonpainful. There is no effusion, he ambulates without a limp, and his knee is stable to varus/valgus stress and to Lachmann and posterior drawer testing. He has one quadrant of patellar translation with a firm endpoint and has no angular deformities on clinical examination. Which of the following radiographs is most associated with this condition?

## Answer explanation

Figure C is a lateral knee radiograph demonstrating irregularity and fragmentation of the tibial tubercle, suggestive of Osgood-Schlatter disease given the patient’s age, history, and clinical presentation.

Osgood-Schlatter disease is a self-limited traction apophysitis of the tibial tubercle. More common in boys than in girls, Osgood-Schlatter disease typically presents between the ages of 5 and 12 in boys and between the ages of 8 and 12 in girls. The traction apophysitis is a consequence of repetitive strain from the strong pull of the quadriceps, which may explain its prevalence in children playing basketball, soccer, gymnastics, and track. While commonly associated with teenage athletes, non-athletes may also develop symptoms consistent with Osgood-Schlatter disease. During growth spurts, increased skeletal lengthening may outpace the ability of the muscle-tendon unit to stretch sufficiently to maintain previous flexibility, leading to increased tension across the apophysis. Radiographs may be normal, however early stages of the disease may manifest as separation of the apophysis from the tibial tubercle, while later stages may demonstrate fragmentation of the tibial tubercle and formation of intratendinous ossicles. Treatment is primarily supportive, with 90% of patients responding well to rest, icing, and activity modification. Although rare, those with persistent symptoms refractory to conservative treatment may benefit from surgical excision of the ossicles or free cartilaginous material.

*Gholve et al. published a 2007 review on Osgood-Schlatter disease detailing the pathogenesis, presentation, imaging, and treatment.* Though primarily a clinical diagnosis, plain radiographs are still recommended to rule out other diagnoses that may present as anterior knee pain. A broad differential includes tibial tubercle avulsion fractures, Sinding-Larsen-Johannson syndrome, fat pad impingement syndrome, injury to the synovial plica, infection, and malignancy. Radiographs may show an irregularity of the apophysis with separation from the tibial tuberosity in the early stages of OSS and fragmentation in the later stages. A persistent bony ossicle may be visible in a few cases after fusion of the tibial epiphysis.

Pihlajamäki et al. published a 2009 retrospective study evaluating the rate of occurrence of surgical treatment of unresolved Osgood-Schlatter disease and the clinical course, radiographic characteristics, and long-term outcomes after surgical treatment in a large population of military recruits. The authors retrospectively reviewed the postoperative courses of 107 patients (117 knees), with a median follow-up time after surgical intervention of ten years. The authors then concluded that surgical treatment of unresolved Osgood-Schlatter disease in skeletally mature young adults does not appear to have long-term deleterious effects, and the long-term outcome of surgical treatment was good with respect to the modified Kujala score, maintenance of physical activity level, and residual pain intensity. However, even after surgery, one of the main symptoms of Osgood-Schlatter disease - i.e., pain when kneeling - was reported by 39% of the patients, although the Kujala score and the visual analog pain score were good.

*Enomoto et al. performed a 2020 in vivo biomechanical study evaluating the effect of the patellar tendon strain rate (SR) on the incidence of Osgood-Schlatter disease compared to healthy controls.* Measurements of tissue elasticity were obtained from the quadriceps muscles and patella tendon using real-time tissue elastography, and from this, the strain rate was calculated. The SR in affected athletes was significantly lower than that in the control group, **suggesting** the patellar tendon experiences a shorter extension per unit of muscle force. Therefore, it is assumed that a tendon with low SR can transmit the muscle force to the bone in a shorter time and increase the mechanical stress to the tibial tubercle.

Figure C is a radiograph of a knee with intratendinous ossifications suggestive of Osgood-Schlatter disease. Figure D is a radiograph of a bipartite patella. Figure E is a radiograph of a tibial spine avulsion fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The history and physical are not consistent with a tibial tubercle avulsion fracture, which would present more acutely and with ambulation difficulties.
Answer 2: The history and physical are not consistent with a patellar sleeve avulsion fracture, which would present with tenderness to palpation at the inferior patellar pole and ambulation difficulties.
Answer 4: A bipartite patella is a normal variant resulting from a fusion failure of the two ossification centers; it is not a painful condition and is often diagnosed incidentally.
Answer 5: The history and physical are not consistent with a tibial spine avulsion fracture, which would present more acutely with an inability to bear weight and severe swelling. A Lachmann test may be positive as well.
