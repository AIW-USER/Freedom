## Question

A 16-year-old swimmer presents with chronic shoulder pain. They report several events where it felt like their shoulder dislocated without significant trauma and never required a reduction in the emergency department. After obtaining a history and performing a physical exam, you recommend 6 months of physical therapy and discuss the importance of maximizing nonoperative options for this condition. What positive physical exam finding is most specific to the patient's condition?

## Options

1. Anterior apprehension
2. Jerk test
3. Kim test
4. Load and shift
5. Sulcus sign

## Correct option

**5. Sulcus sign**

## Answer explanation

This patient has multidirectional instability, which is most associated with the sulcus sign. A sulcus sign of 2 cm has a specificity of up to 97% for multidirectional instability (Answer 5).

Multidirectional instability (MDI) is characterized by dislocations or subluxations in at least two planes. These patients can typically be diagnosed through the history and physical exam. The sulcus sign is an indentation distal to the acromion when a downward force is applied to the arm. This is caused by an incompetent rotator interval and suggests inferior instability. The Beighton scale can also be used to assess generalized ligamentous laxity. Other exam findings of shoulder instability may also be positive, such as the apprehension sign and load and shift test, although they are less specific for MDI.

The mainstay of treatment for shoulder MDI is nonoperative. MDI is fundamentally due to a lack of static shoulder stabilizers. Thus, therapy aims to build up dynamic stabilizers to compensate. Physical therapy focused on rotator cuff strengthening, scapular stability, proprioception, and functional training should be attempted for 6 – 12 months before considering a surgical procedure. Nonoperative management has been shown to achieve satisfactory outcomes in terms of pain and recurrence in most patients with this condition.

*Warby and colleagues evaluated the effectiveness of two different rehabilitation approaches for treating MDI.* *Participants were randomized to undergo either a closed-chain exercise program or an open-chain program.* Findings suggested that both programs were beneficial in improving shoulder stability and function, but the closed-chain exercises demonstrated superior improvements in reducing pain and enhancing stability. This study highlights the potential advantage of closed-chain exercises in conservative treatment for patients with MDI, offering guidance for physical therapy protocols aimed at reducing instability symptoms without surgical intervention.

*Watson and colleagues investigated the impact of a structured, non-surgical rehabilitation program on patients with MDI.* Results indicated significant improvements in pain, increased functional ability, and decreased episodes of instability. **The findings support** conservative rehabilitation as an effective approach for managing MDI, emphasizing that structured exercises can lead to meaningful functional gains and improve quality of life for patients with shoulder instability.

*De Martino and colleagues examined the prevalence and causes of MDI in swimmers, a population particularly vulnerable to this condition due to repetitive overhead arm movements.* The article explains that the high volume of training and specific shoulder motions in swimming—such as those in freestyle, backstroke, and butterfly strokes—place significant stress on the shoulder joint, leading to overstretching of the joint capsule and ligaments. Treatment typically includes rest, and a conservative rehabilitation program focused on strengthening the rotator cuff and scapular muscles to provide stability and support.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 4: The apprehension sign is common in cases of both traumatic and multidirectional instability.
Answers 2 and 3: The Kim and Jerk tests can be seen in both posterior traumatic dislocations and multidirectional instability.
