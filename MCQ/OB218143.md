## Question

She works as a line cook at a local restaurant and drinks a six-pack of beers nightly. She denies a history of intravenous drug use, as well as any recent trauma to the area of interest. She is currently afebrile, her blood pressure is 136/90, and her heart rate is 96 beats per minute. The wrist is aspirated and sent for synovial fluid analysis. ## Answer explanation

The patient has chronic tophaceous gout, as evidenced by the lesions in Figure A, with acute onset gouty wrist pain that would most likely demonstrate negatively birefringent, needle-shaped crystals (Figure D) on synovial fluid crystal analysis.

Gout represents a form of inflammatory arthritis that is caused by the intra-articular deposition of monosodium urate crystals. The condition most commonly occurs in the joints of the lower limbs, particularly the great toe, but can occur in the small joints of the wrist and hand as well. Diagnosis is made by joint aspiration with crystal analysis showing strongly negatively birefringent, needle-shaped intracellular crystals (Figure D). Treatment for acute gout attacks is with anti-inflammatories and supportive care, while chronic gout can be managed medically with drugs such as allopurinol to reduce systemic loads of uric acid that promote peripheral crystalline deposition. It is important to consider that septic arthritis can co-exist with gouty arthritis, so joint aspirations with white blood cell count exceeding 50,000, even in the presence of crystals, should be interpreted cautiously with heavy consideration made on the patient's clinical status, vital signs, and gram stain/culture results.

*De Avila et al. reviewed the ultrasound features of tophi in chronic tophaceous gout.* *The authors reviewed ultrasound images of 138 affected areas from 31 patients with gout, with tophi being classified relative to echogenicity, echotexture, contours, number, and presence or absence of a hypoechoic halo.* **The authors concluded** that tophi are generally hyperechoic, heterogeneous, with poorly defined contours, multiply grouped, and surrounded by an anechoic halo which are important factors in differentiating them from acute infectious sequelae.

*Dalbeth et al. published a review of gout in the Lancet journal.* **The authors note** that an increased concentration of serum urate (hyperuricemia) is the most important risk factor for the development of gout. Serum urate is regulated by urate transporters in the kidney and gut, particularly GLUT9 (SLC2A9), URAT1 (SLC22A12), and ABCG2, with monosodium urate crystal deposition causing activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome via the release of IL-1β, a cascade that has been determined to be important in the initiation of the prototypical "gout flare." **The authors concluded** that for patients with recurrent flares or tophaceous gout, long-term urate-lowering therapy with medications such as allopurinol leads to the dissolution of monosodium urate crystals and ultimately results in the prevention of gout flares and improved quality of life.

Figure A is a clinical picture representative of tophaceous gout of the digits. Figure B represents Gram + cocci in clusters (S. aureus). Figure C represents Gram + spore-forming bacilli. Figure D represents negatively birefringent, needle-shaped crystals (gout). Figure E represents both Gram + cocci and Gram - bacilli. Figure F represents positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped crystals consistent with calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) or pseudogout.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers B, C, and E: Though gout can co-exist with septic arthritis, the patient's benign clinical picture, stable vitals, and presence of tophi make the leading diagnosis of acute gouty arthritis most compelling in this scenario.
Answer F: Figure F represents positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped crystals consistent with calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) or pseudogout, which would not be expected on synovial fluid analysis in a patient with an acute attack of gouty arthritis.
