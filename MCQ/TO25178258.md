## Question

In the development of a Boutonnière deformity, what is the primary anatomical 'trigger' that initiates the cascade leading to PIP flexion and DIP hyperextension?

## Options

1. Dorsal subluxation of the lateral bands
2. Shortening of the oblique retinacular ligament (ORL)
3. Rupture of the volar plate
4. Attenuated central slip and volar displacement of lateral bands

## Correct option

**4. Attenuated central slip and volar displacement of lateral bands**

## Answer explanation

Synovitis of the PIP joint stretches the central slip and causes the lateral bands to shift volarly, becoming flexors of the PIP joint.

The Boutonnière deformity is a classic "zigzag" posture of the finger, characterized by PIP joint flexion and DIP joint hyperextension. The name comes from the French word for "buttonhole," describing how the head of the proximal phalanx "buttons" through the extensor mechanism.

 Pathoanatomy: The Triad of Failure

The deformity is driven by the failure of the central slip, which sets off a biomechanical chain reaction:

 - Central Slip Attenuation/Rupture:
 Synovitis in the PIP joint (or trauma) stretches or ruptures the insertion of the central slip on the base of the middle phalanx.

 - Volar Subluxation of Lateral Bands:
 As the central slip fails, the triangular ligament (which holds the lateral bands dorsally) also attenuates. The lateral bands slide volarly until they drop below the axis of rotation of the PIP joint.

 - Functional Reversal: Once the lateral bands are volar to the joint axis, they become flexors of the PIP joint. All the extensor force is now directed to their distal insertion, pulling the DIP joint into hyperextension.

Clinical Classification

In rheumatoid patients, the deformity is often staged based on flexibility and the state of the joint.

 - Stage I (Mild): The deformity is passively correctable. The PIP can be fully extended and the DIP can be flexed.

 - Stage II (Moderate): The PIP joint has a fixed flexion contracture, but the joint space is radiographically preserved.

 - Stage III (Severe): The PIP joint is fixed, and there is radiographic destruction (arthritis) or severe skin/soft tissue contracture.

 Surgical Management

Surgical outcomes for Boutonnière deformity in RA are notoriously less predictable than for Swan-neck deformity. The goal is to restore the balance of the extensor mechanism.

Soft Tissue Procedures (Stage I & II)

 - Dolphin-Nalebuff Tenotomy (Terminal Extensor Tenotomy): If the PIP is flexible but the patient cannot flex the DIP (causing functional issues with grasp), the terminal extensor tendon is cut over the middle phalanx. This "releases" the tension, allowing the DIP to flex and potentially improving PIP extension.

 - Matev Procedure (Lateral Band Transfer): One lateral band is used to reconstruct the central slip, while the other is left to extend the DIP.

 - Central Slip Reattachment: Only viable if the tissue quality is robust, which is rare in chronic rheumatoid synovitis.

Joint-Level Procedures (Stage III)

 - PIP Arthrodesis: This is the most reliable procedure for Stage III. Fusing the joint in a functional position (40°–50° for the ring/small fingers; 25°–30° for the index) provides a stable, pain-free digit.

 - Silastic Arthroplasty: Occasionally used in the ulnar digits to maintain some motion, but it carries a high risk of recurrent deformity if the soft tissue balance isn't perfectly restored.

 Distinguishing the "Rheumatoid" vs. "Traumatic" Boutonnière

 - Traumatic: Usually an acute rupture of the central slip (e.g., jammed finger). It responds well to 6 weeks of continuous PIP extension splinting (Boutonnière splint).

 - Rheumatoid: The tissue is "sick" (attenuated by synovitis). Splinting is less successful, and the deformity often progresses despite non-operative efforts.
