## Question

A collegiate rower was in an eight-man boat at the Head of the Charles rounding the corner of Eliot Bridge when a competitor's boat collided with their boat just before the finish line. The rower's oar ran aground, forcing it deeply into his palm. He was able to finish the race but has had a nagging pain in his hand since that time. Plain radiographs were initially negative, but are repeated in the office today with the view shown in Figure A. Which of the following injuries has the rower sustained?

![[q218157-a-screenshot-2023-10-13-at-1-38-07-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Dorsal wrist sprain
2. Hook of hamate fracture
3. Pisiform fracture
4. Pisiform dislocation
5. Triquetrum fracture

## Correct option

**2. Hook of hamate fracture**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained a hook of hamate fracture from the forceful blow of his oar running into a rocky shore, initially missed on plain radiographs but clearly shown in the carpal tunnel view obtained in the office.

Hook of hamate fractures often occur from direct trauma while using a racket, bat, or golf club (or in this case, a carbon-fiber oar). Patients typically relay symptoms of pain about the hypothenar eminence, grip weakness, and occasionally paresthesias in the ulnar nerve distribution depending on the involvement at Guyon's canal. Examination most often reveals tenderness over the hook of the hamate. Specific radiographic views that may be helpful in the diagnosis include a carpal tunnel view (Figure A) and a semi-supinated oblique view; however, if these are negative the next best step would be to obtain a CT scan (Illustration B).

*Suh et al. provide a comprehensive current concepts review of carpal fractures.* **The authors note** that fractures of the carpals other than the scaphoid are exceedingly rare and comprise approximately 1.1% of all fractures and, thus, are often missed. **They conclude** that such injuries frequently require a high index of suspicion and the use of advanced imaging such as CT scans for definitive diagnosis.

*Mouzopoulos et al. published a clinical review of hamate fractures.* **The authors note** that fractures of the hamate usually occur through the hook or body of the bone, with Type I fractures involving the hook of the hamate, which can be further subdivided into three subtypes involving: base, waist, and avulsion (tip). **They conclude** that fractures located at the base and proximal third (76%) of the hook are presented more frequently than fractures located at the mid-third (13%) or the distal third (11%).

*Kadar et al. provide a review of the diagnosis and management of hook of hamate fractures.* *The authors retrospectively reviewed 51 patients treated for hook of hamate fractures,* **noting** that the average time to diagnosis was 27 days with clinical tenderness having better sensitivity than carpal tunnel-view radiographs. **The authors concluded** that advanced imaging improved the time to diagnosis and treatment compared to historical case series with nonunion being common in patients with missed fractures or in those treated non-operatively.

Figure A is a carpal tunnel view of the wrist showing a hook of hamate fracture, which is labeled in Illustration A. Illustration B shows the appearance of a hook of hamate fracture on a CT scan.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A dorsal wrist sprain, often associated with a triquetral avulsion fracture, is not seen in this view.
Answers 3-5: This carpal tunnel view radiograph does not show triquetrum or pisiform fractures.
