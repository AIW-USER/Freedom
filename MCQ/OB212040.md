## Question

Which of the following injuries can be managed exclusively by an epitendinous repair?

## Options

1. Complete laceration of ulnar and radial slip of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon to the index finger
2. Partial laceration (~70 percent) of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon of the middle finger
3. Complete laceration of the FDP tendon to the index finger with partial laceration (~50%) of the ulnar slip of the FDS to the middle finger
4. Partial laceration of the ulnar slip of the FDS tendon (60%) and complete laceration of the FDP tendon to the small finger
5. Partial laceration (~50%) of the FDP with complete laceration of ulnar and radial slips of the FDS to the ring finger

## Correct option

**2. Partial laceration (~70 percent) of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon of the middle finger**

## Answer explanation

A partial laceration (up to 75%) of a flexor tendon has been shown to effectively be repaired without gap formation with an epitendinous only repair (no core strands)

Partial injuries to flexor tendons are challenging injuries to manage given the lack of a true consensus on management. Injuries that comprise of less than 60% of the tendon width generally management non-operative. The intact part of the tendon can usually sustain forces of normal unresisted motion, and repaired partially lacerated tendons can actually be weaker than unrepaired ones. However, complications such as triggering or tendon entrapment have been reported in association with unrepaired tendons. Tears between 60 and 75% of the tendon have been shown to be effectively repaired with an epitendinous only to approximate the edges and have sufficient strength without gap formation as compared to those undergoing repair with core strength.

*Haddad et al. compared repair vs non-repair of 75% partially lacerated flexor tendons.* **They found** that epitendinous only or epitendinous + core repairs reduced gap formation (= 1mm at 500 cycles) but there was no difference in gap formation between these 2 groups. **The authors noted** that nonrepaired tendons had early gap formation (at 100 cycles). **The authors concluded** that gapping is reduced with an epitendinous repair (with or without core suture) and noted no increased benefit of core sutures in this subset of flexor tendon injuries.

*Erhard et al. performed a randomized cadaveric analysis of zone 2 partial flexor tendon lacerations.* Thirty-six FDP tendons were harvested and randomly assigned to be subjected to either 50% or 75% partial laceration, and were then assigned to no repair, repair with a running epitendinous suture, or trimming. **The authors noted** that those tendons with a 75% partial laceration had higher mean gliding resistance than those with a 50% laceration (p < 0.05), and the tendons that were repaired with running epitendinous only suture had higher mean gliding resistance than those treated with trimming (p < 0.05). **The authors concluded** that from the perspective of gliding resistance after partial tendon laceration, no repair appears necessary unless triggering is a problem.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1, 3, 4, and 5: Complete laceration of a flexor tendon should be managed with a core and epitendinous repair.
