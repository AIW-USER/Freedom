Chapter: Arthroscopy MCQs — PCL

## Question

A patient is 4 weeks post-operative following an isolated Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) reconstruction. The physical therapist is designing a strengthening protocol. Which of the following exercises applies the highest peak posterior shear force on the tibia and is strictly contraindicated at this stage?

## Options

1. CKC bilateral leg press from 0° to 60° of flexion.
2. OKC active quadriceps extensions from 90° to 0°.
3. CKC wall squats from 0° to 45° of flexion.
4. OKC resisted hamstring curls from 0° to 90° of flexion.

## Correct option

**4. OKC resisted hamstring curls from 0° to 90° of flexion.**

## Answer explanation

The primary function of the PCL is to resist posterior tibial translation. The hamstrings are the primary dynamic posterior translators of the tibia. OKC resisted hamstring curls (especially deep flexion >60°) isolate the hamstrings without the neutralizing effect of quadriceps co-contraction or joint compression, pulling the tibia directly posteriorly and applying massive strain to the healing PCL graft. Conversely, OKC quadriceps extensions (Option B) are actually safe early in PCL rehab as they generate an anterior shear force, which unloads the PCL.

CKC exercises must be modified (the hamstring co-contraction during squats produces posterior tibial translation, which stresses the healing PCL, particularly in deep flexion beyond 60° so CKC exercises upto 60 degree flexion are allowed (option A and C).

Rehabilitation principles: close versus open kinetic chain exercises.

The kinetic chain concept, adapted from mechanical engineering by Arthur Steindler in 1955, classifies exercises based on whether the distal segment of the exercising limb is fixed (closed chain) or free to move (open chain). This distinction has profound implications for the joint forces, muscle activation patterns, and functional relevance of the exercise, and is fundamental to orthopaedic rehabilitation protocols — particularly following knee ligament reconstruction, arthroplasty, and fracture fixation.

Open Kinetic Chain (OKC) Exercises:
The distal segment is free and moves through space while the proximal segment remains relatively stationary. Movement occurs predominantly at a single joint (isolation exercise). Examples include seated knee extension (leg extension machine — the foot is free, the quadriceps extend the knee against resistance), seated hamstring curl (leg curl machine — the foot is free, the hamstrings flex the knee), straight leg raise, seated hip flexion, biceps curl, and ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion with resistance bands.

In OKC exercises, the resistance is applied to the distal segment (typically the foot or hand), and the resulting force vector produces shear forces at the intervening joints. For the knee, OKC extension (leg extension) produces anterior tibial shear — the quadriceps (through the patellar tendon) pulls the tibia anteriorly relative to the femur, directly stressing the ACL. This is particularly significant in the last 30° of extension (0–30°), where the anterior shear force peaks. Additionally, OKC knee extension produces isolated quadriceps contraction without co-contraction of the hamstrings — the hamstrings, which are the dynamic antagonists of anterior tibial translation (functioning synergistically with the ACL), are inactive during OKC extension, removing this protective mechanism.

Closed Kinetic Chain (CKC) Exercises:
The distal segment is fixed (in contact with a stationary surface — typically the ground), and movement occurs simultaneously at multiple joints as the body moves over the fixed distal segment. CKC exercises are inherently multi-joint, multi-muscle activities that mimic functional movement patterns. Examples include squats (partial or full — the foot is fixed on the ground, the ankle, knee, and hip flex and extend simultaneously), lunges, step-ups, leg press (the foot pushes against a fixed platform), wall sits, standing calf raises, push-ups (upper extremity CKC), and functional activities like stair climbing and sit-to-stand transfers.

In CKC exercises, the ground reaction force produces primarily compressive (axial) forces
through the joints rather than shear forces. For the knee, the CKC squat produces simultaneous quadriceps and hamstring co-contraction — both muscle groups are active throughout the movement, with the hamstrings providing a posterior tibial translation force that directly counteracts the anterior shear produced by the quadriceps. This co-contraction protects the ACL and reduces the net anterior tibial translation to near zero. Additionally, the axial compressive load through the tibiofemoral joint during CKC exercises enhances joint congruity and stability (the concave tibial plateau is loaded into the convex femoral condyles, increasing articular contact area and inherent stability).

Clinical Applications in Orthopaedic Rehabilitation

ACL Reconstruction Rehabilitation: This is the clinical scenario where the OKC/CKC distinction has the greatest impact. Modern ACL rehabilitation protocols emphasise CKC exercises from the early postoperative period (mini-squats, leg press, step-ups beginning at 2–4 weeks) because the hamstring co-contraction and compressive loading protect the healing ACL graft from excessive anterior shear stress. OKC knee extension is restricted during the early phases (typically avoided from 0–45° for the first 12 weeks in many protocols, or limited to the 90–45° range where anterior shear is minimal). Full-range OKC extension is introduced gradually after 12–16 weeks when the graft has undergone sufficient maturation to tolerate the anterior shear load. This evidence-based approach has significantly reduced the incidence of graft stretching and failure during rehabilitation.

Patellofemoral Rehabilitation: CKC exercises in the 0–45° flexion range produce lower patellofemoral joint reaction forces than OKC extension — making CKC exercises preferable for patients with patellofemoral pain, chondromalacia, or following patellar stabilisation surgery. Conversely, OKC extension in the 90–45° range (avoiding terminal extension) produces lower patellofemoral forces than the deep flexion phase of CKC squats — demonstrating that neither category is universally superior; the optimal range depends on the specific pathology.

Total Knee Replacement Rehabilitation: Early CKC exercises (sit-to-stand, partial squats, step-ups) promote functional recovery, quadriceps activation in functional patterns, and proprioceptive retraining. OKC exercises (straight leg raises, terminal knee extensions) supplement CKC activities for isolated quadriceps strengthening.

Fracture Rehabilitation: CKC weight-bearing exercises provide controlled axial loading that stimulates fracture callus formation and remodelling (Wolff's law), while the multi-joint activation pattern maintains global lower limb function. OKC exercises maintain isolated muscle strength (particularly when weight-bearing is restricted) without loading the fracture.

PCL Rehabilitation: The protocol is reversed compared to ACL rehabilitation — OKC knee extension is safe
(quadriceps contraction produces anterior tibial translation, which reduces strain on the PCL), while CKC exercises must be modified (the hamstring co-contraction during squats produces posterior tibial translation, which stresses the healing PCL, particularly in deep flexion beyond 60°).

Answer: 4
