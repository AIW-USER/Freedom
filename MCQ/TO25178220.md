Chapter: Trauma MCQs — Sacral Fracture

## Question

A 32-year-old male is brought to the trauma bay following a 20-foot fall. He is hemodynamically stable but has significant pelvic pain. Radiographs and CT scans reveal a vertically displaced Denis Zone II fracture on the right side and a contralateral stable Zone I fracture. The CT scan further demonstrates "dysmorphic" sacral features, including an acutely sloping alar superior border, a non-cylindrical S1 bone corridor, and the presence of accessory S1-S2 foramina.

Question:
Given the patient's anatomy and injury pattern, which of the following is the most appropriate surgical strategy to achieve stable fixation while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury?

## Options

1. Percutaneous 7.3 mm iliosacral screw placement into the S1 vertebral body using a strictly horizontal trajectory on the lateral fluoroscopic view.
2. Open posterior tension band plating across the S1 and S2 segments to avoid the narrow bony corridors.
3. Triangular osteosynthesis combining L5 pedicle screw fixation with an iliac screw and a supplemental trans-sacral screw at the S2 level.
4. Isolated trans-iliac trans-sacral screw placement at the S1 level to provide compression across the Zone II fracture site.

## Correct option

**3. Triangular osteosynthesis combining L5 pedicle screw fixation with an iliac screw and a supplemental trans-sacral screw at the S2 level.**

## Answer explanation

Correct Answer: C

This case tests the intersection of instability patterns and sacral dysmorphism, which occurs in approximately 30–40% of the population.

 - Vertical Instability: A vertically displaced Zone II fracture is inherently unstable. Triangular Osteosynthesis (Option C) is the gold standard for these injuries because it addresses both the vertical shear (via the lumbopelvic distraction/fixation) and the horizontal/rotational instability (via the sacral screw).

 - Dysmorphism Constraints: In a "dysmorphic" sacrum, the S1 corridor is often narrowed, acutely angled, or corticalized, making the safe placement of an S1 iliosacral screw extremely difficult. The L5 nerve root is at high risk if a standard horizontal trajectory (Option A) is used, as the screw will likely breach the cephalad alar cortex.

 - The S2 Corridor: In dysmorphic sacra, the S2 corridor is typically more "normal" or spacious than the S1 corridor. Utilizing the S2 level for a trans-sacral screw provides excellent transverse stability while avoiding the hazardous S1 anatomy.

 - Why others are incorrect: * Option A is dangerous in dysmorphic anatomy; the trajectory must be adjusted (usually posterosuperior to anteroinferior), and a horizontal screw will likely cause an L5 root injury.

 - Option B (Open plating) carries a high risk of wound breakdown in the sacral region and provides inferior biomechanical resistance to vertical shear compared to lumbopelvic fixation.

 - Option D is risky at the S1 level in a dysmorphic sacrum due to the non-cylindrical shape of the corridor; furthermore, isolated transverse screws often fail in high-grade vertical shear injuries without longitudinal support.

Answer: 3
