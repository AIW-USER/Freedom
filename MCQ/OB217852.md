## Question

A 67-year-old patient presents to the clinic for a postoperative evaluation after recently undergoing a revision total knee arthroplasty. A porous tantalum metaphyseal cone and a press-fit diaphyseal engaging stem were utilized in the tibia. In which of the following situations would this component selection be most appropriate?

## Options

1. Intact metaphyseal and diaphyseal bone stock
2. A small symmetric contained metaphyseal defect with intact diaphyseal bone stock
3. A large asymmetric uncontained metaphyseal defect with intact diaphyseal bone stock
4. A large symmetric contained metaphyseal defect with minimal diaphyseal bone stock
5. A small asymmetric uncontained metaphyseal defect with minimal diaphyseal bone stock

## Correct option

**3. A large asymmetric uncontained metaphyseal defect with intact diaphyseal bone stock**

## Answer explanation

An asymmetric uncontained metaphyseal defect with intact diaphysis would be appropriately addressed with a metaphyseal cone and a press-fit diaphyseal engaging stem.

Current options to address metaphyseal bony defects in revision total knee arthroplasty include cemented components, impaction bone grafting, bulk allografts, traditional metal augments, and, more recently, metaphyseal sleeves and porous tantalum metaphyseal cones. Trabecular metal cones in the proximal tibia recreate the cortical rim and provide a stable platform for the final tibial component. Cones may be regarded as metaphyseal anchored metallic bone grafts, which enable a cementless fixation over their porous surface. Sleeves also follow the principle of cement-free metaphyseal anchorage. In contrast to cones, sleeves are firmly attached to the prosthesis. Sleeves are generally used with contained cavitary defects, whereas cones may be used in the setting of an uncontained defect.

*Haidukewych et al. provide a review of indications and techniques used to address metaphyseal fixation in revision TKA.* **They note** that Multiple series have documented good survivorship at short-term follow-up with trabecular metal cones and porous-coated sleeves. These newer technologies offer biologic fixation and help treat bony defects that are not easily managed with other methods. They further stress that familiarity with the rationale and strategies for metaphyseal fixation in revision TKA is a valuable addition to the armamentarium of the revision surgeon.

*Radnay et al. review their experience using ten porous tantalum tibial cones in patients undergoing a revision TKA.* **They report** that at follow-up (average of 10 months), the radiographic evaluation revealed no evidence of loosening or change in position. Strength, range of motion, and stability were comparable to the previously reported series of revision arthroplasties. **They conclude** that trabecular metal cones can help reconstruct large cavitary defects and, along with stem extensions and offset stems, may eliminate the need for extensive bone grafting or structural allograft in revision knee arthroplasty.

*Mabry et al. provide a review on the role of stems and augments for bone loss in revision TKA.* **They note** that the primary treatment options for bone deficiencies include using structural allografts, impaction bone grafting, and prosthetic augments. **They further discuss** that although cementless stems are currently more popular, the available literature suggests that cemented stem fixation provides a more reliable and durable construct for revision knee arthroplasty associated with severe bone deficiency.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Without any notable metaphyseal bone defects, using a porous tantalum metaphyseal cone would not be necessary.
Answers 2 and 4: A small symmetric contained metaphyseal defect would likely be amenable to impacted bone grafting, whereas a large symmetric contained metaphyseal defect would be best addressed with a metaphyseal sleeve. Furthermore, a press-fit diaphyseal engaging stem relies on an intact diaphysis for fixation. A tibia with minimal diaphyseal bone stock would likely necessitate a cemented stem.
Answer 5: Again, a press-fit diaphyseal engaging stem would not be appropriate for minimal diaphyseal bone stock. Furthermore, a small asymmetric uncontained metaphyseal defect may be amenable to a standard metal augment as opposed to a cone.
