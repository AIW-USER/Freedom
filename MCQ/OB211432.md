## Question

A 21-year-old collegiate linebacker with Type I diabetes managed with a continous glucose monitor and insulin presents with left shoulder pain and numbness. He reports the symptoms began two weeks ago following a tackle. On physical exam, he has decreased sensation to light touch over the lateral shoulder, trace weakness to shoulder abduction and elbow flexion with his palms pointing to the ceiling. He otherwise has a negative Hoffman sign bilaterally, normal reflexes in his upper and lower extremities, and is able to walk toe-to-heel without difficulty. An MRI is shown in Figure A and B. Considering the patient wants to return to play as soon as possible, what would be the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Oral methylprednisone taper with immediate return to play
2. EMG and NCS of the left upper extremity with management pending those results
3. NSAIDs and physical therapy, return to play following resolution of symptoms and repeat imaging
4. Posterior cervical C4/5 foraminotomy
5. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion at C4/5

## Correct option

**3. NSAIDs and physical therapy, return to play following resolution of symptoms and repeat imaging**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with clinical and radiographic findings consistent with a C5 radiculopathy secondary to a C4-5 paracentral disc herniations. In the absence of myelopathic symptoms, the first-line of treatment is always conservative management.

Though more common in older populations, cervical disc herniations have become more common in younger athletes and American football in particular. Treating these injuries poses the dilemma of managing athlete expectations of immediate treatment with evidence-based treatment guidelines. First-line treatment for all cervical disc herniations without myelopathy or progressive neurologic deficit should be conservative. If this fails, epidural steroid injections may be pursued. Surgery is reserved for recalcitrant cases.

*Meredith et al. reviewed outcomes of National Football League (NFL) players sustaining cervical disc herniations over a decade.* Sixteen patients sustained this injury, the majority being defensive players. Eight of 13 were treated non-operatively and returned to football, while only 1 of 3 players who underwent surgery returned. **The authors highlight** that athletes may safely return to sports following cervical disc herniations.

*Hsu et al. evaluated the impact of cervical disc herniations among NFL players.* **The authors found** that of the 99 players included, 72% of players who underwent surgery returned to play an average of 29 games, while 46% of those treated non-operatively returned to play for an average of 15 games. This demonstrates the potential benefit of surgical intervention over nonoperative management on career longevity in contact athletes who sustain cervical disc herniations.

Figure A is a sagittal T2 MRI demonstrating a C4/5 disc herniation with no deformation of undue compression of the spinal cord. Figure B is the axial T2 rendering showing a left-sided paracentral disc herniation. Illustration A is a cartoon showing where nerve roots exit and their nomenclature in the cervical and lumbar spine. Note that both far lateral and paracentral disc herniations affect the exiting nerve roots in the cervical spine, unlike in the lumbar spine, due to the more horizontal take-off of the cervical nerve roots.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: An oral methylprednisolone taper is not indicated unless a patient has failed first-line agents such as oral analgesics (NSAIDs and potentially opiods) acutely and remains severely symptomatic.
Answer 2: EMG and NCS are generally not required in the evaluation of cervical disc herniation with characteristic clinical exam findings and concordant imaging.
Answer 4: Surgical intervention is indicated acutely only in the presence of progressive neurologic deficit or myelopathy. Otherwise surgery should be entertained only after failure conservative management. Given the location of the disc herniation, an anterior procedure would furthermore be more appropriate.
Answer 5: Surgical intervention is not indicated in this situation as the patient has not failed an appropriate course of conservative management.
