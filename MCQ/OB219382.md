## Question

A 7-year-old girl with cerebral palsy whose family recently immigrated to the United States is brought to your clinic for evaluation of a limb length discrepancy. She lacks head control, is wheelchair-bound, and requires total care from her family. Initial radiographs of her pelvis are shown in Figure A. Which of the following are included in the muscle groups responsible for this finding?

![[q219382-a-cp-hip-dislocation.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Adductor magnus and sartorius
2. Gluteus minimus and iliopsoas
3. Obturator externus and rectus femoris
4. Gracilis and psoas major
5. Piriformis and sartorius

## Correct option

**4. Gracilis and psoas major**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a hip dislocation in the setting of Gross Motor Functional Classification System (GMFCS) V cerebral palsy. Spasticity of the medial compartment of the thigh (adductor magnus, adductor longus, adductor brevis, and gracilis) and the composite iliopsoas muscle (iliacus, psoas major) is primarily responsible for the progressive development of hip instability, subluxation, and eventual dislocation in children with spastic cerebral palsy.

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a neuromuscular disorder arising from a stable neurologic lesion that may manifest as progressive musculoskeletal symptoms, including dysplasia and instability of the hips. Children are born with normal hip joints; however, the spasticity of the adductors and iliopsoas create joint forces that influence abnormal osseous development of the femoral head and acetabulum, eventually leading to hip dysplasia, posterolateral subluxation, and eventual dislocation. Abnormally developed hip joints in children with CP can be radiographically assessed by a high acetabular index (AI) and posterolateral acetabular deficiency. Nonambulatory patients with spastic CP may also present with a windblown hip deformity, which represents an adduction deformity of one hip and an abduction deformity of the other, resulting in pelvic obliquity. This deformity is caused by asymmetric contraction of the adductors, abductors, internal rotators, and external rotators. The adducted hip is typically located on the high side of the pelvis and is at risk for dislocation. Patients with fixed, windblown deformities of the hip develop infrapelvic obliquity and are, therefore, also at risk for developing a secondary, compensatory scoliosis deformity of the spine.

*Hosseinzadeh et al. published a 2020 review on the management of hip disorders in patients with cerebral palsy.* Seven percent (7%) of independent ambulators and 60% of dependent-sitters with cerebral palsy were reported to have hip subluxation or dislocation. This progressive lateral hip displacement caused by muscle imbalance is rarely preventable without surgical intervention. Children at greatest risk of dislocation were between the ages of 4 and 12 years. Dislocated hips ultimately resulted in difficulty with sitting and were clinically painful in 50% of affected individuals. **They concluded** that, left untreated, hip dislocations are associated with difficult perineal care and hygiene, pelvic obliquity, and scoliosis.

*Huser et al. published a 2018 review on hip surveillance in children with cerebral palsy.* *They re-iterated that the abnormalities seen in the proximal femur are anteversion of the femoral neck, coxa valga, and dysplastic/degenerative changes.* **The authors state** that radiographic and clinical studies have shown that spastic hip adductors and flexors cause the femoral head to be directed into the posterolateral acetabular labrum, leading to deformation of the femoral head by the capsule and superior acetabular rim, and eventual cartilage erosion. Additionally, elevated hip forces (up to 6-fold) in children with spastic hip dysplasia lead to early changes of the acetabulum at around 30 months of age, most commonly in the posterolateral acetabulum.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The sartorius is part of the anterior thigh compartment and not part of either the adductor or iliopsoas muscle groups responsible for CP-associated hip instability.
Answer 2: The gluteus minimus is an abductor and not part of either the adductor or iliopsoas muscle groups responsible for CP-associated hip instability.
Answer 3: The obturator externus is an external rotator of the thigh and is not part of either the adductor or iliopsoas muscle groups responsible for CP-associated hip instability.
Answer 5: Neither the piriformis nor the sartorius are part of either the adductor or iliopsoas muscle groups responsible for CP-associated hip instability.
