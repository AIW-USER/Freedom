## Question

A 68-year-old male presents to your clinic reporting two weeks of right knee pain and difficulty ambulating following a ground-level fall. He has a history of total knee arthroplasty three years prior and was functioning well without pain until the recent fall. On physical exam, he is unable to perform a straight leg raise or actively extend his knee against resistance. Radiographs and MRI are obtained, which confirm the suspected diagnosis, and he elects to undergo a reconstructive procedure as displayed in Figure A. What is the most appropriate postoperative protocol for the patient?

## Options

1. Long leg splint for 2 weeks in 15º of knee flexion, followed by transition to hinged knee brace
2. Hinged knee brace locked in full extension for 2-4 weeks, then increasing 10º weekly
3. Long leg cast in full extension for 6-12 weeks, followed by gradual increase in range of motion in a hinged knee brace
4. Long leg cast for 4 months minimum, followed by gradual increase in range of motion in a hinged knee brace
5. Knee immobilizer for 4 weeks, followed by transition to hinged knee brace starting at 90º flexion limitation and progressing 10º weekly

## Correct option

**3. Long leg cast in full extension for 6-12 weeks, followed by gradual increase in range of motion in a hinged knee brace**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an extensor mechanism disruption and underwent an extensor mechanism mesh reconstruction. Following this procedure, 6–12 weeks of immobilization in full knee extension is recommended to optimize the chances of achieving full knee extension postoperatively while balancing the risk of excessive knee stiffness (answer choice 3).

Extensor mechanism disruption following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) presents a substantial challenge, encompassing quadriceps tendon ruptures, patellar fractures, and patellar tendon ruptures or avulsions. These disruptions can occur intraoperatively, often due to aggressive exposure or over-resection of the patella, or postoperatively, resulting from trauma, component malposition, or compromised soft tissues. Risk factors include component malrotation, excessive joint line elevation, previous lateral release, and compromised local blood supply. While conservative management may be appropriate for partial ruptures with an intact extensor mechanism, surgical intervention, ranging from direct repair with or without augmentation to reconstruction using autografts, allografts, or synthetic materials, is often required. Surgical methods and outcomes vary depending upon the specific structures involved, chronicity of the injury, and presence of bony or implant abnormalities.

*Bates and Springer reviewed extensor mechanism disruption after TKA.* **They found** that risk factors include previous knee surgery, systemic conditions (e.g., renal disease, diabetes mellitus), and surgical factors like component malposition and lateral release, which can be mitigated with appropriate surgical technique. **They concluded** that while nonoperative management is an option for partial disruptions with an intact extensor mechanism, surgical repair or reconstruction is generally needed for complete disruptions, although no single surgical technique has proven consistently superior, and further research is necessary.

*Browne and Hanssen retrospectively reviewed thirteen consecutive patients who underwent extensor mechanism reconstruction for subacute or chronic patellar tendon disruption following total knee arthroplasty, utilizing a synthetic knitted monofilament polypropylene graft.* *At mean follow-up of forty-two months, nine of thirteen patients demonstrated an extensor lag of no greater than 10°, with maintenance of preoperative knee flexion.* **They concluded** this procedure provided reasonably good results for this difficult clinical challenge, with the additional benefit of being significantly less costly than allograft reconstruction.

*Dobbs et al. retrospectively reviewed thirty-four cases of postoperative quadriceps tendon ruptures after TKA (11 with complete ruptures, 23 with partial tears) with at least two years of follow-up.* Ten of eleven patients with complete tears were treated operatively, of which four sustained a rerupture and only four achieved a satisfactory outcome; additionally, eleven of the twenty-six patients managed operatively sustained complications. **They concluded** that postoperative quadriceps ruptures remain a challenging problem, with surgical management fraught with complications and unreliable outcomes.

Figure A is an intraoperative radiograph of an extensor mechanism mesh reconstruction, with the synthetic mesh visualized after it has been fixated to the tibia, but prior to proximal attachment to the extensor mechanism.

Incorrect Answer Choices:
Answer choices 1, 2, and 5: Although direct head-to-head studies of postoperative protocols for this rare but serious complication have not been reported, most surgeons who perform a high volume of mesh reconstruction agree that a prolonged period of knee immobilization in full extension (≥6 weeks) is an important aspect of surgical management, as failed reconstruction due to early or aggressive range-of-motion only further complicates this challenging clinical situation.
Answer choice 4: Immobilization beyond 12 weeks is often not recommended, as beyond this point it would not be expected to achieve any further benefit while increasing the risk for knee stiffness.
