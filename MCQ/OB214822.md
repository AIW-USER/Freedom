## Question

A 55-year-old middle manager at a regional paper supply company in the North-Eastern United States presents with a 6-month history of left hand pain, numbness, and tingling involving the thumb, index, and long fingers. Her symptoms wake her at night despite appropriate splinting. She endorses concomitant neck pain with radiation to the hand as well as forearm pain. Examination demonstrates a positive Tinel sign at the distal forearm. Her altruistic hand surgeon offers her a corticosteroid injection. Which of the following is true regarding this modality?

## Options

1. An injection is contraindicated due to the risk of infection
2. An injection is contraindicated as spinal pathology should always be addressed first
3. An injection is contraindicated as it will not provide complete relief of symptoms
4. An injection is indicated and likely to provide indefinite and complete symptom relief
5. An injection is indicated and likely to provide at least partial symptom relief for a moderate time period

## Correct option

**5. An injection is indicated and likely to provide at least partial symptom relief for a moderate time period**

## Answer explanation

A corticosteroid injection into the carpal tunnel will give the vast majority of patients symptom relief for a number of months.

Corticosteroid injections into the carpal tunnel have diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic benefits in the management of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). It can delineate the degree to which carpal tunnel compression contributes to the patient's symptoms and may provide a period of relief in patients with mild and moderate CTS. More complete and longer periods of relief correlate with more favorable prognoses following carpal tunnel release.

Green's 1984 landmark paper reviewed 222 wrists (6-45 month follow-up) that underwent carpal tunnel injection. Of those patients, 81% obtained relief lasing an average of 3.3 months. Only 46% felt that symptom recurrence was severe enough to warrant a surgical release. *Dr. Green* **concluded** that the injection is an excellent diagnostic tool, there is a strong correlation between injection relief and surgical outcome, poor relief from injection does not mean that a patient is a poor surgical candidate, and some patients will not experience symptom recurrence after injection.

*Ponnappan et al. reviewed etiologies of upper extremity pain and how to differentiate between them clinically.* *The authors discuss neurologic and musculoskeletal etiologies involving the neck, shoulder girdle, elbow, forearm, wrist, and hand and report the diagnostic utility of corticosteroid injections into the carpal tunnel in equivocal cases.*

*Kane et al. reviewed double crush syndrome where a nerve can be compressed at two distinct places along its course.* In carpal tunnel syndrome, concomitant cervical radiculopathy can lead to suboptimal results following carpal tunnel release. **The authors highlight** the importance of recognizing cervical radiculopathy and underlying systemic neuropathy in patients complaining of carpal tunnel symptoms.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The injection is indicated. The risk of infection in carpal tunnel surgery, even after injection, is so low that it is not clear if there is a correlation. Antibiotic prophylaxis in carpal tunnel release is not indicated.
Answer 2: The injection is indicated. Spinal pathology does not always have to be addressed first, especially if carpal tunnel syndrome is causing the majority of symptoms. An injection helps delineate the etiology of pain.
Answer 3: The injection is indicated and a majority of patients will experience some degree of relief.
Answer 4: The injection is indicated, however only a minority of patients will experience symptom relief indefinitely.
