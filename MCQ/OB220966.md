## Question

A 9-month-old infant presents with bilateral hallux varus. Radiographs show no ossification of the first metatarsal epiphysis, but ultrasound confirms the presence of a cartilaginous C-shaped structure extending along the medial diaphysis. The parents ask about treatment options. Which of the following represents the most appropriate management strategy at this time?

## Options

1. Observation with serial radiographs every 6 months until ossification occurs, then proceed with angular osteotomy
2. Immediate surgical excision of the preossified bracket with consideration of polymethylmethacrylate interposition
3. Delay surgery until age 3-4 years when the child can better cooperate with postoperative rehabilitation
4. Distraction with external fixation to correct the angular deformity and restore metatarsal length
5. Serial casting to gradually correct the hallux varus deformity over 6-12 months

## Correct option

**2. Immediate surgical excision of the preossified bracket with consideration of polymethylmethacrylate interposition**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with bilateral hallux varus secondary to a bracket epiphysis. Early surgical excision of the preossified longitudinal epiphyseal bracket (LEB) before ossification is the recommended treatment, ideally performed in the first year of life (Answer 2).

LEB (also called delta phalanx or bracket epiphysis) is a rare congenital bone dysplasia caused by an anomalous C-shaped secondary ossification center that extends longitudinally along the diaphysis, preventing normal transverse bone growth. When this abnormality affects the first metatarsal, it causes progressive shortening and angular deformity of the bone, resulting in hallux varus, a medial deviation of the great toe at the metatarsophalangeal joint. This condition occurs in 2–14% of congenital hand and foot deformities, with bilateral involvement in approximately 75% of cases, and is clinically significant because untreated cases lead to worsening deformity with growth, functional impairment, and cosmetic concerns that often require surgical intervention, ideally within the first year of life.

*Mubarak et al. present a case series on LEB of the metatarsal and outcomes of treatment with central physiolysis in young children.* *Resection of the central tethering portion of the abnormal epiphysis, with interposition (initially fat, later PMMA), reliably restored longitudinal growth and corrected angular deformity, with maintained correction at mid-term follow-up and relatively few recurrences.* **The authors conclude** that early central physiolysis is a physiologic, growth-preserving treatment that effectively corrects both deformity and length in metatarsal LEB when performed before significant secondary joint changes occur.

Choo and Mubarak review LEB as an uncommon growth disorder caused by an anomalous secondary ossification center that extends longitudinally along the diaphysis, most commonly presenting as clinodactyly in the hands and hallux varus in the feet. The authors conclude that early surgical physiolysis is the preferred treatment approach, rather than the previously utilized angular osteotomy.

*Shim et al. evaluated ten feet in eight patients with congenital hallux varus (mean age 33 months, mean follow-up 5.9 years) and* **found** generally favorable surgical outcomes, though results were poorer in cases with underlying LEB. Four feet had associated LEB, and these cases required bony correction with osteotomy, alone or combined with soft-tissue procedures, rather than soft-tissue surgery alone. **The authors emphasized** that failure to recognize LEB preoperatively can lead to residual or recurrent varus.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation with serial radiographs is inappropriate because a LEB causes progressive growth disturbance that will not resolve spontaneously and will worsen with continued growth.
Answer 3: Postponing intervention allows the bracket to ossify and the deformity to progress, missing the optimal window for simple bracket excision that has demonstrated excellent outcomes when early.
Answer 4: External fixation with distraction osteotomy is reserved for older children with established ossified brackets and significant deformity, not for preossified cases where simple bracket excision can restore normal growth.
Answer 5: Casting is effective for soft tissue-based deformities like resistant metatarsus adductus, but cannot address the structural ossification abnormality of an LEB, which requires surgical excision to restore normal growth.
