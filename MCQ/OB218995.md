## Question

A retired ballet dancer presents to the clinic for a pre-operative visit prior to her left total hip arthroplasty. During the visit she states that she has had prior spine surgery. After ordering sitting and standing lateral spinopelvic radiographs, you inform the patient that her pelvis is in a "stuck sitting" position. What complication is she most at risk for if this is not taken into consideration when the acetabular component is positioned from a posterolateral approach?

## Options

1. Abductor dysfunction
2. Anterior dislocation
3. Limb length discrepancy
4. No increased risk of complication
5. Posterior dislocation

## Correct option

**2. Anterior dislocation**

## Answer explanation

In a patient who is "stuck sitting" due to decreased spinopelvic motion after spinal fusion surgery, the patient is most at risk for anterior dislocation if combined anteversion is excessive, even when the surgery is performed from a posterolateral approach.

Recently published literature has helped to improve our understanding of the complex interplay between spine and hip kinematics. In the normal relationship, the pelvis tilts or flexes posteriorly 20-35 degrees and the femur flexes 55-70 degrees when going from standing to sitting, decreasing lumbar lordosis and sacral slope and allowing the patient to sit upright (Illustration A). Abnormal spinopelvic motion is determined by first measuring the sacral slope on both sitting and standing lateral spine-pelvis-hip radiographs (Illustration B). If the difference in the two measurements is < 10 degrees, then the patient is deemed to have a stiff spine and is at increased risk of having an instability event (normal motion is a change in sacral slope between 10 and 30 degrees). To determine whether a patient is "stuck sitting" versus "stuck standing," the anterior pelvic plane (APP) is drawn (Illustration B). If it is tilted posteriorly 10-13 degrees in the setting of reduced pelvic motion, the patient is said to be "stuck sitting" (Illustration C) and is at increased risk of posterior impingement and anterior instability when standing. If the APP is neutral in the setting of reduced pelvic motion, the patient is said to be "stuck standing" (Illustration D) and is at risk for anterior impingement and posterior instability when sitting.

Heckmann, et al provide a current concepts review of spinopelvic biomechanics and total hip arthroplasty. The authors assert that in patients with prior lumbar fusion and/or decreased spinopelvic motion, several treatment principles should be considered regardless of deformity: (1) optimization of femoral head size or use of a dual mobility articulation, (2) careful restoration of offset with a low threshold to use a high-offset stem to avoid bony impingement, (3) use of intra-operative radiographs, navigation, robotic assistance to assist with component positioning to avoid technical errors such as excessive combined anteversion.

Lum, et al. provide a review of the current knowledge on spinopelvic mobility. The authors offer the following goals of cup position in a stiff spine: 45-50 degrees of inclination (50 degrees in elderly patients and 45 degrees in younger patients) and 20-25 degrees of anteversion with ideal combined anteversion of 35-40 degrees.

McKnight, et al. similarly provide updated recommendations on cup positioning that deviate from the originally published Lewinnek safe zone numbers (40 +/- 10 degrees of inclination and 15 +/- 10 degrees of anteversion). In "stuck sitting" deformities, the authors recommend airing on the side of less inclination (35-40 degrees) and anteversion (15-20 degrees) to prevent posterior impingement and subsequent anterior instability.

Illustration B depicts a normal change in sacral slope (SS 45 - 20 = 15) and a vertical anterior pelvic plane (APP), indicating normal spinopelvic motion. Illustration C depicts the expected changes in SS and APP in a "stuck-sitting" deformity, while Illustration D depicts those changes associated with a "stuck-standing" deformity.

Incorrect Answers:
1. Abductor dysfunction would not be expected as a complication of cup positioning in the setting of a stiff spine and a "stuck sitting" deformity. Changes on the femoral side should seek to increase tension on the abductors with increased offset to reduce the risk of impingement and subsequent dislocation.
3. Limb-length discrepancy would not be the most likely complication resulting from cup malpositioning in the setting of a stiff spine.
4. Literature has shown that excessive combined anteversion in the setting of a stiff spinopelvic junction and a "stuck sitting" deformity increases the risk of anterior instability.
5. Increased risk of posterior dislocation would be expected in a patient with a "stuck standing" phenotype, in which the pelvis is extended when sitting, causing anterior acetabular impingement and subsequently posteriorly-directed instability.
