## Question

A 30-year-old male presents to your clinic with complaints of left knee pain that has been present for years. He states he underwent a failed meniscal repair and subsequent subtotal meniscectomy when he was 20 years old due to a collegiate football injury. An AP radiograph is seen in Figure A. No patellofemoral arthritis is noted on the lateral radiograph. On exam, his pain is focal over the medial joint line. He has a 5° flexion contracture and varus alignment of the lower extremity when standing. He has been taking acetaminophen, naproxen, and has had a corticosteroid injection without much improvement. He is hoping to get back to playing recreational soccer and flag football. What is the next best treatment option?

![[q218142-a-medial-knee-arthritis.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Arthroscopic debridement and chondroplasty
2. Medial femoral condyle osteochondral allograft
3. Medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO)
4. Medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA)
5. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA)

## Correct option

**3. Medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO)**

## Answer explanation

This young, active patient with isolated medial joint space narrowing is a good candidate for a medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO) to offload the medial compartment and correct his varus alignment.

HTO is performed in order to correct angular deformities of the knee and prevent/slow progression of arthritic compartment development. The most common indication for an HTO is isolated medial compartment degeneration with varus alignment in a young, active patient who is not a good candidate for uni- or total knee arthroplasty. In a knee with varus alignment, a valgus producing osteotomy is performed. This can be done with a lateral closing wedge osteotomy or a medial opening wedge osteotomy. Many surgeons choose to perform the medial opening wedge HTO as the fibula is not interfering in the approach, the peroneal nerve is not at risk, and the appropriate degree of correction is easier to achieve.

*Rodner et al. looked at how medial opening wedge HTOs affected the sagittal plane alignment and tibiofemoral contact pressures.* *They either placed their medial opening wedge HTO more anteriorly or more posterior to change the degree of slope correction, with anteriorly placed HTOs increasing the posterior tibial slope by 6.6° compared to posteriorly based HTOs.* In cadaver specimens that were ligament deficient, the intra-articular contact pressures were directed more posteriorly in the HTO group compared to those specimens that were ligamentously intact. **They proposed** that given the normal posteromedial wear seen in ACL deficiency at baseline, increasing posterior contact pressures may be an early cause of failure for an HTO performed in this group of patients.

*Whatling et al. studied the effects of HTO on frontal plane motion, gait and patient outcomes.* **They noted** that HTO restored frontal and transverse plane knee joint loading similar to a control group and corrected preoperative gait abnormalities that had been developed as compensatory mechanisms. As expected, the contact pressures were moved increasingly lateral in order to account for off-loading the medial compartment.

Figure A demonstrates an AP radiograph of the left knee showing isolated medial joint space narrowing. Illustration A is the same knee after medial opening wedge HTO, with improvement in coronal alignment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: In a patient with significant joint space narrowing, an arthroscopic procedure is not appropriate and attempts need to be made to alter the lower extremity alignment.
Answer 2: There is no mention in the stem or evidence in the patient's radiographs of a large osteochondral lesion. Additionally, an osteochondral allograft would not be appropriate given his excessive varus alignment.
Answers 4 & 5: This patient is not a good candidate for a partial or total joint replacement given his age and level of activity.
