## Question

## Answer explanation

This male gymnast with loss of extension and a sensation of popping most likely has osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) of the elbow, specifically the capitellum (Answer 3).

OCD of the elbow is a common cause of elbow pain and loss of athletic performance amongst adolescent athletes. It is commonly seen in gymnasts and pitchers and is secondary to repetitive overhead compression-type injuries which lead to vascular insufficiency as a result of microtrauma. The capitellum, which receives its blood supply from both the radial recurrent artery and interosseous recurrent artery, is the most frequently involved location. The most common symptoms include a loss of elbow extension early in the disease and a sensation of catching, locking, and/or grinding later if there are loose bodies present. Radiographs often demonstrate a bony defect in the capitellum, and MRI can be used to assess the lesion size, the extent of edema, and the status of the overlying cartilage.

*Van den Ende et al. provide a review of OCD of the elbow.* **They note** that an important factor in selecting the appropriate treatment is determining lesion stability. **They state** that for stable lesions, an initial trial of activity modification and rest is appropriate. They go on to postulate, however, that surgical intervention is often necessary for unstable lesions or stable lesions which do not respond to conservative treatment.

*Zellner and May published a comprehensive overview of elbow injuries in young athletes.* **They report** that many of these injuries are due to over-specialization, and occur secondary to valgus overload of the elbow. **They go on to state** that pitchers are at particular risk of an elbow injury, most likely as a result of repetitive valgus overload during the late cocking phase of throwing. **They note** that OCD is commonly encountered in young throwers and gymnasts, and most commonly involves the capitellum.

Churchill and co-authors offer a comprehensive review of OCD of the elbow. They state that differentiating OCD from Panner's disease (self-limiting osteochondrosis of the capitellum) is crucial, and note that the latter typically affects boys under the age of 10 with no overuse or traumatic risk factors. The authors note that while initial radiographs are often negative, later capitellar changes including lucencies, flattening, sclerosis, fragmentation, and intra-articular loose bodies are common.

Figure A demonstrates calcifications within the common extensor origin, observed in some cases of lateral epicondylitis. Figure B demonstrates widening of the medial epicondyle physis, consistent with little leaguer's elbow. Figure C demonstrates capitellar flattening and lucencies, consistent with OCD of the capitellum. Figure D demonstrates a medial epicondyle fracture with an incarcerated fragment.
