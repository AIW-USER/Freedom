## Options

1. Synovial fluid analysis demonstrating 16,000 white blood cells/µL and 88% PMNs at 6 weeks postoperatively
2. Increased risk of mortality secondary to hyperemia reaction
3. Decreased risk of Vancouver B intraoperative fracture
4. Increased risk of deep vein thrombosis
5. Decreased risk of postoperative hip dislocation

## Correct option

**1. Synovial fluid analysis demonstrating 16,000 white blood cells/µL and 88% PMNs at 6 weeks postoperatively**

## Answer explanation

This patient has rheumatoid arthritis (RA) based upon the images in Figures A and B. When undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA), compared to patients with osteoarthritis (OA), there is an increased risk of prosthetic joint infection postoperatively (answer choice 1).

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a serious complication following joint arthroplasty and a common cause for revision surgery. It has an incidence of approximately 0.3-1.3% in primary THA, with a 3-4% incidence of revision THA. It is important to take into consideration preoperative risk factors including active local or systemic infection, as well as a previous local surgery or infection. Other risk factors for PJI include immunosuppressant medications, immunosuppressive conditions (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes, chronic renal disease, acute liver failure, malnutrition, and HIV), inflammatory arthropathy (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and ankylosing spondylitis), and modifiable lifestyle factors. All of these risk factors should be evaluated and optimized before surgery. The increased risk of PJI in patients with RA is believed to be multifactorial and caused by the condition itself, associated comorbidities, and immune-modulating medications.

*Burn et al. retrospectively reviewed outcomes following TKA and THA in patients with OA and RA.* The estimated effect of RA, relative to OA, on post-operative scores was -0.05 (95% CI -1.57, 1.48) for the Oxford Knee Score, -0.09 (-0.13, -0.06) for the EQ-5D-3L following TKA, -1.35 (-2.93, -0.22) for the Oxford Hip Score, and -0.08 (-0.12, -0.03) for the EQ-5D-3L following THA. **They concluded** that both TKA and THA led to significant improvements in joint-specific scores and overall quality of life, with those with RA demonstrating slightly less improvement in general quality of life when compared to patients with OA; likely related to the systemic nature of the disease.

*Zhang et al. reviewed the rate of complications in patients with OA and RA undergoing THA.* *This was a meta-analysis and systematic review including 23 studies with a total of 877,695 patients.* **They found** a significant increase in rates of revision, hip dislocation, PJI, wound infection, and revision for late infection compared to those in the OA group. No significant difference found in mortality or deep vein thrombosis rates. **They conclude** that it is important to inform patients about the benefits and increased risks of complications in RA before surgery.

Figure A is a clinical image demonstrating the classic zig-zag deformity in RA with ulnar deviation of the fingers at the MCP joints. Figure B is an AP pelvis radiograph demonstrating bilateral protrusio acetabuli, characteristic of inflammatory arthritis. Figure C is an AP pelvis radiograph with severe osteoarthritis of the right hip and avascular necrosis of the femoral head.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 2-5: There have been significant differences in complication rates with RA patients including increased risk of PJI, intraoperative fracture, revision surgery, hip dislocation, and wound infection. No significant increase in risk of mortality or incidence of DVT has been associated with RA.
