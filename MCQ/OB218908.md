## Question

A 42-year-old female presents to your office approximately six months after undergoing tarsal tunnel release with a nearby foot and ankle surgeon. She continues to have medial/plantar foot pain associated with stinging and burning sensations. She states her symptoms are minimally improved compared to preoperatively. She is requiring gabapentin to improve her pain. You are concerned that she is still suffering from tarsal tunnel syndrome. Which of the following is true of recurrent tarsal tunnel syndrome?

## Options

1. Outcomes of revision tarsal tunnel release are similar to primary
2. Rate of recurrence after tarsal tunnel release is less than 5%
3. Most common cause of recurrence is incomplete release
4. Prolonged motor latencies are more sensitive than sensory
5. EMG is not useful in differentiating recurrent tarsal tunnel syndrome from lumbar radiculopathy

## Correct option

**3. Most common cause of recurrence is incomplete release**

## Answer explanation

The most common reason for the recurrence or persistence of tarsal tunnel syndrome (TTS) is an incomplete release of the compressive structures at the time of primary surgery.

TTS is a compressive neuropathy of the tibial nerve which occurs at the level of the ankle as the nerve passes through the tarsal tunnel. Symptoms include vague medial heel pain with sharp, burning foot pain. Typically, nonoperative measures are attempted initially, including orthotics, anti-inflammatory medications, and sometimes SSRI's. Release of the tarsal tunnel is recommended after 3-6 months of failed conservative management and results are typically best if a specific compressive structure is identified. Recurrence can be problematic and occurs for a multitude of reasons including the incomplete release of compressive structures (most common), adhesive neuritis, neuroma formation, incorrect initial diagnosis, infection, and double crush phenomenon. Outcomes of revision tarsal tunnel release are thought to be poorer than in cases of primary TTS release.

*Nelson described TTS,* **noting** that compression of the tibial nerve occurs as it passes beneath the flexor retinaculum. Diagnosis is made on physical exam with a positive Tinel's test at the ankle and sensory loss in the tibial nerve distribution. **They note** that treatment is aimed at reducing nerve compression and if conservative treatment fails, outcomes of surgical management are better when a compressive lesion has been identified preoperatively.

Gould discussed recurrent TTS, noting that it may be caused by inadequate release, lack of anatomic understanding, anatomic variations, bleeding and subsequent scarring, damage to nerves/branches, nerve hypersensitivity, and pre-existing nerve damage. They discuss revision tarsal tunnel release and note that including barrier materials may help to decrease nerve adherence while also recommending the excision of any neuromas. Finally, if improvement continues to lack, consideration may be given to nerve stimulators and systemic medications to treat the symptoms of TTS.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Revision tarsal tunnel release is less successful than primary release in improving symptoms and some authors do not recommend revision surgery.
Answer 2: Rates of recurrence after primary tarsal tunnel release varies by author but has been described as anywhere from 5-40%.
Answer 4: In most TTS, sensory latencies are increased before motor latencies.
Answer 5: EMG is a helpful diagnostic tool to identify the site of nerve compression, whether that be at the nerve root level or at the level of the tarsal tunnel, or even more distal.
