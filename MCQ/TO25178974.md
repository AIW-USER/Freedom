Chapter: Arthroscopy MCQs — Meniscus Injuries

## Question

A 28-year-old athlete undergoes arthroscopy for a longitudinal tear located 5mm from the meniscocapsular junction. Regarding the zone-specific healing potential and the choice of repair technique, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. The tear is in the Red-White zone, and concurrent ACL reconstruction significantly improves the healing environment through the release of marrow-derived stem cells.
2. The tear is in the White-White zone, and since this area is entirely avascular, partial meniscectomy is the only viable treatment regardless of tear morphology.
3. The tear is in the Red-Red zone, and an inside-out repair technique is preferred over all-inside devices to minimize the risk of injury to the saphenous nerve.
4. The tear is in the Red-White zone, where limited vascularity necessitates the use of biological augmentation like fibrin clot or PRP to ensure healing.

## Correct option

**1. The tear is in the Red-White zone, and concurrent ACL reconstruction significantly improves the healing environment through the release of marrow-derived stem cells.**

## Answer explanation

The Zone: A distance of 5mm from the meniscocapsular junction is the classic definition of the Red-White zone.

 - 0–3mm: Red-Red (High vascularity)

 - 3–5mm: Red-White (Intermediate/Limited vascularity)

 - >5mm: White-White (Avascular)

Because the Red-White zone has limited blood supply compared to the periphery, it often sits in a "grey area" for healing. In an isolated repair (without the hemarthrosis of an ACL reconstruction), augmenting the site with a fibrin clot or PRP
is a standard surgical strategy to introduce the necessary growth factors to "ensure" or bridge that healing process.

Principles of meniscal repair

Meniscal repair aims to restore structural integrity and biomechanical function of the torn meniscus, protecting the articular cartilage from the accelerated degenerative changes that inevitably follow meniscectomy — total meniscectomy increases contact pressures by 235% and leads to radiographic osteoarthritis in 50–70% of patients by 20 years.

General Indications for Meniscal Repair

Tear Location (Vascular Zone): The most important determinant of healing potential. The meniscus is divided into three zones based on perimeniscal capillary penetration:

●Red-Red Zone (peripheral third):
Fully vascularised, >90% healing rate — all tears should be repaired.

●Red-White Zone (middle third):
Partially vascularised, 60–80% healing — repair generally recommended, often with biological augmentation.

●White-White Zone (inner third):
Avascular, <50% healing — traditionally considered irreparable, though biological augmentation techniques are expanding reparability.

Tear Pattern: Longitudinal (vertical) tears are most amenable to repair — collagen fibres are separated but not disrupted. Bucket-handle tears (displaced longitudinal tears) are reparable after reduction. Radial tears disrupt circumferential fibres — repair is more challenging but increasingly performed, particularly for posterior root tears. Complex degenerative tears are generally not reparable.

Other Factors Favouring Repair: Tear length >10 mm, unstable tears (>3 mm displacement on probing), acute tears (<6 weeks, superior healing environment), young active patients, and concurrent ACL reconstruction (which dramatically improves healing rates from 50–60% to 85–95% through the haemarthrosic environment, growth factor release from bone tunnels, and restoration of knee stability).

Meniscal Root Tears: Posterior root tears functionally eliminate hoop stress mechanism, producing biomechanical consequences equivalent to total meniscectomy. Repair (transtibial pullout technique) is strongly indicated to restore hoop stress function.

Inside-Out Technique

Description: Described by Henning (1983) and long considered the gold standard. Long, flexible suture-loaded needles are passed from inside the joint through the meniscal tear, exiting through the capsule and skin. In case of meniscal tears of posterior half of meniscus sutures are retrieved and tied over the capsule through a mandatory posterior safety incision.

Technical Details: Zone-specific cannulae (with different curvatures for posterior, middle, and anterior zones) are inserted through the ipsilateral portal. Non-absorbable braided sutures (2-0 Ethibond or Ti-Cron) are passed through the tear in vertical mattress
configuration (biomechanically superior — captures circumferential collagen fibres perpendicular to their orientation) at 3–5 mm intervals. The posterior incision (3–4 cm) is essential for protecting neurovascular structures and retrieving sutures.

Advantages: Provides the strongest and most reliable fixation — consistently superior pullout strength (80–120 N), stiffness, and load-to-failure compared to all-inside devices. Excellent surgeon control over suture placement, tension, and knot tying. Most extensively documented long-term results with healing rates of 70–90%.

Disadvantages: in case of tear of posterior half of body and posterior root, accessory posterior incision is required to safeguard neurovascular structures — saphenous nerve (infrapatellar branch) medially and common peroneal nerve laterally (the more feared complication, particularly for posterior horn lateral meniscal tears, potentially causing foot drop). More technically demanding and time-consuming than all-inside methods.

Optimal Indications: Tears of the body and anterior horn where all-inside devices may not achieve optimal fixation. Large, complex tears requiring multiple precisely placed sutures. Revision meniscal repair.

Outside-In Technique

Description: Described by Warren (1985) and Morgan (1991). Approaches the tear from outside the knee — 18-gauge spinal needles are inserted percutaneously under arthroscopic visualisation, penetrating capsule and peripheral meniscal rim, crossing the tear, and emerging on the inner meniscal surface. Sutures are threaded through the needles into the joint, retrieved with a grasper, and the process is repeated to create mattress suture configurations. Sutures are tied over the capsule through a small skin incision or intra-articularly.

The Morgan technique refinement uses a mulberry knot (bulky toggle knot) to anchor one suture end on the inner meniscal surface, creating a self-anchoring construct.

Advantages: Particularly well-suited for anterior horn and body tears — regions difficult to access with inside-out cannulae or all-inside devices. The simplest technique to learn with minimal specialised instrumentation (spinal needles, standard sutures, arthroscopic graspers). Lower neurovascular risk than inside-out technique because needle entry is anterior to the posterior neurovascular structures. Excellent directional control of needle placement.

Disadvantages: Less suited for posterior horn tears
— the posterior horn is difficult to reach percutaneously from the anterior aspect without an extreme oblique trajectory. Fixation strength may be inferior to inside-out vertical mattress sutures. Requires coordination between arthroscopic view and percutaneous needle placement.

Optimal Indications: Anterior horn tears (technique of choice for this location), body tears, and as a supplementary technique combined with other methods for complex tears involving multiple zones.

All-Inside Technique

Description and Evolution: Driven by the desire to eliminate accessory posterior incisions and their neurovascular risks. First-generation devices (meniscal arrows, darts, staples — e.g., Meniscus Arrow, BioStinger) were rigid absorbable implants providing fixation through barb mechanisms. Despite simplifying the procedure, they demonstrated inferior biomechanical properties and concerning complications — device migration, articular cartilage damage, foreign body reactions, and cyst formation. Most have been abandoned.

Current second-generation devices are suture-based all-inside systems (e.g., FasT-Fix/FasT-Fix 360 by Smith+Nephew, MaxFire, Meniscal Cinch by Arthrex, Truespan). These combine all-inside convenience with suture-based fixation superiority. The FasT-Fix
— the most widely used — consists of a pre-loaded delivery needle containing two small polymer anchors connected by braided polyester suture. The first anchor is deployed on the capsular (peripheral) side after passing through the tear, the needle is repositioned, and the second anchor is deployed on the meniscal (inner) side. A pre-tied sliding knot is advanced to coapt tear edges. The entire procedure is performed through standard arthroscopic portals without additional incisions.

Modern all-inside devices achieve pullout strengths of 60–80 N — approaching (though not quite equalling) inside-out vertical mattress sutures.

Advantages: Eliminates accessory posterior incisions and their neurovascular risks entirely. Significantly faster — each device deployed in 2–3 minutes versus 15–20 minutes per inside-out suture. Less technically demanding with a shorter learning curve. Ideal for posterior horn tears — the dominant indication and location where these devices excel. No requirement for a posterior assistant. Pre-loaded single-use design ensures consistent deployment.

Disadvantages: Higher cost per device ($300–600 USD each, multiple typically required). Fixation strength marginally inferior to inside-out vertical mattress sutures biomechanically. Limited access to anterior horn and body tears (devices designed primarily for posterior horn access). Small risk of anchor-related complications (migration, cyst formation), though significantly less than first-generation rigid implants. Some configurations produce horizontal rather than vertical mattress orientation.

Optimal Indications: Posterior horn tears of both menisci — the dominant indication where all-inside devices excel. Tears associated with concurrent ACL reconstruction (efficiency reduces overall operative time).

Meniscal Root Repair — Transtibial Pullout Technique

Indications: Posterior meniscal root tears — particularly medial meniscal posterior root tears (MMPRTs) — are functionally equivalent to total meniscectomy because root disruption eliminates the hoop stress mechanism, allowing meniscal extrusion and loss of load-distributing function. Untreated MMPRTs progress rapidly to medial compartment arthrosis. Repair is indicated with minimal pre-existing arthritis (KL grade ≤2), adequate tissue quality, and BMI <35.

Technique: The torn root is identified, debrided, and secured with two heavy non-absorbable sutures (No. 2 FiberWire) using suture-passing devices. A tibial tunnel is drilled from the anteromedial tibial cortex to the anatomical root footprint on the tibial plateau. Sutures are shuttled through the tunnel, and the root is reduced to its anatomical attachment. Sutures are tied over a cortical button on the anterior tibial cortex with the knee in extension.

Outcomes: Healing rates of 70–90% when performed within 3–6 months of injury, with restored meniscal function (reduced extrusion on MRI), normalised contact pressures, and delayed osteoarthritis progression.

Biological Augmentation

Fibrin Clot: Prepared from patient's venous blood, placed in the tear cleft before suture repair. Provides scaffold, growth factors (PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF), and mesenchymal stem cells. Originally described by Arnoczky, with demonstrated improved healing.

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP):
Concentrated autologous platelets providing supraphysiological growth factor concentrations. Evidence mixed but generally supportive, particularly for red-white and white-white zone tears.

Marrow Stimulation (Trephination/Microfracture): Vascular access channels created through the peripheral meniscus or adjacent tibial plateau promote vascular ingrowth and stem cell migration into the tear zone.

Postoperative Rehabilitation Principles

Rehabilitation balances repair protection with joint mobility preservation. Weight-bearing
is generally permitted in extension (axial loading compresses the meniscus against the plateau, promoting tear apposition). Flexion is limited to 90° for 4–6 weeks (deep flexion loads the posterior horn, the most common repair site). Deep squatting and pivoting are restricted for 4–6 months. Full return to unrestricted activity including contact sports at 6–9 months.

Answer: 1
