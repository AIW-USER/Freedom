## Answer explanation

The patient has a congenital cleft hand which has a primarily autosomal dominant mode of transmission (signified by the pedigree in Figure E).

Congenital cleft hand, also known as split-hand malformation or lobster claw deformity, is an extremely rare congenital syndrome characterized by the absence of 1 or more central digits of the hand. Congenital cleft hand, like most other congenital hand conditions, is passed via an autosomal dominant mode of transmission with inherited forms becoming more severe with each generation. Offspring of parents with autosomal dominant disorders only need to get the abnormal gene from one parent in order to inherit the disease. As such patients with this condition should undergo genetic counseling so they are aware of and understand the likelihood of passing the gene to their offspring.

*Guero et al. performed an overview of congenital cleft hand.* *They describe that the condition consists of a sequence of phenotypes, from a minor shortening of the central digit to a complete absence of the third ray and in the most severe cases, absence of two, three, or four rays.* **They note** that the inheritance is mostly autosomal dominant but sporadic cases without a family history are also reported, resulting from a de novo mutation. **They state** that the increased knowledge of this group of disorders has lead to the improvement of surgical procedures and long-term outcomes.

*Gane et al. discussed the inheritance pattern and pathogenesis of congenital cleft hand.* *They cite that the condition is mainly inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with incomplete penetrance and characterized by malformation of the limb involving the central rays.* The pathogenic mechanism of the condition is a failure to maintain signaling from the median apical ectodermal ridge. **They note** that without this signaling, cells of the underlying progress zone stop proliferation and differentiation which in turn results in defects of the central rays.

Figure A is a clinical photo of a cleft hand with the common presentation of missing central rays. Figure B shows an autosomal recessive gene pedigree. Figure C shows an X-linked recessive gene pedigree. Figure D shows an X-linked dominant gene pedigree. Figure E shows an autosomal dominant gene pedigree. Figure F shows a mitochondrial inheritance gene pedigree.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Figure B reveals an autosomal recessive gene pedigree which requires the gene to be passed on from 2 carrier parents in order for transmission to occur to the offspring.
Answer 2: Figure C reveals an X-linked recessive gene pedigree with only female carriers being able to pass the gene off to male offspring.
Answer 3: Figure D reveals an X-linked dominant gene pedigree with all males with the disease passing the gene off to all-female offspring and females with the disease pass the gene off to 50% of children
Answer 5: Figure F reveals a mitochondrial inheritance gene pedigree with all females with the disease passing the gene to all offspring (males and females).
