## Question

A 28-year-old woman presents with two months of progressive dorsal forefoot pain localized to the base of the second toe. She reports difficulty with push-off during running but denies locking or instability. Her radiograph is displayed in Figure A. An MRI is obtained, which confirms the suspected diagnosis, as well as sparing of the plantar articular cartilage. After six weeks of nonoperative management with activity modification, a stiff-soled shoe with a metatarsal pad, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications, and a brief period of immobilization, she continues to have significant pain. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

![[q220944-a-freibergdisease.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Continued nonoperative management for an additional 6–8 weeks
2. Ultrasound-guided intra-articular corticosteroid injection
3. Dorsal closing-wedge (dorsiflexion) osteotomy of the second metatarsal head
4. Interpositional arthroplasty of the second metatarsophalangeal joint
5. Debridement and cancellous bone grafting of the metatarsal head

## Correct option

**3. Dorsal closing-wedge (dorsiflexion) osteotomy of the second metatarsal head**

## Answer explanation

A dorsal closing-wedge (dorsiflexion) osteotomy is the preferred operative option for patients with persistent symptoms following an adequate course of nonoperative management in early to mid–stage Freiberg disease (answer choice 3).

Freiberg disease is defined by osteonecrosis of the metatarsal head, most commonly the second, resulting initially in dorsal articular collapse while the plantar surface remains preserved until later stages. Initial management for all stages is typically nonoperative, utilizing NSAIDs, activity modification, immobilization, and orthotic offloading through metatarsal pads or stiff-soled shoes. When patients with early to mid–stage disease (Smillie I–III) fail nonoperative measures, dorsal closing-wedge osteotomy is a reliable joint-sparing procedure. Advanced degenerative changes may require joint-sacrificing procedures such as interpositional arthroplasty or partial metatarsal head resection (DuVries arthroplasty).

*Seybold and Zide reviewed management of Freiberg disease and* **emphasized** that nonoperative care is first-line for all stages, but surgical intervention is indicated when pain persists or deformity progresses. **They noted** dorsal closing-wedge osteotomy as a preferred joint-sparing technique for early to mid-stage disease.

*Trnka and colleagues reviewed surgical options and* **noted** that after failure of symptom-focused nonoperative strategies, osteotomies that redirect load to preserved plantar cartilage are effective in maintaining joint function. **They concluded** that joint-preserving procedures are optimal for stages with intact plantar cartilage.

Figure A is an oblique radiograph of the right foot, demonstrating flattening of the second metatarsal head without significant joint space collapse.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Additional nonoperative care is unlikely to provide benefit after a complete, appropriate trial has already failed.
Answer choice 2: Steroid injection may offer short-term relief but does not address dorsal overload or prevent further collapse.
Answer choice 4: Interpositional arthroplasty is typically reserved for late-stage disease with diffuse joint destruction, not early dorsal collapse.
Answer choice 5: Debridement with bone grafting is typically a second-line option, but is less predictable and not preferred when plantar cartilage remains intact.
