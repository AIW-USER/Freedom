## Question

A 4-year-old boy falls while building a tree fort and sustains a closed distal radius fracture. His mother takes him to the teaching hospital and radiographs demonstrate a 15-degree apex volar extra-articular distal radius fracture. The child is neurovascularly intact. He is reduced by the on-call resident and follows up in your clinic two weeks later. You repeat X-Rays which demonstrate 12 degrees of apex volar angulation. The child is freely moving all fingers and has no wrist tenderness. What is the best course of action at this point?

## Options

1. Osteoclasis and pin fixation on your next available operating day
2. Conscious sedation and repeat reduction of the displaced fracture
3. Hematoma block and repeat reduction of the displaced fracture
4. Observation, it should take one year or less for the fracture to remodel
5. Observation, it should take two years for the fracture to remodel

## Correct option

**4. Observation, it should take one year or less for the fracture to remodel**

## Answer explanation

A 12-degree deformity within the plane of wrist motion is completely acceptable, and the residual deformity can be expected to remodel conservatively at 1 degree per month, thereby completely removing in approximately one year.

Distal radius fractures account for 40% of all pediatric fractures, with a slight male predominance. There is a high tolerance for residual deformity in skeletally immature children given the significant remodeling potential. This is because the majority (~75%) of skeletal growth of the radius occurs at the distal physis. While remodeling for distal radius fractures can be conservatively estimated at one degree per month, other analyses have reported even more rapid remodeling. Operative indications for pediatric (<9 years old) distal radius fractures include irreducible closed fractures (interposition of periosteum or PQ), grossly contaminated open fractures, malrotation, or post-reduction dorsal angulation of greater than 30 degrees.

*Jeroense et al. reviewed the 40 distal radius fractures in children (aged 3-14) and assessed the rate of remodeling.* **The authors found** that the mean rate of angular remodeling was 2.5 degrees per month (range: 0.4-7.6 degrees per month). They furthermore found a positive correlation with the degree of initial angulation and rate of remodeling and noted that the rate of remodeling decreased with time. They did not find a correlation between the rate remodeling and age/sex.

*Crawford et al. reviewed 51 children (ages 3-10) presenting with marked displacement and angulation of distal radius fractures who were treated with no analgesia/sedation and only application of a gently molded fiberglass cast.* After an average length of immobilization of 42 days, sagittal angulation improved from 4 degrees to 2 degrees, all patients achieved clinical and radiographic union, and all patients AND parents were satisfied. The cost analysis demonstrated that reduction with conscious sedation was 5-6 times more expensive than the experimental protocol in the study and with no added benefit.

*Webb et al. prospectively randomized 113 children with distal radius fractures to either above- or below-elbow immobilization.* **They found** no difference in the change in displacement, angulation, or deviation between the two treatments. The fractures that lost reduction were however associated with higher cast indices (poor cast molding resulting in a cylindrical cast). **The authors additionally found** that patients with a below-elbow cast missed fewer days of school and needed less help with activities of daily living, leading them to advocate for below-elbow casting.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The patient has a tolerable residual degree of deformity in the plane of motion of the wrist. Furthermore, the patient will likely completely remodel within a year. Osteoclasis is therefore unnecessary.
Answers 2 and 3: The fracture has likely formed significant callous at this point and would be very difficult to displace. Furthermore, if the initial reduction and immobilization were appropriate, there is no reason that repeat reduction would be more successful.
Answer 5: Observation is appropriate, however, the fracture will most likely remodel within the first year of injury.
