## Question

A 19-year-old male Army infantry service member presents with the feeling of instability in his right knee. He states he had an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction back in high school during sophomore year secondary to an injury sustained while playing football. He was doing well until he had a twisting knee injury jumping off of a Humvee a month ago. His presenting radiographs are shown in Figure A. His MRI confirms failure of his prior autograft ACL reconstruction with tibial tunnel osteolysis. A CT scan shows a maximal tibial tunnel diameter of 18mm. Which of the following is the best next step in management?

![[q217866-a-acl-osteolysis.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Obtain standing alignment radiographs
2. Referral to physical therapy for definitive management
3. Perform an anterolateral complex reconstruction with iliotibial band tenodesis
4. Single-stage revision ACL reconstruction
5. Two-stage revision ACL reconstruction

## Correct option

**1. Obtain standing alignment radiographs**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a confirmed failed ACL reconstruction and, given his physically demanding occupation, should undergo a revision ACL reconstruction. Given the significant tibial tunnel widening, a two-staged approach should be employed. However, it is prudent to rule out other potential causes of graft failure including coronal plane malalignment.

The number of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction procedures performed in the United States has steadily increased over time. As expected, so too has the correlated number of revision ACL reconstruction cases increased. It is estimated that approximately 13,000 cases of revision ACL reconstruction are performed in the United States annually. Surgeons have the option of performing a single- versus two-staged approach. It is important to rule out potential causes of failure to include prior tunnel malpositioning, lower extremity malalignment, or an excessive posterior tibial slope. While the vast majority of revision cases can be performed in a single-stage manner, there are indications for the two-staged approach: (1) malpositioned bone tunnels with fixation that will interfere with revision tunnel placement, (2) the presence of bone tunnel osteolysis in which the tunnel diameter would not sufficiency support a new ligament graft or (3) if there is an acute infection leading to graft failure. In these cases, it is prudent to perform prior tunnel/hardware removal and bone graft of the osseous defects. After sufficient time has passed for graft incorporation (3-6 months), then a second procedure can be performed with revision ACL reconstruction using the reconstituted bone stock.

*Miller and colleagues reviewed the causes of ACL reconstruction failure and overall management.* *All patients with suspected reinjury should undergo a focused physical examination and evaluation for potential causes for graft failure.* These can include tunnel malpositioning, varus/valgus malalignment, and/or excessive tibial slope (>12 degrees). In cases where notable tunnel widening is suspected or seen on MRI, a CT scan should also be obtained. CT remains the most reliable imaging modality to measure tunnel size in order to plan for bone grafting. **The authors state** that the tunnel diameters should be measured at the widest visualized diameter on the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. Measurements of 15mm or more are consistent with significant osteolysis and should be approached with a two-staged revision (i.e. bone grafting followed by delayed revision ACL reconstruction).

*Erickson and colleagues performed a comprehensive review regarding the epidemiology and surgical approach of two-staged revision ACL reconstruction.* *They discuss the importance of evaluating for potential causes of failure and addressing them at the time of definitive surgery.* **They conclude** that while revision ACL reconstruction carries a more guarded prognosis than primary ACL reconstruction, careful pre-operating planning, meticulous surgical technique, and tailoring patient expectations can increase the number of good results in this challenging complication.

Figure A shows a right knee with metallic interference screws from the prior ACL reconstruction. Osteolysis and widening of the tibial tunnel can be appreciated.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Given this patient’s physically demanding occupational as an infantry service member, he should be indicated for a revision ACL reconstruction over nonoperative management.
Answer 3: The recent STABILITY Trial reported a statistically significant reduction in both ACL graft rupture and persistent rotatory laxity when supplementing revision ACL reconstruction with an anterolateral complex reconstruction. However, the patient should first be evaluated for other risks for graft failure before considering revision ACL reconstruction.
Answer 4 and 5: Given the significant osteolysis of the tibial tunnel (>15mm), this patient should undergo two-stage revision ACL reconstruction. The first operative procedure would include hardware removal and bone grafting followed by a delayed, second operative procedure with revision ACL reconstruction.
