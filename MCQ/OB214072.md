## Options

1. Closed reduction and splinting in the ED with follow up in your nonoperative fracture clinic.
2. Outpatient clinic follow up for surgical scheduling sometime this week
3. Splint the patient in situ and put on your surgical schedule for the morning
4. Emergently bring the patient to the operating room for attempted closed reduction and percutaneous pinning
5. Consult vascular surgery for emergent vascular exploration and reconstruction

## Correct option

**4. Emergently bring the patient to the operating room for attempted closed reduction and percutaneous pinning**

## Answer explanation

Pale and pulseless extremities are emergencies, and in the setting of a supracondylar fracture require immediate closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) in the operating room.

Supracondylar fractures are an extremely common fracture in pediatrics, typically in children between 5-7 years of age. It occurs due to a fall onto an outstretched hand. Extension-type is the most common, but about 5% are flexion-type fractures. Neurovascular compromise can occur with these injuries including injury to the anterior interosseus nerve (AIN), ulnar nerve, radial nerve, and brachial artery. Neuropraxias are often treated with observation alone after CRPP. Vascular injury can present with loss of palpable pulse, but continued perfusion distally. This is often referred to as "pink and pulseless." Some patients, however, present without palpable radial pulse and no perfusion distally, so-called "pale and pulseless." These patients require emergent CRPP with or without vascular exploration depending if distal perfusion is restored after anatomic reduction.

*Badkoobehi et al. review the management of a pulseless supracondylar humerus fracture.* *They cite 20% of supracondylar fracture patients have vascular compromise.* Standard management for pale and pulseless extremities is an emergent reduction and operative fixation. After reduction, 53-72% have restoration of perfusion and pulse. If perfusion is not restored, **they recommend** emergent vascular exploration via an anterior approach as 82% have a concomitant brachial artery injury.

*Han, Wang, and Liu investigated the effects of timing on results and complications in pediatric patients with Gartland III supracondylar fractures comparing CRPP before and after 12 hours post-injury.* *There were 40 patients in the early group and 46 in the late group.* **They found** that delay in surgery greater than 12 hours did not affect outcomes to include time to union, final ROM, iatrogenic nerve injury, transition to open surgery, and compartment syndrome.

*Barrett et al. retrospectively reviewed 35 patients with supracondylar humerus fractures with AIN injuries.* *They investigated the effect of surgical timing to time to return of AIN function and did not find any correlation between the two.* The average time of AIN functional return was 49 days, with 90% recovering by 149 days post-injury.

*Larson et al. retrospectively compared operative treatment of Gartland II supracondylar fractures that occurred within or after 24 hours from injury.* The delay group was treated an average of 163 hours after injury. They did not find a difference in major complications including iatrogenic nerve injury, loss of fixation, postoperative stiffness, and refracture. **They concluded** that it is safe to operatively treat type II supracondylar fractures in a delayed fashion.

Incorrect:
Answer 1: This patient requires emergent surgical intervention. Additionally, type III (as in this patient) supracondylar fractures should be treated surgically.
Answer 2: This patient should be treated emergently.
Answer 3: This patient should be treated emergently, and should be taken that night to restore blood flow distally.
