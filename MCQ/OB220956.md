## Question

A 22-year-old elite athlete reports pain at the great toe metatarsophalangeal joint that occurs during deep squatting and push-off. Examination reveals focal tenderness posterior to the medial malleolus with crepitus during hallux motion, but forced ankle plantarflexion does not reproduce pain. Foot radiographs show an os trigonum, without evidence of bony impingement on plantarflexion views. An MRI scan demonstrates a low-lying muscle belly of a muscle originating from the inferior fibula and interosseous membrane. Which physical examination maneuver best corroborates stenosing tenosynovitis of the involved muscle as the source of her great toe symptoms?

## Options

1. Passive forced ankle plantarflexion while assessing for posterolateral hindfoot pain
2. With maximal knee extension and ankle dorsiflexion, assess passive hallux dorsiflexion
3. Palpation for tenderness behind the peroneal tendons at the posterolateral ankle
4. Forced ankle dorsiflexion with the foot everted to reproduce anterior ankle pain
5. Assessing ankle movement in response to calf-squeeze test

## Correct option

**2. With maximal knee extension and ankle dorsiflexion, assess passive hallux dorsiflexion**

## Answer explanation

Limitation and pain with passive hallux dorsiflexion when the knee is extended, and the ankle is maximally dorsiflexed, is the bedside sign of “functional hallux rigidus,” caused by a low, distal flexor hallucis longus (FHL) muscle belly jamming in a retrotalar fibro-osseous tunnel (Answer choice 2).

FHL tenosynovitis is identifiable by pain localizing posteromedially and may include crepitus along the posteromedial ankle or great toe locking with range of motion. FHL tenosynovitis is associated with Os trigonum syndrome, resulting from repetitive ankle plantarflexion impingement and typically produces posterolateral ankle pain provoked by active or passive plantarflexion. In os trigonum syndrome, lateral radiographs in plantarflexion can show tibio-calcaneal impingement, and MRI may reveal edema and inflammatory changes around the os trigonum and involving the FHL. Although these entities often coexist, detailed evaluation for the presence and symptomatology of each individually is an important element of patient evaluation, as this may guide surgical planning.

Hamilton provided a review of FHL stenosing tenosynovitis and posterior os trigonum impingement in ballet dancers. He highlighted that abnormal distal FHL muscle fibers can be pulled into the tendon sheath “like a cork in a bottle,” producing functional hallux rigidus verified by testing hallux dorsiflexion with the knee extended and ankle fully dorsiflexed; he also noted that posterior impingement tends to be posterolateral and worsened by forced plantarflexion. He reported good outcomes after FHL release, emphasizing that the mere presence of an os trigonum on radiographs does not mandate excision.

*Nault and colleagues reviewed os trigonum syndrome, describing posterolateral tenderness with pain reproduced by passive maximal plantarflexion, frequent coexistence with FHL tenosynovitis, and the importance of clinical localization.* *They also advise addressing a low FHL muscle belly intraoperatively to prevent impingement of the tendon during hallux motion with the ankle in dorsiflexion.*

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Forced plantarflexion reproducing posterolateral ankle pain is typical of os trigonum posterior impingement, not FHL-driven functional hallux rigidus.
Answer choice 3: Posterolateral tenderness behind the peroneals localizes to os trigonum impingement rather than FHL stenosis, which typically causes posteromedial ankle pain.
Answer choice 4: Pain with forced dorsiflexion in eversion suggests anterior ankle impingement.
Answer choice 5: The calf-squeeze (Thompson) test assesses Achilles tendon integrity and is unrelated to FHL stenosis or first MTP pain.
