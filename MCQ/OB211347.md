## Question

A 35-year-old secretary from a small mid-Atlantic paper product distributor has numbness and tingling in her ulnar two digits for the last 7 years, particularly when she is at work. At work, she often has to keep her elbows in a flexed position while typing for long periods of time. She has tried night splints and some ergonomic changes at work, which have not benefited her. She is referred to your clinic and you order electrodiagnostic studies, which demonstrate objective evidence of cubital tunnel syndrome. Which of the following findings may be seen on her nerve conduction studies?

## Options

1. Fibrillations
2. Positive sharp waves
3. Prolonged distal sensory latency
4. Fasciculations
5. Increased nerve conduction velocity

## Correct option

**3. Prolonged distal sensory latency**

## Answer explanation

The patient has cubital tunnel syndrome, which can be potentiated by advancing age, compression, decreased blood flow, fibrosis, and increased tension on the ulnar nerve. The patient’s nerve conduction study would show decreased conduction velocity and increased distal sensory latency.

Electrodiagnostics such as nerve conduction studies (NCS) or electromyography (EMG) are often performed to confirm clinical suspicion of ulnar neuropathy. Conduction velocity below 50m/sec across the elbow, motor latencies >4.3 ms, distal sensory latency of >3.2ms, or a decrease in amplitude of 50% as compared to the contralateral extremity are all considered abnormal and indicative of a compressive neuropathy. Amplitude is an assessment of the number of conducting axons but is somewhat more difficult to test and typically the sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) are affected before the compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs). Early stages of a compressive neuropathy may show no changes on EMG or NCS. However, as the compression progresses, demylelination will occur and the conduction velocity will decrease. EMG may be normal until late in the disease process.

*Lundborg reviewed the history of peripheral nerve repair and* **identified** future directions. **The authors discusses** that repair in the setting of nerve transection begins with formation of receptors on the surfaces of Schwann cells. Neurotrophic factors are released by macrophages and include IL-6, FGFs, Epidermal growth factor, and ILGF. The distal segment of the transected nerve undergoes Wallerian degeneration and is digested by macrophages. This process stimulates Schwann cell mitotic activity which supports axonal growth. **The author concludes** that future directions include focusing on neurotrophic factors and their impact on axonal outgrowth.

*Freedman et al. review electrodiagnostic studies in compressive neuropathies of the upper extremity.* **The authors discuss** that NCS are performed on motor and sensory nerves and assess latency and velocity. The electrical potential created when a motor nerve is stimulated is the CMAP, while EMG evaluates muscles at rest and after stimulation. **The authors note** that EMGs detect insertional activity (may be decreased in fibrotic muscle) and fibrillations (irregular, more acute, activity resulting from axonal damage). They stress that electrodiagnostic studies are a supplement, not a replacement, for a history and physical exam.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Fibrillations are a sign of hyper-sensitive muscle fibers with spontaneous firing and are a finding of nerve injury on EMG, not NCS.
Answer 2: Positive sharp waves are also a sign of abnormal hypersensitive muscle fibers with spontaneous firing and are a finding of nerve injury on EMG, not NCS.
Answer 4: Fasiculations are irregular discharges of a single motor unit and are larger and more complex than fibrillations. Fasiculations are present on nerve injury on the EMG, not NCS.
Answer 5: Decreased (not increased) conduction velocity would be expected with a compressive neuropathy.
