## Question

A 32-year-old male is brought to the emergency department after a table saw injury to his left wrist. He is urgently brought back to the operating room for debridement and irrigation with nerve, artery, and tendon repairs. Upon wound exploration, the median nerve is found to be transected as well as the flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, and partial muscle belly injury to the flexor carpi ulnaris. When compared to the symptoms of acute carpal tunnel syndrome, how would this patient’s injury differ in presentation?

## Options

1. Loss of sensation along the volar aspect of the fourth and fifth digits
2. Loss of sensation along the palmar aspect of the index and middle fingers
3. Loss of sensation along the thenar eminence
4. Loss of sensation along the hypothenar eminence
5. Loss of sensation along the dorsal aspect of the thumb

## Correct option

**3. Loss of sensation along the thenar eminence**

## Answer explanation

The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve branches off approximately 4-8 cm proximal to the distal wrist crease along the ulnar side of the flexor carpi radialis to supply cutaneous innervation to the thenar eminence. It traverses the wrist crease outside the carpal tunnel (Illustration A) but may be injured in volar distal forearm lacerations (Answer 3).

Carpal tunnel syndrome involves compression of the median nerve at the level of the wrist beneath the transverse carpal ligament. In the early stages of the disease, patients endorse isolated paresthesias along the volar thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring fingers. Advanced disease manifests with motor weakness of the muscles innervated by the recurrent motor branch of the median nerve (i.e. abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis, flexor pollicis brevis, radial ½ lumbricals). Just superficial and radial to the carpal tunnel lies the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve. It is often identified during the volar approach of Henry in surgically treating distal radius fractures. While this nerve is spared in the setting of carpal tunnel syndrome, it may be involved in transverse lacerations proximal to the wrist. If neurotmesis is identified, the nerve should be primarily repaired with appropriate technique to retain cutaneous sensation along the radial palm.

*Strohl and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of various compressive neuropathies that may occur within the forearm.* *They provide detailed anatomy reviews of each major nerve, sites of entrapment, and suggested treatment strategies.* **The authors highlight** the importance of corroborating the physical examination findings to nerve conduction/electromyography results to establish each diagnosis.

Smith and Ebraheim provided an anatomic review of the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve. The authors discuss that it consistently originates from the radial side of the median nerve 4-8cm proximal to the wrist crease, travels adjacent to the palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis, and courses superficial to the carpal tunnel to reach the volar surface of the palm. They conclude that understanding its normal anatomical course can prevent iatrogenic injury during volar wrist procedures.

Illustration A demonstrates the anatomic relationship of the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve to the median nerve proper at the wrist. Importantly, it terminates at the thenar eminence after traveling volar to the carpal tunnel.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The volar aspect of the fourth and fifth digits has afferent sensory fibers of the superficial sensory branch of the ulnar nerve. These terminal ulnar nerve fibers exit in isolation from zone 3 of the ulnar tunnel. Barring traumatic injuries, the superficial sensory branch of the ulnar nerve is most injured via compression from an ulnar artery thrombosis or aneurysm.
Answer 2: The digital nerves supplying sensation to the thumb, index, middle, and radial aspect of the ring finger are supplied from the median nerve proper after it passes through the carpal tunnel (i.e. becomes terminal digital nerves). Therefore, loss of sensation along the palmar aspect of the index and middle fingers would be involved in both injuries described.
Answer 4: The hypothenar eminence has afferent sensory fibers from the palmar cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve (i.e. nerve of Henle). These fibers branch off the main ulnar nerve proximal to the wrist at variable distances and patterns as compared to the predictable dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve (which consistently branches 5.5cm proximal to the ulnar head).
Answer 5: The dorsal aspect of the thumb has afferent sensory fibers from the terminal sensory branches of the radial nerve (SBRN). It may be compressed with tight watch bands, handcuffs, or iatrogenically injured during dorso-radial approaches to the wrist (i.e. 1st dorsal compartment release).
