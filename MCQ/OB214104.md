## Question

You are seeing a 12-year-old boy with hemiplegic cerebral palsy in your pediatric clinic for his limb deformity. For which of the following physical examination findings is an isolated gastrocnemius recession best indicated?

## Options

1. When looking at the heel from the back of the patient, the examiner sees the second through fifth toes
2. Absence of ankle plantarflexion when the calf is squeezed
3. Equal ankle dorsiflexion regardless of whether the knee is flexed or extended
4. Increase in ankle dorsiflexion to 15 degrees when knee is flexed, compared to neutral ankle dorsiflexion when knee is extended
5. Inability to perform a single limb heel rise

## Correct option

**4. Increase in ankle dorsiflexion to 15 degrees when knee is flexed, compared to neutral ankle dorsiflexion when knee is extended**

## Answer explanation

A gastrocnemius recession is an option for mild to moderate ankle equinus when the there is an increase in ankle dorsiflexion when the knee is flexed compared to when knee is extended (positive Silfverskiold test).

Ankle equinus in cerebral palsy (CP) results from imbalance of ankle dorsiflexors and plantarflexors (resulting in relative plantar flexion of the hindfoot to the ankle) and spasticity/contracture of the gastrocsoleus complex, despite normal mid- and forefoot alignment. While equinus may be treated with casting (augmented with orthoses and botulinum toxin) or surgery, surgery is an option either after unsuccessful conservative management or in patients with fixed equinus. Equinus for a tight gastrocnemius versus achilles tendon (distinguished by the Silfverskiold test) is addressed with a gastrocnemius recession or tendo-achilles lengthening, respectively.

*Dreher et al. investigated the long-term outcomes after gastrocnemius-soleus intramuscular recession as a part of multilevel surgery in spastic diplegic CP patients.* **They reported** significant improvements in kinematic ankle and gait analysis as well persistent increase in dorsiflexor muscle strength without significant loss of plantar flexor strength one year after surgery. **They concluded** that the procedure results in the satisfactory correction of mild and moderate equinus deformity in pediatric patients with spastic diplegia without much risk for overcorrection.

*Tustin et al. reviewed the updated evidence for casting in the management of ankle equinus in children with CP.* **They reported** that in all of the studies analyzed, there was significant improvement in ankle dorsiflexion and that combined treatment with botulinum toxin and casting offered greater and more sustained ankle dorsiflexion gains compared to botulinum toxin alone. **They concluded** that while casting appears to offer at least short-term improvement in ankle dorsiflexion, the long term outcomes and the ability to avoid surgery is not well studied.

*Shore et al. reviewed the surgical management of equinus deformity in children with CP.* **They reported** that a younger age at the index procedure was associated with an increased risk of recurrent equinus. **They highlighted** that the two most important variables for determining the outcome of surgery for equinus deformity in cerebral palsy was CP subtype (hemiplegia vs diplegia) and age at the time of the procedure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the "too many toes sign" seen with a flatfoot deformity. An isolated gastrocnemius recession would not be indicated here.
Answer 2: This describes the Thompson test, seen with achilles tendon ruptures. A gastrocnemius recession here would worsen the plantarflexion lag.
Answer 3: This suggests achilles tendon tightness, which would require a tendo-achilles lengthening.
Answer 5: Inability to perform a single limb heel rise may be seen in posterior tibial tendon insufficiency. An isolated gastrocnemius recession would not be indicated in this scenario.
