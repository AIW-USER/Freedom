## Question

A 46-year-old recreational athlete presents to the clinic for follow-up of forefoot pain. Pain is located under the second through fourth metatarsal heads. The patient has trialed orthotics with metatarsal pads as well as extensive PT focusing on stretching. A lateral radiograph of the patient’s foot is demonstrated in Figure A. On physical exam, he has tenderness about the plantar aspect of the forefoot. Range of motion of the ankle is to 3° of dorsiflexion with the knee extended and to 16° with the knee flexed. What is the next most appropriate step in management?

![[q218615-a-lateral-foot-radiograph.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Weil osteotomies of the second though fourth metatarsals
2. Posterior tibialis debridement and medial calcaneal slide osteotomy
3. Triple cut tendoachilles lengthening
4. Gastronemius recession
5. Corticosteroid injection into the second through fourth metatarsal phalangeal joints

## Correct option

**4. Gastronemius recession**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with metatarsalgia in the setting of a gastrocnemius contraction that has been recalcitrant to non-operative management. The most appropriate next step in management is a gastrocnemius recession given evidence of isolated gastrocnemius contracture based on the Silfverskiöld test.

Forefoot pain is a common entity that has a multitude of potential causative factors. Appropriate workup with a physical exam and diagnostic imaging is crucial. In the patient above, diagnosis can be alluded to based on the normal lateral foot radiograph with a Silfverskiöld test indicative of an isolated gastrocnemius contractor. The Silfverskiöld test differentiates gastrocnemius tightness from an Achilles tendon contracture by evaluating ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended and then flexed. Gastrocnemius-soleus complex lengthening has gained popularity as a treatment for many foot and ankle disorders caused by contracture of the gastrocnemius muscle. Several approaches to reducing a gastrocsoleus equinus involve a fasciotomy or tenotomy of the gastrocnemius or triceps surae tendon that is either dorsal or ventral and at either proximal or distal level in the triceps surae or gastrocnemius tendon. Illustration A demonstrates commonly described approaches.

*Espinosa et al. provide a review on metatarsalgia (ie, metatarsal pain).* *They describe the great variability in possible causative factors.* **They further note** that nonsurgical management is usually sufficient to achieve satisfactory results. **They further state** that metatarsal osteotomies always fail in the long term and that improved equipment and internal fixation methods may lead to better long-term outcomes.

*Abdulmassih et al. provide a review of contractures involving the triceps surae.* **They note** that during gait, decreased dorsiflexion shifts weight-bearing pressures from the heel to the forefoot, which may result in or exacerbate one of several pathologic conditions. They further describe the nonsurgical management of triceps surae contracture, including splinting and stretching exercises, and provide insight into several surgical lengthening techniques.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The most common indication for a Weil osteotomy is metatarsalgia due to metatarsal overloading but is also commonly used for the treatment of subluxated or dislocated metatarsal phalangeal joints. This patient has metatarsalgia secondary gastrocnemius contractor and performing Weil osteotomies would not appropriately address the causative pathology.
Answer 2: There is no evidence that this patient has pes planus.
Answer 3: As demonstrated by the Silfverskiöld test, this patient has an isolated gastrocnemius contractor. A triple-cut tendoachilles lengthening could be used if the patient had limited dorsiflexion of the ankle with both the knee flexed and extended.
Answer 5: Corticosteroid injection into the second through fourth metatarsal phalangeal joints would not be appropriate for this patient.
