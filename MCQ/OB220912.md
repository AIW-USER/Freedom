## Question

A 11-year-old boy presents after a dirt bike crash with a displaced tibial shaft fracture. On examination, he is anxious and requires increasing doses of analgesia. Which of the following is the most significant risk factor for developing acute compartment syndrome in this scenario?

## Options

1. Age less than 12 years
2. Simple oblique fracture pattern
3. Higher body mass index
4. Closed fracture
5. Absence of ipsilateral fibula fracture

## Correct option

**3. Higher body mass index**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a displaced tibial shaft fracture. Of the options presented, individuals with higher body mass index are at increased risk of developing compartment syndrome after tibial shaft fractures (Answer 3).

Acute compartment syndrome in children and adolescents following tibial shaft fractures is a limb-threatening emergency that occurs in approximately 2-12% of cases, with higher rates in adolescents over 14 years and those injured in high-energy mechanisms such as motor vehicle or motorcycle accidents. Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment using the "three As", including anxiety, agitation, and increasing analgesic requirement, rather than classic adult findings, as pain characterization is difficult in young or non-verbal patients. The major risk factors for pediatric compartment syndrome following a tibial shaft fracture are older age (especially >14 years), male gender, high-energy trauma (notably motor vehicle or motorcycle accidents and pedestrian struck mechanisms), comminuted or segmental fracture patterns, associated ipsilateral fibular fracture, higher body mass index, open fractures, and operative fixation (particularly intramedullary nailing).

*Obey et al performed a retrospective database study that analyzed 50,640 pediatric patients (ages 1-18 years) with tibia fractures.* **They identified** 309 cases of acute compartment syndrome (ACS), yielding an overall incidence of 0.6% (5.5% in open fractures). The strongest predictors of ACS development were age 13-18 years (RR: 4.04), open fractures (RR: 3.83), and motor vehicle crash mechanism (RR: 5.69), with 7.4% of ACS cases diagnosed after discharge from the index admission, particularly in teenagers treated nonoperatively.

*Hogue et al reviewed age-specific, individualized treatment strategies for pediatric tibial shaft fractures, ranging from closed reduction and casting to flexible nails, external fixation, and plate osteosynthesis, with treatment selection based on injury pattern, mechanism, skeletal maturity, and patient size.* Acceptable alignment parameters vary by age due to differing remodeling potential, though all fractures should maintain minimal rotational malalignment and less than 1 cm of shortening, with stable union typically occurring in 3-4 weeks for toddler's fractures and 6-8 weeks for other patterns. Compartment syndrome can occur with both open and closed injuries, while nonunions are rare but more common with external fixation or flexible nailing.

*Villarreal et al* **found** that pediatric patients who developed ACS had a higher BMI compared to those who did not, and multivariable analysis confirmed BMI as an independent risk factor for ACS after tibial shaft fracture. **They concluded** that this relationship may be due to increased soft tissue bulk, which contributes to higher intracompartmental pressures following trauma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Younger age is actually protective, as children have more compliant compartments and lower ACS rates.
Answer 2: Simple fracture patterns are less likely to be associated with high-energy soft-tissue damage related to compartment syndrome.
Answer 4: Open fractures, rather than closed injuries, are more likely to be associated with high-energy soft-tissue damage and compartment syndrome.
Answer 5: Conversely, the presence of an ipsilateral fibular fracture likely reflects higher-energy trauma and more extensive soft tissue injury.
