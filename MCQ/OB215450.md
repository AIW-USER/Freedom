## Question

An 11-year-old, right hand dominant boy complains of right elbow pain after pitching his 6th game of the week. His father is convinced that he "has the stuff" and that it is "only a matter of time" before his son pitches professionally. The community hand surgeon sees the patient for his first visit to a physician for this issue. On exam of the right upper extremity, he lacks terminal elbow extension, has a mild effusion, but has no crepitus through an arc of motion. Between the history, physical exam, and radiograph (Figure A) a diagnosis is made. What is the best treatment at this point for the young hurler?

## Options

1. Open reduction internal fixation of the medial epicondyle with a headless compression screw
2. Open reduction internal fixation of the medial epicondyle with diverging smooth k-wires
3. Cessation of pitching for 3-6 weeks with slow progression back to activity
4. Osteochondral autograft or allograft transplantation surgery (OATS)
5. Arthroscopic debridement and loose body excision

## Correct option

**3. Cessation of pitching for 3-6 weeks with slow progression back to activity**

## Answer explanation

Initial treatment for Osteochondritis Dissecans (OCD) of the elbow is conservative - in this case, cessation of pitching for 3-6 weeks with slow progression back to activity.

OCD is a localized injury and subsequent separation of articular cartilage and subchondral bone, often presenting in the dominant sided capitellum in boys over the age of 10, particularly in those with repetitive overhead activity. It is theorized that OCD results from repetitive compression-type injury (overhead or upper extremity weight-bearing activities) of the immature capitellum causing vascular insufficiency and repetitive microtrauma. Initial treatment involves cessation of the offending activity (often baseball or gymnastics) for 6 weeks followed by a slow return to activity. Non-operative management has a high success rate. For patients with large, unstable lesions which have failed non-operative management or with a loose body, surgical treatment is recommended. Surgical treatments range from arthroscopic loose body excision to OATS.

*Kobayashi et al review lateral compression injuries in the pediatric elbow, including Panner's disease and OCD of the capitellum.* Panner's disease is an osteochondrosis of the capitellum in patients younger than 10. OCD more frequently affects slightly older patients and often includes loose body formation. Panner's disease and OCD likely represent a continuum of disease.

*Ruchelsman et al review the current concepts of OCD of the capitellum.* While Panner's disease is self-limiting of the immature capitellum, OCD is the result of microtrauma and vascular susceptibility. MRI is beneficial in the diagnosis. Early stable lesions are managed with surgery while surgery is reserved for unstable lesions or those who fail conservative management. OATs is promising for large high-grade lesions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-2: The medial epicondyle is not fractured.
Answer 4: Osteochondral autograft or allograft transplantation surgery (OATS) is indicated for large lesions that engage the radial head. However, this patient has not yet attempted conservative management.
Answer 5: Arthroscopic debridement and loose body excision is not indicated as there evidence that this patient has a loose body and he has not attempted conservative management.
