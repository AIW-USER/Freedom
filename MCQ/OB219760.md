## Question

A 21-year-old wrestler presents to the clinic for evaluation of a skin lesion on his neck. On examination, erythematous vesicles are seen with scant clear fluid discharge. The clinical photo is shown in Figure A. What is the most appropriate treatment?

![[q219760-a-herpes-gladiatorum.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Acyclovir and return to sport after five days of treatment with crusting of lesions
2. Clotrimazole cream and return to sport after two days
3. Famciclovir and return to sport after three days of treatment with crusting of lesions
4. Topical mupriocin until the lesions have crusted over
5. Erythromycin and return to sport after crusting has cleared

## Correct option

**1. Acyclovir and return to sport after five days of treatment with crusting of lesions**

## Answer explanation

This 21-year-old wrestler sustained a cutaneous lesion characteristic of herpes gladiatorum, which is most commonly caused by herpes simplex virus-1 (80%; HSV-1). The most appropriate treatment includes an antiviral (acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir) for five days, the lesions have crusted over, and no new lesions have formed over the past three days (Answer 1).

Herpes gladiatorum is a commonly seen skin infection in athletes, with the causative organism being HSV-1. The organism is transmitted via skin-to-skin contact and has an incubation phase of 5-10 days until symptoms such as erythema, itching, burning, and vesicle formation occur. The mainstay management for HSV infections is oral acyclovir, and return to play is indicated when no new lesions have formed for the prior 72 hours, the lesions have crusted over, and the athlete has been on medication for five days.

Mirfazaelian and Daneshbod report on a 21-year-old male wrestler who had a painful neck and facial rash with vesicular lesions for five days. The skin condition was ultimately diagnosed as herpes gladiatorum, caused by HSV-1. The patient received acyclovir as a treatment and could return to sport after the lesions were crusted over without complication.

*Johnson reviewed herpes gladiatorum and other common skin diseases (tinea infections, impetigo, furunculosis, and acne mechanica),* **noting** a rate of 20-40% in college wrestlers, with the most common sites being the head/neck, followed by the extremities. The contagious nature of these infections has prompted sports governing bodies to implement strict return-to-play guidelines. Ultimately, **the author notes** the ubiquity of these skin conditions should allow coaches, athletic trainers, and others to immediately identify them, allowing them to be seen by clinicians and ultimately get treated soon to avoid prolonged missed time from sport.

Paradise and Hu extensively review infectious rashes of all varieties, including bacterial, fungal, viral, and parasitic. The authors highlight their presentation and treatment recommendations for each. Ultimately, they note that the presence of these conditions can lead to prolonged absence from athletic participation, particularly in close-contact sports, and advocate for prompt diagnosis leading to appropriate isolation and treatment, which may prevent outbreaks and limit time away from sport.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: This would be the appropriate treatment if the patient's presentation suggested ringworm (centrifugally spreading pruritic annular patch with an active, scaly border).
Answer 3: Treatment is five days regardless of the type of antiviral employed.
Answer 4: This would be the most appropriate answer if the patient had a presentation for impetigo (erythematous sores around the nose and mouth with golden-brown crusting).
Answer 5: this would be the correct answer for a presentation suggestive of erysipelas (a well-demarcated and raised area of erythema, most commonly seen on the face)
