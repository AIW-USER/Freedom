## Question

A 21-year-old male collegiate baseball pitcher sees you in the office with complaints of nagging deep, posterior shoulder pain that is worse with throwing. He reports that it aggravates him most in the late cocking phase when pitching. On examination, rotator cuff testing is somewhat painful but 5/5 strength is maintained in all planes. His external rotation is 100º, and internal rotation is 45º. Based on these findings, you diagnose him with internal impingement and recommend physical therapy and activity modifications. He calls your office several weeks later without improvement and would like to obtain an MRI. Which of the following findings would be most likely seen on the MRI?

## Options

1. Subscapularis tear
2. Articular sided supraspinatus tearing
3. Bursal sided supraspinatus tearing
4. Bankart lesion
5. Hill Sachs lesion

## Correct option

**2. Articular sided supraspinatus tearing**

## Answer explanation

In internal impingement, pathologic translation of the humeral head during overhead motion causes the undersurface of the rotator cuff to be "pinched" between the humeral head and the glenoid, which leads to articular sided rotator cuff tearing.

Internal impingement is a common cause of shoulder pain, particularly in throwing and overhead athletes. It is thought that posterior capsular tightness and possibly anterior shoulder micro-instability contribute to the pathologic postero-superior translation of the humeral head during the throwing motion. This leads to repetitive impingement of the posterior supraspinatus between the humeral head and glenoid, which has been associated with articular sided rotator cuff tearing. Also seen in cases of internal impingement are posterior and superior labral (SLAP) lesions, posterior capsular scarring (Bennett lesion) and glenoid cartilage defects.

*Drakos et al. reviewed internal impingement in the overhead athlete,* **noting** that it represents a variety of symptoms/pathology including articular sided rotator cuff tearing and internal rotation deficit. It is likely associated with physiologic adaptations that occur during throwing, including increased external rotation, humeral/glenoid retroversion and anterior micro-instability. **The authors recommend** therapy focused on balancing range of motion and improving scapular function before any operative intervention is undertaken.

*Heyworth et al. published an overview of shoulder internal impingement,* **noting** that it represents the excessive contact of the greater tuberosity with the posterosuperior glenoid with the arm abducted and externally rotated. This abnormal degree of contact leads to impingement of the rotator cuff and labral tissue, leading to articular sided rotator cuff tears and posterosuperior labral lesions. The authors talk at length about the mechanics behind this phenomenon and the recommended interventions best for managing it.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Subscapularis tearing would be unlikely in this young patient without any history of anterior shoulder instability or anterior shoulder pain.
Answer 3: Bursal sided rotator cuff tearing would be more likely in the setting of external shoulder impingement (i.e. hooked acromion).
Answer 4: A bankart lesion would also be unlikely in this patient without a prior episode of instability and is not directly associated with internal impingement.
Answer 5: A Hill Sachs lesion is an impaction fracture of the posterosuperior humeral head in the setting of anterior glenohumeral dislocation and is not associated with internal impingement.
