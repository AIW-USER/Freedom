## Question

A 32-year-old female presents to your clinic one week after an emergency department visit for right knee pain following an injury she sustained playing basketball. Radiographs from the emergency department are shown in Figure A. She was initially placed in a knee immobilizer and instructed to bear weight as tolerated in knee extension, with short-term orthopedic follow-up to determine definitive treatment. In clinic, she demonstrates 0 – 130º knee range of motion with mild pain, 5/5 strength in knee extension, and no deficit in straight leg raise. What is the most likely etiology of her radiographic findings?

![[q220389-a-patella-bipartita.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Failure of apoptosis in utero
2. Forceful eccentric contraction of the quadriceps
3. Direct blow to patella
4. Twisting injury of the knee joint
5. Failure of fusion of ossification centers

## Correct option

**5. Failure of fusion of ossification centers**

## Answer explanation

Radiographs demonstrate classic findings of a bipartite patella, which may be mistaken for a patella fracture, particularly in the setting of antecedent trauma. This is a normal anatomic variant, caused by the failure of the fusion of patellar ossification centers (answer choice 5).

Patella fractures, which often result from direct trauma or forceful eccentric extensor mechanism contraction, present on a broad spectrum of severity in a wide variety of patients. Diagnosis can typically be made with plain radiographs, with AP, lateral, and sunrise/merchant views necessary to evaluate the various types of fracture morphology, integrity of the extensor mechanism, and severity of articular incongruity. Management hinges upon the fracture's severity and stability, the extensor mechanism's competence, and patient factors. Many nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures and fracture patterns not disrupting the extensor mechanism (i.e., vertical fractures) may be treated with early weight-bearing as tolerated with the knee immobilized in extension. Open fractures, displaced fractures, or those with a disrupted extensor mechanism typically necessitate operative intervention, typically in the form of open reduction internal fixation, with a multitude of fixation methods described.

Bipartite patella, a congenital condition involving an unfused ossification center, may mimic a patellar fracture and should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Factors that suggest a bipartite patella rather than an acute fracture include the presence of smooth, well-corticated borders, classic superolateral location, and the presence of similar findings in the uninjured contralateral knee. In the rare cases where these present symptomatically, the vast majority may be treated conservatively, however operative management options exist for those who fail conservative measures for at least 6 months.

*Hargett et al. reviewed patella fractures,* **stating** morphology varies with injury mechanism and emphasizing the importance of pre-operative planning to individualize fixation. They advised that surgical intervention is often required in displaced fractures affecting the extensor mechanism and that meticulous soft-tissue handling is paramount to avoid complications given the patella's precarious blood supply. **They conclude** that treatment goals include anatomical reduction, stable fixation, and minimization of postoperative complications, achievable with a principled approach focused on rigid anatomical fixation and meticulous soft-tissue handling.

*Kallini et al. retrospectively reviewed the presentation, treatment, and outcomes of 266 pediatric patients with symptomatic bipartite patella (BPP), comparing those operatively managed (n=27) to those treated nonoperatively (n=239).* **They found** that operatively managed patients experienced a longer symptom duration before presentation (21.5 vs. 7.6 months, P<.001) and were more often older, female, and competitive athletes; surgical techniques varied (fragment excision, lateral release, fragment excision with lateral release, fragment screw fixation, and synchondrosis drilling), and the mean time to return to sport was 2.2 months. **The authors concluded** that although BPP typically responds to nonoperative care, approximately 10% of patients—most commonly competitive female adolescents with prolonged symptoms—require surgery, which has a roughly 15% risk of reoperation.

Figure A is the AP and lateral knee radiographs demonstrating classical findings of a bipartite patella, with the superolateral location and smooth borders of the accessory ossicle.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Failure of apoptosis in utero described the pathophysiology of a discoid meniscus, not bipartite patella
Answer choices 2 and 3: Forceful quadriceps eccentric contraction and a direct blow to the patella represent the typical mechanisms for patella fractures.
Answer choice 4: A variety of knee structures may be injured with significant knee twisting injuries, most commonly menisci or knee ligaments.
