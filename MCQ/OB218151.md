## Question

A 19-year-old collegiate pitcher presents to your clinic with a chief complaint of pain during pitching. He notes that his pain is most severe during the late cocking and early acceleration phase of the throwing cycle. Based on his physical examination and MRI, you diagnose the patient with internal impingement and recommend that he begin a therapy regimen focused on shoulder range of motion and strengthening. Which of the following best describes the clinical and radiographic findings most commonly observed in patients with internal impingement?

## Options

1. Decreased internal and external rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the bursal side of the posteriosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the anterior capsule/glenoid
2. Decreased internal and external rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the bursal side of the posteriosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the posterior capsule/glenoid
3. Increased external rotation and decreased internal rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the anterior capsule/glenoid
4. Increased external rotation and decreased internal rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the posterior capsule/glenoid
5. Increased external rotation and decreased internal rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the bursal side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the posterior capsule/glenoid

## Correct option

**4. Increased external rotation and decreased internal rotation of the shoulder, fraying of the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff, and hypertrophy and scarring of the posterior capsule/glenoid**

## Answer explanation

Patients with internal impingement typically complain of pain during late cocking and early acceleration, present with increased external rotation and decreased internal rotation, and have radiographic evidence of fraying of the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff, posterior and superior labral lesions, hypertrophy, and scarring of the posterior capsule/glenoid (Bennett lesion) and chondral injury to the posterior glenoid (Answer 4).

Internal impingement is a common cause of shoulder pain in overhead athletes that is caused by repetitive impingement between the articular side of the rotator cuff and the posterosuperior glenoid. The impingement occurs during maximal arm abduction and external rotation during the late cocking and early acceleration phases of throwing and causes a "peel-back" phenomenon of the posterosuperior labrum by the biceps. This impingement pattern contrasts with external impingement, which occurs on the bursal side of the rotator cuff. Due to altered glenohumeral mechanics, the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff impinges between the humeral head and glenoid, which often leads to the following pathologic changes:

1. Fraying of the articular side of the posterosuperior rotator cuff at the supraspinatus-infraspinatus interval.
2. Posterior and superior labral lesions
3. Hypertrophy and scarring of the posterior capsule and glenoid (Bennett lesion)
4. Chondral injury to the posterior glenoid

Many of these patients also suffer from glenohumeral internal rotation deficiency (GIRD), characterized by increased external rotation, decreased internal rotation, and overall preservation of the total arc of motion. Rotator cuff weakness is also commonly encountered during physical examination. The first line treatment for internal impingement is physical therapy with a focus on posterior capsular stretching and cessation from throwing. Operative treatment, which varies based on the associated pathology present, should only be considered if patients have failed an appropriately prescribed course of physical therapy and cessation from throwing.

Heyworth and Williams provide an overview of internal impingement of the shoulder. They note that this condition is characterized by repetitive contact of the greater tuberosity of the humeral head with the posterosuperior aspect of the glenoid, which leads to rotator cuff (articular side) and glenoid labrum impingement and damage. The authors note that the underlying etiology of this phenomenon is multifactorial and likely related to a combination of altered glenohumeral biomechanics, subtle glenohumeral instability, posterior capsular contracture, and scapular dyskinesia. In addition, the authors highlight the importance of initial nonoperative management in these patients, as surgical outcomes can be unpredictable, and may greatly impact the patient's ability to continue in his/her sport.

*Neri et al. investigated the outcomes of repair of type II SLAP tears in elite overhead athletes in the presence/absence of concomitant partial-thickness rotator cuff tears.* *Their study population consisted of 23 elite overhead athletes with a minimum follow-up of 1 year.* Overall, **the authors found** that the presence of a partial-thickness rotator cuff tear correlated with a lower return to sport rates, and the overall return to preinjury level of competition was 57%, despite high functional outcome (ASES and KJOC) scores.

*Myers et al. investigated the glenohumeral range of motion deficits and posterior shoulder tightness in throwers with internal impingement.* *Their study population consisted of 11 throwing athletes with internal impingement matched to 11 throwers with no history of injury.* The throwing athletes with internal impingement demonstrated significantly greater internal rotation deficit (p = .03) and posterior shoulder tightness (p = .03) compared with the control group, and there was no significant difference in external rotation gain between the groups (p = .16). Overall, **the authors conclude** that a tightening of the posterior elements of the shoulder may contribute to impingement, highlighting the importance of posterior capsular stretching as an initial treatment strategy in these patients.
