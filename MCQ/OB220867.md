## Question

A six-year-old girl presents with a limp and bilateral hip pain, and her parents deny any history of trauma or systemic illness. Radiographs show delayed ossification and irregularity with symmetrical involvement of both femoral heads. Her father developed early-onset osteoarthritis and her paternal aunt has severe myopia. Physical examination reveals normal stature, no facial dysmorphism, and no hearing loss. Which of the following next steps is most appropriate to clarify the underlying etiology of her hip findings?

## Options

1. Initiate empiric corticosteroid therapy for presumed juvenile idiopathic arthritis
2. Order MRI of the hips to assess for avascular necrosis
3. Perform a skeletal survey and refer for genetic screening with COL2A1 sequencing
4. Recommend activity modification with serial imaging for Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease
5. Obtain laboratory studies for metabolic bone disease

## Correct option

**3. Perform a skeletal survey and refer for genetic screening with COL2A1 sequencing**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation of bilateral hip pain, delayed ossification, and irregularity of both femoral heads with normal stature, absence of facial dysmorphism, and a family history of early-onset osteoarthritis and myopia is suggestive of an inherited skeletal dysplasia. The most appropriate next step to clarify the underlying etiology of her hip findings is to obtain a comprehensive skeletal survey and genetic screening (Answer 2).

COL2A1 genetic disorders often present with early-onset, bilateral, and symmetrical hip involvement, mimicking Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (LCPD). These disorders may be associated with extrasekeletal features, such as high myopia, retinal detachment, hearing loss, and orofacial abnormalities; however, some patients may initially present with minimal or absent extrasekeletal features. In contrast, traditional idiopathic Perthes disease typically presents as a unilateral, progressive osteonecrosis of the femoral head in children aged 4–8 years, with hip pain, limp, and restricted motion. Bilateral involvement in idiopathic Perthes is uncommon (10–15%) and usually asynchronous, whereas synchronous, symmetrical bilateral disease strongly suggests a type II collagen disorder.

*Kannu et al report on two pediatric cases with bilateral hip abnormalities resembling Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, in whom novel mutations in the COL2A1 gene were identified,* **highlighting** the phenotypic overlap between type II collagenopathies and classic Perthes disease. **The authors emphasize** the importance of considering COL2A1 mutations in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with bilateral Perthes-like hip disease, as this has significant implications for management and surveillance for associated complications. **The authors conclude** that genetic testing is instrumental in atypical or bilateral cases of Perthes-like disease, guiding appropriate diagnosis and care.

*Flores-Navarro et al describe a case in which Morquio disease presented with bilateral hip involvement mimicking Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, leading to initial misdiagnosis and delayed recognition of the underlying metabolic disorder.* **The authors emphasize** that careful evaluation of radiographic findings beyond the hips, assessment of other joints, and consideration of family history and multisystem involvement are critical for distinguishing Morquio disease from Perthes-like disease. **They conclude** that early and accurate diagnosis is essential to prevent progression to multi-organ deterioration.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: JIA tends to present with systemic features or asymmetric joint involvement, not isolated symmetric femoral head ossification abnormalities.
Answer 2: AVN is typically unilateral, whereas this child has bilateral, symmetric changes, suggesting a genetic etiology, rendering MRI less useful than skeletal survey and genetic testing.
Answer 4: While this is the initial recommended management in idiopathic Perthes, which presents as unilateral, idiopathic AVN of the femoral head, not bilateral and familial.
Answer 5: Rickets and other metabolic disorders typically present with short stature, bowing, or generalized bone abnormalities.
