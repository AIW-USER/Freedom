## Answer explanation

The posteromedial/posterolateral tibial plateau fracture shown in figure B is the most likely candidate for a prone posterior approach to the knee of the answer choices listed.

Tibial plateau fractures patterns are a reflection of the position of the knee and load that causes failure (e.g. hyperflexion and axial load). There are many surgical approaches described, and choosing an approach that allows direct visualization of the fracture and often buttress plate positioning can facilitate high-quality reductions. Most lateral patterns are addressed through an anterolateral approach and the most common medial patterns are addressed through either a direct medial or posteromedial approach. Variations with extensive posterior involvement may merit more extensile posterior approaches, which are done sometimes with the patient in a prone position.

*Bhattacharyya et al. present a case series of 13 patients treated for posterior shear tibial plateau fractures with a posterior approach.* *Patients were prone and an S-shaped incision over the popliteal fossa was used for the approach.* At the final follow up, 89% of patients were highly satisfied with their operation. They had no neurovascular injuries. They did find approximately a 5-degree average loss of extension in their series of patients.

*He et al. present an 8-patient case series treated for posterior tibial plateau fracture using an inverted L-shaped incision.* In the prone position, the incision extended from lateral in the popliteal fossa to medial where it curved distally. Popliteus and soleus were elevated from the posteromedial tibia for visualization. With this technique, they were able to obtain near-perfect reduction (≤2mm step-off) without complications. The average loss of extension at the final follow up was 3.6 degrees.

*Carlson published a case series of five patients with posterior bicondylar tibial plateau fracture treated with a two-incision posterior approach.* From the prone position, one incision was made posteromedial and a second is made posterolateral. This allowed for direct reduction followed by open reduction and internal fixation. **He found** lateral meniscal injuries in 4 of 5 patients requiring repair. This technique was associated with a flexion contracture of the knee. Ultimately these patients all healed their fractures, averaged final motion from 2-121 degrees flexion, and had no evidence of flexion instability. All patients had some pain with prolonged standing.

Figures A-E demonstrate AP and Lateral X-rays of fractures. Figure A is a Schatzker II tibial plateau fracture. Figure B is a posterior bicondylar tibial plateau fracture. Figure C is a distal femur fracture with an intra-articular split. Figure D is a Schatzker VI tibial plateau fracture with significant comminution. Figure E is a Hohl and Moore type 2 tibial plateau fracture (medial condyle failure that extends into the lateral articular surface).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This lateral split-depression fracture is most commonly treated in the supine position with an anterolateral approach.
Answer 3: Distal femur fractures are most commonly treated in the supine position with lateral or medial parapatellar approaches to the distal femur.
Answer 4: Some surgeons may consider starting fixation of this comminuted bicondylar tibial plateau in a prone position, but it would be more likely that the fracture shown in figure B would be addressed through a posterior approach.
Answer 5: In this Moore II variant fracture pattern, there seems to be a simplicity of the medial condyle with a metaphyseal exit point on the medial border of the tibia; while some Moore II variants have an additional posterolateral shear component, this fracture is less likely to benefit from a prone positioning than is that depicted in answer choice 2.
