## Question

Physical examination demonstrates a dorsolateral talar prominence, hindfoot valgus, and forefoot dorsiflexion. Lateral radiographs demonstrate a dorsal dislocation of the navicular on the talus. ## Answer explanation

The patient described in the vignette presents with congenital vertical talus, which is associated with congenital hip dislocation, as demonstrated in Figure A.

Congenital vertical talus (CVT) is a rare foot deformity present at birth, defined by fixed dorsal dislocation of the talonavicular joint with a rigid equinus contracture of the hindfoot, producing a classic rigid “rocker-bottom” foot. Associated anomalies are common, occurring in approximately 60–85% of cases, and include neuromuscular conditions such as myelomeningocele and arthrogryposis, chromosomal abnormalities including trisomy 18, and genetic syndromes such as Cri-du-Chat. Familial cases of CVT have been linked to HOXD10 mutations. Notably, the rate of concomitant hip dislocation in patients with CVT ranges from approximately 43% to 50%.

*Miller et al. completed a topical review describing CVT as a rare foot deformity that results in significant long-term disability, including pain and functional limitations if left untreated.* The etiology is likely heterogeneous, though growing evidence strongly supports a genetic basis involving genes expressed during early limb development. Historically, management relied on extensive surgical correction with high complication rates. However, contemporary treatment using minimally invasive serial manipulation and casting has demonstrated excellent short-term outcomes in both isolated and non-isolated cases, producing more flexible and functional feet analogous to results achieved with the Ponseti method for clubfoot.

*Aroojis et al. classified 229 patients with CVT into 5 groups based on associated conditions and* **demonstrated** that deformity severity and treatment outcomes varied substantially across groups. Notably, no patients with classic arthrogryposis had CVT, whereas those with distal arthrogryposis exhibited milder deformities that responded well to early one-stage surgical correction. In contrast, CVT associated with generalized syndromes or severe systemic or neurologic involvement was typically severe and refractory to treatment, with most patients unable to ambulate. In these cases, the primary treatment goal should be achieving a pain-free, braceable foot rather than full functional correction.

van Bosse reviewed the management of foot deformities in myelomeningocele and arthrogryposis, emphasizing preservation of foot flexibility and maintaining multiple treatment options for managing recurrence, which is common in both conditions. This principle is fundamental in myelomeningocele, where a stiff, insensate foot markedly increases the risk of skin breakdown and ulceration with progression to osteomyelitis. They discuss historically controversial approaches to treating syndromic foot deformities, highlighting that numerous surgical procedures have been advocated with variable success and high complication rates, and offer practical guidance on selecting appropriate surgical interventions and preferred treatment strategies based on the specific deformity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer B: Amniotic band sequence represents an extrinsic constriction disorder causing distal limb anomalies rather than CVT.
Answer C: Fibular hemimelia is a longitudinal limb deficiency associated with limb-length discrepancy and ankle instability, not CVT.
Answer D: Proximal focal femoral deficiency is a proximal femoral developmental abnormality without an association with CVT.
Answer E: Polydactyly is a digital patterning anomaly and is not associated with CVT.
