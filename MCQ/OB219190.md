## Question

A 32-year-old female presents after being involved in a high-speed motor vehicle collision. Due to difficult extraction, the patient arrived approximately 2 hours after the traumatic event. It is noted on physical exam that the right thigh has a large 8cm medial open wound with no palpable distal pulses. She is found to have a right femoral shaft fracture with a complete transection of her right superficial femoral artery (SFA) and a left distal femur fracture (Figure A). Additional injuries include a liver laceration and traumatic subdural hematoma. Over the next 4 hours, neurosurgery performs an emergent craniotomy followed immediately by exploratory laparotomy performed by general surgery. Orthopedic surgery is then called to the operating room to complete their necessary procedures. At that time, that patient's lactate remains elevated and has continued transfusion requirements. Both neurosurgery and general surgery note her overall guarded prognosis. Which of the following serves as the most appropriate intervention for her orthopedic injuries at that time?

## Options

1. Right femur intramedullary nailing, left knee spanning external fixation, no other interventions
2. Right femur intramedullary nailing with superficial femoral artery shunt placement, left knee spanning external fixation
3. Right femur intramedullary nailing with femoral-popliteal bypass, left knee spanning external fixation
4. Right femur external fixation with superficial femoral artery shunt placement, left knee spanning external fixation
5. Right femur external fixation with femoral-popliteal bypass, left knee spanning external fixation

## Correct option

**4. Right femur external fixation with superficial femoral artery shunt placement, left knee spanning external fixation**

## Answer explanation

Given this patient’s concomitant injuries, lack of resuscitation, and overall guarded prognosis, she would best be served with external fixation to bilateral lower extremities and temporizing vascular shunt placement. Once more adequately stabilized in the ensuing 24-48 hours, shunt conversion to primary repair or bypass reconstruction would then be more appropriate when the patient can better physiologically tolerate the added operative time.

Limb-threatening vascular injuries necessitate expeditious revascularization procedures to minimize warm ischemia time (ideally <8 hours from injury). Thereby, anastomosis of the injured vessel serves to maximize the viability and functional outcomes of limb salvage surgery. Time is tissue. The most common indications for temporary extremity shunts include:
(1) damage control in patients who cannot safely undergo early definitive vascular repair or bypass reconstruction due to concomitant life-threatening injuries (as in this patient)
(2) temporarily restoring blood flow until bony stabilization and/or donor bypass graft harvesting is performed; shunt conversion to definitive vascular treatment is then performed all within the same operative case
(3) as a temporizing measure in remote settings to minimize warm ischemia time until transfer to higher levels of care.

The most commonly utilized device is a simple argyle shunt placed “in-line” (i.e. no excess tubing to avoid coiling and kinking of the shunt lumen; Illustration A). Thrombosis and early shunt failure can be avoided by utilizing an appropriate-sized shunt (avoid under-sizing), using adjunctive local heparinization, and ensuring there is adequate venous outflow. Ultimately, the same principles for any revascularization procedure hold true in the setting of temporizing shunt placement as well. In most cases, fasciotomies should also be performed during the same operative case as shunt placement to avoid reperfusion compartment syndrome.

*Spitler and colleagues provided a comprehensive review article regarding the assessment and interventions available for vascular injuries associated with fractures.* **The authors note** that the order of operative events (i.e. fixation before or after vascular intervention) is commonly debated and is lacking a clinically proven gold standard algorithm. They further acknowledge that while definitive vascular repair or reconstruction at the indexed surgery is preferred, there are many situations in which temporary shunting is prudent with proximal shunts (SFA, brachial artery) have better long-term patency rates than distal shunts (posterior tibial artery, ulnar artery). **They conclude** that early involvement of the patient and their family members is paramount in pursuing limb salvage versus early amputation.

*Subramanian and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institution’s outcomes utilizing temporizing intravascular shunts (TIVS) for traumatic injury limb salvage.* *Among the 60 patients who met the inclusion criteria,* **the authors reported** a shunt thrombosis rate of 5%, amputation rate of 18%, overall survival of 88%, and combination limb/patient survival of 73%. **The authors conclude** supporting the liberal use of TIVS in trauma patients noting their ease of use, ready availability at most centers, and excellent patency rates.

*Inaba and colleagues from the Multicenter Shunt Study Group performed a contemporary multicenter review of TIVS and outcomes in extremity-based trauma.* Among the 213 injuries treated with TIVA, **the authors reported** a 5.6% shunt thrombosis rate, 1.4% shunt dislodgement rate, 81.6% survival rate to definitive repair, a successful limb salvage rate of 96.3% in those who survived with no deaths attributable to shunt complications. **The authors concluded** that TIVS has an acceptable complication burden and no associated mortality attribution, thus considering them a viable treatment option.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1/2/3: While early appropriate care (EAC) supports definitive fixation in adequately resuscitated trauma patients, this patient is noted to have inadequate resuscitation based on elevated lactate levels. Temporizing external fixation of both femur injuries would avoid the added physiologic stress and risk of subsequent pulmonary complications associated with the definitive fixation of inadequately resuscitated patients.
Answer 5: While definitive vascular repair/reconstruction is ideal at the indexed surgery, this patient will have already undergone multiple procedures for life-threatening injuries just prior to orthopedic intervention. A temporizing shunt avoids the added operative time and physiologic demand for definitive bypass procedures, especially if it requires vein autograft harvesting.
