## Question

A 61-year-old male with a lifelong history of type 1 diabetes presents with left foot swelling and erythema that began approximately 4 weeks ago and has progressively worsened. He denies any pain but does have advanced peripheral neuropathy. He denies any associated fevers or chills. On exam, there is diffuse swelling and erythema present without ulcers present. Upon elevating the extremity for two minutes, the erythema resolves. What is the next best treatment option for this patient?

## Options

1. MRI of the left foot and ankle
2. IV antibiotics
3. Total contact cast
4. Midfoot fusion
5. Ring fixator

## Correct option

**3. Total contact cast**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with Charcot arthropathy of the left foot which is best treated initially with total contact casting.

Charcot arthropathy is a structural degradation of the foot in the absence of pain or lack of appropriate pain and is usually associated with increased bone resportion and joint destruction. The most common cause is end organ damage from long-standing, poorly controlled diabetes in the presence of peripheral neuropathy. Initially treatment consists of total contact casting and nonweightbearing for 2-4 months, which can be effective in 75% of cases. Refractory cases may require ostectomy, deformity correction, with or without arthrodesis. The goal of treatment is to achieve a stable, plantigrade foot that can fit within shoewear.

Sämann et al. performed a retrospective study of 164 patients with Charcot arthropathy of the foot. They reported a 50-56% complication rate with a higher recurrence of secondary episodes in patients with type 1 diabetes (41.6% vs 18.3%). They concluded diabetic neuropathy is a risk factor for Charcot foot arthropathy complications.

*Myerson and Edwards reviewed the management of foot and ankle fractures in the presence of peripheral neuropathy.* **The authors reported** neuroarthropathy of the foot and ankle progresses through three phases described by Eichenholtz and often leads to severe deformity complicated by ulcers. The goal of treatment is to achieve a plantigrade, stable, and functional foot that fit appropriately in shoewear.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs of the left foot with lysis of the bones of the midfoot and joint subluxation consistent with Charcot arthropathy.

Incorrect answers
Answer 1: An MRI of the affected extremity will likely demonstrate increased T2 signal changes that may be difficult to differentiate from osteomyelitis. However, given the history and physical exam findings suggesting Charcot arthropathy, an MRI of the foot and ankle would provide little treatment utility.
Answer 2: The patient is unlikely to have a severe infection in the absence of diabetic ulcers and physical exam findings suggestive of Charcot arthropathy. IV antibiotics would not be beneficial.
Answers 4 and 5: Arthrodesis and external fixation are possible treatment options in severe recalcitrant Charcot arthropathy and severe foot deformity causing recurrent ulcers or inability to fit in shoewear.
