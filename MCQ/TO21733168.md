## Question

A gymnast lands up with L5-S1 Grade 3 listhesis. He has sudden onset acute pain but no deficit. L5-S1 region is tender to palpation. Best management plan for the case would be

## Options

1. Hyperextension brace
2. Antilordotic brace
3. Fusion and instrumentation
4. Observation

## Correct option

**4. Observation**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

EXPLANATION: -

The diagnosis of this clinical vignette is Type IIC isthmic Grade 3 spondylolisthesis. Now, the answer is very controversial. A young active person (gymnast) with Grade 3 listhesis is a surgical (spinal fusion) indication but there is no deficit mentioned and no chronicity mentioned. Considering that an observation initially is warranted to tide over acute back pain episode and then electively plan surgical management. Read details regarding Spondylolisthesis below.

SPONDYLOLISTHESIS

Spondylolisthesis is a condition characterized by the subluxation of one vertebral body over the adjacent inferior vertebral body.

Spondylolisthesis can be classified into the degree of the subluxation according to Meyerding’s classification (grade I: <25%; grade II: 25-50%; grade III: 50-75%; grade IV: 75-100%; grade V: >100%). grades I and II are low-grade spondylolisthesis, grades III and IV are high-grade spondylolisthesis. These are more likely to progress and frequently are caused by anatomic deficiencies of the posterior elements and may eventually progress to spondyloptosis (grade V). Less commonly employed for the purpose is the Taillard method, whereby, the sagittal translation is classified in the percentage of the slip of the superior vertebra relative to the superior aspect of the inferior vertebra.

MEYERDING CLASSIFICATION

The Wiltse classification is based on etiology and anatomical factors and spondylolisthesis may be divided into I - dysplastic; II - isthmic; III - degenerative; IV - traumatic; V - pathological; and VI - iatrogenic categories. Type ii and iii are the most clinically relevant types.

![[TO21733168-2.jpg]]

Type ii - isthmic - Isthmic spondylolisthesis (IS) corresponds to an injury of the pars interarticularis (PI) which can occur from stress fractures (type IIA, lytic), elongation (type IIB), or acute fractures (type IIC). The result is a progressive anterior slippage, most commonly at the L5-S1 vertebral level.

Subtype A - lytic - this group represents a fatigue fracture through the pars, common in adults under the age of 50.
Repetitive stress through the pars can over time lead to sclerosis, fracture, and nonunion.

Subtype B - pars elongation - similar to subtype a, this represents a stress reaction at the pars repeated microfractures that heal and elongate over time. As the pars lengthens, the vertebral elements are translated forward in step with the elongation. Commonly seen in adolescents and young adults involved in repetitive extension activities such as gymnastics and football linemen.

Subtype C - acute fracture –more severe trauma that leads to an acute fracture of the pars (as opposed to repetitive stress).

Type iii - degenerative - most common form of spondylolisthesis. Progressive degenerative changes of the facet joint complex and intervertebral disk leads to excessive intervertebral motion which ultimately results in anterior translation.

DIAGNOSIS

Radiographs are able to show anterolisthesis of one vertebral body, without a pars interarticularis defect. A few of the findings that will be seen on an X-ray are disc space narrowing, endplate lesions, osteophytes and facet joint sclerosis. Sagittally-oriented facet joints are also associated with the presence of listhesis, though primarily with anterior translation. Malalignment of the spinal processes on lateral radiographs can be seen due to anterior translation of the entire vertebra since the neural arch is intact as opposed to in isthmic spondylolysis.
On the oblique view of the lumbar spine, the outline of a dog can be seen. The parts of the dog are as follows: the transverse process-the nose; the pedicle-the eye; the pars interarticularis-the neck; the superior articular facet-the ear; the inferior articular facet-the front leg. A break in the neck of the dog, or a dog collar, corresponds to a fracture in the region of the pars interarticularis, which is specific for spondylolysis.

![[TO21733168-3.png]]

MRI is the most helpful method to confirm the canal or foraminal stenosis that accompanies a case of listhesis. Other MRI findings such as facet joint hypertrophy and effusion, synovial cysts, high-grade osteoarthritis and thickened ligamentum flavum are also seen. A “positive sedimentation sign,” describes the nerve roots that are “floating” within the dural sac as opposed to being packed together within the posterior aspect of the dura. This sign, the absence of nerve root sedimentation, has been reported to be associated with symptoms of lumbar spinal stenosis.

TREATMENT

In general, spondylolisthesis itself does not need treatment because the natural history of grade 1 degenerative slips is that they do not progress. Patients with grade I or II slips who are asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic generally have a lower chance of progression and are candidates for nonoperative management or may not need treatment. Nonoperative treatment is successful in most patients, particularly those with mild symptoms. Specific nonoperative measures may vary depending on etiology and may include activity modification. For example, young athletes with isthmic spondylolisthesis who participate in hyperextension athletics such as gymnastics can be treated with nonsteroidal medications, bracing, physical therapy, and selective nerve or pars injections. Typically, physical restrictions may be relaxed as symptoms improve. Most patients experience symptom resolution

Surgical intervention is reserved for those patients whose symptoms are refractory to these measures, or patients with more severe neurologic involvement. Suppose patient has not responded to this nonoperative management after 6 months of care, or is having a meyerding grade of III or greater (high-grade slip), progression of slippage greater than one grade, or a high degree of instability on flexion-extension radiographs, he/she may be considered for surgery (fusion and instrumentation). To say “high instability”, a difference
in a vertebral slip of ≤ 3 mm between flexion and extension has been seen in a majority of asymptomatic subjects, so a cut-off value of > 3 mm has therefore been accepted by many studies as a sign of abnormal sagittal translation.

Surgical treatment of spondylolisthesis usually involves
a combination of decompression, stabilization, and fusion. Stabilization with spinal instrumentation is often utilized to correct deformity and prevent deformity progression. There currently remains a lack of consensus on the decision to reduce slippage versus in-situ fusion during surgical management.
With recent recognition of the importance of slip angle and spinopelvic alignment to global sagittal alignment, more evidence suggests that at least partial reduction of slip angle should be considered in the setting of a high-grade slip. Interbody fusion is also favored in these cases to provide greater stability and increase fusion rates. Advent of transpedicular screwing and the development of spinal instrumentation has led to breakthroughs of transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) and other interbody fusion
techniques. More recent advances include minimally invasive surgery (MIS) and stereotactic spinal guidance. MIS procedures have been shown to decrease muscular injury and
perioperative pain, leading to faster recovery and improved quality of life. Similarly, stereotactic spinal guidance provides invaluable guidance of intraoperative anatomical landmarks and has been correlated with decreased likelihood of complications related to screw misplacement.
