## Question

You see a 56-year-old recreational weightlifter in your office who complains of pain and weakness about his elbow. During a recent lift, he felt a pop in his arm and then noticed weakness with supination and elbow flexion. There is a positive hook test on examination. Given his likely diagnosis, which of the following is true regarding management of his injury?

## Options

1. Nonoperative management results in greater loss of flexion than supination strength
2. Single incision technique utilizes interval between brachioradialis and pronator teres
3. Single incision technique is performed with pronation of forearm to prevent PIN injury
4. Double incision technique results in highest incidence of PIN injury
5. Double incision technique should use interval between anconeus and ulna

## Correct option

**2. Single incision technique utilizes interval between brachioradialis and pronator teres**

## Answer explanation

The single incision technique for distal biceps tendon repairs is performed using the interval between the brachioradialis (BR) and the pronator teres (PT), which, when split, should lead directly to the radial tuberosity.

Distal biceps ruptures present primarily in middle-aged men who are lifting something heavy. The biceps fails with excessive eccentric tension and will rupture off of the biceps tuberosity on the radius. Surgical management to reattach the biceps is usually recommended as nonoperative management leads to loss of supination and, to a lesser degree, flexion strength. Both single- and double-incision approaches are utilized, with different risks. The single-incision technique is performed between the BR and PT and risks nerve injury (lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN) > posterior interosseous nerve (PIN)). The double incision technique has a lower risk of PIN injury, but traditionally has been described as having higher rates of heterotopic ossification, presumably due to periosteal stripping of the ulna. While both approaches are acceptable, specific risks must be taken into consideration and discussed with patients before proceeding with surgery.

*Chavan et al. performed a systematic review of distal biceps repairs, looking at outcomes between different approaches and methods of fixation.* **They reported** that button fixation was strongest biomechanically, and that there was no significant difference between overall complication rates between single- and double-incision techniques. However, there were higher rates of unsatisfactory clinical outcomes and loss of forearm motion in the double-incision group.

*McKee et al. reported on patient outcomes in 53 patients after single-incision distal biceps repairs.* *There were no re-ruptures, 3 transient neuropraxias (2 LABCN, 1 PIN), and DASH scores were similar to population controls at final follow-up.* **The authors concluded** that the single-incision technique was safe and effective in treating distal biceps ruptures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Nonoperative management of distal biceps ruptures results in loss of supination > flexion strength.
Answer 3: The anterior single incision technique is performed with the forearm supinated to prevent injury to the PIN.
Answer 4: The single incision technique, not double, has a higher rate of injury to the PIN.
Answer 5: The double incision technique should use an approach between the ECU and EDC to avoid periosteal stripping and subsequent heterotopic ossification that occurs when instruments are passed adjacent to the ulna.
