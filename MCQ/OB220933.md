## Question

A four-week-old infant presents after the pediatrician noted a mild hip “click.” The patient is a firstborn child and was in breech position. On examination, both hips are stable with negative Barlow and Ortolani tests. Dynamic hip ultrasonography, as shown in Video 1, demonstrates 48% femoral head coverage and an alpha angle of 56°, with no instability during stress maneuvers. What is the most appropriate management at this time?

## Options

1. Start part-time nighttime abduction bracing
2. Initiate Pavlik harness immediately
3. Hip arthrogram with closed reduction and spica casting
4. Order an MRI of the hips to further characterize acetabular morphology
5. Expectant management with repeat ultrasound at 10–12 weeks of age

## Correct option

**5. Expectant management with repeat ultrasound at 10–12 weeks of age**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a stable Graf type IIa (physiologically immature) hip, as ultrasound findings include 48% femoral head coverage and an alpha angle of 56°. Given that the infant is only 4 weeks old, reassurance with repeat examination and ultrasonography at 10-12 weeks is appropriate (Answer 5).

Physiologic immaturity of the hip refers to transient hip laxity or mild dysplasia seen in many newborns, which typically resolves spontaneously without intervention. Studies indicate that 60–80% of clinically abnormal hips and over 90% of ultrasonographically detected immature hips normalize within the first few months of life. Therefore, current guidelines emphasize careful observation and follow-up for physiologically immature hips, reserving treatment for those with persistent or progressive abnormalities beyond 12 weeks of life.

*Paulussen et al. performed a systematic review of infants up to 4 months old with stable developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), comparing 307 hips managed with observation to 237 treated with abduction devices.* Treated hips demonstrated faster short-term improvement in Graf alpha angle and femoral head coverage, but 12% of observed hips ultimately required conversion to abduction therapy. Notably, there were no differences in acetabular index at 3 months between immediate treatment and active monitoring, **suggesting** that while most stable hips improve spontaneously, a subset do not.

*Bakti et al. retrospectively analyzed 948 infants with DDH to identify factors associated with the rate of sonographic improvement during the first 6 months of life.* **They found** that female sex and positive family history were independent predictors of slower normalization of alpha angles, while breech infants tended to normalize faster—likely because they were treated more aggressively and earlier. Treatment strategy mattered, as immediate abduction treatment and watchful waiting for stable, mild DDH showed faster early improvement, whereas delayed initiation of abduction resulted in a slower recovery curve. By 6 months, however, most differences between groups had leveled off, and nearly all hips had normalized, regardless of initial risk factors or treatment pathway.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Nighttime abduction bracing is unnecessary overtreatment for a stable, physiologically immature (Graf IIa) hip that is expected to normalize spontaneously.
Answer 2: A Pavlik harness is reserved for unstable or frankly dysplastic hips, not for a stable hip with mild physiologic immaturity on ultrasound.
Answer 3: Invasive reduction and casting are indicated only for dislocated or irreducible hips, not a clinically stable hip with near-normal ultrasound parameters.
Answer 4: MRI provides no additional useful information in a 4-week-old with a normal exam and physiologically immature ultrasound findings, and is not part of standard DDH evaluation.
