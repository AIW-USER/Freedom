## Question

A 72-year-old male with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and peripheral artery disease presents with six months of gradually worsening lower leg pain. Physical exam demonstrates erythema, swelling, and tenderness to palpation over the anteromedial distal tibia. Serum analysis reveals elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein. An MRI scan is performed and demonstrates an area of abnormal confluent marrow signal throughout the distal tibia. ## Answer explanation

In contrast to acute osteomyelitis, which histologically demonstrates a predominantly neutrophilic response (e.g., microabscesses), chronic osteomyelitis characteristically demonstrates marrow fibrosis with a predominance of chronic inflammatory cells, particularly plasma cells (answer choice 5).

Osteomyelitis is an infection of bone characterized by progressive inflammatory destruction and apposition of new bone. Diagnosis relies on imaging (radiographs/MRI) and organism identification via biopsy and cultures, and treatment typically combines culture-directed antibiotics with surgical debridement of nonviable tissue. Histologically, acute disease shows numerous neutrophils and typically viable osteocytes (although bone necrosis may occur), while chronic disease shows marrow fibrosis, anuclear osteocyte lacunae, and chronic inflammatory cells, particularly plasma cells.

*Sybenga et al. reviewed histopathologic categorization of osteomyelitis (OM) and proposed criteria that help categorize the disease process into acute OM, acute and chronic OM, chronic OM, chronic active OM, and chronic inactive OM.* **They reported** that acute OM is characterized primarily by the presence of aggregates of neutrophils or individual neutrophils actively destroying bone. For chronic phases, **they reported** features such as marrow fibrosis, sequestrum/involucrum, and plasmacytic inflammation as hallmarks of disease.

*Jani and Remagen presented a series of patients with plasma-cell–predominant osteomyelitis and* **argued** that these cases represent “primary chronic osteomyelitis.” They observed consistently increased plasma cells with transitional appearances overlapping with sclerosing osteomyelitis and Brodie’s abscess on histology. **They concluded** that the term “primary chronic osteomyelitis” distinguishes this chronic pattern from acute and secondary forms and from other bone disorders.

Figures:
A) Eosinophil: Mononuclear cell with a single horseshoe-shaped nucleus with 2-3 lobes and strongly eosinophilic cytoplasm.
B) Monocyte: Large mononucleated cell with a large bilobed, kidney-shaped nucleus.
C) Neutrophil: Large cells with lobulated/segmented nuclei with 2-5 lobes.
D) Osteocyte: Mononuclear cell located within a lacuna surrounded by bone matrix.
E) Plasma Cell: Ovoid cell with eccentric nucleus with coarse "clock face" chromatin.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Eosinophils play key roles in allergic or anti-parasitic immune responses.
Answer choice 2: Monocytes (which mature to macrophages) are present in both acute and chronic inflammatory processes.
Answer choice 3: Neutrophils are the hallmark immune cell for acute osteomyelitis.
Answer choice 4: Although empty lacunae can be seen from bone necrosis in acute osteomyelitis, this finding is ultimately more characteristic of chronic osteomyelitis. Thus, an abundance of osteocytes on histology would be more commonly seen in acute osteomyelitis versus chronic osteomyelitis.
