## Question

He spends around 10 hours each day packing heavy rolls of laminate film into boxes. He has exhausted all conservative management options to this point and is now inquiring about surgical management for relief of his symptoms. On exam, he has pain with flexion and extension of the right wrist with 35 degrees and 45 degrees of motion in these directions, respectively. His preoperative PA radiograph is shown in Figure A. ## Answer explanation

Based upon the preoperative wrist radiograph demonstrating isolated radioscaphoid and radiolunate arthritis and the patient's clinical history, the procedure indicated for this patient is a radioscapholunate (RSL) fusion (answer choice 2) to help preserve wrist motion.

Symptomatic radioscaphoid and radiolunate arthritis is an example of nondissociative carpal instability (CIND), which is defined by either radiocarpal or midcarpal instability. Radiocarpal instability occurs between the radius and proximal row, whereas midcarpal instability occurs between the proximal and distal rows. With CIND, associated conditions may include intracarpal injury (scapholunate or lunotriquetral ligament injuries), acute carpal tunnel syndrome, or compartment syndrome. This patient has radiocarpal instability with symptomatic radioscaphoid and radiolunate arthritis with a functional midcarpal joint. Therefore, the best treatment to preserve wrist motion and prevent continued symptomatic radiolunate arthritis is to fuse the radioscaphoid and radiolunate articulations and remove the distal pole of the scaphoid to improve wrist motion further.

*Biswas et al. wrote a review article analyzing the RSL fusion technique with headless compression screws and local autograft through a dorsal approach.* **They noted** improvements in technique and clearly defined indications have helped to reduce the nonunion rates and increase patient satisfaction. These indications include isolated radiocarpal arthritis with a functional midcarpal joint. Their results included radiographic evidence of arthrodesis in all 9 wrists at one year without evidence of midcarpal arthrosis at the final follow-up.

*Ha et al. conducted a study analyzing the long-term outcomes of RSL fusion with and without distal scaphoid and triquetrum excision.* *Three groups were utilized, and 17 total patients were included with a minimum 10-year follow-up.* *Patients with triquetrum excision had a mean 10-degree increase (without statistical significance) in their radial-ulnar arc compared to the other two groups.* **They noted** that clear and strict indications are important, and those patients who achieved good results at 2 years were likely to sustain good long-term outcomes.

*Bain et al. provided insight into decision-making for various techniques in partial carpal fusion procedures.* **They note** that the decision on which procedure is appropriate is based on the etiology of arthritis, which joints are involved, and which joints are spared. The main idea of these procedures is to fuse the involved joints and preserve motion in the spared joints. Common indications include SLAC and SNAC wrist conditions. **They conclude** that if the principles of wrist fusion are adhered to, patients can have a more successful outcome.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: A 4-corner arthrodesis is not indicated in this patient with radiocarpal arthritis as symptomatic wrist motion will remain postoperatively.
Answer choice 3: Capitolunate fusion is not indicated in this patient with a functional midcarpal joint and absent arthrosis of the capitolunate articulation.
Answer choice 4: Total wrist arthroplasty is not indicated in a young manual laborer due to the high rate of failure.
Answer choice 5: A dorsal wrist-spanning plate provides total fusion of the wrist and is reserved as a salvage option if the other procedures listed above fail.
