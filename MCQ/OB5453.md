## Question

All of the following are relative contraindications to performing a transfer of the latissimus dorsi and teres major to the rotator cuff for the treatment of persistent brachial plexus birth palsy in patients with abduction and external rotation weakness EXCEPT:

## Options

1. Humeral head flattening
2. Glenohumeral internal rotation contracture
3. Complete posterior glenohumeral dislocation
4. A convex glenoid
5. An absent glenoid

## Correct option

**2. Glenohumeral internal rotation contracture**

## Answer explanation

Glenohumeral internal rotation contracture is an indication for latissimus transfer rather than a contraindication. Tendon transfer can induce glenohumeral remodeling in the growing child in the context of mild to moderate dysplasia. Patients with advanced glenohumeral dysplasia (convex or absent glenoid, complete dislocation, humeral head flattening) may not be able to remodel sufficiently.

Many brachial plexus birth palsies improve or resolve within 2 years. Patients with persistent palsies develop internal rotation contracture and glenohumeral dysplasia. Younger children with mild to moderate glenohumeral dysplasia have significant remodeling potential, and their glenohumeral morphology can be improved by soft tissue procedures that address their neurologic deficits. Older children with severe glenohumeral dysplasia are more likely to need humeral derotational osteotomy, which improves function, but does alter the natural history of glenohumeral dysplasia.

*Pearl reviewed the evaluation and treatment of the shoulder in children with brachial plexus birth injuries.* **He notes** that restoration of normal glenohumeral anatomy is possible with a variety of methods provided that external rotation is restored and maintained. Balancing external and internal rotation force couples may be challenging, and if external rotation wins out, weakness of internal rotation or decreased internal rotation range of motion will result.

*Waters et al. performed latissimus dorsi and teres major transfer to the rotator cuff in 23 patients who had glenohumeral deformity.* *Nineteen (83%) of the 23 patients demonstrated favorable glenohumeral remodeling at a mean radiographic follow up of 25 months.* **The authors also noted** that patients with severe glenohumeral deformity (humeral head flattening, glenoid absence, complete posterior dislocation) are likely best treated with derotational humeral osteotomy.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, 4 and 5 are all findings associated with a more severe degree of glenohumeral dysplasia. The more severe the dysplasia, the less likely that remodeling will occur after a soft tissue procedure.
