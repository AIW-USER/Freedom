## Question

A 43-year-old male presents with 8 months of left shoulder pain. Examination reveals a positive Hawkins and a positive Neer's sign. He has 5/5 abduction and flexion strength. He has no pain with resisted external rotation. Which of the following is true with respect to partial-thickness rotator cuff tears?

## Options

1. Articular-sided tears are less common than bursal-sided tears
2. Bursal-sided tears are at higher risk of progression than articular-sided tears
3. Tendon repair is recommended when bursal-sided tears are < 25% of the tendon depth
4. Tendon repair is recommended for articular-sided tears > 25 % of the tendon depth
5. Bursal-sided tears are generally less symptomatic than articular-sided tears

## Correct option

**2. Bursal-sided tears are at higher risk of progression than articular-sided tears**

## Answer explanation

Bursal-sided rotator cuff tears (RCT) are at a significantly higher risk of progression than articular-sided tears.

The average thickness (tendon depth) of a normal rotator cuff tendon is 10 to 12 mm, with the footprint having dimensions of ~13 mm (lateral to medial) by ~20 mm (anteroposterior). Partial-thickness RCTs are generally classified as bursal-sided (confined to the bursal surface of the tendon), articular sided (present on the side of the tendon adjacent to the joint) and intratendinous (found within the tendon). The etiology of partial-thickness RCTs can be broadly differentiated into intrinsic, extrinsic, or traumatic causes. Articular-sided tears are more common and tend to develop from intrinsic causes leading to degenerative weakening of the tendon whereas bursal sided tears may be due to extrinsic causes (acromial spurs, AC joint arthritis). Furthermore, the bursal-side of the tendon is composed mostly of tendon bundles, whereas the articular side is a complex of tendon, ligament, and capsule. As such, the bursal side of the tendon is able to undergo greater deformation and has a greater tensile strength than the articular side.

*Matava et al. performed a review of partial-thickness RCTs.* **The authors note** that research into the causes and history of partial tears lags behind that of full-thickness tears and as such, treatment algorithms are not as refined. **The authors note** that that partial-thickness RCTs should be aggressively treated in the active athlete because of the unfavorable natural history of these lesions and the success of accepted surgical algorithms.

*Fukuda performed a review of the classification, etiology, incidence, clinical presentation, and management of partial-thickness RCTs.* *The author cites a prevalence of 7% full-thickness and 13% partial-thickness tears (of which 18% were bursal-sided, 27% articular-sided, and 55% intratendinous) in a previously performed cadaver study.* Regarding treatment, **he notes** that the timing of surgical intervention after failed conservative treatment ranges from a few months to 1.5 years. **He concludes** that while most clinicians agree that tears involving more than 50% of the thickness of the tendon should be repaired, there is no consensus of options for treatment based on the surface area of the lesion

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Articular-sided tears are far more common than bursal-sided tears because the articular-sided portion of the tendon is much weaker than the bursal-side.
Answer 3: Tendon repair is recommended when bursal-sided tears are > 25% of the tendon depth
Answer 4: Tendon repair is recommended for articular-sided tears are > 50% of the tendon depth
Answer 5: Bursal-sided tears are generally more symptomatic than articular sided tears due to the inflammatory response that leads to subacromial bursitis.
