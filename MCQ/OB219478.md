## Question

A 17-year-old female high school soccer player presents to your office after her second patellar dislocation. She had previously been managed nonoperatively with physical therapy and bracing. On exam, she has 3 quadrants of lateral patellar translation, a J-sign, and tightness of the lateral patellar retinaculum. TT-TG is 13 mm and Caton-Deschamps index = 1.15. You recommend surgical intervention at this point. Which procedure should be performed to give this patient the best clinical outcome and lowest incidence of post-operative instability?

## Options

1. Isolated lateral release
2. Isolated lateral retinacular lengthening
3. MPFL reconstruction + lateral release
4. MPFL reconstruction + lateral retinacular lengthening
5. Anteromedializing tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) + MPFL reconstruction + lateral retinacular lengthening

## Correct option

**4. MPFL reconstruction + lateral retinacular lengthening**

## Answer explanation

Reconstruction of the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) and a lateral retinacular lengthening is recommended in this patient to achieve the best clinical outcome and lowest risk for postoperative instability.

Patellar instability is a common issue which is seen more commonly in younger patients and those anatomically predisposed. Anatomic risk factors include trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, lateral patellar tilt, genu valgum, and femoral anteversion. When there is a traumatic patellar dislocation and no obvious anatomic factors contributing, typically there is an injury to the MPFL, which is the primary stabilizer to lateral translation from about 0-30° of flexion. Reconstruction of the MPFL can be considered if the patient fails nonoperative management with physical therapy and bracing. Additionally, if there is tightness of the lateral retinaculum, as evidenced by difficulty elevating the patella or lack of medial patellar glide, a lateral retinacular release or lengthening can be done. There is evidence to support that a lateral retinacular lengthening may be beneficial for patients with regards to clinical outcomes and a lower risk for iatrogenic medial patellar instability in the future.

*Pagenstert et al. looked at lateral patellar retinacular lengthening vs. open release in patients with lateral patellar compression syndrome.* There was an increased risk of developing medial patellar subluxation after lateral release compared to lateral lengthening (5/14 vs. 0/14). Quadriceps atrophy was also found to be more severe in the lateral release group and Kujala outcome scores were also found to be lower. **They postulated** that these differences may be attributable to preservation of the lateral patellar muscle, capsule, and ligamentous complex after lengthening compared to release.

*Dandu et al. reviewed lateral releases and the appropriate clinical settings for their use.* **They recommend** thorough examination to evaluate for anatomic factors that may predispose patients to instability. Additionally, they consider lateral retinacular lengthening to be recommended over release due to the potential for iatrogenic medial instability in the setting of release.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: An isolated lateral release is not recommended to treat lateral patellar instability as it can lead to further instability and does not address the root cause. The main indication for an isolated lateral release is lateral patellar facet compression syndrome even in these cases, a lateral lengthening may be recommended.
Answer 2: Similar to an isolated lateral release, a lateral lengthening does not address the root cause of patellar instability which is laxity of the MPFL and in some cases, bony predisposition.
Answer 3: Compared to lateral release, lateral lengthening has shown improved clinical outcomes and a lower rate of medial patellar instability post-operatively.
Answer 5: This patient does not require a tibial tubercle osteotomy as their TT-TG ratio is only 13 mm. Generally this is not performed when the TT-TG is less than 15-20.
