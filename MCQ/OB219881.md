## Question

A 76-year-old female with right hip osteoarthritis presents to the adult reconstruction clinic for consideration of total hip arthroplasty. The patient is non-diabetic, has no history of tobacco use, and reports the only medication she takes is alendronate. Her surgeon elects to perform cemented total hip arthroplasty. This patient is likely to experience which of the following when compared to a similar patient treated with cementless femoral fixation?

## Options

1. Lower risk of early revision
2. Lower incidence of leg length discrepency
3. Higher risk of iatrogenic fracture
4. Increased risk of early aseptic loosening
5. Similar risk of early periprosthetic fracture

## Correct option

**1. Lower risk of early revision**

## Answer explanation

Elderly patients who undergo cemented total hip arthroplasty (THA) experience lower rates of iatrogenic and early fracture, revision surgery, and aseptic loosening compared to patients treated with cementless femoral components.

In THA, the choice between cemented and press-fit (cementless) femoral stems is influenced by patient age, bone quality, and surgeon preference. Cemented femoral components are often recommended for elderly patients with compromised bone quality, particularly in cases involving femoral neck fractures. Large registry studies have shown that cemented stems reduce fracture rates and lower revision risks than press-fit alternatives. Despite the solid track record of cemented femoral stems, the adoption of cementless hip arthroplasties has steadily increased, particularly in the United States. This gap between evidence and clinical practice is attributed to the convenience of using cementless implants, strong commercial promotion of these options, and limited exposure of U.S. trainees to modern cementing techniques, which may contribute to a preference for cementless systems.

*Tanzer et al. surveyed the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry to identify primary total hip arthroplasty performed with cemented and press-fit components in patients over 75 years old.* **The authors found** a significantly higher early revision rate (< 3 months) among patients treated with cementless stems. More specifically, **they report** that early revision was nine times more common in patients with press-fit stems. **The authors conclude** that contemporary cementing techniques should be integral to orthopedic training to mitigate the risk of revision in this population.

*Emara et al. review modern femoral stem cemented fixation's indications, preoperative precautions, and stepwise technical details.* While still controversial, the authors provide guidelines for selecting patients for cemented fixation. They encourage cemented fixation in elderly patients (>65), femur type C Dorr classification, narrow femoral canal, or severe osteoporosis. Despite correct patient selection, **the authors argue** cementation is only beneficial when meticulous technique is employed. Specifically, **the authors recommend** (1) pulsatile lavage of the femoral canal, (2) utilization of epinephrine-soaked swabs, (3) vacuum cement mixing, (4) retrograde cement introduction, (5) cement pressurization, and (6) the use of stem centralizers. Given the added risk of cardiopulmonary complications during cementing, **the authors recommend** identifying and optimizing the preoperative status of higher-risk patients.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Leg length discrepancy has not been shown to be significantly different between fixation techniques.
Answers 3-5: Registry data suggests that cemented stems experience lower rates of early revision, iatrogenic fracture, early aseptic loosening, and periprosthetic fracture.
