## Question

Mark the incorrect statement regarding Patellar clunk syndrome (PCS)

## Options

1. Patella baja and a low insall salvati ratio predispose to PCS
2. A smaller femoral component and a femoral component implanted in flexion increase incidence of PCS
3. A femoral component with a low Intercondylar box ratio will be a favorable choice to lessen the chances of landing up with PCS
4. Conservative treatment has no role and an arthroscopic synovectomy is must for all cases of PCS

## Correct option

**4. Conservative treatment has no role and an arthroscopic synovectomy is must for all cases of PCS**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

PATELLAR CLUNK SYNDROME

ETIOLOGY

The etiology of patellar clunk syndrome (PCS) tends to be multifactorial involving the prosthetic design (especially femoral component), surgical trauma and scarring, components position, alteration of the joint line, patellar height, patellar tendon length and patellar button-patellar bone composite thickness.

The preoperative risk factors increasing the incidence of PCS include patients with a history of previous knee surgery, patients with patella baja, decreased preoperative patellar tendon length.

Alteration (raising)) of the joint line during surgery leading to patella baja (due to thicker insert or excessive distal femoral resection) or a lower Insall-Salvati ratio, lower patellar component height due to patellar over resection, use of a smaller patellar component, a patellar component that is placed too low on the patella exposing undersurface of quadriceps tendon and anteriorization of the tibial tray are all associated with a higher incidence of patellar clunk syndrome.

Femoral component design is even more important to determine the likelihood of developing PCS post op. The condition is by and large seen with PS designs. There is a correlation between both the use of a smaller (undersized) femoral component as well as a flexed posture of the femoral component with the development of patellar crepitus. It is speculated that femoral component in flexed position increases the posterior condylar offset leading to a relative anterior shift of the intercondylar box, thus predisposing to quadriceps tendon irritation.

Femoral component with higher intercondylar box ratio (trochlear groove extended more proximal and anterior) is also a significant trouble maker.

PS femoral components designs that have a high transition zone from the trochlear groove to the intercondylar box, with an abrupt transition to the distal femoral articular geometry are often trouble makers. This transition zone is defined technically as the intercondylar box ratio (ICBR). ICBR refers to intercondylar box height versus the antero-rposterior height of the femoral component. Femoral components with a high intercondylar box ratio allow contact of the distal quadriceps tendon earlier in flexion than components with a lower ratio increasing the incidence.

So, to prevent PCS: use prosthetic designs with low ICBR, use side-specific femoral components to allocate 7° oblique orientation of the patellar groove, use designs that support posterior shift of the cam-post mechanism with consequent trochlear deepening, and finally lengthening of the trochlea to allow progressive and smoother transition with the distal femoral condyles

And it is imperative during surgery to ensure more exact size matching, avoid insertion of downsized femoral component in flexed position, avoid anterior placement of the tibial tray, avoid any contact of un-resurfaced patella with the femoral prosthetic component by placing the patellar button as proximal as possible, but without overhanging or extending it beyond the superior border of the bony patella, avoid over-resection of the patella, and avoid insertion of small-thin patellar button that induces a thinner patellar composite thickness.

Remember, normal patellae contact the trochlear groove around 20o of knee flexion, and as knee flexion increases the contact stresses increase and shift superiorly. Therefore, the patellar component should be placed as superior as possible to avoid unresurfaced bone contact with the femoral component, but not extend superiorly beyond the superior border of the patella which can result in quadriceps tendon irritation.

Excessive femoral component flexion is generally the outcome when you create a more posterior entry point for intramedullary distal femoral cutting. Due to the anterior bow of the femur, a posterior entry site leads the surgeon to cut the distal femur in excessive flexion. This can be prevented by placing the entry site for intramedullary distal femoral cutting guide jig more anteriorly.

DIAGNOSIS

It’s mostly clinical. USG may be useful and show suprapatellar fibrous tissue.

MANAGEMENT

Quadriceps exercises for a few months, surprisingly, may show improvement over time, for uncertain reasons, in 20 to 60% of patients; so not all patients need surgical management.

Failure of conservative management warrants an arthroscopic debridement of the nodule. An open excision is reserved for those patients in whom arthroscopic treatment fails or in whom it is predicted to perform component revision (for instance a patellar component revision) and/or soft tissue realignment of the extensor mechanism

Open resection through a limited lateral incision associated with patellar tilt in total knee arthroplasty is described. The procedure is done under local anesthesia.

Recurrences do arise after arthroscopic debridement, but very rarely after arthrotomy and patellar button revision.
