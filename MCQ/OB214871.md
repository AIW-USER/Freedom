## Question

A 55-year-old patient returns to your office with arthritic knee pain which has not responded to a medial-offloader brace, physical therapy, or NSAIDs. His radiograph is shown in Figure A. A unicompartmental knee arthroplasty is discussed. Which of the following situations would be a relative contraindication for this procedure?

![[q214871-a-an-ap-knee-x-ray-demonstrating-oa-following-an-acl.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Previous medial meniscal transplant and Grade Ia Lachman on exam
2. Previous ACL reconstruction with Grade Ia Lachman on exam
3. Previous medial meniscal transplant and ACL reconstruction with Grade Ia Lachman on exam
4. Outerbridge IV changes of the medial femoral condyle and and medial tibial plateau
5. Outerbridge IV changes of the medial femoral condyle and Grade IIb Lachman on exam

## Correct option

**5. Outerbridge IV changes of the medial femoral condyle and Grade IIb Lachman on exam**

## Answer explanation

Of the answer choices, the most accepted contraindication is arthritic change in more than a single compartment or ligamentous instability. In this case, arthritis in the medial with a nonfunctioning ACL as evidenced by a grade IIb Lachman test, best fits this definition (Answer 5).

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) has evolved significantly since introduction in the 1970's. Since there there have been multiple generations of implant designs. All of these designs rely on the theory that better knee kinematics will result from replacing only the diseased compartment and not disturbing the other compartments and surrounding tissues. While UKAs are often cited as having quicker recoveries and improved range of motion, these outcomes are dependent on patient selection and indications. Though indications have changed over time, the contraindications for UKA are relatively consistent. Contraindications include significant limb malalignment or flexion deformity, ACL-deficiency, arthritis in other compartments, and inflammatory arthritis. Though there are case series of ACL-deficient knees performing well with UKA, this is a relative contraindication.

Iorio and Healy reviewed treatment option for unicompartmental knee arthritis. They emphasized the need to evaluate the lower limb alignment and ligamentous stability of the knee in order to appropriately treat unicompartmental arthritis. They state that significant limb malalignment should be treated with a corrective osteotomy, most commonly a high-tibial osteotomy.

*Argenson et al. described a case series of 160 UKAs using cemented, metal-backed Miller-Galante UKA implant.* *At an average follow-up of 10 years,* **they showed** a survival rate of 94%, with progression of arthritis in other compartments being the leading cause for revision surgery. This highlights the long-term reliability of UKA for unicompartmental arthritis.

*Cossey and Spriggins reported a cohort study comparing component placement for 30 UKAs done either with or without computer navigation assistance.* *At an average 26 month follow-up, the navigation-assisted cohort showed improved implant placement and alignment compared to the free-hand cohort.* A key downfall of this study is the absence of clinical correlation to these findings.

Figure A is an AP knee radiograph with end-stage medial compartment arthritis and bone tunnels from a previous ACL reconstruction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3- Previous medial meniscal transplant is not a contraindication for UKA.
Answer 2- Grade 1a testing would signify a functioning ACL graft.
Answer 4- These findings alone would be an indication for a UKA.
