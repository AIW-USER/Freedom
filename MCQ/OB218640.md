## Question

A 20-year-old college lacrosse player presents to your clinic with deep-seated hip pain. Radiographs are seen in Figure A. He has increased pain with terminal internal rotation of the hip. He has been taking NSAIDs and has been doing physical therapy for the last 12 weeks without much improvement. An MRI is obtained and shows a labral tear. What is the next most appropriate step?

![[q218640-a-cam-deformity.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Continued physical therapy with focus on core strengthening and stretching of hip flexors
2. Surgical hip dislocation with labral repair and femoral osteoplasty
3. Arthroscopic labral repair and femoral osteoplasty
4. Arthroscopic labral debridement and femoral osteoplasty
5. Arthroscopic labral repair and periacetabular osteotomy

## Correct option

**3. Arthroscopic labral repair and femoral osteoplasty**

## Answer explanation

This young athlete has failed conservative management of femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) with a cam deformity and is therefore indicated for arthroscopic labral repair and femoral osteoplasty (Answer 3).

FAI is characterized by abnormal contact between the proximal femur and the anterolateral aspect of the acetabulum. Patients typically relate symptoms of deep hip pain, especially with flexion and internal rotation. Radiographs may show a cam deformity of the proximal femur, which can be quantified using the alpha angle (Illustration A). MRI often identifies a concomitant labral tear that may need to be addressed. Physical therapy and activity modification can improve symptoms in many cases, but if these efforts fail, arthroscopic labral repair and femoral osteoplasty can be employed. Arthroscopic management is recommended compared to open repair and osteoplasty as this is less traumatic to the soft tissues, has less risk of avascular necrosis, and has demonstrated a quicker return to activity. Labral repair is preferred to debridement as outcomes have been shown to be improved in the young, athletic population.

*Pennock et al. prospectively reviewed nonoperative management of FAI in 93 hips at 12 and 24 months.* *Seventy percent of hips were managed with therapy, rest, and activity modification, 12% required a steroid injection, and 18% ultimately needed surgery, but all three groups saw improvements in their outcome measures at final follow-up.* Interestingly, those who had cam deformities were 4.4x more likely to undergo surgery than those with pincer deformities alone. **The authors concluded** that nonoperative management should be the first-line approach in this group of patients.

*Schmitz et al. imaged asymptomatic volunteers using MRI to evaluate for acetabular labral pathology.* **They identified** paralabral cysts in ~25% of hips and acetabular labral tears in 80-85% of patients. **The authors concluded** that using this MRI protocol, there is a high rate of labral pathology in young, asymptomatic patients.

Figure A shows an AP radiograph of the left hip demonstrating a proximal femoral cam deformity. Illustration A demonstrates how to measure the alpha angle using a line parallel to the femoral neck and a best-fit circle with a line exiting at the junction of the femoral neck and head.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has been through 3 months of physical therapy without benefit and has radiographic evidence of intra-articular hip pathology. Therefore, surgical intervention is indicated.
Answer 2: Arthroscopic intervention has demonstrated a quicker return to activities than surgical hip dislocation, as there is less soft tissue disruption.
Answer 4: Arthroscopic labral repair has demonstrated better outcomes than debridement, and therefore should be performed whenever possible.
Answer 5: Labral repair and periacetabular osteotomy would be indicated in the setting of hip dysplasia and concomitant soft tissue pathology. However, in this case, there is no significant evidence of acetabular dysplasia.
