## Question

A 70-year-old female undergoes primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) for a proximal humerus fracture. What is the most likely outcome difference if she instead underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with subsequent salvage RTSA for hardware failure?

## Options

1. Primary RTSA is not indicated acutely for fracture
2. Salvage RTSA is associated with increased shoulder range of motion
3. Primary RTSA is associated with increased shoulder external rotation compared to salvage rTSA
4. Primary RTSA is associated with equivalent shoulder forward elevation compared to salvage rTSA
5. Salvage RTSA is associated with better patient reported outcomes

## Correct option

**3. Primary RTSA is associated with increased shoulder external rotation compared to salvage rTSA**

## Answer explanation

Proximal humerus fractures in elderly patients can be treated with primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. Data suggests that primary RTSA is associated with an improved range of motion in the planes of forward elevation and external rotation compared to salvage RTSA for failed ORIF (Answer 3).

RTSA is an appropriate index procedure for complex proximal humerus fractures in older individuals. Treating these fractures with ORIF can also be done; however, these patients are at risk for implant failure, malunion, nonunion, and possible shoulder instability if concomitant rotator cuff pathology is present. In these cases, patients may undergo salvage RTSA, which is associated with slightly decreased ROM, lower patient-reported outcomes, and a higher risk of infection.

*Nelson et al. completed a systematic review of five studies (n=251) comparing RTSA as a primary treatment for complex proximal humerus fractures in the elderly with RTSA as a salvage procedure following failed treatments.* Traditionally, ORIF or hemiarthroplasty (HA) has been the surgical treatment of choice; however, these modalities are associated with tuberosity malunion or nonunion. To mitigate these complications, **the authors concluded** that primary RTSA is a viable option for elderly proximal humerus fractures and resulted in a greater range of motion, improved patient-reported outcomes, and fewer complications than salvage RTSA.

*Dezfuli et al. assessed the outcomes of RTSA in 49 patients with proximal humerus fractures treated either as a primary or revision procedure.* RTSA was more effective when performed as a primary procedure for acute fractures or malunions/nonunions compared to revision procedures for failed hemiarthroplasty (HA) or ORIF. Patients treated for acute fractures and malunions/nonunions showed significantly better outcomes in shoulder range of motion and various shoulder function scores than those undergoing revision. The study suggests that RTSA is effective across different treatment scenarios, with better results as a primary procedure.

*Boileau et al. completed a multicenter study that evaluated 203 patients with sequelae of proximal humeral fractures treated with nonconstrained shoulder arthroplasty.* *The study validated a surgical classification for these fracture sequelae.* Good results were seen in Type 1 (impacted fractures with humeral head collapse/necrosis) and Type 2 (nonreducible dislocations) cases, where no greater tuberosity osteotomy was needed. However, poor functional outcomes were observed in Type 3 (nonunions) and Type 4 (severe tuberosity malunions) cases, where osteotomy was required. As a result, the study suggests alternative treatments, such as RTSA for Type 4 and bone grafting for Type 3.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Primary RTSA is a viable option for elderly proximal humerus fractures.
Answer 2 & 4: Salavage RTSA is associated with a significantly worse shoulder range of motion than primary RTSA, specifically in the planes of forward elevation and external rotation.
Answer 5: Salvage RTSA is associated with slightly worse patient-reported outcomes.
