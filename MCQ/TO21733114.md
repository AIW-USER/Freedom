## Question

First movement to be hampered in Adhesive capsulitis.

## Options

1. IR
2. ER
3. Flexion
4. Abduction

## Correct option

**2. ER**

## Answer explanation

Ans: B

EXPLANATION: -
External rotation is the first movement to be lost in frozen shoulder likely because changes start in subsynovial layer of capsule in region of rotator interval

ADHESIVE CAPSULITIS
Adhesive capsulitis (also known as frozen shoulder) is characterized by functional loss of both passive and active shoulder motion commonly associated with diabetes, and thyroid disease more commonly affecting females 40 – 60 yrs.
In terms of pathophysiology it can be
1. Primary or idiopathic form
2. Post-traumatic (following proximal humerus fracture or immobilization for other upper extremity injury)
3. Post-surgical (following rotator cuff repair or axillary dissection for malignancy)
In all scenarios, inflammatory process involving subsynovial layers cause fibroblastic proliferation of joint capsule leading to thickening of glenohumeral ligaments, fibrosis, and adherence of the capsule to itself and humerus. Essential lesion involves the coracohumeral ligament and rotator interval capsule. Fibroblasts or myofibroblasts are present with abundant type III collagen leading to mechanical block to motion.

Clinical Stages
1. Freezing/Painful stage- Gradual onset of diffuse pain (6 wks to 9 months)
2. Frozen/ Stiffness stage- Decreased ROM affecting activities of daily living (4 to 9 months or more)
3. Thawing or Resolution stage- Gradual return of motion (5 to 26 months)

Arthroscopic Stages
Stage 1 Patchy, fibrinous synovitis
Stage 2 Capsular contraction and fibrinous adhesions
Stage 3 Increasing contraction, synovitis resolving
Stage 4 Severe contraction

Diagnosis is mainly clinical. Xray shows disuse osteopenia whereas MRI shows increased synovial thickness > 4mm, inferior capsular fold obliteration, rotator cuff interval scarring & joint capsule hypervascularity.

Management: Physical therapy involving stretching and later strengthening is the mainstay of treatment regardless of stage. Abduction should be avoided initially to prevent impingement until joint motion becomes more supple. Intraarticular or oral steroid may be useful in early phases especially but Longer-term follow-up shows no difference between patients treated with steroids and controls.
Patients who experience a reduction in motion despite 4-6 months of compliant therapy are most likely to require surgery/ manipulation under anaesthesia. Closed manipulation: Up to 94% patients have subjective relief which is sustained at long term follow-up. Technique involves Flexion, Extension, Abduction, Adduction, external and internal Rotation (acronym: FEAR). Post manipulation, 20ml of 0.5% bupivacaine and corticosteroid may be injected into the joint to reduce the inflammatory symptoms and pain.
Arthroscopic capsular release can also be done prior to manipulation as it allows complete inspection of the joint, confirmation of the diagnosis, and a more precise capsulotomy. Anterior release is always done with or without posterior release. Several studies have demonstrated early benefits of routine release of posterior capsule although outcome similar to isolated anterior release. Controlled trials are unavailable to show superiority of Arthroscopic capsular release over manipulation under anaesthesia hence currently, addition of arthroscopy should be reserved only for recalcitrant frozen shoulder patients.
