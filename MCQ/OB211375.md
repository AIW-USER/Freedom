## Question

A 25-year-old male presents to the trauma bay after a motorcycle collision where he hit a tree traveling 40 mph. The initial chest x-ray reveals several broken ribs and a right-sided pneumothorax. Pelvis x-rays reveal a pubic symphysis diastasis of 5 cm. No acetabulum injury is noted. The patient's blood pressure is 80/30 and his heart rate is 165. The decision is made to place a pelvic binder. Which of the following anatomical surface landmarks is the preferred centering location of the pelvic binder?

## Options

1. Iliac wing
2. Anterior superior iliac spine
3. Greater trochanters
4. Anterior inferior iliac spine
5. Posterior superior iliac spine

## Correct option

**3. Greater trochanters**

## Answer explanation

The patient has significant pubic symphysis diastasis and hemodynamic instability. The most appropriate placement of a pelvic binder is centered at the level of the greater trochanter to allow indirect reduction of the pubic symphysis.

APC pelvic ring injuries are associated with diastasis of pubic symphysis and can result in injury to the posterior venous plexus within the pelvis. This can lead to significant intra-pelvic bleeding with the widened pelvis no longer able to tamponade the volume of blood filling in the pelvis. The initial management of these "open-book" pelvic ring injuries in the setting of hemodynamic instability is the placement of a pelvic binder or circumferential sheet at the level of the greater trochanter to allow for indirect reduction of the symphysis. This may be augmented with internal rotation of lower extremities and taping at ankles. Some contraindications to a pelvic binder are LC-type pelvis fractures given the possibility of worsening internal rotation of the injured hemipelvis and acetabulum fractures with the binder causing worsening femoral protrusio. Furthermore, care should be taken with pelvic binders since they can cause skin necrosis if left in place for too long. Lastly, one should note that placement of a binder can mask pelvic ring injuries, creating false-negative radiographs and CT images

*Bottlang et al. performed a review of emergent management of open-book pelvic ring fractures with use of circumferential compression at 3 distinct levels (centered at the greater trochanter, centered at the AIIS and centered at the iliac wing).* **The authors noted** that the least amount of force required to achieve indirect reduction of the pubic symphysis occurred when the pelvic sling was applied at the level of the greater trochanter. **The authors concluded** that for optimal reduction of open-book pelvic fractures, a pelvic sling should be applied around the greater trochanters.

*Born et al. performed an 18-month prospective study to determine the incidence and spectrum of delayed recognition of skeletal injury at a Level I trauma center.* **The authors noted** a delayed diagnosis of 39 fractures was made in 26 of 1,006 consecutive blunt trauma patients. The delay in recognition ranged from 1-91 days. **They noted** that twenty-one (55%) of the fractures were not X-rayed at the time of admission, but nine (23%) fractures were clearly visible on admission films. In only two instances was a second anesthetic exposure required for operative therapy. **The authors concluded** that continued clinical and radiologic surveillance is required in multiply injured patients to prevent musculoskeletal diagnostic failure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, 4, 5: The pelvic binder should be centered at the level of the greater trochanter for optimal placement to close down an open-book pelvis injury.
