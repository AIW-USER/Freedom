## Question

A 66-year-old man presents to your clinic with progressive collapsing flatfoot and anterolateral ankle pain that has worsened over the past two years despite orthotics and bracing. Non-weight-bearing radiographs are displayed in figures A and B. On examination, his hindfoot is resting in valgus and is minimally correctable. Over the past year, he has had three tibiotalar corticosteroid injections, each with significant pain relief. The surgeon plans an isolated total ankle arthroplasty. Which of the following postoperative outcomes is most likely?

![[q220905-b-screen-shot-2025-11-12-at-8-45-38-pm.jpg]]

## Options

1. Lateral polyethylene edge-loading and progressive lateral talar tilt
2. Durable neutral alignment with symmetric polyethylene wear and sustained pain relief
3. Medial gutter impingement due to overconstraint of the deltoid ligament
4. Subtalar joint nonunion requiring revision surgery
5. Peroneal tendon rupture caused by overcorrection into varus

## Correct option

**1. Lateral polyethylene edge-loading and progressive lateral talar tilt**

## Answer explanation

Persistent coronal malalignment leads to asymmetric contact stresses and early mechanical failure despite proper component positioning. If valgus malalignment and deltoid insufficiency are not addressed at the time of total ankle arthroplasty (TAA), the patient is at risk of polyethylene edge-loading and progressive lateral talar tilt (answer choice 1).

Total ankle arthroplasty is a motion-preserving option for end-stage ankle arthritis, but durable outcomes depend on restoring stable, neutral alignment and balancing surrounding soft tissues. In valgus ankles associated with progressive collapsing flatfoot deformity (stage IV), the deltoid ligament is often incompetent, and the hindfoot is misaligned; both require correction to prevent edge-loading and implant failure. Adjunct procedures (e.g., deltoid reconstruction, osteotomies, arthrodeses) are often required to achieve a plantigrade foot beneath the prosthesis. When the ankle is the primary pain generator—as suggested by complete relief after intra-articular injection—tibiotalar reconstruction plus alignment correction provides the best long-term pain relief and function.

*Dodd and Daniels reviewed total ankle arthroplasty performed in the presence of coronal plane deformity.* **They found** that acceptable outcomes are achievable when malalignment is corrected, often requiring ancillary procedures to address deltoid insufficiency and hindfoot deformity. **They concluded** that failure to restore neutral alignment predisposes to edge-loading, persistent tilt, and early implant failure.

*Jackson et al. reviewed adult acquired flatfoot deformity and described stage IV disease as tibiotalar valgus with deltoid insufficiency and ankle arthritis.* **They noted** that treatment must re-establish medial-sided stability and correct hindfoot alignment to permit durable ankle-salvage procedures. **They concluded** that combined procedures are commonly necessary to achieve a stable, plantigrade limb with lasting symptom relief.

Figures A and B are non-weightbearing anteroposterior and lateral ankle radiographs, respectively, demonstrating a pes planovalgus deformity with concomitant ankle arthritis and lateral talar tilt. Note, given these are non-weightbearing views, the degree of joint space narrowing is likely underestimated.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 2: Symmetric wear and durable relief are unlikely without correcting valgus and deltoid insufficiency; persistent malalignment drives asymmetric loading and failure.
Answer choice 3: Medial gutter impingement from overconstraint would require overtightening of the deltoid, which is not being reconstructed here; the more typical problem in uncorrected valgus is lateral edge-loading.
Answer choice 4: Subtalar nonunion is a potential concern when a subtalar fusion is performed, which did not occur in this case. Despite this risk, this may be performed either concurrently during the index TAA or in a staged fashion, depending on patient symptoms and radiographic evaluation.
Answer choice 5: Peroneal tendon rupture is not the predictable outcome of leaving the ankle in valgus.
