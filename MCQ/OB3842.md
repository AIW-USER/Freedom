## Question

A 21-year-old man suffered a traumatic laceration to his lateral right knee 2 years ago, causing a complete transection of the common peroneal nerve. Despite a nerve graft procedure at the time of injury, he has a persistent foot drop and an EMG showing no anterior or lateral compartment function. He has full strength in the deep and superficial posterior muscle compartments. He has been wearing an ankle-foot orthosis (AFO) but would like to discuss surgical methods to eliminate his need for an orthosis. Which of the following tendons in Figure A would be most optimal for a single-tendon transfer in this patient?

## Correct option

**2. B**

## Answer explanation

For a young patient with a persistent foot drop after a traumatic peroneal nerve laceration, a split posterior tibial tendon transfer is the best treatment option to regain some dorsiflexion. The structure labeled B in the image corresponds with the posterior tibialis tendon.

In patients with permanent nerve injury and no signs of meaningful neurological recovery, surgical treatments for improved function often involves tendon transfers. A successful tendon transfer requires an intact donor tendon with adequate innervation and strength to provide function. In the case of a chronic common peroneal nerve injury, the deep and superficial posterior compartments are intact, therefore a split posterior tibial tendon transfer would provide a functioning tendon to allow for some dorsiflexion and less reliance on an AFO.

*Wagenaar et al. reported on the results of posterior tibial tendon transfer for 12 patients with chronic foot-drop.* **They found** patient satisfaction was high and that 10 of the 11 patients that needed an AFO before surgery no longer required one following the procedure. **They concluded** that attachment of a split posterior tibial tendon to the extensor tendons proximal to the ankle joint provides results equivalent to other procedures for foot drop and can be considered a viable option.

Movahedi Yeganeh reported on 15 patients who underwent a combined PTT, flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon, and flexor digitorum longus (FDL) transfer for ankle dorsiflexion as well as toe extension. They found foot drop correction was excellent in 60% of patients and toe extension evaluation was excellent in 47% of patients. They concluded that the Movahedi Tendon Transfer method as above was a reliable method to achieve balance toe and ankle dorsiflexion following common peroneal nerve palsy.

Figure A shows a T1 coronal oblique image of the ankle. Label A is the tibialis anterior, B is the posterior tibialis tendon, C is the flexor digitorum longus tendon, D is the Achilles, and E is the peroneal tendons.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1 - The tibialis anterior is nonfunctional in this patient and would therefore not be a good candidate donor tendon for transfer.
Answer 3 - The flexor digitorum longus tendon could be used as an adjunct to achieve toe extension, however, would not be adequate in isolation to achieve ankle dorsiflexion.
Answer 4 - The Achilles is likely tight in this patient given the history of foot drop. An Achilles or gastrocnemius lengthening is reasonable for this patient but a transfer of the Achilles’ tendon has not been described and would result in a loss of plantarflexion.
Answer 5 - The peroneal tendons are nonfunctional in this patient and would therefore not be a good donor tendon for transfer.
