## Question

Figure A is a 3D CT reconstruction of a 23-year-old male with recurrent anterior shoulder dislocations. He has had no prior surgical interventions. He would now like to discuss surgery. Measurement utilizing best-fit circle technique estimates 30% glenoid bone loss. Which of the following would be MOST appropriate for this patient?

![[q211887-a-glenoid-loss.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Arthroscopic bankart repair
2. Arthroscopic bankart repair with remplissage
3. Open capsular shift
4. Open coracoid transfer
5. Open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**4. Open coracoid transfer**

## Answer explanation

This patient has recurrent anterior glenohumeral instability with >20% glenoid bone loss. With significant glenoid bone loss, an open coracoid transfer (Latarjet procedure) would be most indicated.

Risk factors for recurrent anterior shoulder instability include age <25 years, male gender, an osseous defect of the anterior glenoid (Bankart) or posterior humeral head (Hill-Sachs), and contact sports. Soft-tissue repairs (Bankart) for anterior glenoid bony deficiencies exceeding >20-25% are associated with high recurrence rates. This amount of deficiency gives an inverted pear deformity to glenoid. In these patients, a bone-augmentation procedure such as an open or arthroscopic coracoid transfer or structural iliac crest graft should be considered. The Latarjet procedure is believed to provide a triple effect: bony restraint by increasing the glenoid track, a sling effect by allowing the conjoined tendon to tension the subscapularis, and a capsule reconstruction.

*Sugaya et al. reports on an arthroscopic method of osseous reconstruction of the glenoid without bone-grafting.* **They report** good or excellent result with 95% of patients returning to the sport they had played prior to the injury. **They concluded** that arthroscopic osseous Bankart repair with use of suture anchors yields a successful outcome even in shoulders with a chronic large glenoid defect.

*Burkhart et al. reviewed the results of 194 consecutive arthroscopic Bankart repairs in order to identify specific factors related to recurrence of instability.* **They report** that those patients with and without significant bone defects had a 4% and 67% recurrence rate, respectively. **They also noted** that contact athletes with significant bone defects had an 89% recurrence rate. **They concluded** that arthroscopic Bankart repairs give results equal to open Bankart repairs if there are no significant structural bone deficits (engaging Hill-Sachs or inverted-pear Bankart lesions), patients with significant bone deficits are not candidates for arthroscopic Bankart repair, and a coracoid bone graft procedure should be considered in those with significant glenoid bone loss.

Figure A is a 3D reconstruction of the scapula demonstrating significant anterior glenoid bone loss. Illustration A demonstrates the best-fit circle technique to estimate glenoid bone loss.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A soft-tissue only procedure such as a Bankart repair will have a high failure rate when significant glenoid bone loss is evident
Answer 2: The addition of a Rempliassage is useful with a large Hill-Sachs lesion, but is still not indicated with significant glenoid bone loss
Answer 3: Open capsular shift may be considered in multidirectional instability patients who fail conservative management, but is not indicated with significant glenoid bone loss
Answer 5: Open reduction and internal fixation may be indicated with large acute glenoid rim fractures but is not recommended for recurrent anterior shoulder instability with significant glenoid bone loss
