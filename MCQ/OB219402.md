## Question

A 12-year-old male sustained a right Salter-Harris IV distal femur fracture 6 months ago and returns for follow-up. At this visit, no growth disturbance was observed on radiographs. The patient's mother asks if the patient should return for another radiograph. What is the most appropriate way to counsel the patient and their mother?

## Options

1. The patient can stop returning for annual follow up at the age of 16 as he will be skeletally mature
2. The patient does not need to return for follow-up as methods for predicting limb length discrepancy depend on current height
3. The patient should be followed for a period of at least 2 years
4. The patient does not need to return, as only one radiograph is required to determine if growth inhibition is occurring
5. The patient can stop returning for annual follow-ups as males are skeletally mature by 14 years old

## Correct option

**3. The patient should be followed for a period of at least 2 years**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a physeal injury of the distal femur and will require ongoing monitoring for two years to evaluate for clinically significant growth disturbance (CSGD) (Answer 3).

In distal femur growth plate fractures, the fracture most often occurs in the hypertrophic zone of the cartilage. This zone is characterized by chondrocytes that are no longer proliferating and have begun to enlarge, making it structurally weaker and more susceptible to injury. Additionally, it is crucial to determine skeletal age as it can differ by >1 year from chronological age in 26% of children. An accurate skeletal age enables more precise predictions of potential limb length discrepancy and helps timing possible growth modulation (temporary or permanent epiphysiodesis) or limb-lengthening. There are various methods to predict growth disturbances, and causes, such as trauma, congenital deficiency, and infection, can lead to different growth arrest patterns. Multiple radiographs are necessary to determine the type of disturbance, as growth disturbance graph characteristics can change as skeletal maturity approaches.

*Yamamura et al. completed a retrospective cohort study analyzing the cumulative incidence of CSGD (a growth disturbance requiring subsequent physeal bar resection, osteotomy, and/or epiphysiodesis) associated with the distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal tibia physeal fractures.* **Their results demonstrate** that all CSGDs occurred within 2 years of injury, and the risk of CSGD peaked at 10.2 years old for males and 9.1 years old for females.

*Makarov et al. completed a retrospective review to determine the timing of epiphysiodesis to correct limb length discrepancy.* *Their review included 77 patients whose skeletal age was determined using 3 serial hand and wrist radiographs at least 6 months apart.* The predicted leg lengths and residual discrepancies at maturity were compared to actual outcomes using the White-Menelaus, Anderson-Green, Moseley, and multiplier methods. **Their results demonstrated** that skeletal age differed by over one year from chronological age in 26% of observations, improving prediction accuracy when used instead of chronological age.

*Paley et al. introduced the multiplier method for predicting differences in limb lengths.* **The authors concluded** that the femur and tibia multipliers are consistent across all percentile groups, differing only by age and gender. The multiplier values are independent of generation, height, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, or race. The authors clinically confirmed the accuracy of this method by assessing patients who underwent limb-lengthening or epiphysiodesis. The method was as accurate or more accurate than the Moseley method for predicting limb lengths.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1&5: On average, males reach skeletal maturity at 16 years old and females at 14. However, skeletal and chronological age can differ by over one year in 26% of patients
Answer 2: Methods for predicting limb length discrepancy are independent of height
Answer 4: Growth disturbance after a physeal injury requires multiple radiographs to determine the type of growth inhibition
