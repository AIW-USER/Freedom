## Question

A 38-year-old male construction worker presents for a follow-up visit of a painful lesion with overlying skin changes over the proximal interphalangeal joint of the 2nd toe that has been present for over 3 years. He has tried wearing shoes with a high toe box, cushioned toe sleeves, and anti-inflammatory medications, but continues to experience discomfort that prevents him to work somedays. On physical examination there is PIP joint flexion with DIP extension that is not passively correctable. What is the best treatment option for this patient?

## Options

1. Weil osteotomy
2. PIP resection arthroplasty with K-wire fixation
3. Flexor digitorum longus tendon transfer
4. Extensor digitorum longus lengthening
5. DIP joint fusion with K-wire fixation

## Correct option

**2. PIP resection arthroplasty with K-wire fixation**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a rigid hammertoe deformity that has failed conservative treatment. The best surgical option for this patient would be a proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint resection arthroplasty with K-wire fixation.

Hammertoe deformities are the result of PIP joint flexion with distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint extension and can lead to a painful lesion, or corn, on the dorsum of the PIP joint. The overpull of the extensor digitorum longus (EDL) is typically the underlying mechanism in addition to intrinsic muscle weakness. Nonoperative management includes high toe box shoes and padded toes sleeves. Surgical management is dictated by the flexibility of the PIP joint deformity with flexible deformities managed with flexor digitorum longus (FDL) transfer and rigid deformities managed with PIP joint resection arthroplasty or fusion. Similarly, mallet toe deformities are the result of overpull of the FDL resulting in a hyperflexion deformity of the DIP joint and potentially leading to ulcers or calluses of the toe pulp.

*Kramer et al. performed a retrospective study of 2,698 hammertoe deformities treated with PIP resection arthroplasty or PIP fusion and stabilized with K-wire fixation.* **The authors reported** a 5.6% deformity recurrence rate with a 3.5% revision surgery rate. Additionally, there was a 3.5% pin migration rate, 0.3% pin tract infection rate, and a 0.6% vascular compromise rate leading to a 0.4% amputation rate. **The authors concluded** that K-wire fixation of hammertoe deformity correction is associated with low complication rates, low treatment cost, and good fixation maintenance.

*Shirzad et al. reviewed the management of lesser toe deformities.* **The authors recommended** that surgical treatment of flexible hammertoe deformities includes an FDL transfer whereas rigid deformities are managed with PIP joint resection arthroplasty or fusion. Further, **they recommended** that any other symptomatic toe deformity be addressed concurrently at the time of the procedure. **The authors concluded** that correctly understanding the pathology of the underlying toe deformity allows for optimal surgical treatment for refractory cases.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Weil osteotomy is performed when there is an MTP joint subluxation that is irreducible or there is transfer metatarsalgia. This would not address a hammertoe deformity.
Answer 3: FDL tendon transfer is a treatment option for patients with a flexible hammertoe deformity. This patient has a rigid deformity that requires a resection arthroplasty.
Answer 4: EDL lengthening is a treatment option for a claw toe deformity.
Answer 5: DIP joint fusion is a treatment option for a rigid mallet toe deformity.
