Chapter: Arthroscopy MCQs — PCL

## Question

Which of the following will not be effective way to tackle a PCL reconstruction failure secondary to “Killer Turn”

## Options

1. Making tibial tunnel in lower part of PCL footprint
2. Using aperture fixation (interference screws) rather than suspensory (endobuttons)
3. Making tibial tunnel antero laterally
4. Using the transtibial tunnel technique in place of inlay method

## Correct option

**4. Using the transtibial tunnel technique in place of inlay method**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D
EXPLANATION: -
The most common technique for the reconstruction of the PCL is the transtibial tunnel technique in which the graft passes into and is fixed within the tunnels simulating the attachments of antero lateral bundle of PCL on tibia and femur. The main biomechanical disadvantage of this technique is that the graft has to make an acute bend around the proximal posterior tibia. This bend has been referred to as the “killer turn” (Fig 1).

![[TO21733233-1.jpg]]

Fig 1: Diagrammatic depiction of “Killer Turn”.
The so called “killer turn” has been hypothesized to enforce high forces within the graft as it turns around the sharp edge of the posterior tibial tunnel exit, thereby producing abrasion, thinning, and permanent elongation of the graft1 subsequently leading to graft failure. Several biomechanical studies have verified the wearing of the graft at the “killer turn”, although very few studies provided a quantitative evaluation of graft elongation. According to a study by Markolf et al2, the mean thinning at the “killer turn” of graft was 40.6% and the mean elongation was (9.8 mm/15 mm) 65.3%.

Various methods to reduce the killer turn and resultant laxity have been discussed below.

• Tibial tunnel position (Fig 2):

By making the tibial tunnel lower than the native footprint the more acute killer turn is divided into two lower smooth turns thus reducing the strain on the graft3.

![[TO21733233-2.jpg]]

Fig 2: Acute killer turn angle divided into two smooth turns by a lowering tibial tunnel exit on proximal posterior tibia

• Method of fixation:

By using aperture fixation instead of extracortical/ suspensory fixation also, we can reduce this wearing.

![[TO21733233-3.jpg]]

Fig 3: Fig A shows suspensory fixation that can have graft micromotion while Fig B shows Aperture fixation with a stiffer graft implant construct

From the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction principles, it is well known that extracortical soft tissue graft fixation leads to micromotions of the graft within the tunnel that might disturb tendon to bone healing. This effect is well known as the “bungee cord effect.” In PCL reconstruction, these motions could lead to abrasion of the PCL graft as it turns around the sharp edge of the tibial tunnel exit when the knee is loaded. The aperture fixation of PCL grafts includes another advantage. From the ACL, it is well known that an aperture fixation close to the joint line increases the stiffness of the graft–implant construct and thereby increases the stability of the ACL reconstruction. A recent study using a robotic/universal force moment sensor testing system was able to show that the same is relevant for PCL reconstruction.

• Using anterolateral tibial tunnel (Fig 4):

The anterolateral route tibial tunnel significantly reduced the sharp graft angulation ("killer turn") at the graft tunnel margin of the proximal tibia, but it did not increase the joint translation as compared with the traditional anteromedial route tibial tunnel4. The anterolateral route tibial tunnel is thought to be a better choice when arthroscopic PCL reconstruction is performed with the transtibial tunnel technique. A theoretical drawback here remains the fact that less acute graft angulation may increase joint translation because of a decrease in graft compressive force.

![[TO21733233-4.jpg]]

Fig 4: Antero lateral tibial tunnel

• Smothering the tibial aperture:

After drilling the tibial tunnel, the aperture at the articulator end can be smoothened using punch or a rasp. This in turn reduces the wearing of the graft and prevents laxity.

• Inlay technique:

In order to avoid the “killer turn” seen in transtibial techniques, Jakob et al. and later Berg developed and popularized the open tibial inlay method of PCL reconstruction which relies on a posterior arthrotomy and direct visualization of the PCL tibial attachment site. The open tibial inlay technique allows for anatomic placement of the PCL graft at its tibial attachment site (Fig 5) and direct bone-to-bone healing for added fixation strength5 .

![[TO21733233-5.jpg]]

Fig 5: Depiction of Tibial inlay technique for reconstruction of PCL

• All inside PCL Reconstruction:

The all-inside technique allows the creation of half tunnels both in the femur and in the tibia, preserving bone stock, minimizing the risk of tunnel widening and tunnel convergence in case of multiligamentous knee reconstruction. Moreover, the creation of sockets instead of full tunnels minimizes the “windshield effect,” and the all-inside technique allows the surgeon to reduce the “killer turn” encountered in the transtibial technique, which has been associated with graft failure.
