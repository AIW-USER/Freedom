## Question

All are FALSE regarding puncture wounds of foot EXCEPT

## Options

1. The commonest organism causing Osteomyelitis is Staph aureus
2. Needles are the commonest objects involved
3. Antibiotic prophylaxis should be given against Pseudomonas if puncture occurs through sole of a shoe
4. Tetanus toxoid should be given in clean and minor wounds also if previous tetanus immunization status is unknown

## Correct option

**3. Antibiotic prophylaxis should be given against Pseudomonas if puncture occurs through sole of a shoe**

## Answer explanation

PUNCTURE WOUNDS OF FOOT

Puncture wounds are penetrating injuries caused by a sharp pointed object and can present with or without a retained foreign body. Nails most frequently cause such wounds, but other items such as needles, glass, wood, plastic, and sharp metal objects can also be responsible.

Puncture wounds are clinically important as they can lead to complications such as soft tissue infections, deep abscess, pyogenic granuloma, epidermal inclusion cysts and even osteomyelitis (if penetration goes to cartilage/ periosteum). Approximately 10% of these injuries result in infections and 1-2% of these will develop osteomyelitis.

Risk factors associated with development of infection post puncture injury include characteristics of the wound such as timing (presentation > 6 hours), location of puncture, depth, contamination level of the penetrating object, and retained foreign body. Elderly, immunocompromised, and patients with peripheral vascular disease and diabetes are at higher risk for infections associated with puncture wounds. Diabetes mellitus is the most significant risk factor associated with these infections and often these cases land up with Osteomyelitis.

Inciting organism: Most soft tissue infections (cellulitis or abscess) associated with puncture wounds are caused by Staphylococcus species that usually colonize the skin. Pseudomonas is the most common cause leading to osteomyelitis. Pseudomonas is also common when puncture injury occurs through the rubber sole of shoes. Puncture wounds occurring in bodies of water may involve Pseudomonas, Aeromonas hydrophila, Vibrio species, and Mycobacterium marinum. Eikenella is part of the normal oropharyngeal flora and is implicated in infection involving toothpick injuries.

RADIOLOGY

Detection of any retained foreign bodies is of paramount importance as it can lead to complications. Around 40% of retained foreign bodies are missed on initial examination and hence radiographs with multiple views of the injury site are recommended for all puncture wounds except in the case of knife wounds. X-ray will allow for the identification of radiopaque retained foreign bodies, fractures, and bony changes associated with infections in the region.

Ultrasound is a reliable tool used to detect associated abscesses and retained foreign bodies that are not radio-opaque, especially organic ones that are missed on x-ray. USG can pick up as minimum as 5mm long wooden foreign bodies.

MRI studies are indicated when there is concern for osteomyelitis

TREATMENT

Treatment can be conservative with antibiotics or surgical debridement depending on chronicity of wound, patient comorbidities and presence of complications viz. soft tissue infection or osteomyelitis.

The use of prophylactic antibiotics in all cases is not advised. Antibiotics prophylaxis is recommended for use in highly contaminated, presentation greater than 6 hrs, and at-risk patients (those with diabetes or immunosuppression). When indicated, first-generation oral cephalosporin, antistaphylococcal penicillin, fluoroquinolone, or macrolide are recommended. For wounds through soles of shoes, antibiotics with antipseudomonal activity, oral fluoroquinolone such as ciprofloxacin are needed.
