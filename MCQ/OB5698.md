## Question

A 14-year-old gymnast sustained an injury to the posterior aspect of her thigh while participating in the vault at the Junior Olympics. She was unable to finish competing secondary to pain. Initial radiographs taken at the training facility were negative. After returning home, 10-days later, she continues to have significant posterior thigh pain and ambulates with a limp. She has weakness with hip extension and knee flexion. What is the best next step to confirm the diagnosis?

## Options

1. CT
2. MRI
3. EMG
4. Bone scan
5. Cortisone injection

## Correct option

**2. MRI**

## Answer explanation

The patient likely sustained an ischial tuberosity avulsion fracture due to the forceful contraction of her hamstrings. If plain radiographs are normal, MRI is the best next step as it allows you to evaluate surrounding soft tissues and for subperiosteal fluid.

Ischial tuberosity avulsion fractures occur in adolescents between the ages of 13-17 as the ischial tuberosity begins to ossify. During this time period, the ischial tuberosity is 2-5x weaker than the ligament, leading to bony avulsions. These injuries occur following a forceful contraction of the hamstrings and are commonly seen in football, soccer, track and field or gymnastics athletes. The initial radiographic workup consists of plain x-rays. If these are negative, MRI is indicated as it allows for evaluation of the surrounding soft tissues and for subperiosteal fluid. Ultrasound may also be used as a diagnostic tool, however, studies have shown mixed results, and many have concluded that it is inferior to MRI.

*Schiller et al review lower extremity avulsion fractures in the pediatric and adolescent athletes.* **They report** that many of these injuries are missed due to a lack of imaging. **They conclude** that in patients with normal radiographs, ultrasound is inferior to MRI in the diagnosis of ischial tuberosity avulsion fractures.

*McKinney et al review apophyseal avulsion fractures of the hip and pelvis in pediatric patients.* **They report** that these injuries are often missed and that a thorough understanding of anatomy and physical exam findings are crucial to accurate diagnose of apophyseal avulsions. **They conclude** that if missed, these injuries have the potential to be debilitating for the pediatric athlete.

*Kocher et al review hip disorders in pediatric athletes.* **They report** the three most common sites of apophyseal avulsion injuries to be the ischial tuberosity, anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) and anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). **They conclude** that most patients can be treated nonoperatively, however, as a general rule, large fragments displaced >2cm may require operative treatment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: CT scan is best for determining the amount of displacement of a bony avulsion, and is not necessary in patients with normal plain films.
Answer 3: EMG is not necessary as this patient had a traumatic event and has weakness from an apophyseal avulsion fracture.
Answer 4: A bone scan is not necessary for the work-up of apophyseal avulsion injuries.
Answer 5: Cortisone injection has no role in the treatment of ischial tuberosity avulsion fractures.
