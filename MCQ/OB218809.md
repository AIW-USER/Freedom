## Question

A 65-year-old patient reports worsening startup pain 2 months after undergoing total hip arthroplasty. Figures A and B show postoperative radiographs at 6 weeks and 3 months, respectively. Laboratory studies are unremarkable (normal CBC count, C-reactive protein, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate). Aspiration is performed, providing a WBC of 150, a PMN % of 17, and negative cultures. Which of the following options is most appropriate?

![[q218809-a-69a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

![[q218809-b-69b.jpg]]
*Figure B*

## Options

1. Revision to a modular tapered fluted titanium stem and to a jumbo cup with acetabular augments
2. Obtain an MRI with Metal Artifact Reduction Sequence (MARS)
3. Physical therapy for quadriceps strengthening
4. Resection arthroplasty, antibiotic spacer, and intravenous antibiotics
5. Revision to a nonmodular tapered fluted titanium stem

## Correct option

**5. Revision to a nonmodular tapered fluted titanium stem**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with symptomatic, aseptic femoral component loosening. Appropriate management consists of revision of the femoral component.

Aseptic loosening remains one of the most common indications for revision total hip arthroplasty (THA). After infection has been ruled-out, management is determined by gauging the patient’s symptoms, the rate of progression of the subsidence, and the amount of femoral bone loss. In the setting of Paprosky Type I, II, and IIIA defects of the femur, revision to an uncemented, fully porous-coated stem is advised.

*Weeden et al. analyzed a cohort of 188 femoral revisions with a mean follow up of 14.2 years.* **They found** 4.1% of stems experienced mechanical failure. **They recommend** the use of extensively coated femoral stems in revision hip arthroplasty.

*Kelmer et al. performed a retrospective review of 535 THA revisions.* **They found** that mechanical failure was the most common reason for revision (36.5% of revisions) overall. **They concluded** that within 2 years after primary THA infection and fracture were the most common, while after 2 years, mechanical failure and metallosis were the most common.

Figure A shows a THA with an undersized femoral component in varus. Figure B shows subsidence of the femoral stem.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Revision to either a modular or nonmodular tapered fluted titanium stem is an acceptable option. Revision of the acetabular component in this case would be dependent on further investigation to determine whether the cup is in optimal version and inclination. The inclusion of acetabular augments make this selection clearly wrong, however, as there is obviously minimal to no acetabular bone loss evident on the AP hip radiograph.
Answer 2: MARS MRI is useful to evaluate for the presence of a pseudotumor in the setting of metal-on-metal corrosion. This is typically more chronic in nature with metal-on-metal bearing surfaces or at the trunnion when a metallic head component is used.
Answer 3: This patient has a mechanical cause for their symptoms (loosening of the femoral component). Ignoring that cause and sending the patient to further physical therapy is unlikely to help and the patient’s condition is likely to continue worsening.
Answer 4: This would be appropriate for chronic periprosthetic joint infection (PJI).
