## Question

A 61-year-old banker presents with shoulder pain and a progressive decline in range of motion since undergoing a total shoulder replacement surgery 5 years ago. He initially did well and was able to get back to weightlifting and golfing, but has recently had difficulty lifting his arm above the horizon and has trouble reaching across his body to put his seat belt on in the car. An MRI of his affected shoulder shows partial-thickness, attritional tearing of his supraspinatus. If pre-operative planning for revision surgery reveals the finding demonstrated in Figure A, which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's treatment failure?

## Options

1. Component malposition
2. Component sizing
3. Excessive component wear
4. Subscapularis repair failure
5. Treatment with inappropriate category of implant

## Correct option

**2. Component sizing**

## Answer explanation

The patient has an overstuffed glenohumeral joint, as evidenced by the red circle in Figure A, shown larger than Iannotti's best-fit circle outlined in yellow. Inappropriate component sizing can lead to eventual loss of motion, attritional rotator cuff tears, and ultimate failure of an initially successful total shoulder arthroplasty (Answer 2).

Shoulder arthritis is currently treated most frequently with either anatomic or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. When either anatomic total shoulder or hemiarthroplasty is being performed, management of the humeral side involves ensuring accurate pre-operative and intra-operative measurements of the humeral head to allow for anatomic substitution. This is important in this setting because the size of the humeral head implant affects soft tissue balancing, modifies the center of rotation, and alters the rotator cuff lever arms, consequently affecting the shoulder’s ultimate range of motion, strength, and function. While restoring physiological soft tissue tensioning provides stability and prevents complications such as aseptic loosening and osteolysis, overstuffing of the glenohumeral joint with an oversized humeral prosthesis decreases the shoulder’s range of motion and alters the lever arm, adding stress to the rotator cuff and potentially leading to the risk of secondary rotator cuff failure. The best-fit circle described by Iannotti is based on three points: the lateral cortex below the flare of the greater tuberosity and the lateral and medial ends of the anatomic neck (Illustration A) on a Grashey AP radiograph. A resurfaced humeral head that falls outside of this best-fit circle is seen as "overstuffing" the glenohumeral joint, predisposing to the aforementioned risk factors for failure.

*Mechlenburg et al. retrospectively reviewed the Copeland resurfacing humeral head implant (RHHI) and* **found** that it does not restore humeral head anatomy. The authors retrieved pre- and postoperative radiographs from 71 of 91 possible patients operated with a Copeland RHHI from 2005 to 2009, from which a radiologist measured the length of the gleno-humeral offset (LGHO), and collected patient-reported quality of life and functional outcomes as measured by Western Ontario Osteoarthritis of the Shoulder Index (WOOS). **They concluded** that the Copeland RHHI causes significantly increased LGHO and leads to overstuffing in the shoulder joint, while the WOOS score in the cohort was comparable to that for all other Danish patients operated with a Copeland RHHI.

*Geervliet et al. also looked at overstuffing in resurfacing hemiarthroplasty as a potential risk for failure.* *The authors evaluated the inter-observer variability of (1) the critical shoulder angle (CSA), (2) the length of the glenohumeral offset (LGHO), and (3) the anatomic center of rotation (COR) in a 48-patient cohort operated with a Global Conservative Anatomic Prosthesis (CAP) RHHI.* **They found** and concluded that inter-observer reliability showed excellent reliability for the CSA, LGHO, and the COR, but that in contrast with the CSA and LGHO, a correlation could only be made between clinical failure and revision surgery in cases of a deviation of the COR greater than 5 mm.

Figure A is a Grashey AP radiograph of a shoulder demonstrating Iannotti's best-fit circle (yellow) and the post-op best-fit circle (red), showing an overstuffed glenohumeral joint. Illustration A demonstrates the markers of the best fit-circle, as described by Iannotti, with (A) representing the lateral cortex below the flare of the greater tuberosity, (B) the lateral end of the anatomic neck, and (C) the medial end of the anatomic neck.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is no obvious radiographic evidence of component malpositioning on the provided film.
Answer 3: Excessive component wear is unlikely at 5 years post-op without evidence of radiographic loosening.
Answer 4: Subscapularis repair failure would be more likely to present earlier post-operatively and with subjective instability than with progressive pain and decline in function associated with overstuffing and attritional supraspinatus tearing.
Answer 5: There is no indication that an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty was the incorrect index procedure for this patient.
