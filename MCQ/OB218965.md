## Question

A 19-year-old college baseball pitcher presents to your office with complaints of ongoing right shoulder pain for the last several months. He describes the pain as deep within the shoulder. Radiographs show a Bennett lesion. On exam, his total arc of rotational motion is decreased compared to his non-dominant arm. Strength is 5/5 in flexion, abduction, and rotation. Jobe's test and Hawkins test are both negative. He ultimately fails nonoperative management and undergoes arthroscopic evaluation where fraying of the posterosuperior labrum is found. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. SLAP tear
2. Articular-sided rotator cuff tear
3. Internal impingement
4. Subacromial impingement
5. Calcific tendonitis

## Correct option

**3. Internal impingement**

## Answer explanation

Given the findings of decreased total rotational range of motion, deep shoulder pain, Bennett lesion, and fraying of the posterosuperior labrum, this patient likely has internal impingement.

Internal impingement is a cause of shoulder pain primarily in overhead throwers/athletes due to the adaptive changes that take place with these activities. It is thought to cause symptoms when the undersurface of the rotator cuff and posterosuperior labrum are dynamically compressed between the greater tuberosity and glenoid. Tightness of the posterior capsule, particularly the posterior band of the IGHL, are associated with a translation of the humeral head which further contributes to internal impingement. For this reason, internal impingement is closely associated with patients who have glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD), although in recent years, total arc of motion has become a topic of interest as it relates to injury. These findings are in contrast to subacromial ("external") impingement, which causes compression of the bursal side of the rotator cuff. Radiographically, patients with internal impingement may have a "Bennett lesion," which is a traction osteophyte off of the posteroinferior glenoid. On MRI, associated fraying/tearing of the posterosuperior labrum is often seen.

*Takahashi et al. performed an MRI study comparing the throwing and non-throwing shoulders of college baseball players to better evaluate the location of impingement.* *They collected MRI measurements with the shoulder in 90° of abduction and at 90° and 100° of external rotation and noted that the area of impingement between the rotator cuff muscles and the greater tuberosity/posterior glenoid was increased in the throwing shoulder than the non-throwing shoulder.* Greater tuberosity cystic changes and posterior labral degeneration were both more common findings in the throwing shoulder of these athletes.

*Paley et al. evaluated the arthroscopic findings of professional overhead throwing athletes with internal impingement.* **They noted** that with the arm in a throwing position, all 41 athletes had contact between either the rotator cuff undersurface and the posterosuperior glenoid or osteochondral lesions. **They also noted** a high percentage of undersurface cuff fraying, posterosuperior labral fraying and anterior labral fraying in this population.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tear may present with similar vague, deep shoulder pain but would be identified during the arthroscopic evaluation.
Answer 2: While articular-sided rotator cuff tears may occur in patients with internal impingement over time, there are no clinical or arthroscopic findings that would indicate a rotator cuff tear.
Answer 4: Subacromial impingement would more likely cause a bursal-sided rotator cuff tear that would lead to a positive Hawkins sign.
Answer 5: Calcific tendonitis can cause shoulder pain and decreased ROM but occurs near the rotator cuff insertion, and would be noted on radiographs and arthroscopically.
