## Question

A 78-year-old man presents to your office with new onset right knee pain 3 years after total knee replacement. The pain is worse with weightbearing and he now has to use a cane. Ibuprofen initially gave him pain relief, but he now reports constant pain. Figure A and B represent his 1-year and 3-year (current) radiographs, respectively. ESR is 14, CRP 2.3, and an aspirate reveals 900 nucleated cells with a negative gram stain and negative leukocyte esterase. What is the best next step in management?

## Options

1. Bone scan to evaluate for component loosening
2. 2-stage revision arthroplasty for infection
3. Revision of tibial component with metaphyseal sleeve and long stem
4. CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis
5. Observation of an osteolytic lesion

## Correct option

**4. CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a large osteolytic lesion involving the proximal tibia concerning for metastatic disease. Staging studies should be performed with either a CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis or a whole body PET/CT scan.

The workup of bony metastases should start with history/physical examination (focused on thyroid, breast and prostate), laboratory studies (serum/urine electrophoresis, PSA, liver enzymes) as clinically indicated, and imaging (bone scan, chest radiograph, CT scans). Staging studies can either include a CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis or a whole body PET/CT scan. If the tests are undiagnostic, a biopsy of the most accessible lesion is indicated.

*Scolaro et al. authored a review of the surgical management of metastatic long bone fractures.* **They state** that the goal of surgery is to obtain a rigid mechanical construct, which allows for early mobility and weight bearing. This can be achieved using internal fixation with polymethylmethacrylate cement or segmental resection and joint reconstruction. Prosthetic joint arthroplasty is a more reliable means of fracture management when insufficient bone is present for fixation.

*Haidukewych reviewed metastatic disease around the hip.* **He also emphasizes** that a thorough pre-operative evaluation is recommended for medical optimization and to ensure that the lesion is, in fact, a metastasis and not a primary bone malignancy. The general treatment option of metastatic disease around the hip include radiation for lower risk lesions, internal fixation, and arthroplasty. Radiation may also be useful postoperatively to minimize construct failure due to tumor progression.

Figure A is an AP/lateral radiograph of well-positioned total knee components without evidence of complications. Figure B is an AP/Lateral radiograph showing a lytic lesion at the tip of the tibial component with evidence of cortical thinning. There is no associated radiolucency around the femoral component. The fact that this lesion only involves the tip of the tibial component makes osteolysis from polyethylene wear less likely. More commonly both the femoral and tibial sides would be involved and the osteolysis progresses in the regions closer to the articular surface at the margins of the components.
Illustration A is the MSIS infection criteria for periprosthetic joint infection.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: A bone scan is often part of the metastasis of unknown origin workup, but would not be used to look for component loosening in this case.
Answer 2: This patient does not have evidence of infection.
Answer 3: This patient should first undergo metastatic workup before any discussion of surgical intervention.
Answer 5: Observation of this new lytic lesion with evidence of cortical destruction would be inappropriate in this setting.
