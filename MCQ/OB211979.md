## Question

A 19-year-old male collegiate football player presents to your office with left ankle pain after twisting his ankle during winter conditioning 2 weeks ago. Pain is located to the lateral ankle and has improved with rest and therapy. He is able to ride a stationary bike with minimal pain. He states this is the fifth time he has sprained his ankle, and third episode within the last year. He endorses frequent instability of the ankle, especially with cutting maneuvers. On exam, there is tenderness to palpation at the tip and posterior border of the fibula. There is increased translation with anterior drawer and pain is reproduced with passive dorsiflexion and eversion of the ankle. MRI of the ankle demonstrates chronic ATFL and CFL tears, as well as peroneal tendon tearing and degeneration. When addressing the peroneal tendons, which of the following is an indication for peroneal tendon debridement and tubularization only?

## Options

1. 30% longitudinal peroneus brevis (PB) tear with tendon subluxation and shallow fibular groove
2. 30% longitudinal PB tear and peroneus longus (PL) tear
3. 60% longitudinal PB tear
4. 60% longitudinal PB and PL tear with preserved muscle excursion
5. 60% longitudinal PB and PL tear with no muscle excursion

## Correct option

**2. 30% longitudinal PB tear and peroneus longus (PL) tear**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with chronic lateral ankle instability and peroneal tendon pathology. Peroneal tendon debridement and tubularization is indicated in patients with symptomatic peroneal tendon tears that involve less than 50% of the tendon.

Peroneal tendon pathology is often missed in patients with lateral ankle pain. Up to 77% of patients with lateral ankle instability have concomitant peroneal tendon tears. Peroneal tendon tears occur with rapid dorsiflexion of an inverted foot, leading to contraction of the peroneals which results in subsequent tendon strain. A longitudinal tear of the PB tendon is the most common pattern of injury. Peroneal tendon tears are often associated with chronic lateral ligamentous instability (i.e. ATFL, CFL). Patients will present with lateral ankle pain and a feeling of instability. Physical exam may demonstrate tenderness of the tendons, tendon subluxation, positive drawer test, and positive compression tests.

*Maffulli et al. reviewed the management of acute and chronic ankle instability.* **They found** that acute ankle injuries are best treated with brief immobilization and functional rehabilitation in most patients, with surgery reserved for high-demand athletes. Chronic ankle instability is multi-factorial and challenging to treat. Patients who fail rehabilitation may benefit from surgical ligamentous repair or reconstruction procedures. **They determined** that acute ankle instability is best managed nonoperatively, whereas ligamentous repair/reconstruction procedures are beneficial in the patient with chronic instability who fails rehabilitation.

*Philbin et al. reviewed peroneal tendon injuries and the role of the peroneal tendons in patients with ankle instability.* **They found** that 40% of patients who sustain an ankle sprain develop subsequent chronic lateral ankle pain. Peroneal tendon pathology, such as tears, subluxation, or dislocation, is one of several causes of chronic lateral ankle pain. **They determined** that proper diagnosis and management of peroneal tendon pathology in the patient with chronic lateral ankle pain is associated with favorable outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Tendon debridement and tubularization is indicated in patients with simple tears involving less than 50% of the tendon. However, groove deepening should be performed in conjunction with repair if a shallow groove is present.
Answer 3: Complex PB tears (>50%) are best treated with tenodesis to PL.
Answer 4: Interposition grafting is indicated in patients with complex PB and PL tears with preserved muscle excursion.
Answer 5: FHL/FDL tendon transfer is indicated in patients with complex PB and PL tears with no muscle excursion.
