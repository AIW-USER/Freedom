## Question

You are rounding on a patient who underwent lower extremity four-compartment fasciotomies and tibia intramedullary nail fixation two days ago. After being intubated and sedated for three days, the patient is now alert and questioning the reason for the incisions on his leg. As the chief resident, you're tasked with explaining the most sensitive and specific method of detecting compartment syndrome in a sedated and intubated patient, for which you diligently convey what method?

![[q219206-a-tibia.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. One-time needle measurement upon arrival in the intensive care
2. CT angiogram to evaluate for expanding hematoma
3. Continuous monitoring of compartment pressure
4. Repeated neurovascular checks by the same orthopedic surgeon
5. Repeated neurovascular examinations by different orthopedic surgeons

## Correct option

**3. Continuous monitoring of compartment pressure**

## Answer explanation

Diagnosing acute compartment syndrome (ACS) in obtunded or intubated patients poses a challenge for orthopedic surgeons. Utilizing objective compartment pressure monitoring is the most effective method for diagnosis. Continuous monitoring boasts a sensitivity of 94%, specificity of 98%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 93%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 99%.

The aforementioned sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were associated with a protocol that utilized a slit catheter placed within the anterior compartment of the lower leg. Clinicians should be cautious when using pressure measurements to guide decisions in patients at risk of ACS as spatial variation in compartment pressures does exist. It has been documented that the highest pressures are typically found within 5 cm of the fracture and more centrally in the muscle. It remains unclear whether obtaining pressures near the fracture or measuring further away would provide a more representative pressure for the compartment as a whole.

*Schmidt et al. reviewed the current guidelines for the identification and treatment of ACS.* **They concluded** ACS presents challenges in diagnosis and treatment, with surgical fasciotomy being the primary effective intervention. Delayed fasciotomy significantly impacts outcomes, **emphasizing** the importance of early intervention. Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment and intramuscular pressure (IMP) measurement. IMP measurement, ideally continuous, guides surgical decisions due to the variability and difficulty in quantifying clinical findings.

*McQueen et al. reviewed continuous IMP measurements utilizing a protocol that utilized a slit catheter placed within the anterior compartment of the lower leg.* *In their review, 850 patients met inclusion criteria with a mean age of 38 years old.* One hundred and fifty-two patients (17.9%) underwent fasciotomy for acute compartment syndrome: 141 were true positives, 6 false positives, and 5 false negatives. Among the 698 patients (82.1%) who didn't undergo fasciotomy, 689 had no evidence of late sequelae of ACS at a mean follow-up of 59 weeks. The estimated sensitivity of intramuscular pressure monitoring for suspected ACS was 94%, with specificity of 98%, positive predictive value of 93%, and negative predictive value of 99%.

Incorrect Answers:

Answer 1: A single measurement will not provide an accurate picture of intramuscular pressures in a patient with developing compartment syndrome.
Answer 2: Although expanding hematomas can be present in patients who have compartment syndrome, CTA has not been described as a diagnostic modality for compartment syndrome.
Answer 4&5: While neurovascular checks are essential to optimal patient care, this is a subjective measurement with variability between individual exams as well as different practitioners.
