## Question

A 3-year-old previously healthy male presents to the emergency department with right elbow pain after falling from a swing one hour prior. On physical exam, there is a gross elbow deformity, no skin puckering, moderate swelling, and no evidence of a poke hole. He is able to wiggle all fingers but is crying and not cooperative with sensory exam. His hand is pale and pulses are markedly diminished from the contralateral extremity. What is likely to be his definitive treatment?

## Options

1. Observation
2. Closed reduction and casting
3. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning
4. Open reduction internal fixation
5. Casting and delayed fixation

## Correct option

**3. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning**

## Answer explanation

A closed supracondylar humerus fracture with a compromised vascular status requires emergent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning.

Supracondylar fractures most often result from forcible hyperextension at the elbow. The periosteum acts as a hinge, but rotational forces may allow the fracture to tear the periosteum. This can result in gross displacement, button-holing of the brachialis muscle, and place the neurovascular structures at risk. As with most displaced fractures and dislocations, the first step is obtaining a reduction and re-evaluating pulses, as arteries may be kinked or in spasm. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) will likely restore pulses in this patient. Serial vascular exams are warranted in the post-operative period. If pulses do not return in a timely manner and the hand is pale, open exploration with possible vascular repair and possible fasciotomies is indicated.

*Brighton et al. provided commentary on strategies for the successful management of pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures.* **They propose** that most of these fractures can be managed with CRPP. They add that utilizing joystick wires and leverage pins may aid in reduction. Furthermore, crossed or lateral pin techniques with maximal spread and bicortical engagement are essential to maintain reduction and prevent subsequent malunion.

*Mulpuri et al. conducted a meta-analysis to establish evidence-based guidelines for the management of displaced supracondylar humerus fractures.* Based on their review, **they suggest** CRPP for displaced fractures, two or three lateral diverging pins, avoidance of a medial pin, and consideration of open reduction for displaced fractures following closed reduction if malunion occurs. **They conclude** that the management of pediatric supracondylar fractures remains controversial.

Figure A shows a Type III supracondylar right humerus fracture. Illustration A shows this fracture treated with closed reduction and 3 lateral divergent Kirschner wires.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A supracondylar fracture with an abnormal vascular exam cannot be observed.
Answer 2: A Type III supracondylar fracture should not be treated with closed reduction and casting.
Answer 4: CRPP should be attempted prior to open reduction.
Answer 5: Casting and delayed fixation would not address the urgency of the vascular dysfunction.
