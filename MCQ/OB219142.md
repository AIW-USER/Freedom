## Question

A 72-year-old female presents as a polytrauma patient after being involved in a motor vehicle rollover collision. Her only orthopedic injury is a type 3A open fracture as shown in Figure A. After initial stabilization in the trauma bay, the patient undergoes formal irrigation and debridement, external fixator application, and primary closure as seen in Figures B-C. There is difficulty maintaining an adequate reduction of the distal radius despite the placement of the external fixator with two connecting rods between the radius and metacarpal threaded pins. Which of the following would be considered the most appropriate next step in management to increase the stiffness of this external fixator system?

## Options

1. Remove one of the connecting rods
2. Convert the current 3mm diameter threaded pins in the metacarpal to 6mm diameter threaded pins
3. Decrease the distance between pins in the same bone segment
4. Move the connecting rods closer to the skin and underlying bone
5. Exchange current connecting rods for ones with a smaller diameter

## Correct option

**4. Move the connecting rods closer to the skin and underlying bone**

## Answer explanation

A conventional external fixator used for the temporizing stabilization of an open fracture relies on various biomechanical principles for maintaining adequate reduction. Moving the connecting rods closer to the skin and bone is a fast, reliable method to improve construct stiffness without the risk of major complications (Answer 4).

External fixators are incredibly versatile implants with a wide array of clinical indications in both acute injuries and chronic deformities. In the setting of acute trauma, external fixators are most used as a temporizing measure to stabilize a fracture with a planned second-stage procedure (i.e. intramedullary nailing or open reduction internal fixation) at a later point in time. This staged approach allows the patient to be stabilized of other injuries, provides a period of soft tissue rest and fracture edema subsidence, and equips the surgeon with additional time for definitive treatment planning. In the setting of these popular trauma pin-to-bar constructs, pins are placed into bone proximal and distal to the fracture utilizing a minimally invasive approach to minimizing soft tissue damage. The connecting rods are configured to the pins using connectors (Illustration A). A reduction maneuver is performed, and the connectors are tightened down to lock in the construct while the fracture is held in the reduced position (Illustration B). There are a variety of techniques used to maximize the construct stiffness. This includes utilizing larger pin diameters, coating the pins with hydroxyapatite, increasing the distance between pins in the same bone segment, decreasing the distance between the connecting rod and bone, increasing the number of connecting rods, or utilizing a multiplanar fixator (Illustration C).

Fragomen and Rozbruch provided their classic review article regarding the biomechanics of external fixation in orthopaedic surgery. Their predominant focus revolves around the indications and clinical application for external fixation use. They further discuss techniques utilized in order to maximize stability for unilateral (i.e. uniplanar/multiplanar) and circular ring fixator constructs.

*Bliven and colleagues provided a similar review of the basic principles of external fixation and various modern applications.* *They discuss the major benefits are most often encountered in the setting of trauma with the ability to stabilize a fracture without the need for open reduction or invasive surgery.* However, **the authors note** the drawbacks including the bulky frames restricting limb function, greater time and effort required for pin site care, and the risk of pin site infection. **They conclude** that technology continues to advance to more innovative systems, however, the basic principles for external fixation remain persistent.

*Sellei and colleagues performed a biomechanical study of the Hoffman 3 external fixation system and reviewed the advancement of external fixator design features that enhance construct stiffness.* **They found** the Hoffman 3 system provided superior stiffness compared to alternative similar external fixation systems predominantly owed to the thicker diameter connecting rods. **The authors concluded** that the rigidity of any fixation system configuration may be increased with greater intra-segment pin distance, decrease bone-to-rod distance, decreased bone-to-clamp distance, and double stacking of the frame assembly.

Figure A provided radiographic imaging of a highly comminuted distal radius fracture. Figures B and C show intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging confirming bicortical pins placed in the radius and metacarpal, respectively. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Removing one of the connecting rods would significantly reduce the stiffness of the construct. While a single rod may provide an axial distraction at a fracture site, placing a second rod (especially in a different plane) provides significant added rotational stability.
Answer 2: Converting the current 3mm diameter threaded pins in the metacarpal to 6mm diameter threaded pins would significantly increase the risk of fracturing the metacarpal. For this reason, each company makes varying pin sizes to accommodate appropriate sizing for the bone into which the pins are placed. Other, simpler steps should be taken first such as moving the connecting rods closer to the underlying bone or adding another connecting rod.
Answer 3: Increasing the distance between pins in the same bone segment would allow for better control of each segment via increased moment arm and therefore would increase construct stiffness.
Answer 5: Exchanging the connecting rods for ones with a smaller diameter would decrease the construct stiffness.
