Chapter: Spine MCQs — Chance Fracture (Flexion-Distraction Injury)

## Question

A 26-year-old male is brought to the emergency department following a high-speed motor vehicle accident. He complains of severe mid-back pain. On physical examination, there is a visible transverse ecchymosis across the anterior abdomen and palpable widening of the spinous processes at the L1-L2 level. Neurological examination is unremarkable (ASIA E). Lateral Radiograph shows increased distance between the L1 and L2 spinous processes with an associated 15% anterior compression of the L2 vertebral body. CT scan shows a transverse fracture line extending through the L2 spinous process, laminae, pedicles, and exiting through the superior endplate of the L2 vertebral body. MRI shows significant signal intensity (edema) within the L1-L2 interspinous ligaments and posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), but the L2-L3 disc space and annulus appear intact.

Which of the following represents the most appropriate management plan for this patient, and what is the primary anatomical justification for that choice?

## Options

1. Conservative: Immobilization in a hyperextension cast/brace for 12 weeks; justified by the purely osseous nature of the fracture line
2. Surgical: Posterior stabilization and fusion; justified by the presence of a "seatbelt sign" indicating high-risk occult intra-abdominal injury.
3. Surgical: Posterior instrumental stabilization (likely short-segment); justified by the disruption of the Posterior Ligamentous Complex (PLC) and the fracture's extension through the superior endplate.
4. Conservative: Early mobilization with a thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO); justified by the patient's intact neurological status and minimal vertebral body compression (<20%).

## Correct option

**3. Surgical: Posterior instrumental stabilization (likely short-segment); justified by the disruption of the Posterior Ligamentous Complex (PLC) and the fracture's extension through the superior endplate.**

## Answer explanation

Correct Answer: C

This is a classic description of a chance fracture.

While classic Chance fractures are often described as purely bony (which historically favored conservative management due to high healing potential of cancellous bone), this case describes a bony-ligamentous variant. The MRI evidence of signal intensity in the interspinous ligaments and PLL confirms disruption of the Posterior Ligamentous Complex (PLC). Ligamentous disruptions do not heal with the same predictability or strength as purely osseous fractures.

Conservative management in a hyperextension brace is highly successful only in purely bony Chance fractures where the fracture line passes entirely through the pedicles and vertebral body without significant ligamentous instability. The involvement of the PLL and interspinous ligaments in this patient significantly increases the risk of progressive kyphosis and non-union if treated non-operatively.

The exit of the fracture through the superior endplate (rather than a clean transverse bony break through the mid-body) often creates an unstable hinge point. In the presence of PLC mid-substance tears, the posterior "tension band" of the spine is lost. Surgical stabilization restores this tension band immediately.

Surgical Indications in Chance Fractures:

According to the TLICS (Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score), this injury would score highly:

 - Distraction mechanism: 4 points.

 - PLC Distraction (suspected/confirmed): 2–3 points.

 - Neurological status (Intact): 0 points.

 - Total Score: 6–7 (Surgical intervention is typically recommended for scores 5 or greater).

Key Takeaway for Exams

In thoracolumbar trauma, the integrity of the PLC is the "gatekeeper" for decision-making.

 - Bony Chance: Often Conservative (Hyperextension).

 - Ligamentous/Mixed Chance: Usually Surgical (Posterior Instrumentation).

Management of a Chance fracture depends on three critical factors: whether the injury is predominantly osseous or ligamentous, the integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex, and the neurological status of the patient. Associated intra-abdominal injuries must be addressed concurrently by the surgical team.

Non-Operative Management

Non-operative management is appropriate in a select subset of patients who fulfil all
of the following criteria: a purely osseous Chance fracture (fracture line through bone without significant ligamentous disruption), an intact PLC on MRI, no neurological deficit, no significant kyphotic deformity (less than 15–20°), and a TLICS score of ≤3.

The mainstay of conservative treatment is hyperextension bracing. A thoracolumbar spinal orthosis (TLSO) or a Jewett hyperextension brace is applied, which counteracts the flexion mechanism of injury by maintaining the spine in extension and promoting healing of the osseous fracture. The brace is worn for 12–16 weeks, during which the patient is mobilised with the brace in situ. Serial standing radiographs are obtained at 2-week intervals initially, then monthly, to monitor for progressive kyphosis or loss of reduction. Functional rehabilitation and core strengthening exercises are initiated once the fracture shows signs of union.

Purely osseous Chance fractures have a good healing potential with conservative management because cancellous bone-to-bone healing is reliable. Union rates exceeding 90% have been reported in appropriately selected patients.

However, strict compliance with the brace and close radiological follow-up are essential. Any progression of kyphosis beyond 10° from baseline, new neurological symptoms, or failure to achieve union by 16 weeks should prompt reconsideration towards surgical intervention.

Operative Management

Surgical treatment is indicated when one or more of the following conditions exist: a ligamentous or mixed osteoligamentous Chance fracture (soft tissue disruption does not heal reliably with bracing), disruption of the PLC (TLICS ≥5), neurological deficit, significant kyphotic deformity (>15–20°), associated injuries requiring prone or supine positioning that preclude bracing, or inability to comply with prolonged brace wear.

Posterior Short-Segment Pedicle Screw Fixation: This is the most commonly employed surgical technique. Pedicle screws are placed one level above and one level below the fractured vertebra (e.g., D9 and D11), and connected with rods. This provides a posterior tension band reconstruction that directly counteracts the flexion-distraction mechanism. The construct is placed in slight compression across the posterior elements to reduce the distraction component. If the bone quality is adequate and the fracture pattern permits, inclusion of the fractured vertebra (D10) with pedicle screws at the fracture level — a so-called short-segment fixation with index level screws — enhances construct stability and may improve correction of kyphosis.

The advantages of posterior-only fixation include direct access to the injured posterior tension band, relatively straightforward surgical technique, short operative time, and avoidance of the morbidity associated with anterior approaches at the thoracic level. At D10, the proximity of the diaphragm, pleura, and great vessels makes anterior approaches considerably more morbid.

Posterior Compression and Direct Repair: In purely osseous variants where anatomy permits, direct compression across the fracture through the pedicle screw construct can reduce the fracture and restore anatomical alignment. If a significant lamina fracture with dural exposure is present, dural integrity must be assessed and repaired if necessary.

Fusion Considerations: Whether formal posterolateral fusion is performed in addition to instrumentation remains a subject of debate. In ligamentous injuries, fusion is generally recommended because the disrupted ligaments do not heal with sufficient strength, and implant failure or progressive deformity may occur after implant removal if fusion is not achieved. In purely osseous injuries treated surgically (for instance, due to an associated neurological deficit), fusion may not be strictly necessary if osseous healing is anticipated, though most surgeons perform it as a standard practice. Autologous local bone graft from the decompression (if performed) or iliac crest bone graft is used.

Decompression: Formal decompression (laminectomy) is indicated only if there is neurological deficit with demonstrable cord or neural element compression on imaging. In the absence of anterior compression, posterior distraction and realignment through ligamentotaxis may indirectly decompress the neural elements. Laminectomy should be performed cautiously in the setting of a Chance fracture, as removal of the posterior elements in an already posteriorly disrupted spine can further destabilise the construct.

Management of Associated Injuries

As discussed, intra-abdominal injuries coexist frequently with Chance fractures and may take surgical priority. In cases of bowel perforation or mesenteric vascular injury, emergent laparotomy is performed first, and spinal fixation is staged or performed in the same sitting depending on the patient's haemodynamic stability and overall condition. Close coordination between the orthopaedic/spine team and the general/trauma surgery team is essential.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Following surgical fixation, early mobilisation is encouraged — typically within 24–48 hours with appropriate analgesia. A TLSO brace may be used for additional support for 6–8 weeks postoperatively, though the need for this is reduced with rigid pedicle screw fixation. Progressive core strengthening, proprioceptive training, and functional rehabilitation are initiated under physiotherapy guidance. Neurological recovery, if applicable, is monitored serially using the ASIA scale. Implant removal is generally considered after 12–18 months if solid fusion is confirmed, particularly in young patients, though this remains a clinical decision.

Prognosis

The overall prognosis is favourable with timely management. Purely osseous Chance fractures treated conservatively or surgically have excellent outcomes with near-normal spinal function. Ligamentous injuries have a less predictable course and may result in residual stiffness or pain at the fused segment. Neurological outcomes depend on the severity of the initial cord injury and the timing of decompression.
