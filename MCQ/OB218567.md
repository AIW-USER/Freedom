## Options

1. Radiograph shown in Figure B with an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise and severe sinus tarsi pain
2. Radiograph shown in Figure B with an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise and severe sinus tarsi and ankle pain
3. Radiographs shown in Figure C with an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise and with severe sinus tarsi and ankle pain
4. Radiographs shown in Figure D with a valgus hindfoot which corrects with Coleman block testing, and an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise
5. Radiographs shown in Figure D with a valgus hindfoot which does not correct with Coleman block testing, and an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise

## Correct option

**1. Radiograph shown in Figure B with an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise and severe sinus tarsi pain**

## Answer explanation

The patient underwent a triple (calcaneocuboid, talonavicular, and subtalar joints) arthrodesis. This procedure is typically performed for patients with Stage III posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI), characterized by subtalar arthritis, arch collapse, severe sinus tarsi pain, and an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise (Answer 1).

PTTI is the most common cause of adult-acquired flatfoot deformity. The pathologic process is hallmarked by attenuation and tenosynovitis of the posterior tibial tendon. This tendinopathy leads to a predictable pattern of medial arch collapse and arthritic change about the foot. Illustration A is a chart of the classification system for PTTI. Treatment for early-stage PTTI is centered around bracing and orthotics. With disease progression, continued symptoms often demand surgical intervention. Stage III posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI), characterized by subtalar arthritis, an arch collapse deformity, severe sinus tarsi pain, and an inability to perform a single-leg heel raise, as is the case in our patient, is best addressed with a triple arthrodesis. If tibiotalar arthritis was present, the inclusion of that joint into the fusion construct would be necessitated.

Ahmad and Pedowitz offer a comprehensive review of the triple-arthrodesis for advanced-stage PTTI. Indications, as described by the authors, include rigid hindfoot arthritis with a preserved tibiotalar joint. They highlight the importance of viewing this procedure as a salvage procedure and emphasize the importance of exhaustion of nonoperative management prior to surgical intervention. They note that both single and dual-incision approaches can be utilized, and caution surgeons regarding the complication profile for the procedure.

Seybold and Coetzee provide a comprehensive surgical technique for triple arthrodesis. They note that careful patient selection is crucial. Intraoperatively, thorough joint preparation is a key step in ensuring a solid fusion mass. In addition, they offer recommendations for mid and hindfoot reduction in order to achieve an acceptable fusion position. *They also reported on the findings of Pell et al. in which* **the authors observed** a 98% fusion rate at a minimum follow-up of 2 years for triple arthrodesis.

Figure A demonstrates a patient status post triple arthrodesis. Figure B demonstrates advanced calcaneocuboid, talonavicular, and subtalar arthritis, in the setting of a well-preserved ankle joint, consistent with Stage III PTTI. Figure C demonstrates hindfoot and ankle arthritis, consistent with Stage IV PTTI. Figure D demonstrates loss of medial arch as is typically seen in early-stage PTTI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 3: Patients with ankle pain or radiographs consistent with ankle arthritis are best treated with tibiotalocalcaneal fusion.
Answers 4 and 5: Patients with early-stage PTTI are best treated with a combination of tendon transfers, osteotomies, and soft-tissue balancing procedures. Triple arthrodesis would be overly aggressive for both of these patients.
