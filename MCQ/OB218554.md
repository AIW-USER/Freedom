## Question

A 10-year-old male patient is referred to your clinic by his primary care provider with concerns for a left knee sprain which he sustained during a football game. The patient is unable to bear weight without pain. His radiographs are normal, and he arrives with a recently completed MRI, which is available for review in Figure A. Which of the following is the most common long-term sequelae of this patients injury?

![[q218554-a-knee-mri-peds.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Genu valgum
2. Genu varus
3. Leg length discrepancy
4. Persistent valgus laxity
5. Persistent varus laxity

## Correct option

**3. Leg length discrepancy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a Salter-Harris Type I physeal fracture of his distal femur. His injury's most common long-term sequelae is leg length discrepancy secondary to physeal injury (Answer 3).

Distal femur physeal injuries are common fractures in pediatric patients and are often sustained secondary to direct knee trauma. While the most common sub-type is a Salter-Harris Type II fracture, occult fractures (Salter-Harris I) can occur through the zone of hypertrophy of the physis (Illustration A). These injuries are frequently misdiagnosed as knee sprains and are commonly missed on plain radiographs. While Salter-Harris Type II fractures are associated with the development of angular deformities, Salter-Harris Type I and V fractures can lead to the development of a leg-length discrepancy. Most of the lower extremity's growth comes from the distal femur and proximal tibia, and injuries to the distal femur physis can cause a discrepancy of up to 9mm/year up to skeletal maturity.

*Arkader et al. performed a multi-center prospective review of pediatric patients who sustained distal femur physeal injuries.* *Their study included 73 patients with a mean age of 10 years.* *18 patients had sustained a Salter-Harris Type I fracture, with a complication rate of 29.4%.* *Overall, 9 patients had a leg-length discrepancy at final follow-up and 8 patients had an angular deformity.* **They note** that these injuries, specifically Salter-Harris Type II injuries, are associated with a high rate of subsequent complications.

Eid and Hafez report on 151 patients with distal femur physeal injuries. Mean age at the time of injury was 12.3 years, and patients were followed for an average of 8.2 years. 39 patients sustained Salter-Harris Type I fractures; of these 39 patients, 15 (38.4%) of patients had shortening or premature growth arrest, and 11 (28.2%) had a measurable angular deformity. The authors conclude that these injuries are fraught with complications, and all injuries, regardless of subtype, require radiographic surveillance.

Figure A demonstrates a combined coronal and sagittal T2-weighted MRI of a skeletally immature left knee, with increased T2-fluid intense signal along the physis consistent with a Salter-Harris Type I fracture. Notice the distinct lack of signal at the proximal tibial physis. Illustration A is a micro-histological cross-section of the physis; section D is the hypertrophic zone, which contains the zone of provisional calcification, the site of Salter-Harris type physeal injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Angular deformity typically occurs secondary to Salter-Harris Type II injuries, with the direction of subsequent deformity dependent on which portion of the physis remains uninjured.
Answers 4 and 5: Varus and valgus laxity and or instability can occur secondary to collateral or cruciate ligament injury, neither of which are present in this patient. Injuries to the collaterals are common in distal femur physeal injuries, however.
