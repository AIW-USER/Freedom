Chapter: Pediatric Orthopedics MCQs — Congenital Radio-Ulnar Synostosis

## Question

A patient has presented with congenital radio ulnar synostosis. On X ray there is a long osseous radio ulnar synostosis visible and the radial head is hypoplastic and posteriorly dislocated. As per the Cleary and Omer classification, the deformity will be classified as

## Options

1. Type 3
2. Type 2
3. Type 4
4. Type 1

## Correct option

**1. Type 3**

## Answer explanation

CONGENITAL RADIO ULNAR SYNOSTOSIS

The condition refers to the congenital presence of a bony bridge connecting the proximal radius and ulna. Forearm begins as a single cartilaginous anlage that divides from distal to proximal into the radius and ulna in the 7th week in utero. Failure of differentiation results in synostosis in proximal aspect of the forearm between two bones that fixes the forearm in pronation (average 30 degree pronation). The condition is bilateral in more than half of cases and almost 20% cases may have a positive family history. Males are slightly more commonly involved. Apert and Klinefelter syndrome are common associations.

The deformity often goes unnoticed (average age of diagnosis is 6 years) especially in unilateral cases unless fixed forearm pronation is limiting functional activities as many people develop compensatory motions as mentioned below:

- shoulder abduction - compensates for loss of active pronation

- shoulder adduction - compensates for loss of active supination

 Diagnosis is made from X ray and the deformity is classified by either of the two ways:

- Wilkie Classification
- Type 1: Medullary canals of radius and ulna are joined. Usually bilateral. Radial head is not dislocated.
- Type 2: Radial head is dislocated (anteriorly or posteriorly) and its usually unilateral.

- Cleary and Omer classification (better)
- Type 1: Synostosis is cartilaginous (non osseous) and radial head is normal and not dislocated
- Type 2: Osseous synostosis is there and radial head is normal and not dislocated
- Type 3: Long osseous synostosis is visible and radial head is hypoplastic and posteriorly dislocated
- Type 4: Short osseous synostosis is visible on X rays and radial head is mushroom shaped and dislocated anteriorly

TREATMENT: Treatment is usually observation in patients who are asymptomatic (especially unilateral cases). Operative management is indicated in patients with deformity limiting activities of daily living (usually in cases with pronation deformity > 60 degrees). Optimal age is taken to be 3-6 years.

Surgical Procedures: Surgical excision of synostosis with interposition of soft tissues like anconeus muscle gives only slight correction (owing to high degree of soft tissue contractures) and recurrence occurs in almost 100% cases unless interposition involves a vascularized fascio-fat graft (recurrence is negligible). Procedure of choice is Forearm Derotational Osteotomy. As described by Lin et Percutaneous drill assisted osteotomies of radius and ulna can be done and then a POP case given. Post operatively 10 days later, manipulation in plaster is done to place the forearm in desired functional position which is usually 0-30 degree pronation in unilateral cases while in bilateral cases dominant forearm is placed in 20 degree pronation and non dominant in neutral (c.f. olden days when mobiles and laptops were not there non dominant forearm was placed in 20 degree supination).

Notable complications with Lin et al method include: Loss of correction (usually up to 20 degree), compartment syndrome, PIN palsy.

Answer: 1
