## Question

A 73-year-old female with a history of low back pain presents to the emergency department after a fall at home. She is ambulatory and neurovascularly intact without long tract signs in the emergency department. She is ultimately found to have an isolated L4 compression fracture. Assuming the patient is treated nonoperatively, which of the following is most accurate regarding her risk of sustaining another fragility fracture in the future?

## Options

1. Vertebral compression fractures are often isolated and she is not at risk for another compression fracture in the future
2. Avoiding exercise will help lower her risk of sustaining another fragility fracture
3. Starting teriparatide will likely increase her bone density, but will put her at risk for atypical femur fractures
4. She is not a candidate for osteoporosis treatment unless a DEXA scan reveals a T score <-2.5
5. Without osteoporosis treatment, there is roughly a 40% chance she would sustain an additional fragility fracture within the next two years

## Correct option

**5. Without osteoporosis treatment, there is roughly a 40% chance she would sustain an additional fragility fracture within the next two years**

## Answer explanation

Osteoporotic compression fractures are a type of fragility fracture. They commonly occur in elderly, post-menopausal females, and are the most common type of fragility fracture. Once a patient is found to have one compression fracture, they are at risk for future compression fractures. The majority of isolated compression fractures can be treated nonoperatively. Operative treatment is typically reserved for patients with persistent pain after about 4-6 weeks, though some surgeons now advocate for earlier surgical intervention. Surgery typically involves either kyphoplasty or vertebroplasty. Regardless of whether the patient is treated nonoperatively or operatively, it is paramount that these patients undergo a bone health work-up, as treatment for osteoporosis can significantly lower the risk of future fragility fractures in this population.

*Barton et al. conducted a large-scale retrospective cohort study of 2933 patients who presented with a new vertebral compression fracture.* *The mean follow-up time was 2 years post fracture.* *Of these patients, 98% had not received a DEXA scan in the 2 years preceding the fracture or 1 year after the fracture.* Only 7% of patients were started on antiresorptive therapy after their fracture; 73% of patients were never prescribed antiresorptive therapy. Within 2 years after the initial fracture, 38% of patients sustained a second fragility fracture. **The authors concluded** that patients with fragility fractures are often undertreated, and that appropriate workup and treatment is critical to lowering the risk of future fractures.

*Gupta et al. performed a retrospective cohort study of 399 adult spinal deformity patients who underwent deformity correction and fusion.* Specifically, they evaluated whether these patients had been diagnosed with osteoporosis and whether they had received treatment before surgery. **They found** that only 32.8% of patients had a diagnosis of osteoporosis before surgery, but only 34.4% of these patients had received treatment for osteoporosis. While the population studied is slightly different than those with osteoporotic compression fractures, this study further highlights that the recognition and treatment of osteoporosis is lacking generally across the field of spine surgery.

Muñoz et al. authored a detailed review article about the diagnosis and treatment of osteoporosis. They highlighted that treatment consists of a multi-disciplinary approach with medication and activity modifications. In particular, they reviewed the importance of exercise to increase bone mineral density and outlined when medications such as bisphosphonates or teriparatide should be considered.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Sustaining one fragility fracture places patients at an elevated risk for future fragility fractures.
Answer 2: Exercise, even high-intensity activity, can increase bone mineral density and should be encouraged in patients with osteoporosis.
Answer 3: Teriparatide can increase bone density but does not carry the same risk of atypical femur fractures that bisphosphonates do.
Answer 4: The patient has already sustained a fragility fracture and should be treated for osteoporosis even if her T score is not < -2.5.
