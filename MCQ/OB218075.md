## Question

## Options

1. Failure of radial head internal fixation
2. Persistent instability
3. Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) injury
4. Post-traumatic stiffness
5. Superficial branch of the radial nerve injury

## Correct option

**4. Post-traumatic stiffness**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a "terrible triad" injury, which can be treated with either radial head open reduction and internal fixation versus arthroplasty, coronoid fixation, and lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) repair. The most common complication associated with this procedure is post-traumatic elbow stiffness (Answer 4).

"Terrible triad" injuries involve radial head/neck fractures, coronoid fractures, and elbow dislocations indicating lateral ligamentous structure (LUCL) incompetence. The mechanism of injury typically involves a fall onto an extended arm that results in a combination of valgus, axial, and posterolateral rotary forces on the elbow. The associated ligamentous and capsular structures tend to fail in a predictable sequence from lateral to medial: (1) LUCL, (2) anterior capsule (associated with the tip of the coronoid), (3) medial ulnar collateral ligament (MUCL). Ultimately, this constellation of injuries requires surgical intervention to restore elbow function. Small displaced radial head fractures without comminution can undergo internal fixation, but fractures involving >40% of the radial head surface and those with significant comminution are best treated with radial head arthroplasty. Associated coronoid fractures in this setting typically involve >50% of the coronoid height (Type III) and should be fixed through the radial head/neck defect laterally prior to performing the radial head replacement. The LUCL, which is most often avulsed proximally from its lateral epicondylar origin, can then be repaired, typically with suture anchors or transosseous sutures. If persistent instability is noted, especially with extension beyond 30 degrees, the MUCL should be assessed for injury and repaired accordingly, as this is often the last structure that fails in the injury sequence, as mentioned above.

*Rineer et al. reviewed a cohort of 121 Mason Type II radial head fractures (displaced > 2mm) treated over the course of six years.* **The authors noted** that only 30 (25%) were classified as having any degree of cortical contact, while 91 (75%) had no cortical contact. Of the ones without cortical contact, 91% were associated with a complex elbow injury. **The authors concluded** that complete loss of cortical contact of at least one fracture fragment is strongly predictive of a complex injury pattern.

*Chen et al. published a comparison between radial head replacement and open reduction and internal fixation in the clinical treatment of unstable, multi-fragmented radial head fractures.* *The authors performed a prospective, randomized, controlled trial of 45 patients and concluded that metal prosthetic radial head replacement was more effective than traditional open reduction and internal fixation in clinical practice.*

*Ring et al. published on 11 patients with posterior dislocation of the elbow and fractures of the radial head and coronoid, four of which had radial head resection instead of repair/replacement and none of which had fixation of the associated coronoid fracture.* **They concluded** that elbow fracture-dislocations that involve a fracture of the coronoid process in addition to a fracture of the radial head are very unstable and prone to numerous complications and that, with operative treatment, the surgeon should attempt to restore stability by providing radiocapitellar contact (preserving the radial head when possible and replacing it with a prosthesis otherwise), repairing the lateral collateral ligament, and perhaps performing internal fixation of the coronoid fracture.

Figures A-C are AP, oblique, and lateral radiographs of the elbow, respectively, showing a Mason IV radial head fracture and a large associated coronoid fracture in the setting of a posterolaterally dislocated elbow joint. Illustration A demonstrates the coronoid fracture with a red arrow.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Failure of radial head internal fixation is of concern when this is used; however, in this patient, given the age and comminution, a radial head replacement would likely be more appropriate.
Answer 2: Persistent instability can occur, but is less likely when the coronoid and LUCL +/- MUCL are repaired appropriately, as would be indicated in this patient. In general, post-traumatic stiffness of the elbow is more likely to occur than persistent instability if all of the aforementioned structures are appropriately repaired.
Answer 3: PIN injury can occur during the lateral approach to the elbow, but this does not occur as commonly as post-traumatic stiffness. During the approach, the PIN will be protected if the dissection remains proximal to the annular ligament, release of the supinator is performed along the posterior border of the radius with the arm in full pronation, and anteromedial retraction is minimized.
Answer 5: The radial nerve and its superficial branch are more anterior at this level when a lateral approach to the elbow is used, and should be out of the operative field if the joint is not entered anteriorly.
