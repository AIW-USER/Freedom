## Question

Figures A and B are the radiographs of a 76-year-old male who sustained a fall early today. The patient reports having a successful TKA performed 3 years ago. Since the time of surgery, he has reported no pain or issues with his prosthesis. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

## Options

1. Open reduction internal fixation only
2. Open reduction internal fixation with revision arthroplasty
3. Retrograde intramedullary nailing only
4. Revision arthroplasty only
5. CT scan following external fixation

## Correct option

**4. Revision arthroplasty only**

## Answer explanation

Radiographs demonstrate a periprosthetic distal femur fracture occurring distal to the anterior flange in a patient with poor bone stock. This should be treated with revision arthroplasty (distal femoral replacement).

Periprosthetic distal femur fractures are becoming more common as we continue to perform more TKAs on an aging population. These injuries are most commonly classified utilizing the Lewis and Rorabeck or Su classifications. Treatment largely depends on fracture location, implant stability and host bone stock. Fractures occurring proximal to a stable prosthesis can be treated with either retrograde IMN or ORIF depending on whether or not the femoral component will accommodate an intramedullary nail. Fractures occurring distal to the anterior flange can be treated with ORIF or revision arthroplasty depending on the stability of the implant and the amount of host bone available for fixation. Radiographs in Figure A and B demonstrate a very distal fracture with minimal intact host bone available for fixation as well as the medial condyle broken free and thus, the best treatment option would be a distal femoral replacement (revision arthroplasty).

*Tharani et al review the treatment of distal femur periprosthetic fractures.* **They report** that treatment should focus on returning the patient to their pre injury functional status and that fracture characteristics and implant stability should be critically evaluated. **They conclude** that revision arthroplasty should be performed in patients in which stable fixation and adequate alignment cannot be achieved by other means.

*Kim et al review periprosthetic fractures after total knee arthroplasty.* *They report on a new classification for periprosthetic fractures in which Type III is defined as a periprosthetic fracture that is reducible or irreducible in a patient with poor bone stock or implants that are loose or malpositioned.* **They conclude** that revision arthroplasty is the best treatment for this situation.

*Kuzyk et al review revision TKA for the management of periprosthetic fractures.* **They report** that variable outcomes have been achieved following osteosynthesis of complex distal femur periprosthetic fractures. **They conclude** that revision arthroplasty results in the ability to reliably restore alignment, rotation and stability couples with early mobilization, making it an ideal procedure for many complex periprosthetic distal femur fractures.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs demonstrating a Su III periprosthetic distal femur fracture with poor host bone stock distally. Illustration A are the postoperative radiographs following distal femoral replacement. Illustration B outlines the Su classification. Illustration C outlines the Lewis and Rorabeck classification.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: There is not enough host bone distally to reliably perform ORIF in this patient.
Answer 3: Retrograde IMN is not a valid treatment option for periprosthetic distal femur fractures occurring distal to the anterior flange of the prosthesis.
Answer 5: Obtaining a CT scan following external fixation would not provide any useful information that would change treatment in this patient.
