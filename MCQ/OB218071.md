## Question

A 52-year-old male is participating in an arm wrestling competition when he hears a loud snap. He presents to the emergency room and is noted to have a humeral shaft fracture, as seen in Figure A. He is placed in a Sarmiento brace, but at his follow-up visit two weeks later he is still having significant pain and requests operative intervention. Which of the following exploits an intramuscular approach that can be used for this injury with an internervous plane?

![[q218071-a-left-humeral-shaft-fx.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Deltopectoral approach
2. Anterior approach
3. Triceps-splitting approach
4. Paratricipital approach
5. Deltoid split approach

## Correct option

**2. Anterior approach**

## Answer explanation

The anterior approach to the humerus utilizes an intramuscular approach which splits the dual-innervated brachialis muscle between the radial and musculocutaneous nerves.

Humeral shaft fractures are relatively common upper extremity fractures. While many are amenable to nonoperative management, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) may be indicated with persistent pain or fracture site motion. Several approaches to the humeral shaft may be utilized. A posterior approach to the humeral shaft can be performed by using a triceps-splitting or a paratricipital approach but the radial nerve should be protected, regardless. An anterior approach can provide an extensile approach to the humeral shaft. It is performed by retracting the biceps muscle and splitting the brachialis muscle belly which has a dual innervation with the musculocutaneous and radial nerves, protecting it from denervation.

Figure A shows an AP radiograph of a left arm demonstrating a comminuted humeral shaft fracture.

*Gerwin et al. described alternative exposures of the posterior humeral diaphysis.* Specifically, they worked to describe the course of the radial nerve with reference to different posterior humeral exposures, **noting** that it crossed at an average of 20 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle and 14 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle. **They noted** identification and mobilization of the radial nerve as well as reflection of the medial and lateral heads of the triceps improved the amount of proximal humerus that could be exposed from posteriorly.

*Capo et al. reviewed different exposures for humeral fracture fixation.* **They note** that midshaft humerus fractures can be plated from anterior or posterior but more proximal fractures can be difficult to visualize from posteriorly and an anterior approach may be necessary. Regardless of which approach is used, neurovascular structures should be identified and respected.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The deltopectoral approach can be extended into an anterolateral approach but, on its own, it has an intermuscular plane (pectoralis major/deltoid), rather than the use of a brachialis split.
Answer 3: The triceps splitting approach uses an intramuscular split but does not split two nervous planes, as the triceps is completely innervated by the radial nerve.
Answer 4: The paratricipital approach works in windows medial and lateral to the triceps muscle/tendon and is not intramuscular in nature.
Answer 5: The deltoid split approach can be used for humeral intramedullary nailing. It does utilize an intramuscular split but only the axillary nerve is affected.
