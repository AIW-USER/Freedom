## Question

At a multidisciplinary tumor board, five patients with metastatic femoral lesions scheduled for intramedullary nail stabilization are discussed. Based on the information below, which patient has the highest preoperative risk of local tumor progression after fixation?

## Options

1. 61-year-old with a solitary renal cell carcinoma femoral metastasis; intramedullary nail and postoperative radiation planned
2. 63-year-old with estrogen-receptor–positive breast carcinoma and a 9-cm diaphyseal lesion and widespread skeletal metastases; antegrade nail planned
3. 64-year-old with non–small cell lung carcinoma and liver metastases on systemic chemotherapy; retrograde distal femoral nail planned
4. 60-year-old with multiple myeloma on antiresorptive therapy; impending subtrochanteric fracture; antegrade nail plus adjuvant radiation planned
5. 65-year-old with castration-resistant prostate carcinoma presenting with a completed distal femur pathologic fracture; retrograde nail planned

## Correct option

**1. 61-year-old with a solitary renal cell carcinoma femoral metastasis; intramedullary nail and postoperative radiation planned**

## Answer explanation

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) histology is the most consistent preoperative predictor of local tumor progression after intramedullary nail stabilization. In contrast, factors such as visceral metastases, other skeletal metastases, fracture status, lesion size, or nail direction have not shown a clear association with progression in contemporary cohorts (answer choice 1).

Metastatic disease of the extremity is the most common cause of destructive bone lesions in adults. A systematic initial evaluation (history, exam, radiographs, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) can identify the primary tumor in about 85% of cases; if occult, biopsy is essential to exclude a primary bone tumor. Common primary malignancies with bony metastasis include breast, lung, thyroid, kidney, and prostate, with frequent appendicular involvement in the proximal femur and humerus. Treatment goals include pain control, preservation of independence, and fracture prevention, typically integrating surgical stabilization and radiation when indicated. Except for cases involving the femoral neck, a cephalomedullary nail with adjuvant radiation remains the most common modality for stabilization of femoral lesions; uniquely, RCC’s relative radioresistance and propensity for lesion progression support the consideration of wide resection/reconstruction in select solitary lesions.

*Arpornsuksant and colleagues reviewed 122 adults who underwent intramedullary nailing for impending or completed long-bone pathologic fractures.* **They found** RCC histology (but not visceral metastases, other skeletal metastases, radiation therapy, systemic therapy, anti-resorptive therapy, fracture type, or direction of nail insertion) was associated with local progression; additionally, patients with local progression tended to be younger than those who did not progress (56±8 years versus 63±10 years; p = 0.02). **They concluded** that, while progression and reoperation are generally uncommon, renal cell carcinoma confers elevated risk, warranting caution against indiscriminate intramedullary nailing.

*Alvi and Damron retrospectively analyzed 96 femoral/humeral stabilizations performed with a strategy favoring full-length intramedullary devices and long-stem arthroplasty.* **They reported** 12% local progression, 6.3% reoperation for symptomatic failure, and 12.5% physiologic (likely embolic) complications, concluding that while “protecting the entire bone” may lower reoperation risk in some cases, the rarity of new in-bone lesions must be balanced against embolic risks.

*Miller et al. reviewed 112 intramedullary reconstructions for metastatic disease and* **identified** 10 revisions at a median of 17.9 months for causes including surgeon error, tumor progression, nonunion, and hardware failure. Progressive RCC contributed to several revisions; **they concluded** that failures may be minimized with proper technique and, when feasible, intralesional curettage/resection for RCC.

Incorrect Answer Choices
Answer Choices 2-5: Although primary tumor characteristics, metastatic burden, adjuvant radiation, or systemic therapies may play a role in affecting overall patient outcomes, they have not been shown as predictors of local progression.
