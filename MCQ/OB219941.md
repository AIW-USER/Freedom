## Question

A 25-year-old female undergoes reverse periacetabular osteotomy after progressive hip pain and failed conservative management. Which of the following imaging parameters are most indicative of the pathology that led to this surgical decision?

## Options

1. Lateral Center edge angle = 18 degrees, Positive Posterior Wall Sign
2. Lateral Center edge angle = 21 degrees, Negative Posterior Wall Sign
3. Lateral Center edge angle = 23 degrees, Tonnis Angle 8
4. Lateral Center edge angle = 30 degrees, Positive Posterior Wall Sign
5. Lateral Center edge angle = 30 degrees, Tonnis Angle 8

## Correct option

**1. Lateral Center edge angle = 18 degrees, Positive Posterior Wall Sign**

## Answer explanation

A REVERSE periacetabular osteotomy is most often performed in the setting of hip dysplasia, namely acetabular retroversion, which is consistent with a lateral center edge angle of < 20 degrees and a positive posterior wall sign (Answer 1).

Hip dysplasia is characterized by abnormal development of the hip joint, which can lead to instability, abnormal joint loading, and early osteoarthritis. Radiographic parameters are essential for diagnosing and assessing the severity of hip dysplasia. The lateral center-edge angle (LCEA) assesses the degree of lateral coverage of the femoral head by the acetabulum. An LCEA below 20 degrees indicates dysplasia, with lower angles corresponding to more severe forms. The Tönnis angle, also known as the acetabular index, measures the slope of the weight-bearing zone of the acetabulum; an angle above 10 degrees is considered dysplastic, suggesting insufficient acetabular coverage.

Another key indicator is the posterior wall sign, which evaluates the position of the posterior acetabular wall relative to the center of the femoral head. A positive posterior wall sign (when the posterior wall falls medially to the center) suggests posterior instability. Together, these radiographic markers guide clinicians in diagnosis and treatment planning, which may range from observation in mild cases to surgical reorientation, such as periacetabular osteotomy, in more severe cases to improve joint stability and delay degenerative changes.

Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) and reverse PAO are surgical procedures used to treat specific types of acetabular deformities. Each of these procedures aims to correct acetabular dysplasia by rotating the acetabulum to improve femoral head coverage and stability. A reverse PAO is used for cases where the acetabulum is excessively retroverted.

*Vahedi et al. reviewed their series of 51 patients with acetabular retroversion who had undergone femoroacetabular osteoplasty (FAO).* *They compared this cohort with 550 patients without dysplasia or retroversion who underwent FAO by the same surgeon.* **The authors report** a lower mean modified Harris hip score and Short-Form 36 Health Survey and a higher percentage of failure among retroversion patients compared to the controls. These results indicate that acetabular retroversion resulting in femoroacetabular impingement may be treated by FAO. Still, the outcome appears to be less optimal compared to patients with femoroacetabular impingement and no evidence of dysplasia and acetabular retroversion.

Zurmühle et al. reviewed their series of 279 hips with symptomatic acetabular retroversion undergoing either anteverting periacetabular-osteotomy (PAO) or acetabular rim trimming through a surgical hip dislocation. They report increased survivorship at 10 years in hips undergoing anteverting PAO for acetabular retroversion compared with acetabular rim trimming. Based on their results, the authors conclude that anteverting PAO may be the more appropriate treatment for hips with substantial acetabular retroversion.

*Peters et al. prospectively collected clinical data on 60 hips with symptomatic femoroacetabular impingement that had radiographic evidence of acetabular retroversion.* *The authors used an image-based treatment algorithm examining measurements of acetabular coverage (LCEA and the posterior wall sign) and condition of acetabular cartilage to direct treatment of acetabular retroversion.* *This led them to perform a PAO in 30 hips and a surgical dislocation and osteochondroplasty (SDO) of the femoral head-neck junction and acetabular rim in the remaining 30 patients.* Both treatment groups saw improvement in their Harris Hip score, and 90% experienced elimination of the crossover sign and correction of the posterior wall sign. **The authors conclude** that acetabular retroversion, deficient posterior and/or lateral acetabular coverage, and intact hyaline cartilage can be effectively treated with acetabular reorientation, while retroverted hips with anterior over-coverage but sufficient posterior coverage are effectively treated with osteochondroplasty of the acetabulum and proximal femur.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: An LCEA of 21 is considered borderline, and a negative posterior wall sign would be normal. If this patient was a candidate for PAO, they would not need a REVERSE PAO, as the posterior wall sign is negative.
Answer 3: An LCEA of 23 is borderline but a Tonnis angle <10 is normal. This patient would not require a REVERSE PAO.
Answer 4: LCEA > 25 is considered normal despite the positive posterior wall sign, indicating retroversion.
Answer 5: LCEA > 25 and Tonnis < 10 are considered normal.
