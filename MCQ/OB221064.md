## Question

A 58-year-old man with end-stage post-traumatic ankle arthritis presents to discuss surgical options. He is an active individual who enjoys hiking and wants to maintain as much functional mobility as possible. He subsequently undergoes total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) with a modern fixed-bearing prosthesis, and two years postoperatively, he undergoes 3D gait analysis during level walking and stair ascent. Based on biomechanical gait analysis, which parameter would be expected to show the greatest difference between this patient's TAA and a matched ankle arthrodesis patient during level walking?

## Options

1. Hip range of motion in the sagittal plane
2. Peak plantarflexion power generation
3. Walking speed and cadence
4. Vertical ground reaction force at heel strike
5. Coronal plane ankle eversion

## Correct option

**2. Peak plantarflexion power generation**

## Answer explanation

Peak plantarflexion power generation is significantly greater in TAA patients compared to ankle arthrodesis patients (Answer 2).

Total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) patients demonstrate superior sagittal plane motion and power generation compared to ankle arthrodesis patients. During level walking, TAA provides approximately 43% greater sagittal ankle range of motion (21.1° vs 14.7°) and significantly greater peak plantarflexion power generation during push-off. Segmental motion is also enhanced, with both forefoot-to-tibia and hindfoot-to-tibia motion significantly greater in TAA, allowing gait mechanics that more closely approximate normal ankle function, although neither procedure fully restores normal gait. In contrast, arthrodesis patients develop compensatory strategies, including increased hip range of motion and altered ground reaction forces to accommodate the loss of ankle mobility. These differences may be amplified during stair ascent.

Brockett and Chapman provide a comprehensive introduction to ankle biomechanics, covering the complex bony anatomy, joint motions, and ligamentous structures that contribute to ankle stability and function. They review normal gait cycle movements and conclude that surgical interventions such as total ankle replacement or ankle fusion alter these biomechanical patterns.

*Singer et al. compared gait analysis and patient-reported outcomes in 17 patients who underwent total ankle arthroplasty and 17 who underwent ankle arthrodesis for isolated end-stage ankle osteoarthritis and compared these with 10 healthy controls.* *Patients who underwent arthroplasty demonstrated gait patterns more closely resembling normal gait than arthrodesis patients, with significantly greater sagittal plane motion (18.1° versus 13.7°) and dorsiflexion (11.9° versus 6.8°), though plantar flexion remained abnormal in both groups, and neither group achieved fully normalized gait.* Despite biomechanical differences favoring arthroplasty, **they conclude** that both treatment groups showed similar improvements in patient-reported outcome scores on the Ankle Osteoarthritis Scale and Short Form-36.

*Sanders et al. confirmed these findings during both level walking and stair ascent,* **showing** TAA patients had significantly greater sagittal ankle ROM (25° vs 17.1° during stair ascent), greater forefoot-tibia and hindfoot-tibia motion, and higher peak plantarflexion power compared to arthrodesis patients. **They conclude** that total ankle replacement better preserves biomechanical function during activities of daily living beyond level walking.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Ankle arthrodesis patients often demonstrate increased hip ROM as a compensatory mechanism for the loss of ankle motion. This represents an adaptation to maintain functional gait rather than a primary difference favoring TAA. The hip compensates for the fused ankle by increasing its excursion during the gait cycle.
Answer 3: While some studies have shown modest improvements in walking speed with TAA compared to arthrodesis, the differences are not as pronounced as the discrepancy in plantarflexion power generation between the two procedures.
Answer 4: Arthrodesis patients may actually show increased transient force at heel strike as a compensatory mechanism. This represents an adaptation in the arthrodesis group, but it is not the most substantial biomechanical distinction between the procedures.
Answer 5: Ankle arthrodesis patients may have better coronal plane eversion compared to TAA patients. The primary biomechanical advantages of TAA are in the sagittal plane, not the coronal plane.
