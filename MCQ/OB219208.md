## Question

An otherwise healthy 72-year-old male presents with a three-part proximal humerus fracture (Figure A). He elects to undergo a trial of nonoperative measurement. However, at his 3-month follow-up appointment, he endorses significant activity-limiting pain (Figure B). After discussing options with his orthopaedic surgeon, he undergoes a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. The surgeon elects to use a 135-degree revision stem implant, with a 10-degree inferior tilt and inferior offset glenosphere (Figure C). The subscapularis was not repaired at the end of the case. As a result of bony resection, which of the following complications is this patient most likely to develop?

## Options

1. Glenosphere component failure
2. Acromial stress fracture
3. Clinically significant loss of external rotation motion
4. Scapular notching
5. Periprosthetic infection

## Correct option

**3. Clinically significant loss of external rotation motion**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) with the removal of the greater tuberosity. They would be expected to have clinically significant loss of external rotation (Answer 3).

The indications for performing rTSA have expanded over the past few decades. These implants are being increasingly utilized for the treatment of 3- and 4-part proximal humerus fractures in older patients. When performed for fracture indication, there are considerable implications in the healing of the greater tuberosity to the remaining bone and/or prosthesis. With successful greater tuberosity healing, the supraspinatus and infraspinatus impart significantly improved range of motion and thus functional outcomes. If the greater tuberosity is irreparable or goes onto nonunion, a significant loss of external rotation motion can be expected (i.e. loss of infraspinatus). As a result, patients may not be able to perform certain activities that require sufficient external rotation, including overhead movements (i.e. combing their hair). Conversely, the deltoid can still provide sufficient force to overcome the loss of the supraspinatus function, albeit weaker, for most activities of daily living.

*Boileau and colleagues performed a retrospective review of 38 patients treated with a rTSA and tuberosity reattachment for the indication of 3- and 4-part proximal humerus fractures.* **The authors reported** an 84% greater tuberosity union rate with 6 cases complicated by tuberosity resorption or nonunion with migration. In those 6 cases, patients demonstrated significantly lower range of motion with external rotation and forward elevation. Further, these patients demonstrated lower patient-reported outcomes. **The authors conclude** that tuberosity reconstruction and healing in rTSA for fractures improves forward elevation, external rotation, and patient satisfaction.

*Simovitch and colleagues performed a similar retrospective review involving 55 patients treated with a rTSA and tuberosity reattachment for the indication of 3- and 4-part proximal humerus fractures.* **They reported** an 83% union rate with 7 cases complicated by greater tuberosity reconstruction failure. In those 7 cases, patients demonstrated significantly worse external rotation range of motion and the inability to perform relevant movements (i.e. combing one’s own hair). **The authors concluded** that greater tuberosity healing significantly impacts patients’ ability to perform external rotation-type activities.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Glenoid component failure (i.e. loosening or fracture) is the most common complication requiring revision rTSA. This risk further increases with each revision rTSA surgery secondary to bone loss. However, removal of the greater tuberosity has not been associated with an increased risk for glenoid component loosening.
Answer 2: Acromial stress fractures most often occur in female, osteoporotic patients secondary to acute or repetitive traumatic events where terminal abduction results in the proximal humerus/implant impaction against the acromion. These injuries are correlated with overall worse functional outcome scores. However, removal of the greater tuberosity has not been associated with an increased risk for acromial stress fractures.
Answer 2: Scapular notching occurs when the humeral implant component abuts against the inferior glenoid with terminal adduction. It can be avoided with the use of lower neck shaft angle (135°) implants, placing the glenosphere with 10 degrees of inferior tilt, or using inferior offset glenosphere implants (all of which this patient received). However, removal of the tuberosities has not been associated with an increased risk for scapular notching.
Answer 3: Patients at the greatest risk for shoulder periprosthetic joint infection include younger males, active smokers, and patients who have undergone multiple shoulder arthroplasty surgeries. However, removal of the greater tuberosity has not been associated with an increased risk for periprosthetic infection.
