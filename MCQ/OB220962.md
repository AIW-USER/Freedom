## Question

A 67-year-old man presents with several months of progressive difficulty clearing his right foot while walking. He has a long history of lower back pain and radicular pain down his leg, but denies a specific traumatic event. His medical history is notable for long-standing diabetes and multiple corticosteroid injections to the ankle for anterior ankle pain. On physical examination, he has a steppage gait and weakness with ankle dorsiflexion. There is a painless mass at the anterior ankle. Extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus are functioning normally. Passive ankle range of motion is full. Plain radiographs are unremarkable. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Acute tibialis anterior tendon laceration
2. Anterior ankle impingement syndrome
3. Attritional rupture of the tibialis anterior tendon
4. Deep peroneal nerve palsy
5. Lumbar spinal stenosis

## Correct option

**3. Attritional rupture of the tibialis anterior tendon**

## Answer explanation

The most likely diagnosis is an attritional rupture of the tibialis anterior tendon. Attritional rupture of the tibialis anterior tendon typically occurs in older patients and is often atraumatic, associated with chronic degenerative changes, diabetes, inflammatory conditions, or corticosteroid exposure. Patients present with progressive foot drop, preserved toe extension, and a painless anterior ankle mass representing the retracted tendon. This presentation distinguishes it from acute traumatic rupture or neurologic causes of dorsiflexion weakness.

*Levitsky and colleagues* **identified** patient-specific risk factors associated with anterior tibial tendon pathology. **The authors found** that patients with anterior tibial tendon pathology were significantly more likely to be older and to have comorbid conditions such as diabetes mellitus, inflammatory arthritis, and a history of local corticosteroid injections. **They concluded** that anterior tibial tendon pathology is largely degenerative and multifactorial, and that awareness of these risk factors may improve early recognition and diagnosis of this often-missed condition.

*Harkin and colleagues reviewed the evaluation and management of acute and chronic tibialis anterior tendon rupture and tendinopathy.* **The authors reported** that acute ruptures treated surgically and chronic ruptures managed with reconstruction or tendon transfer generally resulted in improved dorsiflexion strength and gait, whereas nonoperative treatment was reserved for low-demand patients with persistent functional deficits. **They concluded** that treatment should be individualized based on chronicity, patient demand, and tissue quality, with operative management preferred for most active patients to restore function and prevent long-term disability.

*Vosoughi and colleagues reviewed the existing literature on tibialis anterior tendon rupture and provided evidence-based management recommendations.* **The authors found** that surgical treatment, including primary repair for acute ruptures and reconstruction or tendon transfer for chronic ruptures, was associated with better functional outcomes and patient satisfaction compared with nonoperative management, particularly in active patients. **They concluded** that early diagnosis and operative treatment are recommended for most patients, with nonoperative management reserved for elderly or low-demand individuals.

Incorrect answers:
Answer choice 1: Acute laceration is typically associated with a clear traumatic event, such as a sharp injury or sudden forced plantarflexion, and presents with an abrupt onset of symptoms rather than progressive weakness over months.
Answer choice 2: Anterior ankle impingement does not cause progressive dorsiflexion weakness, steppage gait, or a palpable anterior ankle mass consistent with tendon retraction.
Answer choice 4: Common peroneal nerve palsy would cause weakness of ankle dorsiflexion and toe extension, resulting in dysfunction of the extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus.
Answer choice 5: Lumbar spinal stenosis typically presents with back pain or radicular symptoms, sensory changes in the medial leg, and weakness involving multiple muscle groups, including quadriceps and tibialis anterior.
