## Question

A 33-year-old civil engineer presents with 3 months of worsening medial elbow pain. She recently started playing in a recreational bowling league with her colleagues. She endorses pain with resisted wrist flexion and pronation on examination, has a Tinel's sign over the cubital tunnel, and endorses increasing paresthesias about the ulnar two digits of the hand with flexion-compression testing at the elbow. She undergoes six months of conservative treatment, including counter-force bracing, kinesiology taping, and extracorporeal shockwave therapy, which have not helped her symptoms. An EMG/NCS demonstrates moderate to severe ulnar neuropathy at the elbow. A radiograph of her elbow is shown in Figure A. When patients with this condition are treated surgically, which of the following factors has been linked to inferior clinical outcomes?

## Options

1. A prolonged trial of non-operative management
2. Degree of ulnar nerve involvement
3. Failure to re-attach the flexor pronator mass tendinous insertion
4. Number of distinct flexor-pronator tendons involved
5. Use of extracorporeal shockwave therapy

## Correct option

**2. Degree of ulnar nerve involvement**

## Answer explanation

Patients with medial epicondylitis and concomitant moderate to severe ulnar neuropathy at the elbow have been shown to have inferior clinical outcomes when treated operatively with concomitant ulnar nerve transposition as compared to individuals with mild or no ulnar neuropathy treated surgically for the same condition.

Medial epicondylitis, also colloquially known as "golfer's elbow," represents an overuse syndrome of the flexor-pronator mass that is 5 to 10x less common than lateral epicondylitis, or "tennis elbow." Diagnosis can be made with tenderness about the medial epicondyle that is made worse with resisted forearm pronation and wrist flexion. Treatment is generally non-operative with rest, anti-inflammatory use, activity modification, and bracing. Though the predominant pathology involves angiofibroblastic hyperplasia of the tendinous origins of the flexor-pronator mass, it is important to note that local inflammation about the medial epicondyle may cause involvement of the ulnar nerve. The presence of ulnar nerve symptoms, when documented as moderate or severe with an EMG/NCS, has been associated with worse outcomes after operative treatment for recalcitrant disease. Operative treatment often involves debridement of the flexor-pronator mass, with or without microfracture of the medial epicondyle and re-attachment of the tendinous origin, though some studies have shown no difference in outcomes with debridement alone.

*Amin et al. provide a JAAOS yellow journal review article on medial epicondylitis.* **The authors note** that flexor-pronator tendon degeneration represents a staged process of pathologic change in the tendon that can result in structural breakdown and irreparable fibrosis or calcification. In terms of operative management, **they conclude** that for elite athletes with distinct tendon disruption or patients with symptoms that are refractory to nonsurgical treatment, open débridement and simultaneous treatment of secondary pathologies can be beneficial, with such patients typically returning to pre-injury level of performance at 3 to 6 months postoperatively.

*Gabel et al. evaluated 30 patients with medial epicondylitis and concomitant mild or moderate to severe ulnar neuropathy treated surgically.* *Of the 25 elbows with no or mild ulnar neuritis, 24 had excellent or good outcomes at a mean 7-year follow-up.* *However, three of the five elbows with moderate or severe ulnar neuropathy had fair or poor results at the final follow-up.* **The authors concluded** that the only preoperative or intraoperative parameter that correlated to outcome was the severity of the ulnar neuropathy.

*Arevalo et al. published a recent 2023 AJSM systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies describing open surgery for medial epicondylitis, 7 of which involved repair of the debrided tendinous origin and 6 of which did not.* **The authors found** that, in 479 elbows at a mean follow-up of 4.6 years, complication rates averaged 4.3% with success rates for return to sport and work averaging 81-100% for both cohorts. **They concluded** that regardless of surgical technique performed, patients generally demonstrated an improvement in PROs, and an encouraging number returned to work with limited complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Most patients with medial epicondylitis are treated successfully non-operatively, with operative intervention only indicated after a prolonged period of conservative management of at least 6 months.
*Answer 3: As described by Arevalo et al, failure to re-attach the tendinous origin of the flexor-pronator mass after debridement has been shown to result in equivalent outcomes.*
Answer 4: The origin of the flexor-pronator mass represents a confluence of five distinct muscular tendons forming a common tendon, thus the number of tendons involved is not relevant.
Answer 5: The use of extracorporeal shockwave therapy has not been shown to be particularly useful or detrimental.
