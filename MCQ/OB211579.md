## Question

A 25-year-old male professional football player presents with severe right knee pain after he was tackled head-on at knee level by an opposing player. On physical examination, there is a large effusion of the right knee. Figure A is the clinical photograph with the patient laying on the exam table. Dial testing demonstrates 20° of increased external rotation of the right foot in 30° and 90° of knee flexion. What structures are most likely injured in this patient?

![[q211579-a-posterior-sag-sign.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. ACL only
2. PLC and ACL
3. PCL only
4. PCL and PLC
5. PCL and ACL

## Correct option

**4. PCL and PLC**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a posterior sag sign and positive dial test in both 30° and 90° of knee flexion consistent with a posterolateral corner (PLC) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injury.

Injuries to the PCL are the result of either direct blow mechanisms to the proximal tibia in a flexed knee (dashboard injury), noncontact hyperflexion injury in a plantar-flexed foot, and hyperextension injuries. Disruption of the PCL compromises the major restraint of posterior tibial translation. Physical exam findings demonstrate posterior instability, such as a posterior drawer test, posterior sag test, and quadriceps active test. Isolated injuries of the PCL are generally treated with nonoperative measures, including protected weight-bearing, quadriceps strengthening, and immobilization. Multiligamentous knee injuries, bone avulsion injuries with isolated grade II or III PCL injuries, and chronic PCL injuries with a functionally unstable knee are treated with PCL reconstruction.

*Lubowitz et al. reviewed physical examination maneuvers for knee instability.* For PCL injuries, the authors described the posterior drawer test, posterior Lachman test, posterior sag test, and quadriceps active test. **The authors concluded** that a thorough clinical examination of knee injuries is paramount for an accurate diagnosis.

*Wind et al. reviewed the evaluation and treatment of PCL injuries.* **The authors recommended** repair of acute PCL injuries with grade III laxity, boney avulsions, and multi-ligamentous knee injuries with the tibial inlay being the author's preferred technique. **They concluded** that most surgeons would agree on the indications for surgical treatment of PCL injuries but a discussion remains regarding the optimal surgical technique.

*Bronstein et al. reviewed the physical examination of knee ligament injuries.* For PLC injuries, the authors described varus thrust gait, external rotation recurvatum test, posterolateral rotary drawer test, dial test, and standing apprehension test. **The authors concluded** that a thorough clinical examination can efficiently diagnose various knee pathologies. An increase in side to side difference of external rotation at 30 degrees of knee flexion in indicative of a PLC injury while an increased dial at 30 and 90 degrees indicates a PLC and PCL injury.

Image A is the clinical photograph of a patient with a high degree of posterior tibial sag on the femur, signifying a PCL injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are tested with a Lachman's, anterior drawer, and/or pivot shift test.
Answer 2: An ACL and PLC injury would be suggestive of a positive Lachman's test and a positive dial test at 30° degrees of knee flexion.
Answer 3: An isolated PCL injury is considered positive with a posterior drawer, posterior sag, and quadriceps active test. A dial test that is positive at 30° and 90° suggests a combined PCL and PLC injury.
Answer 5: A PCL and ACL injury would be present with a combined Lachman's, posterior drawer, anterior drawer, pivot shift test, and posterior sag test
