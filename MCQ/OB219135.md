## Question

Your practice CEO calls a meeting with you and your partners to discuss the changing trends in reimbursements and their predicted effects on ongoing collections and subsequent practice financials. She specifically notes that a greater emphasis is being placed on patient-reported outcome testing. When considering the best way to collect this information so that it can be reported to insurance companies, which of the following outcome measure tools based on the item response theory (IRT) of testing can be administered most quickly and to the broadest patient population while remaining highly reliable?

## Options

1. Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand questionnaire (DASH)
2. Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM)
3. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)
4. Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand questionnaire (QuickDASH)
5. The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey questionnaire (SF-36)

## Correct option

**3. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)**

## Answer explanation

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) tests are based on the item response theory (IRT) of testing and, when compared to so-called "legacy" tests, can be administered more quickly and applied to a broader population of patients while remaining highly reliable.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are tools that assess clinical outcomes from the patient’s perspective. Historically, numerous methods of testing these outcomes were created, and these include commonly used multi-specialty items such as the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey questionnaire (SF-36) and the EuroQol-5D, as well specialty-specific tools such as the Harris Hip Score, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), DASH, QuickDash, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) to name just a few. Collectively, this validated group of measures are referred to as "legacy" tests. Unfortunately, many of these legacy measures are only validated for certain orthopedic conditions in specific patient populations, limiting the ability to compare and interpret results among various studies. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), thus, was developed by the National Institutes of Health under the 2004 “Roadmap to Medical Research” initiative as an effort to address the need for more valid, reliable, and generalizable measures of clinical outcomes that are important to patients. PROMIS effectively measures patients’ self-reported capability and can be administered as a short-form or as a computer adaptive test (CAT), which uses item-response theory to customize item delivery to an examinee by selecting each subsequent item based on answers to previous items, thereby allowing for a high level of precision using fewer questions.

*Fidai et al. performed a systematic review of patient-reported outcomes measurement information systems and legacy patient-reported outcomes measures in the field of Orthopaedics.* *A general linear model and paired t-tests were used to assess for differences between legacy patient-reported outcome measures and PROMIS in 18 studies.* **The authors concluded** that PROMIS scores correlate strongly, particularly in lower extremity patients, with some of the most commonly used legacy measures in orthopaedics, and that PROMIS can be administered quicker and applied to a broader patient population while remaining highly reliable.

*Brodke et al. published a JAAOS article on PROMIS for orthopaedic outcomes measurements.* **The authors note** that PROMIS has been validated in patient populations with orthopaedic disorders of the foot and ankle, upper extremity, and spine and has demonstrated a marked improvement in measurement characteristics and reduced patient and administrative burden. **They conclude** that PROMIS Physical Function measures are useful for assessing orthopaedic outcomes and are superior to legacy measures in several key populations.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,2,4,5: DASH, QuickDash, FAAM, and the SF-36 all represent legacy measures, which are not based on item response theory (IRT) and are not as reliable in the setting of broad application across large patient populations as PROMIS.
