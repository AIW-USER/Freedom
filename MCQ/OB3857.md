## Question

A 26-year-old active woman is seen for persistent lateral ankle pain. She has tried a course of treatment with NSAIDs, physical therapy and bracing. Plain films are unremarkable, and her MRI image is shown in Figures A and B. What is the distal insertion point of the injured structure?

![[q3857-b-peroneus-brevis-mri-2.jpg]]

## Options

1. Navicular and medial cuneiform
2. Base of distal phalanx of big toe
3. First metatarsal base and medial cuneiform
4. Fifth metatarsal base
5. Base of distal phalanx of digits 2-5

## Correct option

**4. Fifth metatarsal base**

## Answer explanation

The injured structure is the peroneus brevis (PB) as evidenced by the longitudinal tear noted in the MRI scans (the immediate tendon noted posterior to the distal fibula). The PB inserts on the base of the 5th metatarsal.

PB tears tend to occur with rapid dorsiflexion of an inverted foot leading to a rapid reflexive contraction of the peroneal tendons (also can cause peroneal tendon dislocation). Symptoms of isolated tears are generally lateral ankle pain over the peroneal tendons without instability or subluxation of the tendons. MRI studies are generally required for an accurate diagnosis. First-line treatment is conservative with NSAIDs and bracing with operative treatment consisting of debridement, tendon repair, and tubularization depending on the size of the tear and quality of the tendon.

*Recht and Donley performed a review of the MRI imaging of the foot and ankle.* **They noted** the importance of MRI for appropriate evaluation of tendon pathology, occult fracture, as well as osteochondral lesions or infections. Furthermore, MRI is helpful in further characterization of soft tissue abnormalities such as fibromatosis, interdigital neuromas, tarsal tunnel syndrome, and tumors. **The authors conclude** that MRI high-resolution images in multiple planes assist in obtaining the appropriate diagnosis and help guide surgical management.

*Taljanovic et al. reviewed the different components that may lead to peroneal tendon pathology on MRI studies.* **They noted** several anatomic variants, including a flat or convex fibular retromalleolar groove, hypertrophy of the peroneal tubercle at the lateral aspect of the calcaneus, an accessory peroneus quartus muscle, a low-lying peroneus brevis muscle belly, and an os peroneum, that may all predispose to peroneal tendon injuries. **The authors conclude** that an understanding of the current treatment approaches for partial- and full-thickness peroneal tendon tears, subluxation and dislocation of these tendons with superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) injuries, intrasheath subluxations, and peroneal tendonopathy and tenosynovitis can help physicians achieve a favorable outcome.

Figure A and B are T2-weighted axial MRI cuts of left ankle showing a PB split longitudinal tendon tear. Illustration A is Figure A with all the tendons appropriately labeled.
