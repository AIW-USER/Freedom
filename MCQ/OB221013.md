## Question

A 22-year-old collegiate soccer player sustains a twisting injury to his foot while pivoting. He has midfoot pain and plantar ecchymosis, and initial non–weightbearing radiographs show no obvious displacement. A CT scan shows no fracture. Given concern for a Lisfranc injury, what is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Allow return to play with a stiff-soled shoe
2. Obtain an MRI to evaluate the Lisfranc ligament
3. Obtain weightbearing radiographs of both feet
4. Proceed with operative fixation of the Lisfranc joint
5. Treat with a short leg cast and non–weightbearing for 6 weeks

## Correct option

**3. Obtain weightbearing radiographs of both feet**

## Answer explanation

In a patient with high clinical suspicion and negative initial imaging, the next best step would be to obtain weight-bearing radiographs of both feet to assess midfoot stability (answer choice 1).

Lisfranc injuries are frequently missed on non–weightbearing radiographs, especially when the injury is nondisplaced or purely ligamentous. Clinical findings such as plantar ecchymosis and midfoot pain should raise suspicion for a Lisfranc injury even when initial imaging appears normal. Weight-bearing radiographs can reveal subtle diastasis or malalignment of the tarsometatarsal joints that is not evident without axial load. Obtaining bilateral weight-bearing views allows comparison with the contralateral foot and is the most appropriate next diagnostic step before determining treatment. Demonstrated instability on weight-bearing imaging generally warrants operative fixation, while stable injuries can be managed nonoperatively.

*Weatherford et al. reviewed the anatomy, injury patterns, diagnostic strategies, and treatment options for tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc) joint injuries.* **The authors emphasize** that subtle Lisfranc injuries are commonly missed on non–weightbearing radiographs and that weightbearing imaging and clinical suspicion are critical for diagnosis, with instability being the key determinant of management. **They conclude** that stable injuries may be treated nonoperatively, while unstable injuries require anatomic reduction and fixation to optimize functional outcomes and reduce post-traumatic arthritis.

*Carter et al. reviewed contemporary evidence regarding the diagnosis, classification, and management of Lisfranc injuries, with a focus on evolving surgical strategies.* **The authors highlight** improved outcomes with early diagnosis, weight-bearing imaging for subtle injuries, and anatomic reduction, noting ongoing debate between primary arthrodesis and ORIF depending on injury pattern. **They conclude** that treatment should be individualized based on stability and joint involvement, with primary arthrodesis favored for purely ligamentous or severe injuries and ORIF remaining appropriate in select cases.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Early return to play increases the risk of progression to displacement and chronic midfoot instability.
Answer 2: MRI may identify ligamentous injury, but it does not assess dynamic instability as effectively as weight-bearing radiographs.
Answer 4: Operative management is reserved for confirmed unstable injuries.
Answer 5: Treatment with a short-leg cast and non-weight-bearing for 6 weeks is appropriate only after confirming the injury is stable.
