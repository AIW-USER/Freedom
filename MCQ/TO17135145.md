Chapter: Spine MCQs — Scheuermann’s Disease

## Question

Not true regarding Scheuermann’s disease

## Options

1. Starts in child, progresses at puberty and is more common in boys than girls
2. Smooth curve of dorsal spine with compensatory hyper lordosis in lumbar spine
3. Rarely associated with neurological deficit and cardiac complications
4. Cannot be corrected by postural control

## Correct option

**4. Cannot be corrected by postural control**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

Scheuermann's Disease (described in 1920) is a rigid form of structural spinal kyphosis classified best as a juvenile dorsal kyphosis affecting children between 12-17 years age. It is the commonest structural kyphosis in adolescents (mean age onset 10-12 years) and overall, the second most common spinal deformity in this age after idiopathic scoliosis.

The disease is characterized by anterior wedging of > 5 degrees across three or more consecutive vertebrae, most commonly in the thoracic spine (apex T6-8), although rarely lumbar spine may be involved (poorer prognosis in lumbar spine involvement). Males are involved more commonly than the females.

Etiology

The exact etiology of Scheuermann’s disease is not clear. The most widely accepted theory suggests that the kyphosis and vertebral wedging are caused by a developmental error in collagen aggregation which results in a defective endochondral ossification in the vertebral end plates. Herniation of the anterior disc material leading to loss of anterior disc height, relative Osteomalacia anteriorly in vertebrae leading to anterior wedging or altered biomechanics leading to anterior growth arrest are other theories in pipeline. What so ever, radiological findings, suggest it to be osteochondritis of the ring epiphyses of the vertebrae. An autosomal dominant inheritance pattern is now very much accepted.

Clinical Presentation

Patients mostly seek consultation either for cosmetic issues or a dull back pain.

Physical examination may reveal a kyphosis that generally ranges between 20 degrees to 45 degrees. There may be compensatory Lumbar/ cervical hyper lordosis. Patients often have tight hamstrings, iliopsoas and anterior shoulder. Frank neurological deficits are rare.

On imaging, one third of the cases may be found to have Spondylolysis in lumbar region while another one third could have scoliosis. Presence of dural cysts is also not uncommon.

Sorenson criteria (on X ray) may be used to diagnose Scheuermann’s kyphosis.

• Anterior wedging of more than 5° of three consecutive adjacent vertebrae at the apex of deformity.

• Irregular vertebral apophyseal lines.

• Presence of Schmorl nodes

• Narrowing of intervertebral disk spaces

Hyperextension lateral radiograph (supine lateral radiograph with patient lying in hyperextension over a bolster) may be ordered to separate out a postural kyphosis. Scheuermann's kyphosis is usually inflexible on bending radiographs. MRI is indicated only prior to surgery to look for associated disc herniations, epidural cysts, spinal cord abnormalities, and spinal stenosis.

Treatment

The natural history of the disease is benign so most patients are managed conservatively. Nonoperative regime mainly consists of stretching exercises, postural training, physical therapy for core muscle strengthening and bracing.

Bracing with an extension-type orthosis (Jewitt type - with high chest pad) is mostly employed for cases with curves < 75 degree where growth is still pending. A correction of up to 15 degrees can at times be obtained or at least a progression can be halted.

Operative treatment is warranted for patients with curves > 75 degrees or those with neurological deficits. Operative procedure consists of Anterior release (rarely done now due to strong pedicle screw construct) f/b posterior spinal osteotomy (Smith Peterson) and fusion. Avoid correcting more than 50% of deformity as deficits often can develop during aggressive corrections. Goal is to have a final kyphosis < 40-50 degree.

Complications

Neurologic complications reported rate varies from 0.6-0.8%.

- higher than idiopathic scoliosis corrective surgeries
- typically, due to spinal cord stretching (ensure there is enough posterior column shortening)
- overall incidence of complications does not differ between anterior/posterior versus posterior alone procedure

Distal junctional kyphosis occurs in 20-30% of patients. It’s avoided by making proper selection of fusion levels (use the first stable sagittal vertebra) and avoiding overcorrection (correction should not exceed 50% of original curve)

Proximal junctional kyphosis (less common than distal) typically occurs secondary to overcorrection and negative sagittal balance

Pseudarthrosis, hardware failure leading to loss of correction are other possible issues.

A rare possibility of Superior mesenteric artery syndrome also remains.
