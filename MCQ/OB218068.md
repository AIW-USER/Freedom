## Question

A 62-year-old male presents with increasing hip and groin pain, particularly with walking up stairs when his hip is flexed. He has attempted physical therapy, corticosteroid injections, and activity modifications, however, his pain is recalcitrant to these conservative measures. He has a history of lupus with multiple flare-ups treated with prolonged periods of prednisone usage. He also smokes a ½ pack per day but is otherwise healthy. ## Options

1. Over resection of the CAM deformity may result in a femoral neck fracture
2. His symptoms may progress despite core decompression
3. Procurement of adjacent vascularized bone graft may result in a tibial stress fracture
4. Risk for aseptic lymphocyte-dominant vasculitis associated lesion with resurfacing implant
5. Higher risk for post-operative dislocation compared to osteoarthritic patients

## Correct option

**5. Higher risk for post-operative dislocation compared to osteoarthritic patients**

## Answer explanation

This patient has radiographic evidence of avascular necrosis within the left femoral head. Given the patient's age and the size of the lesion, the most appropriate and definitive operative treatment would be a total hip replacement (THA). In general, THA performed for AVN has higher complications (intraoperative canal perforation and post-operative dislocation) compared to standard osteoarthritic patients.

Avascular necrosis (AVN; also referred to as osteonecrosis) is a multifactorial disease in which vascular disruption, intravascular obliteration, and/or extravascular compression are all implicated in the underlying etiology. In the hip, AVN typically follows a progressive course leading to femoral head collapse and hip joint destruction. While nonoperative management can be considered in small lesions in asymptomatic young patients, most symptomatic cases require operative intervention. In general, the two presiding operative pathways include (1) hip preservation or (2) hip replacement. Hip preservation procedures include core decompression, osteochondral allograft transplantation, fibular strut autograft, or rotational osteotomies. Hip replacement procedures include hip resurfacing or total hip replacement. Preservation of the femoral head is preferable in younger patients without head collapse. Older patients with larger lesions, multiple comorbidities, and/or femoral head collapse should undergo arthroplasty as a single, definitive surgery to provide lasting relief of their symptoms. The localized inflammatory reaction to AVN, combined with prolonged periods of immobilization and/or chronic corticosteroid use, results in poor bone quality and soft tissue laxity which may result in increased risk for intraoperative canal perforation with the femoral stem, medial wall breach when reaming the acetabulum, or post-operative dislocation.

*Zalavras and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of the diagnosis and treatment of osteonecrosis of the femoral head.* **They emphasize** the importance of identifying subchondral collapse on radiographic or advanced imaging, as it is considered a poor prognostic indicator for hip preservation surgeries. They further discuss the hip preservation pathway for younger patients without collapse versus the hip replacement pathway for older patients and younger patients with femoral head collapse. The authors discuss the potential complications of each treatment, including the higher risk of intraoperative fracture or post-operative dislocation after THA for AVN.

*Swarup and colleagues performed a single institution, retrospective review of young (< 35 years old) hip AVN patients treated with THA).* **The authors reported** a 10-year survival rate of 86% and a 20-year survival rate of 66%. Further, **they reported** significant improvement in hip functional scores after THA surgery with lower improvements if the patient required revision THA. **They concluded** that primary THA is a good treatment option for these patients, however, the long-term need for revision should be considered and discussed with each patient.

*Osawa and colleagues performed a single-institution, retrospective case-control study comparing the outcomes of cementless THA for osteonecrosis (AVN, case group) to THA for osteoarthritis (control group).* **The authors reported** an equivalent ten-year implant survival rate (97.5% for AVN, 98.2% for osteoarthritis) with similar complication rates (5% for AVN, 3% for osteoarthritis). The authors acknowledge that patients with AVN have considerably less soft tissue constraint and potentially higher ROM than osteoarthritic patients, making them more susceptible to dislocation. **The authors conclude** that with newer implants the functional outcomes, implant survival, and rate of complications for cementless THAs are comparable for AVN and osteonecrosis.

Figure A and B show radiographic images of this older patient with a considerably large AVN lesion to the posterior-superior weight-bearing portion of the femoral head without evidence of subchondral collapse.

Incorrect Answers:

Answer 1. Over-resection of the CAM deformity resulting in femoral neck fracture is a potential concern in patients with femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) treated with osteochondroplasty.
Answer 2: Core decompression is a well-established treatment for AVN of the femoral head with overall positive results particularly when utilized in early stages of disease (pre-collapse) in the younger population. However, older patients with multiple comorbidities or advanced disease are most reliably treated with arthroplasty over hip preservation procedures.
Answer 3. Vascularized fibular strut autograft inserted into the femoral neck is a technically demanding procedure, with promising results in younger patients treated via the hip preservation pathway. Tibial stress fracture is a well-described, potential complication as a result of the increased weight-bearing load of the tibia after fibular graft procurement.
Answer 4. Hip resurfacing may be considered in younger patients treated via the hip replacement pathway. Hip resurfacing is relatively contraindicated in hip AVN patients with large-sized lesions or who are actively on systematic steroid medications, as there is a much higher rate of failure in these patients. Development of aseptic lymphocyte-dominant vasculitis-associated lesions (ALVAL) would be considered in patients treated with metal-on-metal implants (i.e. hip resurfacing).
