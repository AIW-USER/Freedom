## Question

You are paged by the emergency department to evaluate for hand compartment syndrome in a 28-year-old mechanic who sustained a burn injury to the wrist 10 hours prior. On examination, he is found to have an eschar around 270 degrees of his wrist. Which physical examination finding would support the early diagnosis of compartment syndrome in this patient?

## Options

1. Absent peripheral pulses
2. Delta P of 38 mmHg
3. Exacerbation of symptoms with phalangeal extension
4. Hand held in an intrinsic plus position
5. Paralysis of the thenar and hypothenar muscules

## Correct option

**3. Exacerbation of symptoms with phalangeal extension**

## Answer explanation

This patient's history is concerning for compartment syndrome. Pain with passive stretch of fingers is the most sensitive finding for compartment syndrome of the hand.

Hand & forearm compartment syndrome are devastating upper extremity conditions where the osseofascial compartment pressure rise to a level that decreases perfusion to the hand or forearm and may lead to irreversible muscle and neurovascular damage. Diagnosis is made with the presence of severe and progressive hand or forearm pain that worsens with passive finger or wrist motion, respectively. Firmness and decreased compressibility of the compartments is often present. Needle compartment pressures are diagnostic in cases of inconclusive physical exam findings and in sedated patients. Treatment is emergent fasciotomies.

*Fischer et al. reviewed the safety and effectiveness of immediate enzymatic debridement to prevent the need for operative escharotomy because of burn-induced compartment syndrome in selected patients.* **They reported** that enzymatic debridement provided sufficient eschar removal in all patients and conversion to conventional operative escharotomy was not necessary for any patient. **They concluded** that enzymatic debridement is safe and effective for the prevention of burn-induced compartment syndrome after deep circumferential burns at the upper extremity, thus making operative escharotomy unnecessary.

*Ipaktchi et al. reviewed the use of early fasciotomy for upper extremity compartment syndrome.* **They reported** that for this condition, a high level of alertness to clinical symptoms such as pain to passive stretch and increasing pain or analgesic requirements is key to not miss the diagnosis in the alert patient. **They concluded** that there are ten designated hand compartments that can be affected in hand compartment syndrome and early intervention is key when this condition is diagnosed.

*Lee et al. reviewed high-voltage electrical injuries in the upper extremities.* **They reported** that cutaneous burns often underestimate the true extent of the injury because most current is through the deep tissues. **They concluded** that emergent surgical exploration is reserved for patients with compartment syndrome.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Absent peripheral pulses are a LATE finding and amputation is usually inevitable in these cases
Answer 2: Compartment syndrome is treated with emergent fasciotomies with a Delta P <30 mmHg (intracompartment pressure is within 30 mmHg of the diastolic blood pressure (Delta P))
Answer 4: Palpable swelling is often evident and reveals a tense hand held in the intrinsic MINUS position, which is the most consistent clinical finding
Answer 5: Paralysis is a LATE finding and full recovery is rare in these cases
