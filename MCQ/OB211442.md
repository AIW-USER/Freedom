## Question

One evening, a 25-year-old male patient comes in after rolling over an ATV. His ER evaluation showed a closed injury in Figures A and B, and you place him on the schedule for the next day for fixation. While rounding the next morning, however, the nurse tells you he has been asking more and more frequently for pain medication which does not seem to be controlling his pain. Repeat evaluation shows the patient in significant pain and distress with tense leg compartments. You indicate him for emergent fasciotomies. Performing fasciotomies have what effect on this patient's developing pathology?

## Options

1. Decrease arterial inflow to the leg
2. Increase interstitial pressures of the leg
3. Decrease muscular perfusion
4. Increase venous outflow from the leg
5. Decrease intracompartmental bleeding

## Correct option

**4. Increase venous outflow from the leg**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a closed, comminuted tibial shaft fracture with signs and symptoms consistent with the development of compartment syndrome. Fasciotomies decrease intracompartmental pressures which allow for venous outflow of blood.

Compartment syndrome of the leg occurs most commonly after fractures, although can result from numerous etiologies. The pathoanatomy of compartment syndrome is started by local trauma followed by bleeding and edema, which increase interstitial pressures within the leg compartments. This increasing pressure eventually causes vascular occlusions of low-pressure veins decreasing outflow compared to an inflow of blood. Decrease blood flow causes myoneural ischemia and cellular death. Compartment Syndrome is an orthopedic emergency, as late myonecrosis and scarring can have significant functional limitations. Treatment is with fasciotomies to allow for muscle expansion and decreasing interstitial pressures.

*McQueen et al. retrospectively reviewed 164 patients over an 8-year period who developed compartment syndrome to identify contributing factors.* **They found** that 69% of cases developed after fractures and most commonly at the tibial diaphysis. **They found** 4.3% of all tibial shaft fractures treated over this period developed compartment syndrome, with rates higher in patients under 35 years old.

*Olson and Glasgow performed a literature review of compartment syndrome.* *They discuss etiologies, pathoanatomy of ischemia, and diagnostic criteria to include the six Ps: pain, pressure, pulselessness, paralysis, paresthesia, and pallor.* The most sensitive finding is pain out of proportion with exacerbation with passive stretch. They also discuss one versus two-incision treatment options, and the timing of skin closure.

Figures A and B demonstrate AP and lateral X-rays, respectively, of a left tibial and fibular shaft fractures with comminution.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Fasciotomies would increase arterial inflow by decreasing intracompartmental pressures.
Answer 2: Fasciotomies would decrease interstitial pressures by increasing the area in which swelling is allowed.
Answer 3: Fasciotomies increase muscle perfusion by increasing blood flow to the muscles (increase outflow and inflow of blood).
Answer 5: Fasciotomies do not address the etiology of bleeding, and potentially increase bleeding by decreasing tamponade.
