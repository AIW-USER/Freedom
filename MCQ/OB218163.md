## Question

## Options

1. > 3mm of translation with the radius pull test
2. Delayed initial presentation > 6 weeks
3. > 3 independent fracture fragments of the radial head
4. Low demand or sedentary patient
5. > 30% involvement of the articular surface of the radial head

## Correct option

**1. > 3mm of translation with the radius pull test**

## Answer explanation

A radius pull test exhibiting greater than 3mm of translation is indicative of longitudinal forearm instability and represents a contraindication to isolated radial head excision, as depicted in Figure A.

Radial head fractures are relatively common intra-articular elbow fractures. When the fractures are small (< 2mm displacement) and do not cause a bony block to motion, they are most often treated non-operatively (Mason Type I). However, when there is > 2mm of displacement (Mason II) that causes a bony block to motion or significant comminution (Mason Type III), either radial head ORIF or replacement is indicated. Radial head resection (Figure B) can also be done in the setting of symptomatic, chronic fractures, particularly for low-demand, sedentary patients. The important contraindication to be aware of when considering this treatment option is longitudinal forearm instability caused by interosseous ligament injury that will cause proximal migration of the radius and subsequent symptomatic ulnocarpal impaction (Illustration B). The presence of longitudinal instability can be tested intra-operatively with the radial pull test. In the setting of > 3mm of translation, instability is inferred and radial head excision should not be performed.

*Burkhart et al. reviewed fractures of the radial head.* **The authors recommend** viewing radial head fractures not merely as osseous lesions but as osteoligamentous lesions, given the high rate of concomitant ligament tears. **They note** that the treatment of choice for 2 to 5 mm displaced partial articular fractures remains debatable, with several investigators reporting good results of nonoperative treatment comparable to open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) but with lower complication rates. **They conclude** that If anatomic and stable reduction cannot be achieved, radial head arthroplasty remains the treatment of choice.

*Petersen et al. describe radial head replacement through a Kocher approach.* *The authors provide a case report with a video supplement of a radial head replacement procedure for a 3-part radial head fracture in a 23-year-old male.* **They concluded** that radial head replacement provides a good alternative to ORIF, producing similar elbow range of motion and providing good stability when compared with the contralateral uninjured elbow.

Figure A depicts radiographs of a comminuted radial head fracture. Figure B is a post-operative image representing the result of a radial head excision. Figure C depicts a post-operative radiograph following radial head replacement. Illustration A depicts the Mason classification. Illustration B depicts interosseous ligament injury and subsequent longitudinal forearm instability (Essex-Lopresti injury).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-5: These answer choices all represent indications to perform a radial head excision procedure; however, this should not be performed in isolation in the setting of radial forearm longitudinal instability.
