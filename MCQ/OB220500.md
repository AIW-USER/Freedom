## Question

A 56-year-old female presents to the emergency department with 6 months of worsening thoracic back pain, with a new onset inability to ambulate. During the patient's workup, she underwent a chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT, as demonstrated in Figure A. An MRI of the entire spine is subsequently ordered and is shown in Figures B and C. On physical examination, the patient has bilateral hyperreflexic patella and Achilles reflexes, gait instability, and marked weakness in bilateral lower extremities. Following completion of her diagnostic workup, her Tokuhashi score was calculated as 11. What is the best course of management at this time?

## Options

1. Embolization, posterior spinal decompression, instrumented fusion, postoperative radiotherapy
2. Urgent thoracic corpectomy, instrumented fusion with postoperative radiotherapy
3. Preoperative radiotherapy, thoracic corpectomy, and instrumented fusion
4. Palliative care unit
5. Embolization, thoracic corpectomy, instrumented fusion with postoperative radiotherapy

## Correct option

**5. Embolization, thoracic corpectomy, instrumented fusion with postoperative radiotherapy**

## Answer explanation

The patient has renal cell carcinoma with spinal cord compression secondary to metastatic disease burden. This clinical scenario in a patient with a calculated life expectancy of 6-12 months (Tokuhashi Score of 9-11) mandates embolization, surgical decompression, and instrumented fusion with postoperative radiotherapy (Answer 5).

The management of spinal metastatic disease is anchored in two complementary decision-making frameworks: the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS) and the NOMS (Neurologic, Oncologic, Mechanical, Systemic) paradigm. SINS has standardized the assessment of spinal instability in the oncologic setting, improving interdisciplinary communication and correlating reliably with pain severity and functional impairment. The NOMS framework integrates neurologic status, tumor histology and radiosensitivity, mechanical integrity, and overall systemic burden to tailor individualized treatment strategies, including the selection of surgical versus radiotherapeutic interventions. Additional operative considerations include preoperative embolization—particularly critical for hypervascular lesions such as renal cell and thyroid carcinoma—to minimize intraoperative blood loss. In this case, the patient presents with progressive neurologic decline and imaging indicative of a renal cell carcinoma primary, making preoperative embolization, surgical decompression, instrumented stabilization, and postoperative radiotherapy the most appropriate management course.

*Barzilai et al. reviewed the essential concepts for the management of metastatic spine disease.* **The authors state** that recent advances in spinal oncology have emphasized the critical role of the orthopedic surgeon in managing spinal metastatic disease, particularly as patients live longer and demand durable local control and preserved function. The integration of advanced imaging, precision oncologic therapies, and minimally invasive surgical techniques has transformed care, requiring close collaboration within multidisciplinary teams. Key decision-making tools include SINS, the Epidural Spinal Cord Compression (ESCC) scale, tumor histology, and the genomic profile to guide treatment planning. **The authors conclude** that surgical indications remain focused on mechanical instability and high-grade cord compression, with operative goals shifting toward less invasive approaches that maximize the quality of life and functional outcomes.

*Xiong et al. analyzed the clinicopathologic features and prognostic factors of primary spinal epidural lymphomas (PSELs), offering insights into malignant spinal cord compressive lesions distinct from metastatic disease.* **The authors state** that combined modality treatment (surgery with chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy) was associated with significantly improved disease-free survival, though not overall survival in PSEL. Thoracic spine location was identified as a poor prognostic factor, while favorable outcomes were linked to cervical spine involvement, B-cell histology, female sex, and multimodal therapy. Despite the rarity of PSEL, the 3-year overall survival was relatively high at 81.1%, with nearly half of the patients achieving tumor-free status. **The authors conclude** that these findings support an aggressive, multidisciplinary approach to optimize outcomes in PSEL management.

Figure A depicts an axial CT demonstrating a mass in the left kidney indicative of renal cell carcinoma. ​Figures B and C depict axial and sagittal T2-weighted MRI scans showing metastatic disease in the thoracic spine with resultant spinal cord compression. Illustrations A and B demonstrate AP and lateral radiographs of the thoracic spine status post T10-L2 posterior spinal instrumented fusion with T12 corpectomy and associated coil embolization.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Posterior decompression alone would not eliminate the primary source of cord compression from anteriorly based metastatic disease burden.
Answer 2: In a patient with renal cell carcinoma, embolization should occur before surgical management to minimize intraoperative blood loss.
Answer 3: In a patient with cord compression, preoperative radiotherapy for a radioresistant disease process is inappropriate.
Answer 4: This patient has a life expectancy of >6 months, which makes palliative care inappropriate.
