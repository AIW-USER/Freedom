## Question

You are consulted on a 64-year-old male with a past medical history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, intravenous drug use, and chronic kidney disease who was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) yesterday for sepsis believed to be secondary to volar forearm cellulitis. The ICU provider noted the patient deteriorated overnight, requiring intubation, and has since required norepinephrine. On examination, there is poorly demarcated erythema on the volar forearm with significant edema and bullae formation. The patient is subsequently brought to the operating room for incision and debridement, where infection, skin, and underlying fascia are excised, with a defect measuring 20 by 45 centimeters. Which of the following is most predictive of mortality in this patient?

## Options

1. History of IV drug abuse
2. Concominant cellulitic infection
3. ICU admission
4. Vasopressor requirement
5. Size of initial debridement

## Correct option

**4. Vasopressor requirement**

## Answer explanation

This patient has become septic secondary to necrotizing fasciitis, requiring intubation. The strongest predictor for mortality in this patient is the requirement for vasopressors (Answer 4).

Necrotizing fasciitis is a severe infection of the soft tissue and underlying fascia, ultimately eliciting a robust inflammatory response, causing rapid deterioration in patients. Early signs may demonstrate a benign presentation similar to cellulitis (poorly demarcated erythema with swelling). While cellulitis can and is often seen with necrotizing fasciitis, repeated examinations would reveal worsening pain, bullae formation, and systemic signs (high fever, tachycardia, hypotension). Several factors have been linked to necrotizing fasciitis, with immune-suppressing comorbidities like diabetes, AIDS, and cancer being commonly associated; moreover, lifestyle factors like intravenous drug use also portend risk for the condition. While these factors increase the risk of infection, they do not function as predictors for mortality. The requirement for vasopressors outside of operative anesthesia has been shown to be the strongest predictor for mortality in patients.

*Crowe et al. performed a retrospective institutional database review to examine predictors of outcomes in 99 patients with necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTI) of the upper extremity.* Following multivariate regression, vasopressor use was found to be the only predictor for in-hospital mortality. It was also a predictor for final wound defect size. Though an admission serum creatinine of 1.6 or greater was associated with amputation on bivariate analysis, this was not deemed a risk factor with multivariate analysis. **The authors concluded** that early identification and surgical debridement are crucial to reducing the burden of disease and mitigating the risk of poor outcomes.

*Chauhan et al. reviewed upper extremity necrotizing fasciitis, discussing the clinical presentation, diagnosis, and treatment.* *They review the risk factors for both the development of and prognosis after the onset of necrotizing fasciitis.* **The authors note** pain, erythema, and edema as the most common signs of infection, while IV drug use, smoking, and diabetes were also commonly associated risk factors for infection. Meanwhile, heart disease, white counts greater than 30,000, and creatinine greater than 2 mg/dL were independent risk factors for death. The authors recognize the varied presentation of upper extremity NSTIs and encourage a high degree of suspicion for the condition in patients with the aforementioned risk factors.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: while IV drug abuse is a risk factor for the development of necrotizing fasciitis, it is not a predictor of mortality.
Answer 2: concomitant cellulitis is not predictive of NSTI outcomes.
Answer 3: while vasopressors predict mortality, ICU admission alone does not.
Answer 5: the size of the initial debridement is most predictive of final wound defect size, not mortality.
