## Question

A 41-year-old male laborer presents with worsening left great toe pain, associated plantar lesser toe pain, and callosities. Examination reveals a well-healed incision with no erythema and 35° of dorsiflexion. Radiographs of the left foot are shown in Figure A. Laboratory testing reveals normal ESR, CRP, and white blood cell count from joint aspiration. He has failed all conservative measures and states he would like to proceed with surgical intervention. Which of the following is the next best step in surgical management?

![[q3872-a-failed-first-mtp-arthroplasty.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Revision arthroplasty with a long-stemmed prosthesis
2. Removal of hardware and conversion to silastic implant
3. Removal of hardware, I&D, antibiotic spacer placement, and delayed reimplantation
4. Implant removal and synovectomy
5. Implant removal, synovectomy, structural bone grafting, and arthrodesis

## Correct option

**5. Implant removal, synovectomy, structural bone grafting, and arthrodesis**

## Answer explanation

Mechanical failure of a 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (MTPJ) arthroplasty with great toe pain and transfer metatarsalgia is best treated with removal of hardware, synovectomy, structural bone grafting, and joint arthrodesis.

A 1st MTPJ arthroplasty may fail for a variety of reasons, the most common being aseptic implant loosening, subsidence, or infection. Removal of implants and arthrodesis is the most reliable surgical option. However, it is often complicated by significant bone loss following removal of implants. Structural bone grafting is therefore used to restore the original length and alignment of the hallux to achieve fusion and limit postoperative transfer metatarsalgia. If an infection is found upon workup, arthrodesis is often staged following treatment with an antibiotic-eluting spacer.

*Sullivan reviewed the literature regarding various surgical implants (silastic, hemiarthroplasty, total arthroplasty) for the treatment of hallux rigidus.* Among the various total joint arthroplasty options, radiolucency was seen anywhere from 23%-100% of the time with a high rate of salvage or revision surgery. It was concluded that the high complication rate of total joint arthroplasty for hallux rigidus makes it difficult to recommend their use compared to other treatment modalities.

*Fuhrmann et al. reported on their outcomes of 43 1st MTPJ arthroplasties.* *At an average follow-up of three years,* **they found** radiolucency around 23% of the phalangeal components and 10% of the metatarsal components. **They concluded** that MTPJ arthroplasty provides some unique advantages in the treatment of hallux rigidus; however, further research on design and fixation should be done to minimize complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Revision with a long-stemmed prosthesis would risk further loosening of the proximal phalanx component and continued transfer metatarsalgia pain.
Answer 2: Conversion to a silastic implant would likely lead to continued pain given the resurfaced proximal phalanx, shortening, and worsening transfer metatarsalgia.
Answer 3: This patient had a negative infectious workup so two-stage reimplantation would be an unnecessary procedure.
Answer 4: Implant removal and synovectomy would fail to address the lesser forefoot pain and would leave this patient with very limited push-off strength.
