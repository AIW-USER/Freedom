## Question

A skeletally immature basketball player presents to your clinic 1 week after twisting his left ankle. He has an antalgic gait with swelling to his lateral ankle. Radiographs are shown in Figure A. Which of the following is most likely to be seen on his ankle MRI?

![[q215421-a-normal-ankle-x-ray-7-year-old-2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Diffuse distal fibula marrow signal without associated fracture line
2. Fibular physis widening with increased physeal signal
3. Nondisplaced talar dome cartilage lesion with high signal
4. Signal in the lateral ligamentous complex with disruption or morphological changes
5. Signal and split tearing within the peroneus brevis tendon

## Correct option

**4. Signal in the lateral ligamentous complex with disruption or morphological changes**

## Answer explanation

A lateral ligament sprain is the most common injury seen in the setting of pediatric ankle inversion injury. An MRI of this patient’s ankle would most likely demonstrate increased signal intensity with morphological changes and/or disruption in the lateral ligament complex.

The conventional wisdom that children ‘don’t get ankle sprains’ dates back to 1963 when Salter and Harris suggested that pediatric growth plate injuries are more common than ligamentous injuries. This commonly leads clinicians to falsely diagnose children with traumatic ankle injuries and negative radiographs with a Salter-Harris type I fracture which is frequently treated with a period of cast immobilization and non-weight bearing. Recent MRI studies have challenged this notion and suggest that lateral ligament injury is the most common finding in pediatric ankle inversion injuries.

*Boutis et al.* (2010) evaluated children with isolated lateral ankle injuries without radiograph-visible fractures utilizing MRI. They looked at 18 children with an average age of 8.7 years and found 14 (78%) had evidence of ligamentous sprains, 11 (61%) had bony contusions, 1 patient (6%) had a subtle fibular avulsion fracture, and another was found to have a minor articular cartilage injury. None of the patients had evidence of a Salter-Harris type I distal fibula fracture on MRI.

*Boutis et al.* *(2016) prospectively evaluated 135 skeletally immature children with ankle inversion injuries in the setting of negative radiographs.* Using MRI **they found** that 80% had lateral ligamentous injuries, 28% had occult fibular avulsion fractures, 22% had isolated bone contusions, and 3% had Salter-Harris type I distal fibula fractures. **They concluded** that a majority of these injuries can be successfully treated with a removable ankle brace and a self-regulated return to activities.

Figure A demonstrates a skeletally immature left ankle without any osseous abnormalities. Illustration A is a T2-weighted MRI demonstrating a Salter-Harris type I fracture with physeal widening and increased signal uptake in the growth plate (white arrowhead) and elevated periosteum by the subperiosteal hematoma extending proximally from the growth plate (blue arrowhead).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: These findings represent a bone contusion. Commonly seen in this population, but not as common as lateral ligament injury.
Answer 2: These findings represent a Salter-Harris type I fracture, which is not as commonly seen in this setting as a lateral ligament injury
Answer 3: These findings represent an osteochondral lesion of the talus, which is not as commonly seen in this setting as a lateral ligament injury.
Answer 5: These findings represent a peroneal brevis tendon tear. While commonly seen in an adolescent/adult population after ankle inversion injuries, it is not frequently seen in this pediatric population.
