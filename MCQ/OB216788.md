## Question

A 26-year-old computer analyst presents for evaluation of his right shoulder. He recently underwent closed reduction in the Emergency Department for his second dislocation while playing recreational basketball. Imaging reveals a soft-tissue Bankart injury with a small "on-track" Hill-Sachs lesion. The decision is made to proceed with Bankart repair. Which of the following is true of Arthroscopic versus Open Bankart repair?

## Options

1. Arthroscopic Bankart repair has a lower failure rate
2. Arthroscopic Bankart repair has a greater return to activity
3. Have similar dislocation rates
4. Open Bankart repair has a faster return to sports
5. Open Bankart repair leads to greater postoperative osteoarthritis

## Correct option

**3. Have similar dislocation rates**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a recurrent shoulder dislocation with a soft-tissue Bankart injury. Randomized controlled trials have shown no statistically significant difference in re-dislocation rates, return to activity, or functional outcomes between the arthroscopic and open repair groups.

Traumatic anterior shoulder instability, also referred to as TUBS (Traumatic Unilateral dislocations with a Bankart lesion requiring Surgery), are traumatic shoulder injuries that generally occur as a result of an anterior force to the shoulder while it is in an abducted and externally rotated position. Diagnosis is made clinically with the presence of positive anterior instability provocative tests and confirmed with MRI studies that may reveal labrum and/or bony injuries of the glenoid and proximal humerus (Hill-Sachs). Treatment may be nonoperative or operative depending on chronicity of symptoms, recurrence of instability, and the severity of labrum and/or glenoid bone defects. Indications for Bankart repair +/- a capsular shift include a first-time traumatic shoulder dislocation with Bankart lesion confirmed by MRI in an athlete <25 years of age, high demand and/or collision athletes, recurrent dislocation/subluxation following nonoperative management, or minimal glenoid bone loss.

*Mologne et al. reviewed the outcomes of arthroscopic stabilization for recurrent anterior shoulder instability in patients with a mean anteroinferior glenoid bone deficiency of 25% (range, 20%-30%).* **They reported** that 9.5% of patients experienced symptoms of recurrent subluxation. **They concluded** that arthroscopic stabilization for recurrent instability, even in the presence of a significant bony defect of the glenoid, can yield a stable shoulder; however, outcomes are not as predictable especially in attritional bone loss cases.

*Godin et al. reviewed the literature to provide clinical recommendations regarding the most appropriate therapeutic intervention for recurrent anterior shoulder instability.* *They reported on four randomized controlled trials that compared the use of arthroscopic and open repair for recurrent anterior shoulder dislocations, and found that these studies show no statistically significant difference between the two operative approaches.* **They concluded** that the available evidence from randomized controlled trials does not show a statistically significant difference in re-dislocation rates, return to activity, and functional outcomes between the arthroscopic and open repair groups.

*Harris et al. reviewed the long-term outcomes in patients who have undergone open or arthroscopic Bankart repair.* **They report** that surgical treatment of anterior shoulder instability using arthroscopic suture anchor or open Bankart techniques yields similar long-term clinical outcomes, with no significant difference in the rate of recurrent instability, clinical outcome scores, or rate of return to sport. **They concluded** that no significant difference was shown in the incidence of postoperative osteoarthritis with open versus arthroscopic suture anchor repair.
