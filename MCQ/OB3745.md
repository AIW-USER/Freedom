## Question

A 73-year-old female presents for evaluation of midline thoracolumbar back pain which she states began approximately four weeks ago. She is unsure of what precipitated this pain but believes it may have started when lifting a chair at home. The pain is severe and she has difficulty standing for long periods of time, and her walking is limited to short distances around the house. A plain radiograph is shown in Figure A. Which study would best characterize the acuity of the finding in Figure A?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Computed tomography (CT) scan of the lumbar spine
2. CT myelogram of the lumbar spine
3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine
4. Flexion and extension plain radiographs of the lumbar spine
5. Indium-labeled white blood cell scan

## Correct option

**3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a compression fracture of the first lumbar vertebra. MRI scanning will best characterize the age of this fracture.

Although CT may be useful for detection of fractures not readily visible on plain radiography, MRI provides more information when the fracture is readily diagnosed on plain films. The amount of bone edema on MRI inversely correlates with the age of the fracture. MRI can also diagnose pathologic lesions that may have caused the fracture and can evaluate the neurologic elements and degree of spinal stenosis.

Figure A is a lateral lumbar radiograph showing an anterior compression fracture of the L1 vertebra. Illustration A shows a characteristic edema pattern for acute vertebral compression fracture.

*Esses et al. authored a clinical practice guideline on the treatment of symptomatic osteoporotic spinal compression fractures.* The only 'strong' recommendation was against the use of vertebroplasty for treatment of these fractures. There was a moderate recommendation in favor of calcitonin for four weeks following diagnosis.

*Schilcher et al. penned a letter to the editor of the New England Journal of Medicine in which* **they noted** that the age-adjusted relative risk of an atypical femoral fracture was 55.2 for women and 54.1 for men. Risk accumulates with progressive use, with relative risk reaching 126.0 after four years.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 2: The fracture is well characterized on plain films so the osseous detail provided by CT may not be necessary. Although callus or sclerosis seen on CT may give some indication of chronicity, MRI is more sensitive for determining fracture age.
Answer 4: Flexion-extension views may give an indication of stability but do not contribute information of age of fracture.
Answer 5: A white blood cell scan would be used to evaluate for infection.
