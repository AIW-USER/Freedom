## Question

A 55-year-old construction worker with diet-controlled type II diabetes mellitus presents to your office for evaluation of a painful bump over the lateral aspect of his forefoot. He has tried wide toebox shoes, padding/band-aids, and custom orthotics without significant long-term relief. On radiographs, he has a 4-5 intermetatarsal angle (IMA) of 13 degrees, a fifth metatarsal head width of 16mm, and a congruent fifth metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint. What is the best treatment for this patient?

## Options

1. Condylectomy of the fifth metatarsal head
2. Diaphyseal metatarsal osteotomy alone
3. Diaphyseal metatarsal osteotomy and fifth metatarsal condylectomy
4. Distal fifth metatarsal osteotomy and medial soft tissue release
5. Medial fifth MTP joint soft tissue release and lateral MTP joint imbrication

## Correct option

**3. Diaphyseal metatarsal osteotomy and fifth metatarsal condylectomy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has clinical and radiographic evidence of a bunionette deformity with a widened intermetatarsal angle (IMA), increased metatarsal head width, and a congruent fifth metatarsophalangeal joint. Due to the increased IMA and wide condylar width, this patient would best be treated with a diaphyseal metatarsal osteotomy and lateral condylectomy to fully correct his deformity.

Similar to the more common bunion deformity, bunionettes can occur due to proximal and distal deformities, with each requiring unique surgical strategies for optimal correction. Increased IMA (normal 6.5-8 degrees) requires a metatarsal osteotomy to narrow the IMA. Typically a diaphyseal osteotomy is used for IMA >12 degrees while a distal chevron-style osteotomy is used for IMA 9-12 degrees. Condylar widening can result in a residual perceived bunionette despite a normal IMA and incomplete relief. This should also be managed with a lateral condylectomy.

Cohen and Nicholson provide an excellent review on the bunionette deformity. They describe the various deformities that lead to a bunionette and the non-surgical and surgical means to manage them. Most patients can be managed with wide-toe shoes and padding, but when that fails, surgery should be considered. Appropriate interpretation of patient presentation and radiographs will help guide the surgeon in developing a tailored surgical plan for each individual patient.

The Clinical Practice Guideline Forefoot Disorders Panel wrote an extensive review on the management of bunionette deformities. The flowsheet on step-wise management is shown below. They recommend exhaustive non-surgical management, as a vast majority of patients respond well, but they recognize a need for surgical intervention in select patients. They state the goal of surgical management is to decrease the bony lateral prominence as this drives the soft tissue disorders that cause pain, which are primarily hyperkeratoses and ulcers.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has an increased IMA (>12 deg), which would require a diaphyseal osteotomy plus a lateral condylectomy of the fifth metatarsal.
Answer 2: The patient's condylar width is greater than normal (<13mm), which may lead to residual lateral prominence and continued symptoms if a condylectomy is not performed.
Answer 4: The patient's IMA is >12 deg which would typically require a diaphyseal osteotomy rather than a distal osteotomy.
Answer 5: Soft tissue-only management would not correct the underlying bony deformity. Also, the patient has a congruent MTPJ, which would not require correction of the surrounding soft tissue structures.
