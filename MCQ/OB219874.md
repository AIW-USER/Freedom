## Answer explanation

Out of the given options, an ulnar shortening osteotomy is the preferred operative intervention for ulnar impaction syndrome, as demonstrated by the MRI in Figure A (Answer 4).

Ulnar impaction syndrome is a common condition precipitated by impaction between the ulnar styloid and the triquetrum, resulting in ulnar-sided wrist pain. Described as a degenerative condition resulting from excessive load transfer across the ulnocarpal joint associated with the abutment of the ulnar pole against the ulnar carpus, it most commonly occurs in the ulnar-positive variant wrist. Still, it may occur in neutral or ulnar negative variance. Prolonged impaction leads to degenerative changes in the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC), chondromalacia of the lunate, triquetrum, and distal ulnar head, instability of the lunotriquetral joint, and finally, degenerative arthrosis of the ulnocarpal and distal radioulnar joints (DRUJ). Nonoperative treatment includes immobilization, sport-specific bracing, and physical therapy, which may be a definitive treatment for non-athletes. In the athletic population, however, symptoms often recur. Nonoperative treatment modalities are frequently used to help the athlete complete the season before definitive surgical management, which most often consists of an ulnar-shortening osteotomy.

*Abzug, Little, and Kozin published a 2014 JAAOS review on post-traumatic physeal arrest of the distal radius, which can result in a forearm growth differential resulting in ulnar abutment.* *Patients may present with ulnar-sided wrist pain and/or deformity, such as prominence of the ulnar head.* Depending on the amount of growth remaining, management may range from observation (in those with little growth remaining) to bar resection, epiphysiodesis, ulnar shortening, or radial lengthening. Distraction osteogenesis may also be used in cases of large bone length discrepancies.

*Acott and Greenberg published a 2020 review on ulnar abutment syndrome in the athlete.* Acquired ulnar positive variance may be seen in gymnasts or football linemen with distal radial physeal growth arrest due to repetitive loading of the distal radial physis that occurs before skeletal maturity. Patients usually present with insidious onset ulnar wrist pain with a positive ulnocarpal stress test. Nonoperative treatment may be attempted based on the athlete’s sport, season (in-season or off-season), and functional demands. Surgical treatment focuses on mechanically decreasing ulnocarpal joint load and includes an open wafer resection procedure, diaphyseal ulnar shortening osteotomy, and ulnar metaphyseal closing wedge osteotomy.

*Mauck et al. published a 2020 review on distal radial physeal stress syndrome, dubbed “gymnast’s wrist”.* **The authors emphasize** that the distal radial physis is a common site for injury in gymnasts because of the significant loads applied during upper extremity weight-bearing, with pain reported in up to 88% of gymnasts. In addition to high-impact loading, the wrist is exposed to repetitive motion, axial compression, torsional forces, and distraction. Treatment varies by site and type of arrest, but radial lengthening or ulnar shortening is usually reserved for skeletally mature patients.
