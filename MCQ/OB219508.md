## Question

A surgeon develops a resection plan with a robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty system which calls for an 8mm cut of the medial posterior femoral condyle. After completing the femoral resection the surgeon measures the medial posterior femoral condyle and discovers 13mm of posterior femur was removed. A similar error occurs on the lateral side despite using appropriate landmarks duing the registration process. If the surgeon does not address this unexpected increase in the flexion gap, what symptoms will the patient most likely develop post-operatively?

## Options

1. Hyperextension of the knee joint
2. Decreased terminal flexion
3. Difficulty rising from a seated position
4. Instability in the coronal plane with the knee extended
5. Hamstring weakness

## Correct option

**3. Difficulty rising from a seated position**

## Answer explanation

Flexion instability is caused by an increase in the flexion gap in comparison to the extension gap. Patients with flexion instability typically present with recurrent effusions, quadriceps weakness, diffuse pain, and difficulty rising from a seated position.

During total knee arthroplasty, strict attention must be given to the gap between the femur and tibia in both full extension and 90 degrees of flexion. These gaps are respectively called the extension and flexion gaps. The tibial cut affects both extension and flexion gaps. While the distal femoral cut primarily affects the extension gap, the posterior femoral cut affects the flexion gap. Over-aggressive posterior femoral cuts, increased tibial slope, and undersized femoral components can all cause flexion instability. Patients with flexion instability are often unhappy with their total knee and present with the classic aforementioned constellation of symptoms, which include recurrent effusions, quadriceps weakness, difficulty with stairs, and difficulty rising from seated positions.

*Stambough et al published a review article regarding the causes, diagnosis, and treatment of flexion instability after total knee arthroplasty.* As flexion instability is a clinical diagnosis, **they recommend** a thorough workup including in-depth history, serum inflammatory markers, aspiration as indicated, and physical exam. There is no consensus on diagnostic exam findings, but excessive anteroposterior translation is often reproduced at 90 degrees of flexion.

*Al-Jabri et al published a systematic review of the management of instability after primary total knee arthroplasty.* **They report** that there is limited evidence to support the non-operative management of flexion instability. Due to the mechanical nature of the problem, **they recommend** revision surgery. They reviewed a separate case series of 60 patients who had significant improvement in the mean knee society scores and no reports of instability occurred after revision surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Flexion instability does not lead to hyperextension of the knee joint. Some patients develop paradoxical flexion contractures due to fatigue of the quadriceps.
Answer 2: Flexion instability often leads to an increase (not a decrease) in terminal flexion.
Answer 4: Instability in knee extension is not associated with an excessive flexion gap.
Answer 5: Quadriceps weakness is associated with flexion instability.
