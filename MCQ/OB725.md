## Question

A 34-year-old seamstress was diagnosed with Parsonage-Turner brachial neuritis in the right upper extremity one month ago. She has weak forearm pronation with the elbow in the flexed position. She denies any current sensory changes and admits vague and deep right forearm pain. A clinical image of her hands attempting to make a clenched fist is shown in Figure A. Which of the following most likely represents her diagnosis and treatment?

![[q725-a-ain-palsy.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome treated with observation
2. Posterior interosseous nerve syndrome with release of the Arcade of Frohse
3. Pronator syndrome with surgical release of the lacertus fibrosis
4. Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome with surgical release of Gantzer's muscle
5. Posterior interosseous nerve syndrome treated with observation

## Correct option

**1. Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome treated with observation**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) syndrome which is often seen in conjunction with brachial neuritis (Parsonage-Turner Syndrome).

AIN syndrome leads to pure motor palsies of the flexor pollicis longus and the two radial profundus tendons, leading to the clinical image shown in Figure A and the inability to perform an A-OK sign. The pronator quadratus is also involved and can be tested with the elbow held in a flexed position to neutralize the humeral head of the pronator teres muscle. No sensory changes occur, and electromyographic (EMG) and nerve conduction (NCV) studies are often helpful in establishing the diagnosis. Treatment of AIN syndrome most commonly involves a prolonged nonoperative course and rarely surgical decompression.

*The Level 5 review article by Rodner et al. discusses that AIN syndrome usually resolves with time, particularly if the lesion is secondary to neuritis.* Observation for 3 to 6 months with splinting at 90 degrees is favored before surgical treatment.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choices 2 and 5: PIN nerve syndrome would include the inability to perform retropulsion on the exam, not difficulty with IP joint flexion.
Answer choice 3: Pronator syndrome typically presents with proximal forearm pain, and sensory changes would be present, unlike this case.
Answer choice 4: AIN syndrome is treated initially with nonoperative treatment. However, operative treatment can be pursued if these conservative options fail to improve symptoms.
