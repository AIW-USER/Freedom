## Question

A 45 years male has come with proximal arm pain. There is no significant trauma history. X ray revealed pathological fracture proximal humerus with moth eaten appearance. The most likely diagnosis from the list is

## Options

1. Osteosarcoma
2. Ewings sarcoma
3. Lymphoma
4. Chordoma

## Correct option

**3. Lymphoma**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C

EXPLANATION: -

The main features that should be assessed for a neoplastic bone lesion:
• Location within bone
• Zone of transition/ Margins
• Matrix
• Morphology
• Periosteal reaction
• Size
• Cortical involvement
• Extra-osseous or soft-tissue component

Of particular importance are the top two points.

Fig: Location of various bone tumors
Margins: A lot of information can be revealed by close analysis of tumor margins. For this purpose the margins are divided into three types. While Type 1 are well defined, Type 2 and 3 margins are nongeographic and consist of ill-defined “fields” of bone destruction.

![[TO21733172-2.jpg]]

Type I margins are round or oval and typically correspond to less-aggressive tumours.
Type 1A - sclerotic margins. They are seen in SBC, Fibrous dysplasia, Enchondroma, Chondroblastoma, GCT, Chondrosarcoma

![[TO21733172-3.png]]

Type 1B - ill defined non sclerotic. These are seen in GCT, Enchondroma, Chondroblastoma, Multiple Myeloma, Metastasis, Fibrous dysplasia, Chondrosarcoma

![[TO21733172-4.png]]

Type 1C - lytic ill defined margins. These are seen with Chondrosarcoma, Osteosarcoma, GCT, Metastasis, Infection, Eosinophilic granuloma, Lymphoma

![[TO21733172-5.png]]

Type 2 margins- These refer to moth eaten-multiple scattered osteolytic holes that vary in size and shape and can arise separately on a background of relatively intact cortex. These are mostly seen with Multiple Myeloma, Metastasis, Infection, Eosinophilic granuloma, Osteosarcoma, Chondrosarcoma, Lymphoma

![[TO21733172-6.png]]

Type 3 margins – There are poorly demarcated, numerous foci of lytic lesions running parallel to long axis of bone. The type 3 margins correspond to the most highly aggressive appearance and is described as permeative.
These may be seen with Ewings sarcoma, Infection, Multiple Myeloma, Metastasis, Lymphoma, Osteosarcoma.

![[TO21733172-7.png]]

In given question when we consider age, location and X ray picture (moth eaten) Lymphoma seems to be best pick.

PRIMARY BONE LYMPHOMA
Primary bone lymphoma (PBL) is a subtype of lymphoma that exclusively affects skeletal tissue. World health organization (WHO) classification of soft tissues and bone tumors defined PBL as either single osseous lesions without regional lymph node involvement, or tumor involvement of multiple osseous sites without associated visceral or lymph node disease. The prevalence of PBL is estimated to be 3–7% amongst primary bone tumors. The median age range for patient's diagnosed with PBL is 45 to 60 years old. There is male preponderance with a male-to-female ratio ranging from 1.2 to 1.8. Pain (82–92%) and swelling (34–45%) of the involved site are two of the most common clinical manifestations of this disease and another common presentation could include pathological fractures.
Radiographic findings for cases of PBL can be difficult to distinguish from other primary bone tumors such as osteosarcoma, ewing's sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma. Plain x-ray films are the initial diagnostic test of choice. PBL most commonly presents as osteolytic (moth eaten/ permeative) or osteoblastic lesions with disease involvement of the cortex and reactive periosteal change. CT and MRI can be helpful for detecting extent and extraosseous involvement and planning.
Treatment of choice is chemo. Chemotherapeutic regimens usually consist of anthracycline-based combination therapies such as cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (chop) with or without rituximab.
