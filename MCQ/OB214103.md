## Question

A 6-year-old male presents with complaints of right hip pain for the past 3 days with refusal to bear weight. He has a fever of 102°F, a CRP of 13.1 mg/dl (nl <2.0), ESR 65 mm/h (nl <20), and WBC of 13.8 (nl <10.0). Figure A is the ultrasound that was obtained in the emergency department. The decision is made to aspirate the hip. Which of the following is representative of the most common causative organism of this condition in this patient?

![[q214103-a-hip-ultrasound.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Catalase and coagulase positive, gram-positive cocci in clusters
2. Catalase positive and coagulase negative, gram-positive cocci in clusters
3. Catalase and coagulase negative, gram-positive cocci in clusters
4. Catalase and coagulase negative, gram-positive cocci in chains
5. Catalase and coagulase positive, gram-positive cocci in chains

## Correct option

**1. Catalase and coagulase positive, gram-positive cocci in clusters**

## Answer explanation

The patient's clinical presentation is concerning for septic arthritis of the hip. Staphylococcus aureus, gram-positive cocci in clusters, is the most common infectious agent in pediatric patients greater than 2-year-old.

The Kocher criteria is commonly utilized when evaluating a patient with suspected septic arthropathy of the hip. The Kocher criteria includes a WBC >12,000 cells/µl of serum, inability to bear weight, history of fever > 101.3°F (38.5°C), and ESR > 40 mm/h. When all 4 are positive, the probability of septic arthritis exceeds 99%. Additionally, this patient has an elevated CRP. Organisms implicated in pediatric septic arthritis vary by age. Neonates are at risk of infection with group B streptococcus and E. coli. In pediatric patients >2-years-old, S. aureus remains the most common organism. Staph aureus is a catalase and coagulase-positive organism. In adolescents, N. gonorrhea remains a characteristic infectious agent. Treatment of non-gonococcal septic arthritis includes surgical drainage followed by intravenous antibiotics.

*Kishibe et al. reviewed a case of a septic hip with hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae in a 14-year-old patient.* **They report** that despite antibiotics and surgical intervention, septic arthritis developed into osteomyelitis of the femoral head and myositis, which required long-term antibiotic therapy and additional surgical intervention. **They concluded** that treatment plans should mainly consist of antibiotic therapy and surgical intervention and that clinicians should be aware of the increasing incidence of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae infection.

*Williams et al. retrospectively reviewed the clinical course of children diagnosed with K. kingae septic arthritis.* **They found** 2 children who had PCR positive synovial fluid samples for K. kingae for a septic hip. **They concluded** that a high index of suspicion in young children presenting with joint inflammation, especially if indices of infection are mild and that it is likely that children historically treated with antibiotics for "culture-negative" septic arthritis were infected with K. kingae.

*Livingston et al. attempted to identify risk factors associated with repeat surgical irrigation in pediatric septic hip arthritis.* *In this single-center retrospective case-control study, patients who underwent ≥2 washouts (26 patients) were compared with those who had only 1 washout (63 patients).* **They report** that the most common reason for repeat washout was persistent fever (N=21), followed by persistently elevated laboratory values (N=13), abnormal magnetic resonance imaging findings (N=12), and continued pain (N=12). **They concluded** that cases of pediatric septic arthritis which undergo repeat washout are associated with left shift, high postoperative temperatures, and positive cultures, and have more frequent misdiagnosis leading to delayed treatment and subsequent medical complications.
