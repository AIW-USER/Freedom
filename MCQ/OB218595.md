## Question

A 45-year-old male presents to your office with complaints of ankle pain and weakness after an injury he sustained overseas two years ago. He now has significant weakness with ankle plantarflexion and his ankle rests in slight dorsiflexion while prone with his knees flexed to 90°. His injury has never been formally treated and is now affecting his gait and daily activities. An MRI is ordered which demonstrates a chronic mid-substance Achilles tendon rupture with 3 cm of retraction. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Percutaneous Achilles tendon repair
2. End-to-end open Achilles tendon repair
3. Peroneus brevis transfer
4. Gastrocnemius-soleus VY advancement +/- FHL transfer
5. Allograft reconstruction

## Correct option

**4. Gastrocnemius-soleus VY advancement +/- FHL transfer**

## Answer explanation

In cases of chronic Achilles rupture with 2-5 cm of retraction, gastrocnemius-soleus VY advancement +/- flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon transfer is the preferred treatment.

Chronic Achilles tendon ruptures pose a difficult problem to orthopedic surgeons, as the proximal tendon stump retracts and scars to the surrounding structures. Achilles tendon ruptures are typically defined as chronic after 6 weeks or so of being untreated. Classically, those with <2 cm of tendon retraction can be treated with attempted end-to-end repair. Chronic ruptures with 2-5 cm of retraction are best addressed with gastrocnemius-soleus VY advancement +/- FHL tendon transfer. FHL transfer has been shown to be biomechanically stronger than other options such as peroneus brevis and plantaris transfers. With >5 cm of retraction, an FHL tendon transfer is indicated, and in some cases, an allograft reconstruction may be considered.

*Padanilam et al. reviewed chronic Achilles tendon ruptures,* **noting** that surgical treatment is almost always indicated to maximize these patient's function. In this review they talk about different methods that have been utilized to treat chronic Achilles ruptures including gastrocnemius-soleus VY advancement, Achilles fascial turndown flaps, local/free tendon transfers and synthetic grafts. At the time of this study being published, **they note** there were no great comparative studies to assist in choosing a particular procedure.

*Rahm et al. looked at the clinical and radiographic outcomes of FHL tendon transfers in cases of chronic irreparable Achilles tendon ruptures.* *They used two different FHL transfer techniques, using both transtendinous and transosseous methods of fixation.* *Both groups showed considerable postoperative improvement in function but there were no significant differences in clinical outcomes overall.*

*Den Hartog et al. reviewed surgical strategies for neglected Achilles tendon ruptures.* **The authors note** that ruptures older than 6 weeks old have significant amounts of scarring and retraction which can make primary repair difficult. They go on to discuss many of the aforementioned operative strategies and the techniques associated with them.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: With a chronic Achilles tendon rupture and residual defect >2 cm, end-to-end repair is not a viable option because the gastrocnemius-soleus complex is typically contracted and the tendon gap is too large.
Answer 3: Peroneus brevis tendon transfer was a historical choice for treating chronic Achilles ruptures, however, results have proven better with FHL transfer with better longevity and less eversion weakness.
Answer 5: Allograft reconstruction of the Achilles tendon is typically reserved for situations where there the defect is >5 cm and unable to be adequately managed with a VY advancement and FHL transfer.
