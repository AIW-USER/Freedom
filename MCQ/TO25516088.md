## Question

A 2-year-old child presents with severe rachitic deformities, profound hypocalcemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and total body alopecia. Serum 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D levels are measured to be >1000 pg/mL (normal: 18-72 pg/mL). Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management strategy to address the severe symptomatic hypocalcemia in this specific condition?

## Options

1. Subcutaneous recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH)
2. Subcutaneous Burosumab
3. High-dose oral calcitriol and elemental calcium
4. Prolonged high-dose intravenous calcium infusions

## Correct option

**4. Prolonged high-dose intravenous calcium infusions**

## Answer explanation

The patient has VDDR Type 2A (Vitamin D Receptor mutation), presenting with extreme end-organ resistance and alopecia. Bypassing the gut with massive intravenous calcium infusions is often the only way to maintain normocalcemia and heal the bones.

 Key Clinical Considerations & Monitoring

 X-Linked Hypophosphatemia (XLH)

 Conventional: Oral phosphate salts (divided 3–5 times/day) + Active Vitamin D (Calcitriol/Alfacalcidol).

 Targeted Biologic: Burosumab (anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody). Now considered first-line for children over 6 months and adults.

 Conventional therapy risks: Nephrocalcinosis and tertiary hyperparathyroidism. Requires frequent renal ultrasounds. Burosumab avoids these risks and superiorly corrects bone deformities.

 Autosomal Dominant Hypophosphatemic Rickets (ADHR)

 Oral phosphate + Active Vitamin D (Calcitriol).

 Iron supplementation.

 Because iron deficiency increases FGF23 transcription and triggers ADHR expression, iron repletion can sometimes normalize FGF23 levels and resolve the phenotype without long-term phosphate/calcitriol therapy.

 Autosomal Recessive Hypophosphatemic Rickets (ARHR)

 Oral phosphate + Active Vitamin D (Calcitriol).

 Burosumab (off-label use being explored in severe cases).

 High risk of severe enthesopathies and vascular calcification (especially in ENPP1 mutations). Conventional therapy must be carefully monitored to avoid exacerbating ectopic calcification.

 Hereditary Hypophosphatemic Rickets with Hypercalciuria (HHRH)

 Oral phosphate supplementation ONLY.

 None specific. Maintain adequate hydration to prevent nephrolithiasis.

 Contraindication: Active Vitamin D is strictly contraindicated. 1,25(OH)₂D levels are already elevated, and adding more will worsen hypercalciuria and rapidly cause kidney stones.

 Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets Type 1A (VDDR 1A)

 Active Vitamin D (Calcitriol or Alfacalcidol) in physiological maintenance doses.

 Adequate dietary or supplemental Calcium to ensure substrate for bone mineralization.

 Excellent and rapid response to calcitriol. Patients essentially live normal lives once the missing active metabolite is replaced. Monitor for hypercalcemia to titrate dose.

 Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets Type 1B (VDDR 1B)

 Calcidiol (25-hydroxyvitamin D) replacement.

 High-dose standard Vitamin D (Cholecalciferol) may be tried but is often less effective.

 Bypasses the hepatic defect. Monitor serum 25(OH)D and calcium levels to ensure adequate replacement without toxicity.

 Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets Type 2A & 2B (VDDR 2A/2B)

 Very high doses of oral Calcitriol (up to 10-100x normal) + very high doses of oral elemental Calcium.

 Prolonged Intravenous (IV) Calcium infusions (via central line) if oral therapy fails.

 The most difficult type to treat due to end-organ resistance. Alopecia (in 2A) does not respond to calcium therapy. Once bones heal with IV calcium, transition to massive oral calcium doses may be attempted to maintain normocalcemia.

Answer: 4
