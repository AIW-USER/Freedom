## Question

A 28-year-old professional baseball pitcher presents to the clinic for evaluation of medial-sided elbow pain that occurred acutely while pitching in a game two days ago. On examination, there is pain and swelling overlying the medial epicondyle. Moreover, pain is reproduced with valgus stress when pulling on the patient's thumb with the elbow flexed at 90 degrees, in addition to throughout elbow flexion and extension. The structure suspected to be injured in this patient is most commonly injured during which phase of throwing?

## Options

1. Wind-up
2. Early cocking
3. Late cocking
4. Deceleration
5. Follow-through

## Correct option

**3. Late cocking**

## Answer explanation

This 28-year-old male presents to the clinic with a presentation suggesting a medial ulnar collateral ligament (mUCL) tear while playing baseball. The throwing phases that subject the greatest valgus stress to the mUCL are the late cocking and early acceleration phases (Answer 3).

Medial ulnar collateral ligament injuries are rare among the general population, with the injury most commonly seen in skeletally mature pitchers and other overhead athletes. Athletes, in particular, are most at risk for mUCL injuries due to overuse, often stemming from too high of pitch counts, too short of rest between performances, or not taking seasons off throughout the year. Regardless, microtrauma of the mUCL occurs from repetitive valgus stress moments, which occur most significantly during the late cocking and early acceleration phase. With continued trauma over time, the anterior band of the UCL ruptures, leading to pain and decreased performance (loss of velocity and accuracy when pitching).

*Bruce and Andrews reviewed mUCL injuries in throwing athletes, discussing the anatomy and physiology of the ligament before delving into patient history, physical examination, imaging, prevention, and treatment.* **The authors highlight** pitching and resting guidelines for youth pitchers provided by the USA Baseball Medical Committee to limit the risk of injury. Interestingly, **the authors report** a discrepant existing body of literature concerning the impact of off-speed pitching (curveballs, sliders) on shoulder or elbow injuries requiring surgery, and instead highlight the more severe implications associated with overuse, noting a >500% risk for injury in patients pitching greater than 8 months out of the year.

*Smith et al. provide an overview detailing the physical examination of the elbow.* **The authors believe** that a physical examination of the elbow is crucial and most often allows one to formulate a diagnosis, with adjunctive imaging serving only as a confirmatory test. **The authors recommend** beginning the examination with palpation, range of motion, and strength, before going into special testing. **The authors conclude** that special testing, when performed accurately, can be more sensitive than advanced imaging.

*Stanborough et al. discuss the utility of the MRI for soft-tissue elbow injuries.* While an excellent adjunct when evaluating a patient, **the authors believe** a physical exam remains the gold standard for diagnosing most pathologies around the elbow. Nonetheless, when utilizing MRI, the authors highly recommend a step-wise approach to evaluating MRI of the elbow.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: minimal stress is imparted on the shoulder and elbow during this phase
Answer 2: early cocking demands the most activation of the deltoid and experiences a far lesser degree of stress compared to the late cocking phase
Answer 4: while the deceleration phase also possesses high degrees of stress on the upper extremity, this stress is mostly subjected to the labrum, biceps, and rotator cuff
Answer 5: minimal stress to the mUCL occurs in this phase
