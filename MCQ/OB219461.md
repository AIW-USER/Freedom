## Question

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement has demonstrated durable fixation of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) components and excellent long-term implant survivorship. However, improvements in the manufacturing designs have resurged enthusiasm for the use of cementless TKA components in the appropriately indicated patient population. In what patient population does evidence best support cementless (i.e. press-fit) TKA in comparison to conventional cemented TKA?

## Options

1. Younger, obese males without other significant comorbidities
2. Younger, obese females with a DEXA T-score of -1.9
3. Middle-aged women with rheumatoid arthritis
4. Older females (70+ years) without significant comorbidities
5. Older male (70+ years) smokers without other comorbidities

## Correct option

**1. Younger, obese males without other significant comorbidities**

## Answer explanation

In some studies, younger, obese males have demonstrated lower rates of tibial component aseptic loosening with the use of press-fit TKA components (Answer 1). Of note, these findings are based on retrospective cohort studies with short and intermediate (5-10 years) results.

Obesity is a growing public health concern, particularly in the United States. In addition to the overall detrimental health effects with resultant shorter lifespans, obesity also places patients at a significantly increased risk of needing a total knee replacement (TKA). These obese patients undergoing TKA are at an increased risk for perioperative complications such as infection, cardiac arrest, and need for revision surgery. Therefore, efforts towards minimizing these increased complications are prudent. In some studies, hydroxyapatite-coated cementless implants have demonstrated lower rates of revision due to aseptic loosing (particularly involving the tibial tray) at short-midterm follow-up. Further, infection rates have been shown to be comparable (with some studies showing lower) to standard cemented arthroplasty components. For these reasons, modern cementless implants are drawing increasing attention within the arthroplasty community. However, these findings are based on retrospective studies from a small number of centers. Prospective, randomized controlled trials and long-term follow-up studies are required.

*Sinicrope and colleagues pooled retrospective results from two institutions on the outcomes using primary cementless, posterior-stabilized TKA implants in morbidly obese patients (n=108).* *When compared to a cemented implant cohort (n=85), cementless implants demonstrated a significantly lower revision rate (0.9%: 5 failures; 1 for aseptic tibial loosening) with a minimum 5-year follow-up.* The cemented cohort demonstrated 22 failures (18.8%: 22 failures; 16 for aseptic loosening). **The authors concluded** that the use of cementless TKA in morbidly obese patients has the potential for durable biologic fixation and increased survivorship compared to cemented implant fixation.

*Anis and colleagues performed a case-control analysis using propensity score matching to compare post-operative infection rates between cemented and cementless TKA in their institutional data.* After matching for age, sex, BMI, and Charlson’s comorbidity index on a 1:1 ratio, **the authors identified** 133 cementless and 3,047 cemented TKAs. **They reported** no difference between both groups regarding infection rates (periprosthetic joint infections and surgical site infections). These results were consistent after utilizing multivariable analysis adjusting for procedure-related factors. **The authors concluded** that post-operative infection rates were similar between both fixation modalities even after accounting for a range of patient- and procedure-related factors.

*Grau and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institutional cohort in which a tantalum-backed, cementless patella component was utilized during primary TKA.* *Despite having radiolucent lines present in 15 cases (6.2%) that have a 2-year follow-up and 6 cases (14%) that have a 5-year follow-up,* **the authors report** no components needing to be revised for aseptic loosening with 100% survivorship. **The authors concluded** their cementless patellar components demonstrate excellent survivorship at 2-year and 5-year follow-ups.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2-5: All of these patients are at increased risk for poor bone quality (osteoporosis or osteopenia) either due to their age, medical comorbidities, or confirmed diagnostic testing. For this reason, it would be prudent to utilize PMMA cement in such primary TKA settings.
