## Question

A 17-year-old female with an L5 level myelomeningocele presents to your office with chronic pain in her bilateral feet. On exam, she demonstrates hindfoot valgus deformities with forefoot abduction bilaterally. A lateral plain radiograph taken in the office today is shown in Figure A. She has tried multiple rounds of ankle-foot orthoses without much relief or correction. She is inquiring about further treatment options in the form of surgical correction today. Which of the following deformities, if present preoperatively, tends to lead to less favorable results following surgical intervention?

![[q220879-a-newankle.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Ankle equinus
2. Tibiotalar valgus
3. Calcaneovalgus
4. Rigid forefoot abduction
5. Hypermobility of the 1st tarsometatarsal joint

## Correct option

**2. Tibiotalar valgus**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an L5 level myelomeningocele and an associated calcaneovalgus foot deformity. It is important to evaluate for tibiotalar valgus deformities before surgical correction, as these are associated with less favorable results (Answer 2).

Myelodysplasia (myelomeningocele and spina bifida cystica) is a group of congenital disorders caused by various chromosomal abnormalities that lead to the failure or closure of the fetal spinal cord. Patients present with various anatomical abnormalities involving the spine, hip, knee, or foot. This condition has been highly associated with foot or ankle deformities (60-90%) due to the high incidence of lower nerve root (L1-S1) involvement. With an L5 level myelomeningocele, the common foot deformity associated with this is calcaneovalgus. These deformities are diagnosed on physical exam with hindfoot valgus and forefoot abduction, and with plain radiographs of the foot and ankle. It is important to get standing AP ankle radiographs to evaluate for talar tilt or tibiotalar valgus. First-line treatment is conservative and consists of either serial casting or ankle-foot orthosis (AFO) wear. If this fails to correct fixed deformities, surgical correction, including a calcaneal slide osteotomy, calcaneal lengthening osteotomy, subtalar arthrodesis, and Grice extra-articular arthrodesis, is typically indicated.

Doğan et al. evaluated the results of calcaneal lengthening via a Modified Evans osteotomy technique in 11 patients (6 males and 5 females; 22 feet) with flexible pes planovalgus deformities. They included 4 idiopathic patients and those with cerebral palsy (n=5), myelomeningocele (n=4), and sensorimotor polyneuropathy (n=1), with a mean age of 10.8 years. Radiographically, 5 feet (22.7%), 13 feet (59.1%), and four feet (18.2%) were assessed as perfect, good, and fair, respectively. An average of 7.3 mm (range 4 to 9 mm) of calcaneal lengthening was obtained (p<0.05) with this technique. In conclusion, calcaneal lengthening demonstrates favorable results in patients with symptomatic pes planovalgus foot deformities.

*Noonan et al. reviewed foot deformities in pediatric patients with myelomeningocele.* **They note** that foot deformities are present in almost all patients paralyzed with myelomeningocele. It is important to perform a thorough physical exam to evaluate which muscles are functional and which are weakened or absent to help understand the associated foot deformity. **They conclude** that this fundamental understanding will help provide successful correction with orthotics or surgery and result in functional improvements.

*van Bosse et al. reviewed syndromic foot deformities in patients with myelomeningocele and arthrogryposis.* **They note** that treatment strategies should seek to avoid the development of a stiffened foot while also allowing the opportunity to regain correction when a relapse occurs. **They report** that studies have found a success rate between 61-80% with corrective osteotomies in the foot. The 20-39% failure rate is attributed to unrecognized ankle valgus. They explain that ankle valgus deformities occur in L5 or higher functional levels, where the weakened soleus muscle exerts less of a downward pull on the fibula and thus a gradual shortening of the fibula. As the valgus deformity develops, this causes a lateral shift of the mechanical axis, which increases articular stress on the lateral aspect of the talus, driving progressive subluxation and arthritic changes despite a correct foot osteotomy. **They conclude** that stiffness is important to avoid in both of these syndromic conditions, especially myelomeningocele, since a stiff foot increases the risk of skin ulcers, leading to osteomyelitis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Ankle equinus would be an uncommon deformity in patients with an L5 myelomeningocele, because the gastrocnemius/soleus (S1), tibialis posterior (L4-5), and peroneal muscles (L5-S1) are typically weak.
Answer 3: Calcaneovalgus deformities are commonly found in patients with an L5 myelomeningocele and would not lead to poor outcomes.
Answer 4: Rigid forefoot abduction can be seen in patients with an L5 myelomeningocele. This deformity can be surgically treated with calcaneal or lateral column lengthening and would not lead to poor outcomes.
Answer 5: Hypermobility of the 1st TMT joint is not uncommon. These can be treated with a fusion of the 1st TMT joint and would not lead to poor overall outcomes.
