## Question

## Options

1. Both Figures A and B should have neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to wide resection
2. Both Figures A and B should have primary wide resection without neoadjuvant chemotherapy
3. Figure A should have primary wide resection and Figure B should have an amputation
4. Figure A should have neoadjuvant radiation and Figure B should have neoadjuvant chemotherapy
5. Figure A should have primary wide resection and Figure B should have neoadjuvant chemotherapy then wide resection

## Correct option

**5. Figure A should have primary wide resection and Figure B should have neoadjuvant chemotherapy then wide resection**

## Answer explanation

While both of the patients in question have been diagnosed with osteosarcoma, the histologic subtypes and typical treatment regimens are different. Figure A is a patient with parosteal distal femur osteosarcoma, and Figure B is a patient with intramedullary osteosarcoma.

Parosteal osteosarcoma is a low-grade variant that is routinely treated via wide resection alone. This can be in the form of complete resection of the entire bone, or through navigation/custom cutting jig technology if a "regional wide resection" is possible. This can be done to preserve the native joint while resecting the tumor en-bloc and supplementing with structural allograft or autograft. Parosteal osteosarcoma has a "stuck-on" appearance and classically presents in the posterior distal femur. Intramedullary/conventional osteosarcoma is routinely high-grade and is typically treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, wide resection, and adjuvant chemotherapy. This higher-grade sarcoma is within the medullary canal itself and imaging shows a blastic bony lesion with aggressive features like periosteal reactions with a sunburst pattern, Codman's triangle, and bony erosion with soft tissue expansion.

*Messerschmitt et al provide an overview of osteosarcoma epidemiology, presentation, and treatment.* **They state** that approximately 20% of all osteosarcoma patients present with clinically detectable metastases, while an unknown percentage likely has micro-metastases not found in initial staging studies. For high-grade tumors, the gold-standard treatment is neoadjuvant chemotherapy, wide resection with or without reconstruction, and adjuvant chemotherapy. They challenge future physicians and scientists to improve survival and function through advances in chemotherapy and surgical techniques.

Dr. Benjamin shares a historical perspective on the development and use of chemotherapy in the treatment of osteosarcoma. He discusses the important successes and failures of those at Memorial Sloan Kettering, MD Anderson, and the Rizzoli Institute experienced to develop the current chemotherapy protocol that is responsible for improved 5-year and 10-year survival in patients with osteosarcoma. The current gold-standard chemotherapy regimen contains high-dose methotrexate, adriamycin, and cisplatin (also known as MAP therapy).

Figure A is the lateral radiograph of a knee showing parosteal osteosarcoma. Figure B is the lateral radiograph of a knee showing an aggressive sclerotic appearing lesion within the medullary canal with cortical erosion and soft tissue expansion, most consistent with intramedullary osteosarcoma.

INCORRECT ANSWERS:
Answer 1: Parosteal osteosarcoma is a low-grade neoplasm that has low metastatic and recurrent potential, and can be treated with primary wide resection without chemotherapy.
Answer 2: Intramedullary osteosarcoma is routinely high-grade and is treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, wide resection, and adjuvant chemotherapy.
Answer 3: Advances in surgical technique and endoprostheses have become the standard, while amputation is typically reserved for cases where preservation of limb function AND adequate surgical margins is not possible.
Answer 4: Radiation does not play a major role in the treatment of osteosarcoma.
