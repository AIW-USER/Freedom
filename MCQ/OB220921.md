## Question

A 9-month-old boy presents to your office with the deformity in bilateral feet shown in Figure A. On physical exam, the patient has resting equinovarus deformities present in both feet with notable forefoot adduction. The patient is not yet ambulatory. You counsel the family on the recommended treatment. In this patient, what is true regarding this proposed intervention?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Ponseti casting has a 50% success rate and will likely result in a poor outcome in this patient
2. The Ponseti technique involves abducting the foot and rotating it laterally around a fixed talus
3. Hindfoot varus needs to be corrected with the first cast to allow for correction of forefoot adduction with the second cast
4. Forefoot abduction and hindfoot equinovarus deformities are most commonly seen in clubfoot
5. Ponseti casting typically only requires 1 round of serial manipulation and the cast is removed in 4 weeks

## Correct option

**2. The Ponseti technique involves abducting the foot and rotating it laterally around a fixed talus**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with bilateral clubfeet. The gold standard treatment in this condition utilizes the Ponseti casting technique, which involves multiple rounds of serial manipulation and casting to correct the cavus, hindfoot varus, hindfoot equinus, and forefoot adduction deformities. Overall, this technique consists of abducting the foot and rotating it laterally around a fixed talus (Answer 2).

Congenital talipes equinovarus, also known as clubfoot, is a common idiopathic deformity of the foot. It is the most common musculoskeletal birth defect found more frequently in males (2:1), with an overall incidence of 1:1,000. A genetic component is strongly suggested with a 25% familial occurrence rate. Clubfoot consists of cavus, hindfoot equinovarus, and forefoot adduction deformities. Diagnosis is made clinically with a resting equinovarus deformity of the foot. Treatment most commonly begins with Ponseti casting, which involves multiple rounds of serial manipulation and casting +/- tendoachilles lengthening and tibialis anterior tendon transfer to correct residual deformities.

*Rieger and Dobbs performed a narrative review of congenital clubfoot deformities.* **They report** that clubfoot is a congenital idiopathic equinovarus deformity. The primary goal of treatment is to obtain a functional, pain-free, plantigrade foot that permits activities of daily living. They explain that the Ponseti method is the gold standard for treatment, involving serial manipulation and casting with an Achilles tenotomy to correct equinus. This is followed by maintenance foot abduction bracing to prevent relapse of deformity. **They conclude** that the Ponseti method remains the dominant treatment for clubfoot, and recurrence is a recognized challenge, with bracing compliance being an important factor.

*Cady et al. performed a clinical review on idiopathic congenital clubfoot deformities.* **They report** it is usually sufficient to clinically diagnose clubfoot in newborns. Distinguishing clubfoot deformities from positional plantar deformities such as metatarsus adductus and calcaneovalgus is critical because these lesser deformities often resolve spontaneously. **They conclude** that patient compliance is important to treatment success and, therefore, parental education and support are essential for treatment adherence and long-term success.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Ponseti casting is the gold standard treatment for clubfoot and has a >90% success rate.
Answer 3: The first cast typically corrects the cavus foot deformity, which can take more than 1-2 rounds. Hindfoot varus is corrected later after forefoot adduction.
Answer 4: Forefoot adduction (not abduction) is found in clubfoot.
Answer 5: Ponseti casting typically requires multiple rounds of weekly serial manipulation and casting; 1 round of manipulation and casting is not as effective.
