## Question

An 18-year-old female college student presents to your clinic with complaints of long-standing hip pain that has bothered her during her high school career as a soccer player. On examination, her pain is worst with internal rotation of the hip and axial loading. Radiographs are obtained and show no arthritic changes and no obvious cam or pincer deformity. You measure her center-edge angle (CEA) at 20° and note her acetabulum appears somewhat retroverted. An MRI was ordered by an outside surgeon and is also reviewed, demonstrating evidence of labral tearing. Which of the following is true regarding the management of this patient?

## Options

1. Continued bony remodeling will occur and symptomatic management should continue with activity modification and physical therapy
2. Surgical hip dislocation is required to completely address her labral pathology
3. Dega osteotomy is required to address acetabular retroversion
4. Secondary hip instability is a concern following periacetabular osteotomy
5. Long term outcomes and survivorship of Bernese periacetabular osteotomy are improved compared to arthroscopic management

## Correct option

**5. Long term outcomes and survivorship of Bernese periacetabular osteotomy are improved compared to arthroscopic management**

## Answer explanation

In a skeletally mature patient with evidence of hip dysplasia, periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) has demonstrated superior long-term outcomes at >10 years compared to arthroscopic management of labral pathology.

Adult hip dysplasia is caused by a shallow acetabulum and lack of bony coverage for the femoral head. It is thought to be an under diagnosed cause of eventual hip osteoarthritis and need for total hip arthroplasty. It can be quantified on radiographs by a lateral center edge angle (LCEA) <25° or an anterior center edge angle <25° on a false profile view. A crossing sign or positive posterior wall sign may indicate the presence of concomitant acetabular retroversion. Many of these patients will have concomitant labral pathology seen on MRI due to the excess forces placed through the labrum to stabilize the hip joint. Supportive care options are limited if patients remain persistently painful. Regarding operative management, arthroscopic hip labral repair/debridement is not recommended alone in dysplastic patients because of concern for secondary instability, progression of arthritis and lack of long-term function improvements in outcome scores. A periacetabular osteotomy (Bernese/Ganz) is a useful tool in improving hip alignment while maintaining stability. Long-term studies have shown durable results with >90% survivorship at 10-15 years with maintenance of function.

Zurmühle et al compared arthroscopic labral rim trimming and PAO in patients with dysplasia and acetabular retroversion. Sixty-seven hips underwent PAO while 57 underwent arthroscopic rim trimming with the 5-year survivorship being similar in the two groups, but the 10-year outcomes being improved in the PAO group (79% vs 23%). From these results, they concluded that the PAO may be especially indicated in this subset of patients with acetabular retroversion.

*Harris et al. reviewed the characteristics and treatment of hip dysplasia noting that there tends to be a spectrum starting with hip pain and instability, leading to eventual arthritis.* The location and degree of uncoverage is important to consider in treatment algorithms, with symptomatic dysplasia usually best treated by PAO, rather than arthroscopic management alone. **The authors note** that there is evidence supporting the ability to decrease the eventual risk for hip arthritis by performing a PAO in this population.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The triradiate cartilage begins closing around age 10 and the different apophyses of the pelvis subsequently close in years following. By this patients age (18), there is no expected growth remaining in the pelvis, and therefore, continued nonoperative management is not recommended if the patient is persistently symptomatic.
Answer 2: Surgical hip dislocation is not required to address labral pathology, as this can be done arthroscopically. However, arthroscopic management in a dysplastic hip is not recommended, as improvement lacks longevity.
Answer 3: A Dega osteotomy is a volume reducing osteotomy that is usually reserved for cases of neuromuscular developmental hip dysplasia. It relies on an osteotomy that hinges on the triradiate cartilage and does not violate the sciatic notch.
Answer 4: Secondary hip instability is a concern after arthroscopic labral debridement/trimming, but is not seen in the setting of a PAO.
