## Question

A 32-year-old woman presents to the orthopedic clinic complaining of heel pain, especially with running. She has had 3 months of reduced physical activity over the winter months and is now returning to running in the spring. She has tried physical therapy for months along with NSAIDs with minimal improvement. On examination, she is noted to have a painful plantar callus under her 1st metatarsal head. In knee extension, the ankle dorsiflexes to -5 degrees, whereas in knee flexion the ankle dorsiflexes to 10 degrees. What procedure most appropriately addresses her pathology?

## Options

1. Flexor hallucis longus (FHL)-to-Achilles transfer
2. Hoke triple-cut Achilles lengthening
3. Gasctrocnemius recession (Strayer procedure)
4. First metatarsal extension osteotomy
5. Posterior ankle capsule release

## Correct option

**3. Gasctrocnemius recession (Strayer procedure)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a plantar callus due to forefoot overloading with a positive Silfverskiold test indicative of gastrocnemius contracture. Isolated gastrocnemius contracture can be treated with a Strayer procedure, or gastrocnemius recession.

The Silfverskiold test differentiates between gastrocnemius and Achilles contracture. With the knee extended, ankle dorsiflexion is assessed. The knee is then flexed and if dorsiflexion improves from shy of neutral to past neutral with knee flexion, this suggests a gastrocnemius contracture, as this muscle crosses the knee joint. If there is no change in ankle dorsiflexion with knee flexion, there is an equinus contracture and an Achilles lengthening is the appropriate surgical procedure of choice. The Strayer procedure involves a fascial release of the gastrocnemius muscle belly for recession and gentle lengthening. This is indicated once nonoperative modalities have failed and the patient remains symptomatic.

*Barske et al. review current concepts on equinus contracture and gastrocnemius recession.* *The authors discuss the contribution of equinus contracture to various foot pathologies as well as the clinical presentation and management.* **They support** the efficacy of the Strayer (primarily gastrocnemius) as well as Vulpius lengthening (gastrocnemius and soleus fascial release) procedures in addressing this pathology.

*Hsu et al. review surgical recession techniques for gastrocnemius contracture.* *The authors discuss the surgical considerations of proximal, middle, and distally-based releases.* In addition to the Strayer and Vulpius procedures (distal), they also review the Baumann procedure (middle), which utilizes a medial approach to release of the anterior aponeurosis of the gastrocnemius muscle belly.

*Chen et al. discussed Achilles lengthening procedures and the associated pathologies they are used to address.* *They review the anatomic basis for the various procedures and compare the indications, technical pearls, and pitfalls specific to gastrocnemius recession and tendo-Achilles lengthening.*

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: FHL-to-Achilles transfer may be used to augment an Achilles tendon repair in the setting of extensive tendon degeneration requiring debridement or an incompetent tendon.
Answer 2: The Hoke triple-cut Achilles lengthening is utilized to address Achilles tendon contracture. This patient has an isolated gastrocnemius contracture as evidenced by the positive Silverskiold test.
Answer 4: A 1st metatarsal extension osteotomy may be undertaken to address a cavus foot deformity that is accompanied by a flexible hindfoot. This would not address a gastrocnemius contracture in this patient and would therefore not off-load the forefoot.
Answer 5: The patient is able to adequately dorsiflex the ankle with the knee flexion, suggesting that the gastrocnemius is the cause of the contracture and not the posterior capsule.
