## Question

A 13-year-old male has had a several year history of right ankle pain and frequent sprains. Over the past year it became progressively worse until he could not participate fully in athletics and other activities due to pain. He recently wore a weight-bearing cast for 4 weeks and rested from all activities followed by formal physical therapy and use of a custom orthosis. His ankle felt initially better, but within a week of returning to his activities the pain returned. On physical exam he has a rigid flat foot, and no instability about the ankle. Figure A is a lateral radiograph of his ankle. What is the next best step in management?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Non-weight bearing cast for 4 weeks followed by physical therapy
2. Brostrom ligamentous reconstruction
3. Resection of calcaneonavicular coalition with fat or muscle interposition
4. Resection of talocalcaneal coalition with fat or muscle interporition
5. Excision of accessory navicular

## Correct option

**3. Resection of calcaneonavicular coalition with fat or muscle interposition**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a calcaneonavicular (CN) coalition that remains symptomatic despite a trial of non-operative measures. Surgery is indicated for resection of the coalition with fat or muscle interposition.

Tarsal coalitions are a common cause of rigid flat foot that typically become symptomatic between the ages of 9-13 years. Conservative measures should always be first line treatment including activity modifications, orthotics (ex - UCBL), and a trial of weight-bearing short leg casting. If symptoms and limitations persist despite these measures then surgery should be considered. There are a variety of described procedures to treat tarsal coalitions, but for young patients without evidence of subtalar arthritis surgery commonly entails a resection of the coalition and either muscle or fat interposition.

Vincent provides a review of tarsal coalitions. He notes that conservative measures should be attempted and have a good rate of success and should include 4 weeks of weight-bearing casting followed by use of a UCBL. If these measures fail then surgery is indicated.

Mosca reviews evaluation and treatment of painful tarsal coalitions. He also notes that conservative measures often provide relief and should be attempted first. If these fail surgery is considered. He recommends imaging with CT or MRI to fully evaluate the extent of the coalition prior to surgery.

Figure A is an oblique radiograph of a foot showing a CN coalition.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Conservative measures have been attempted appropriately and failed to provide relief. Trial of non-weight-bearing cast is not needed.
Answer 2: This patient does not have instability secondary to ligamentous disruption, and reconstruction is not indicated.
Answer 4: The patient does not have a talocalcaneal coalition.
Answer 5: This patient does not have a painful accessory navicular.
