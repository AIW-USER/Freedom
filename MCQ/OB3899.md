## Question

A 23-year-old recreational soccer player presents with complaints of dorsal-medial foot pain. He has sustained 2 ankle sprains within the last 3 months. On physical examination, he has tenderness to palpation over the medial aspect of his foot. Imaging of his ankle is shown in Figure A. What is the next best step in management?

![[q3899-a-c-sign.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. CT scan
2. MRI
3. Radiographs of the contralateral foot
4. Immobilization
5. Sinus tarsi injection

## Correct option

**4. Immobilization**

## Answer explanation

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the ankle demonstrating the "C-sign" which signifies a talocalcaneal coalition. Immobilization is the next best step in management.

Tarsal coalitions represent an abnormal fusion between two adjacent tarsal bones. This is most likely due to a failure of segmentation of the primitive mesenchyme. Approximately 90% of coalitions affect either the subtalar joint or the space between the calcaneus and the navicular. In a talocalcaneal coalition, a coalition exists between the medial facet and the talus which may manifest as medial foot pain and tenderness. Other symptoms typically include hindfoot or midfoot pain, recurrent ankle sprains, difficulty ambulating on uneven surfaces, or decreased subtalar motion. Initial treatment consists of a brief period of immobilization. If non-operative treatments fail, surgical treatment is considered and consists of coalition resection and interposition of soft tissue.

*Scott et al. performed a study in 7 adult patients with calcaneonavicular coalitions treated with coalition resection and extensor digitorum brevis interposition.* *The average age was 41 years and none of the patients had evidence of degenerative arthritis.* *At follow-up, American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot scores were recorded and charts were reviewed.* **They found** at a mean follow-up of 56.5 months, the average AOFAS score was 87 and review of radiographs revealed no degenerative changes or coalition recurrence. **They conclude** that this procedure is an excellent alternative to arthrodesis in patients over 18-years of age.

*Varner et al. reviewed 32 feet in 27 adults with the diagnosis of tarsal coalitions.* There were 18 subtalar coalitions, 14 calcaneonavicular coalitions, and 1 naviculocuneiform coalition. Clinically, 22 feet had a neutral heel and 7 had a valgus heel with flattening of the longitudinal arch. Subtalar motion was decreased in 23 feet and 11 feet were asymptomatic. Nonoperative treatment consisting of activity modification, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications and casting were successful in the majority of patients. **They conclude** that the treatment of a symptomatic tarsal coalition in adults is similar to children.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: CT and/or MRI may be obtained if the diagnosis is uncertain or for pre-operative planning.
Answer 3: Radiographs of the contralateral foot are not indicated at this time as the diagnosis can be made with the radiographs provided.
Answer 5: A sinus tarsi injection is not indicated in the treatment of a talocalcaneal coalition.
