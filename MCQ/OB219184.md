## Answer explanation

Fracture reduction followed by independent lag-by-technique screw fixation is one technique that provides compression to ensure primary bone healing. The utilization of an adjunctive laterally placed neutralization plate provides additional protection against both shearing and rotational forces which could loosen the lag screw and displace the fracture (Answer 5).

In the mid-20th century, a group of Swiss surgeons pioneered fracture fixation principles with the introduction of the AO foundation. The four residing principles of fracture fixation include:

1. Fracture reduction is performed to restore anatomic relationships
2. The fixation must provide absolute or relative stability, as indicated, to ensure healing
3. There must be preservation of the blood supply to soft tissues and bone
4. Collectively, these should allow early and safe mobilization of the injured part and the patient as a whole

It is imperative that surgeons have a working understanding of these principles and the various fixation strategies to ensure appropriate osseous healing. Simple fracture patterns that allow direct cortical apposition can be treated with fixation that compresses at the fracture site for primary (intramembranous) healing via absolute stability. Conversely, comminuted fracture patterns that are not amenable to direct cortical apposition can be treated with techniques/implants that promote secondary (endochondral) healing via relative stability. In any case, the ultimate goal is to re-establish the appropriate length, rotation, and alignment of the fractured bone.

Gautier and Sommer provided a comprehensive review of fracture fixation principles in response to the introduction of novel locked plating systems in the early 2000s. They discuss that locking compression plate (LCP) systems allow for the use of either standard cortical or novel locking screws and can be utilized for compression plating, bridge plating, or a hybrid construct. They further describe the biomechanical advantage of using bicortical locking screws for fixation in osteoporotic bone. The authors conclude by providing illustrative examples of different fracture fixation concepts and their recommended appropriate use guidelines.

*Beltran and colleagues provided a review article on stress modulation of fracture fixation implants.* *The authors define stress modulation as harnessing surgeon-controlled fixation variables to create an environment that promotes reliable and timely bone healing.* **They discuss** that these variables include implant metallurgy and geometry, application of biomechanical principles, and achieving a balance between flexibility and stiffness. **The authors conclude** that when performed appropriately, the modulation of plate stiffness can provide the best chance of union and avoiding fracture complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient was treated with primary compression via independent lag screw fixation. However, their adjunctive fixation includes an anti-glide plate placed in the posterolateral position without distal fixation. This adjunctive fixation only resists posterior and proximal displacing shearing forces on the distal fragment (i.e. buttressing effect) and would not impart significant protection against rotational displacement.
Answer 2: This distal fibula fracture was fixated utilizing a lag-by-design intramedullary screw. While this can provide compression at the fracture site for primary bone healing, there is no adjunctive fixation to resist shearing and rotational forces.
Answer 3: This patient sustained a comminuted fracture with bridge plating that broadly spans past the fracture site both proximally and distally. This long plating with screws placed away from the fracture sites ensures a long working length to encourage indirect, secondary bone healing. In critiquing evaluating this specific case, a longer plate could have been utilized to further increase the working length for optimal indirect bone healing.
Answer 4: This patient was treated with an intramedullary nail with distal interlocking screws, which collectively imparts relative stability at the fracture site for secondary bone healing. This does not provide compression for primary bone healing as described in the question vignette.
