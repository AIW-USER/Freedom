## Question

A 68-year-old college professor is about to undergo a total knee arthroplasty. Figure A demonstrates the procedure for making the distal femoral cut during the surgery. Assuming an anatomic lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) of 81°, a medial proximal tibia angle (MPTA) of 87°, and an anatomic femoral axis that differs from the mechanical axis by 6 ° of valgus, what would you plan to set the cutting guide to on your intramedullary sword if you wish to make your cut parallel to the native joint line?

![[q218136-a-screenshot-2023-07-30-at-9-12-32-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. 0° of valgus
2. 3 ° of valgus
3. 6 ° of valgus
4. 9 ° of valgus
5. 3° of varus

## Correct option

**4. 9 ° of valgus**

## Answer explanation

Given an LDFA of 81°, the distal femur is in 9° of valgus at the joint line; thus, if one aims to cut the distal femur so that it is parallel to the native joint line, a 9 ° valgus cut would need to be made (Answer 4). Though this is not done when aiming for neutral mechanical alignment (the guide would be set to 6° valgus in this case), followers of kinematic alignment principles do make their distal femur cuts more valgus to more closely match the native joint line.

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has historically been performed using neutral mechanical alignment as the target alignment to promote implant survivorship. On average, the distal femoral joint line sits at 9° of valgus in relation to the midline, whereas the tibial joint line sits at 3° of varus relative to the midline (Illustration A). The goal of neutral mechanical alignment in TKA is to create a joint line that is perpendicular to the mechanical axis of the limb, theoretically resulting in an even distribution of loads across the medial and lateral surfaces of the components. Given that the anatomic axis of the femur varies from the mechanical axis by 4-6° of valgus, a 4-6 ° cut of the distal femur must be made to make the cut perpendicular to the native femoral mechanical axis. However, this places the femoral component perpendicular to the mechanical axis, not in parallel to the native joint line. Those who favor a kinematic approach to alignment, arguing that an individual’s native knee alignment and joint line do not always fall along the neutral mechanical axis of the tibia and femur, aim to place the femoral component in 2-4° more valgus to more appropriately match the native joint line. Similar principles are applied to placing the tibia into more varus. Though a complete discussion of kinematic alignment is beyond the scope of this question, it is important to understand the degree to which the mechanical and anatomic axes of the femur differ and how they relate to the native distal femoral joint line so that the different ways of performing total knee arthroplasty can be understood.

*Jaffe et al. provide a review article on alignment and balance methods in total knee arthroplasty.* In terms of mechanical alignment, **the authors note** that the goal is to create a joint line that is perpendicular to the mechanical axis of the limb, whereas the concept of kinematic alignment of the knee is that mechanical alignment in TKA creates an altered anatomy and alignment compared to the native knee, potentially leading to patient dissatisfaction, although no concrete data are available to support this concept.

*Bellemans et al. in their Ranawat-award-winning article discuss the concept of constitutional varus and whether or not neutral mechanical alignment is normal for all patients.* In a study of 250 asymptomatic, young volunteers, full-leg standing radiographs were studied, with the ultimate finding that 32% of men and 17% of women had constitutional varus knees with a natural mechanical alignment of 3° varus or more. **The authors concluded** that an important fraction of the normal population has a natural alignment at the end of growth of 3° varus or more, and that restoration of mechanical alignment to neutral in these cases may not be desirable and would be unnatural for them.

Illustration A demonstrates the typical anatomic and mechanical axes of the femur and tibia, with an LDFA of 81° (indicating 9° of distal femur valgus) and an MPTA of 87° (indicating 3° of proximal tibia varus).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3 and 5: If trying to parallel the native joint line with the given LDFA and MPTA measurements, a 9° valgus cut would need to be made. If following the principles of neutral mechanical alignment, a 6° valgus cut would be made so as to make the distal femur perpendicular to the mechanical axis of the femur.
