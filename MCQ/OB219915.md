## Question

A 35-year-old male with a past medical history of leukemia treated with high-dose steroids presents with shoulder pain for the last year. He denies any specific trauma or injury to the shoulder. He has tried six weeks of physical therapy and corticosteroid injections which have not improved his symptoms. An MRI of his shoulder demonstrates humeral head flattening and collapse without significant cartilage damage to the glenoid. There is no rotator cuff pathology seen. What procedure is most indicated for this patient's condition?

## Options

1. Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty
2. Core decompression
3. Hemiarthroplasty
4. "Ream and run" procedure
5. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

## Correct option

**3. Hemiarthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

The most appropriate procedure for this patient with humeral head collapse and intact glenoid cartilage would be a hemiarthroplasty (Answer 3).

There are several procedures used to address humeral head avascular necrosis (AVN) depending on the disease severity and age of the patient. Patients with mild disease where there is sclerosis, but no subchondral fracture, can be treated with core decompression. In young patients, hemiarthroplasty is the treatment of choice when the disease progresses to subchondral fracture, flattening, or collapse of the humeral head with an intact glenoid. This preserves glenoid bone stock for the total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) that will likely be performed in the future. A total shoulder arthroplasty may be necessary if the glenoid also demonstrates degenerative changes. Older patients with rotator cuff pathology may require a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA).

*McLaughlin and colleagues compared RTSA and TSA in treating humeral head AVN.* **They found** that both surgical approaches offer similar outcomes regarding shoulder function, pain relief, and complication rates. The choice between RSA and TSA should consider patient-specific factors, such as age, rotator cuff integrity, and overall bone quality. Ultimately, RSA can be as effective as TSA for managing AVN in the humeral head, particularly in patients with compromised rotator cuff function.

*Burrus and colleagues examined the postoperative complications associated with shoulder arthroplasty for humeral head AVN.* **The authors found** patients with AVN undergoing shoulder arthroplasty face a higher risk of complications compared to other shoulder conditions. The increased risk is attributed to factors like poor bone quality, medical comorbidities, and the underlying pathology of AVN. Despite the higher complication rates, shoulder arthroplasty remains a viable treatment option, with careful preoperative planning needed to optimize outcomes in these patients.

*Kennon and colleagues evaluated outcomes of core decompression and arthroplasty in treating atraumatic osteonecrosis of the humeral head.* Core decompression, a joint-preserving technique, can be effective in early-stage disease, reducing pain and delaying the need for arthroplasty. However, in advanced cases, arthroplasty, including total or reverse shoulder replacement, is more successful in restoring function and relieving pain. The study highlights the importance of the disease stage in determining the appropriate treatment strategy and improving long-term outcomes.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty would not be the best initial procedure for this patient due to his young age and preserved glenoid cartilage.
Answer 2: Core decompression is a viable treatment option for mild disease without subchondral collapse. Once there is humeral head flattening it is not beneficial.
Answer 4: The "ream and run" procedure involves a hemiarthroplasty and then reaming the glenoid. This is not necessary due to the patient's intact glenoid cartilage
Answer 5: Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty would not be indicated due to this patient's age and his intact rotator cuff.
