## Question

Case: A 48-year-old male amateur marathon runner presents with a 2-year history of progressive medial-sided left knee pain. He has failed 6 months of structured physical therapy and corticosteroid injections. On examination, he has a noticeable varus thrust during the stance phase of gait. Range of motion is 5° to 120°. Standing long-alignment radiographs reveal a mechanical axis passing through the medial tibial spine (Varus deformity of 8°). MRI shows isolated Grade IV chondral loss in the medial compartment, a functional ACL, and a horizontal cleavage tear of the lateral meniscus body without displacement or chondral loss in the lateral compartment.

Question:
Which of the following is the most appropriate surgical recommendation for this patient?

## Options

1. Medial Fixed-Bearing Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty (UKA)
2. Medial Opening-Wedge High Tibial Osteotomy (HTO)
3. Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA)
4. Medial Mobile-Bearing UKA combined with Lateral Meniscal Repair

## Correct option

**2. Medial Opening-Wedge High Tibial Osteotomy (HTO)**

## Answer explanation

Explanation and Rationale: The clinical decision-making here hinges on the patient's age, activity level, and the specific "red flags" in the imaging.

 - Patient Profile: At 48 years old with a high-impact lifestyle (marathon running), a biological joint-preserving procedure like HTO is preferred over UKA. HTO allows for a return to high-impact sports, whereas running is generally discouraged following UKA due to risks of accelerated polyethylene wear and aseptic loosening.

 - Varus Thrust: The presence of a dynamic varus thrust indicates that realigning the mechanical axis (HTO) will provide better functional stability than simply resurfacing the joint (UKA).

 - Lateral Meniscus: The MRI finding of a lateral meniscal tear is a relative contraindication for UKA, as it suggests the "buffer" for the lateral compartment is compromised. Conversely, for an HTO, as long as there is no significant lateral chondral loss, the lateral compartment can typically tolerate the increased load of a valgus-producing osteotomy.

 - ACL Status: While an intact ACL is required for UKA, it also makes the patient an excellent candidate for HTO.

Understanding the HTO versus UKA for medial compartment OA

1. The Dynamic Varus Thrust

A varus thrust is a sudden lateral outward movement of the knee during the early stance phase of gait.

 - Why it is a Contraindication for UKA: UKA is a resurfacing procedure; it replaces the "tread" on the tire but does not fix the "alignment" of the car. In a knee with a dynamic thrust, there is often significant ligamentous laxity or profound bony deformity. Placing a UKA in this environment subjects the small prosthesis to massive eccentric loading and shear forces during every step. This leads to early aseptic loosening, tibial component subsidence, or rapid polyethylene wear.

 - Why it Favors HTO: HTO is a realignment procedure. Its primary goal is to shift the mechanical axis. By correcting the bony varus, the HTO eliminates the "lever arm" that causes the thrust. It stabilizes the knee mechanically, protecting the joint from the repetitive dynamic shocks of the gait cycle.

2. The Lateral Meniscal Tear

The integrity of the lateral compartment is the single most important prerequisite for any medial compartment procedure, but the tolerance levels differ between UKA and HTO.

 - Why it is a Contraindication for UKA: Modern UKA criteria (such as the Kozinn and Scott criteria) strictly require a functional lateral compartment. A lateral meniscal tear—especially a root tear or a complex body tear—compromises the shock-absorption and load-distribution of the lateral side. If you perform a medial UKA, the lateral compartment must now bear a more "normal" load. If the lateral meniscus is torn, the lateral cartilage will rapidly degenerate, leading to "point loading" and global knee failure (progression to TKA).

 - Why it Favors HTO (with caution):
 HTO is often performed in younger, high-demand patients where we are willing to accept some lateral pathology to avoid arthroplasty. While a significant
 lateral meniscal loss is a contraindication for HTO (as the lateral side will be overloaded by the valgus correction), a simple, stable tear is often better tolerated in an HTO than in a UKA. However, if the lateral meniscus is non-functional, neither procedure is ideal, and TKA may be the only durable option.
