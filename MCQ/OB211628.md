## Answer explanation

A femoral neck fracture nonunion in a young patient would be best treated with a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy to enable union and preserve native femoral anatomy.

Femoral neck fractures in young patients are often due to high energy mechanisms. Given the intracapsular location of the fracture, these injuries are prone to nonunion possibly due to the synovial fluid environment that hinders normally healing response. As such, these injuries need to be anatomically reduced to mitigate nonunion. In cases of nonunion, treatment with a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy has been reported to have a high subsequent union rate. Mechanically, this functions to redirect the fracture plane more horizontally and convert shear forces into compressive forces with weight-bearing. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head is another devastating complication of femoral neck fractures and is often treated with arthroplasty when there is significant femoral head collapse.

*Said et al. retrospectively reported 36 cases of femoral neck nonunions treated with valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy.* *The authors used a 130° angled blade-plate which resulted in fracture union in 97% of patients at an average of 4 months time-to-union.* **The authors recommended** performing a valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy for relatively young patients with femoral neck nonunions.

*Deakin et al. reviewed the management of femoral neck nonunions with valgus osteotomy.* The published literature reports union rates between 80-90% with valgus osteotomy with avascular necrosis occurring in up to 20% of cases. **They recommended** careful patient selection for valgus osteotomies as some patients may be best treated with arthroplasty.

*Singh et al. retrospectively studied reconstruction nail (RN) and sliding hip screw (SHS) with compression plate fixation for ipsilateral femoral neck and shaft fractures in 27 patients.* **The authors reported** the average time to fracture union for the femoral neck fracture was 15.2 weeks and 17.1 weeks in the SHS and RN, respectively, with no difference in femoral shaft time to union clinical results between the two groups. **They concluded** that both fixation strategies are effective and the use of the SHS with compression plating may be technically easier, but femoral neck fixation should be performed before shaft fixation.

Figure B is an AP pelvis radiograph with a nonunion of a right femoral neck fracture that underwent ORIF. Figure C is an AP pelvis radiograph with a cross-over sign consistent with pelvic retroversion. Figure D is an AP radiograph of the pelvis with dysplasia of the right hip. Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Figure A depicts post-traumatic avascular necrosis of the femoral head with significant collapse, which would be ideally addressed with arthroplasty.
Answer 3: Figure C depicts an AP pelvis radiograph with a cross-over sign suggesting pelvic retroversion. In the presence of impingement symptoms, a periacetabular osteotomy would be an ideal surgical treatment option.
Answer 4: Figure D is an AP pelvis radiograph with significant hip dysplasia and proximal migration of the femoral head. This would require a subtrochanteric osteotomy and total hip arthroplasty.
Figure 5: Figure E is an AP pelvis radiograph with metastatic lytic lesions of the femoral head, neck, and intertrochanteric region. A cemented arthroplasty would be the most appropriate treatment for this.
