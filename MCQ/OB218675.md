## Question

A 62-year-old male presents to the emergency department with an ulceration along his right foot, as shown in Figure A. He has a longstanding history of type II diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia but states he hasn’t seen a doctor in years. His labs are as follows:

WBC: 12,000 cells per mL (normal: 4,000-11,000 cells per mL)
ESR: 72 mm/hr (normal: 0-20 mm/hr)
CRP is 5.6 mg/dL (normal: 0.8-1.0mg/dL)
Glucose: 315 mg/dL (normal: 70-100 mg/dL)
Hb A1C: 9.2% (normal: 4-5.6%)

Which of the following is associated with successful healing of his current ulceration and a decrease in the risk for recurrent ulcerations?

![[q218675-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Maintaining his current serum Hb A1C levels
2. Transcutaneous oxygen pressure of 24 mmHg
3. Transition to narrow forefoot box shoes
4. Performing an Achilles lengthening procedure
5. Performing a percutaneous flexor tenotomy

## Correct option

**4. Performing an Achilles lengthening procedure**

## Answer explanation

Unrecognized equinus contractures can predispose diabetic patients towards forming foot ulcerations overlying the plantar surface of the 1st/2nd metatarsal heads. Treating the equinus contracture (i.e. Achilles tendon lengthening) thereby offloads the area of ulceration, promotes healing of the ulceration, and prevents recurrences.

Diabetic foot ulcerations are attributed to multifactorial causes including peripheral vascular disease, peripheral neuropathy, poor-fitting shoe wear, and localized infection. The three pillars of treatment are to (1) ensure there is sufficient blood supply to the area, (2) treat any underlying infection, and (3) achieve appropriate pressure relief from the ulceration site. Nonoperative treatments aimed at pressure relief include shoe modifications, total contact casting, or utilizing a crow boot. While these modalities help treat active ulcerations, they do not prevent recurrences. This patient’s ulceration is located at the metatarsal heads, suggestive of an underlying equinus contracture. Treating the equinus contracture (i.e. performing a lengthening procedure) to offload pressure at the ulceration site has been shown to significantly decrease the risk of recurrent ulcerations in this area. A Silfverskiöld test would help determine the most appropriate lengthening procedure. Isolated gastrocnemius contractures may be treated with a gastrocnemius recession (i.e. Strayer) procedure whereas Achilles tendon contracture requires formal Achilles tendon lengthening.

*Lewis and Lipp performed a systematic review of the available randomized control trials that evaluated the effects of pressure-relieving interventions in the healing of diabetic foot ulcerations.* *After pooling the results of 14 trials,* **the authors reported** that non-removable casts were associated with significantly improved healing rates compared to removable casts or simple dressings alone. **They also found** that Achilles tendon lengthening further improved healing rates in forefoot plantar ulcerations. **The authors concluded** that non-removable, pressure-relieving casts are most effective at treating plantar foot ulcerations and when combined with Achilles tendon lengthening, were even more successful in forefoot-specific ulcerations.

*Dallimore and Kaminski performed a systematic review investigating the effectiveness of Achilles tendon lengthening, gastrocnemius recession, and selective plantar fascia release in healing and preventing diabetic foot ulcers.* **The authors found** that while these lengthening procedures did not significantly improve the healing rate or time to healing of active ulcerations when compared to total contact casting alone, they did significantly reduce the risk for recurrence. **The authors concluded** that these lengthening procedures are viable treatment options for the management and prevention of diabetic foot ulcerations.

Figure A demonstrates a diabetic foot ulceration along the plantar surface overlying the medial metatarsal heads.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Hb A1C levels above 8% are associated with poor healing potential.
Answer 2: Transcutaneous oxygen pressures below 30-40 mmHg are associated with poor healing potential.
Answer 3: Wide forefoot box shoes and pressure-dispersing inserts or offloading rocker sole shoes are associated with improved healing and lower recurrence rates.
Answer 5: Percutaneous flexor tenotomies have been shown to improve healing rates in diabetic apical TOE ulcerations via passively correcting toe deformities and thereby offloading the ulceration site.
