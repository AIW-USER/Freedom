## Question

A 17-year-old female basketball player suffers a non-contact knee injury during practice. On exam, she has a moderate effusion, grade 2B Lachman, positive lateral McMurray's test, mild laxity with a firm endpoint with a valgus stress test, and genu recurvatum of 5-10 deg with 130 deg of flexion and normal limb alignment. She undergoes ACL reconstruction using a 9mm quadriceps tendon autograft and meniscus repair. She returns to play 10 months postoperatively. One year after surgery she reinjured her ACL during a game. Figure A shows her current X-rays and her calculated tibial slope is 8 degrees. Which of the following factors put this patient at risk for graft failure?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Graft size
2. Associated injuries
3. Preoperative exam findings
4. Tibial slope
5. Malposition of tibial tunnel

## Correct option

**3. Preoperative exam findings**

## Answer explanation

This patient retore her ACL most likely due to her preoperative hyperextension, a known risk factor for ACL graft failure.

While ACL reconstruction has consistently demonstrated good outcomes, graft failure is still reported to be about 5-15%. There are several risk factors for graft failure with the most common being trauma. Technical errors are the second leading cause with anterior femoral tunnel placement being the most frequent error. There are several other factors including graft size (<8mm), allograft use particularly in adolescents, missed concomitant injuries (ie PLC or meniscus tear), and limb malalignment (varus knee or posterior tibial slope >12 degrees). Preoperative risk factors such as younger age, contact sports, and knee hyperextension are also critical to identify. Doing so allows surgeons to risk stratify patients and counsel them appropriately, as well as potentially altering the operative plan (i.e. add lateral extra-articular tenodesis) or the return to sports protocol.

The MARS Group studied the prevalence of knee hyperextension (HE) in revision ACLR and its effect on 2-year outcomes. They analyzed 1145 patients and passive knee HE ≥5° was present in 33% of patients. They identified three variables that were predictors of graft failure including younger age, use of allograft, and HE ≥5°. HE ≥5° carried a >2-fold risk of subsequent graft rupture in revision ACL.

*Magnussen et al analyzed patients from the MOON study to understand the effects of residual laxity on PROs after primary ACLR.* *They included 381 patients with six-year follow-up in their analysis.* *They assessed the residual side-to-side laxity using a KT-1000 arthrometer (<-1 mm, -1 to 2 mm, 2 to 6 mm, and >6 mm) at 2 years postoperatively.* **They found** that increased residual laxity (>6mm) at 2 years was predictive of decreased PROs from 2 to 6 years postoperatively.

*Guimaraes et al performed a matched cohort analysis of 204 patients who had primary ACL reconstruction with hamstring tendon autograft.* *They divided and separated them into two groups based on the presence of greater than or less than 5 degrees of passive hyperextension.* The hyperextension group had a higher risk of graft failure (14.7% vs 2.9%) and worse Lysholm knee scale scores.

Incorrect Answers
Answer 1: Graft size <8mm is a risk factor for graft failure. This patient's graft size was appropriate.
Answer 2: Missed concomitant injuries, such as PLC or meniscus tears, place abnormal stress on the ACL graft and can lead to failure. However, this patient had a meniscus repair that was appropriately addressed at the index procedure.
Answer 4: Posterior tibial slope > 12 degrees is associated with graft failure, however, this patient appears to have normal tibial slope.
Answer 5: Incorrect tunnel placement is the most common technical error. The tibial tunnel placement appears appropriate based on the postoperative images.
