## Options

1. Removal of implants, debridement, and placement of antibiotic cement spacer
2. Compression plating with autologous bone grafting
3. Application of multiplanar external fixation
4. Use of a functional brace
5. Retention of current implants with observation

## Correct option

**2. Compression plating with autologous bone grafting**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a humeral shaft nonunion after open reduction internal fixation (ORIF). Of the revision options listed, compression plating with autologous bone grafting is most likely to lead to union (Answer 2).

Humeral shaft fractures comprise 3-5% of all fractures, affecting a bimodal distribution of age groups, with 60% occurring in patients older than 50 years. Risk factors for humeral shaft fractures include prior trauma, smoking in men, elderly age, and osteoporosis. The incidence of primary nonunion is 2-33% and 5-10% in nonoperative versus operative management, respectively. There are several known biologic and mechanical risk factors for nonunion, including endocrinopathies (osteoporosis, Vitamin D deficiency), infection, open fracture, tobacco use, malnutrition, obesity, and shoulder/elbow stiffness. Treatment of nonunion is directed at addressing the underlying etiology. For mechanical instability, compression plating +/- bone grafting or intramedullary nailing with bone grafting represent viable treatment options with union rates greater than 85%.

*Naclerio et al. performed a topical review on humeral shaft nonunion, discussing key principles of diagnosis and treatment.* **The authors argue** that many humeral shaft fractures can be treated non-operatively. In cases of nonunion after surgical treatment, infection must be investigated, and occult infection may exist even with a negative workup. Improved fixation should be utilized in the revision setting, including the use of longer plates. Finally, intramedullary exchange nailing is not an effective option for nonunion in isolation, and augmentation with plate fixation or autologous bone grafting should be used.

Bégué et al. completed a systematic review on humeral shaft nonunion, assessing risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment strategies. The authors state that glenohumeral arthritis, malnutrition, NSAID use, smoking, obesity, and alcohol consumption are known risk factors for nonunion. Treatment depends on the type of nonunion present, including atrophic, hypertrophic, and nonunions with a bony defect present. In hypertrophic nonunions, additional stability at the fracture site is obtained with compression plating with bone grafting. Atrophic nonunions require infection workup with 3-5 cultures, resection of necrotic bone, and plating with possible shortening. Nonunions with bone loss may be treated with the induced membrane technique, fibular autograft and allograft, or cancellous allograft with fixation.

*Peters et al performed a systematic review with 36 studies evaluating surgical treatments for humeral shaft nonunion, comparing union rates and complication profiles.* Plate fixation with autologous bone grafting (ABG) had the highest union rate at 98% (n=672), followed by 95% (n=19) with plate fixation without ABG, 92% (n=91) with bone strut fixation, 88% (n=164) with intramedullary nailing (IMN) plus ABG, 66% (n=78) with IMN without ABG, and 98% (n=152) with external fixation. Complication rates were 12% for plate fixation with ABG, 15% for IMN with ABG, 8% for IMN without ABG, 20% for bone strut fixation, and 22% for external fixation. Based on these findings, plate fixation with ABG remains the most reliable strategy, balancing high union rates with a relatively low complication profile.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: In the setting of normal inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP), the patient is unlikely to have a septic nonunion. Resection of infected bone at the fracture site with placement of an antibiotic spacer is a viable option for septic nonunion with bone loss.
Answer 3: External fixation may be used in the setting of polytrauma or extensive soft tissue damage after initial injury. However, in the absence of these factors, revision ORIF with compression plating is preferred after nonunion with failure of fixation.
Answer 4: Functional bracing may be used as an initial management strategy in patients opting for closed treatment, but has demonstrated higher nonunion rates for index treatment and is reserved for high-risk, low-demand patients unable to tolerate operative intervention.
Answer 5: Retention of current hardware is unlikely to be successful, given hardware failure with screw pullout and no evidence of progression to union on current CT imaging.
