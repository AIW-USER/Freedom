## Question

A 14-year-old boy comes to the clinic with 5 months of back pain. Figure A shows a standing radiograph that was obtained by his pediatrician. Figure B shows a subsequent MRI that was obtained. His neurologic examination is normal. CT guided needle biopsy shows a mycobacteria species and the patient is started on appropriate pharmacologic therapy. Once the child has reached the healed phase, which of the following is the most predictive of kyphosis progression?

## Options

1. Lesions isolated to the thoracic spine
2. Age on presentation
3. The number of levels involved
4. Timing to initiation of full-time TLSO bracing
5. Anterior and posterior element involvement

## Correct option

**5. Anterior and posterior element involvement**

## Answer explanation

The child has a diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis. Involvement of the anterior and posterior elements is the variable that is most predictive of kyphosis progression once the patient has reached the healed phase.

Pediatric spinal TB is an often indolent and slow-growing disease and without proper treatment can lead to severe deformity and the potential for neurologic compromise. Once the medical treatment is complete and the bone has healed, predicting the progression of kyphosis is of interest similar to predicting the progression of scoliosis as surgery could stabilize the spine and prevent progression. Factors that suggest further progression of the healed TB spine include thoracolumbar level or any of the four radiographic signs of a 'spine at risk'. These four signs include separated facets, posterior retropulsion, lateral translation, or the toppling sign. Having 2 or more of these signs predicts an increase in 30 degrees of kyphosis and a final angle of 60 degrees for which surgery should be recommended.

*Rasouli et al. reviewed all papers on spinal TB between 1990 and 2011.* **They note** current difficulties in management include drug-resistant TB strains as well as infections in patients with immunocompromised states (HIV). **They stated** children may require earlier surgical intervention given their growth potential and potential for severe kyphotic deformity.

*Jain et al. reviewed tuberculosis of the spine.* **They stated** in children with spinal TB progression of kyphosis does continue even when the lesion has healed. **They recommend** patients with a kyphosis deformity of >60 degrees on presentation or a kyphotic deformity that is likely to progress should be treated with surgery.

*Rajasekaran et al. studied 61 children who were appropriately treated for spinal tuberculosis.* *They developed a radiographic scoring system with 4 markers of a 'spine at risk' that predicted progression of deformity during the healed phase.* These markers were surrogates of posterior stability, which when compromised leads to the rapid progression of kyphosis.

Figure A shows a standing radiograph of the lumbar spine of a skeletally immature patient with the destruction of the anterior half of the L1 vertebral body and relative kyphosis at that level. Figure B shows an MRI of the lumbar spine with L1 vertebral body endplate destruction and early L2 involvement with edema at the superior and inferior vertebral bodies. Illustration A shows the toppling sign from Rajasekaran described as the anterior aspect of the normal superior vertebral body contacting the superior endplate of the normal inferior endplate.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: TB disease isolated to the thoracic spine improved more once healed when compared to disease isolated to the thoracolumbar spine.
Answer 2: Younger age on presentation can mean more severe deformities on presentation but it does not appear to affect progression in the healed phase.
Answer 3: The number of levels involved in the disease has not been shown to best predict progression in the healed phase.
Answer 4: Timing to full-time bracing has not been shown to slow the progression of kyphosis in healed spinal TB.
