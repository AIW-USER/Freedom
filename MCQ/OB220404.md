## Question

A 62-year-old male previously underwent unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), which has functioned well for him. He now presents with severe pain affecting the contralateral knee with radiographs shown in Figure A. Due to tricompartmental arthritis, he is offered total knee arthroplasty (TKA). He discusses the risks and benefits with his surgeon. Compared to UKA, he is counseled to expect which of the following after his TKA procedure?

![[q220404-a-kellgren-3.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Reduced mortality rate
2. Decreased risk of early periprosthetic fracture
3. Higher rate of all-cause revision
4. Lower risk of periprosthetic joint infection
5. Shorter hospital length of stay

## Correct option

**2. Decreased risk of early periprosthetic fracture**

## Answer explanation

Periprosthetic fracture (Answer 2) is more likely to occur in the first 90 days after UKA when compared to TKA.

While the absolute risk for periprosthetic fracture is low in both TKA and UKA, UKA patients are twice as likely to experience early fracture. Other common causes for revision of UKA include progression of arthritis (most common), aseptic loosening, and polyethylene wear. Notably, patients undergoing UKA are at a relatively lower risk of initial medical complications, including venous thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, and death when compared to their TKA counterparts. Furthermore, rates of periprosthetic joint infection and stiffness are lower in UKA versus TKA.

*Mohammad et al. compared TKA and UKA periprosthetic fracture risk in a large national database,* **demonstrating** that patients who underwent UKA were twice as likely to fracture in the first 3 months postoperatively. Conversely, after 3 months, periprosthetic fracture was approximately twice as likely to occur in TKA than UKA, and TKA had a significantly increased cumulative fracture rate in long-term follow-up of 10 years.

*Hansen et al. evaluated 90-day complication rates and revision-free survival at 0.5, 2, 5, 7, and up to 10 years post-operatively in UKA versus TKA patients from two large databases.* The UKA cohorts had lower rates of wound complications, pulmonary embolism, stiffness, periprosthetic joint infection, myocardial infarction, readmission, and death. However, by 7 years post-surgery, UKA survivorship was significantly lower in both cohorts (74.4% & 80.9%) when compared to TKA (91.9% & 95.7%).

*Argenson et al. evaluated 62 patients (70 knees) at a mean follow-up of 20 years after undergoing UKA.* *Fourteen of the 70 knees required a revision procedure, with causes including progression of arthritis (n=12), aseptic loosening (n=2), polyethylene wear (n=5), and late infection (n=1).*

Price and Svard review the results of 682 medial Oxford UKA. Of these patients, 29 had revision procedures: 10 for arthritis progression, nine for aseptic loosening, five for infection, two bearing dislocations, and three for unexplained pain.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Compared to unicompartmental arthroplasty, early medical complications, including mortality, are higher after TKA.
Answer 3: TKA has a lower all-cause revision rate when compared to unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), especially at mid- to long-term follow-up.
Answer 4 & 5: Periprosthetic joint infection and increased hospital length of stay are both more likely to occur after TKA.
