## Question

Figure A shows a hand radiograph of a 27-year-old male who presents to your clinic after punching a wall. He complains of pain primarily over his small finger. You do not appreciate any fracture, but inform the patient of his additional radiographic findings. He states he has no history of pain or injury elsewhere in his hand and has never seen an orthopedic surgeon before. Based on the most likely radiographic diagnosis, which of the following is true?

![[q217821-a-enchondroma-wo-fracture.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Rate of malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma is 5-8%
2. Best treated with curettage and bone grafting in setting of pathologic fracture
3. MRI is necessary for diagnosis
4. Caused by incomplete intramembranous ossification
5. Most commonly classified as a Enneking Stage 2

## Correct option

**2. Best treated with curettage and bone grafting in setting of pathologic fracture**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a 2nd digit proximal phalanx enchondroma, which if associated with a fracture should be treated with curettage and bone grafting.

Enchondromas are the 2nd most common benign cartilage lesions known and are usually found in the diaphysis or metaphysis. They are most prevalent in the hand, followed by the distal femur, proximal humerus, and tibia. They are thought to be caused by a failure of endochondral ossification. Pain is uncommonly associated with enchondromas and usually, pain is related to a separate cause. However, a fracture can occur through an enchondroma as the bone is inherently weakened when cortical expansion and thinning are present. If a pathologic fracture occurs, curettage and bone grafting is the recommended treatment. Malignant transformation is very uncommon (<1%) with solitary enchondromas, but does increase in likelihood with associated conditions such as Ollier's disease and Maffucci's syndrome.

*Sassoon et al. reviewed 102 surgically treated hand enchondromas.* **They noted** that 61% achieved complete healing radiographically and a full range of motion was achieved in 67% of cases. 93% of the lesions remained recurrence-free, with patients with multiple enchondromas and Maffucci's syndrome have a higher rate of recurrence.

*Jacobson et al. presented a case report of an index finger proximal phalanx solitary enchondroma.* *They review their treatment algorithm with regards to nonoperative vs. operative management and also discuss different types of bone graft/substitutes.* *They go on to discuss the patient's follow-up and results of curettage with and without bone grafting.*

Figure A is a hand radiograph demonstrating an enchondroma of the index finger proximal phalanx with a well-defined medullary lesion and cortical expansion/thinning.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The rate of malignant transformation in solitary enchondromas is <1%.
Answer 3: MRI is typically not necessary for diagnosis in enchondromas, as they have well-defined radiographic features.
Answer 4: It is thought that enchondromas develop due to incomplete endochondral ossification as fragments of epiphyseal cartilage displace into the metaphysis and proliferate.
Answer 5: Enchondromas are latent lesions, meaning they are classified as Enneking Stage 1 in the benign lesion staging system.
