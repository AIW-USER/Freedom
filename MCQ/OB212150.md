## Question

A 38-year-old female employee of a city Parks and Recreation department in a small Mid-Western town presents with persistent right elbow pain. Her symptoms are made worse when she removes thick brush or lifts heavy objects in the care of her small municipal pony. For the last 3 years, under the care of a rheumatologist, she has taken an appropriate disease-modifying anti-rheumatoid drug (DMARD) regimen. Despite this, on exam, she has a pronounced joint effusion, elbow flexion arc of 45°, and crepitus with forearm rotation. Her elbow radiographs reveal preservation of her joint ulnohumeral space. Her hand surgeon has recommended a synovectomy with radial head resection. Which of the following factors does not represent an indication to perform a synovectomy with or without radial head resection?

## Options

1. Female gender
2. Preserved joint space on radiographs
3. Heavy work duties
4. Young age on appropriate DMARD
5. Early stage of disease

## Correct option

**1. Female gender**

## Answer explanation

Preserved joint space, young age on appropriate DMARD, early stage of disease in a patient with preserved joint space would all be indications to perform a more limited and palliative procedure, such as a synovectomy with radial head resection.

Rheumatoid arthritis is typically managed medically with DMARDs, which are extremely successful. For the symptomatic rheumatoid elbow, treatment often begins with injections. Operative management often begins with arthroscopic or open synovectomy, capsular release, and possible radial head excision. Interpositional arthroplasty (Achilles tendon allograft, dermal allograft, anconeus autograft) covers the damaged articular surface and avoids complications associated with total elbow arthroplasty. Total elbow arthroplasty replaces the damaged articular surface, but there is a permanent lifting restriction (5 pounds) and other complications are inherent (loosening and infection).

*Lonner and Stuchin published a retrospective case review of 12 elbows that underwent synovectomy, radial head excision, and anterior capsular release for rheumatoid arthritis and followed the patients for over 6 years.* Flexion-extension and pronation-supination both improved, pain was improved in all cases, and every patient had functional improvement. There was no significant disease progression on radiographs over time. **The authors report** that this technique is both safe and effective.

*Tanaka et al. reviewed the results of open (53 patients) versus arthroscopic (58 patients) synovectomy of the elbow in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.* More patients (70% vs 48%) who underwent arthroscopic synovectomy were mildly or not painful at follow up. There was no difference in overall results and both cohorts had a low rate of conversion to total elbow arthroplasty (3 open and 6 arthroscopic). **The authors report** that arthroscopic debridement and synovectomy is a reliable procedure for patients with a stiff rheumatoid elbow.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Preserved joint space would be a contraindication to a total joint replacement. Instead, a joint preserving procedure like a synovectomy would be indicated.
Answer 3: Heavy work duties may rule out a total elbow arthroplasty, but these duties could still be performed with a synovectomy.
Answer 4: A younger patient who is currently controlled on DMARDs. as in this case, would likely be effectively treated with a joint preserving procedure like a synovectomy.
Answer 5: A patient who is in the early stages of rheumatoid arthritis may be best treated with a joint preserving procedure, like a synovectomy.
