## Question

## Options

1. Improved American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle/hindfoot score
2. Improved 36-Item ShortForm Survey (SF-36) score
3. Decreased great toe plantar flexion strength
4. Increased ankle plantar flexion strength
5. No difference in outcomes

## Correct option

**4. Increased ankle plantar flexion strength**

## Answer explanation

Flexor hallucis longus (FHL) transfer in overweight patients has been associated with increased ankle plantar flexion strength.

Achilles tendinosis refers to a degenerative process of the tendon without evidence of inflammatory changes on histology or clinically. Non-operative treatment (first-line) may involve the following: activity modification, orthotics, heel lifts, analgesics, corticosteroid injections, eccentric training, extracorporeal shock wave therapy, sclerosing agents, electrocoagulation, and laser therapy. There exists no evidence to date supporting the use of platelet rich plasma in treating this condition. Operative treatment may involve the following: percutaneous longitudinal tenotomy, minimally invasive tendon stripping, endoscopic tendon debridement, and open tendon debridement and repair with or without augmentation. Classically, if more than 50% of the diseased tendon is removed FHL augmentation is performed.

*Hunt et al. compared the surgical treatment of insertional Achilles tendinopathy with or without FHL transfer.* *They performed a prospective, randomized study and measured ankle and hallux plantar flexion strength, AOFAS, pain on Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and self-reported satisfaction at a minimum of 1-year follow-up.* **They found** no difference in any of the patient-reported outcomes, no loss in hallux plantar flexion strength, and improved ankle plantar flexion strength.

*Elias et al. examined the outcomes of chronic insertional Achilles tendinosis using FHL autograft through a single incision technique.* *They included 40 patients with persistent chronic Achilles tendinosis who underwent surgery and measured ankle strength, active range of motion, AOFAS, pain on VAS, and self-reported satisfaction at an average of 27 months follow-up.* **They found** improvements in both the AOFAS score, VAS score, no loss of plantar flexion strength/power, and an average of 4-degrees loss of active range of motion post-op.

Figure A is a clinical photo demonstrating a Haglund's deformity. Figure B is a STIR MRI showing thickening of the Achilles tendon, Haglund's deformity, and inflammatory changes. Figure C an MRI with an arrow pointing to the FHL tendon at the ankle.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: There is no evidence supporting improved AOFAS or SF-36 scores in this setting comparing treatment modalities.
Answer 3: Studies have not demonstrated plantar flexion weakness of the great toe with FHL transfer, surprisingly.
Answer 5: Studies have demonstrated increased plantar flexion strength with FHL transfer.
