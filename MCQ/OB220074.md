## Question

A 65-year-old right-hand dominant female presents with a long-standing history of right index finger pain and catching while making a fist. She is diagnosed with stenosing tenosynovitis and receives two corticosteroid injections 6 months apart which provided relief. Her last injection was 2 months ago, but she is now presenting with more locking during flexion of her right index finger. The patient is consented for a trigger finger release. When should this procedure be scheduled to reduce her risk of postoperative infection?

## Options

1. Tomorrow
2. Next week
3. Two weeks
4. Three weeks
5. 1.5 months

## Correct option

**5. 1.5 months**

## Answer explanation

Since the patient received a corticosteroid injection 2 months ago, to reduce her risk of postoperative infection, the most favorable time to schedule her surgery would be in 1.5 months (answer choice 5).

Stenosing tenosynovitis occurs at the A1 pulley, also known as a trigger finger. Fibrocartilaginous metaplasia of the tendon or pulley occurs, and pain ensues with clicking, catching, and/or locking of the affected finger. Corticosteroid injections are a common conservative treatment option for this condition. However, injections within 90 days of surgery have been shown to increase the risk of postoperative infection.

*Koopman et al. performed a study to evaluate risk factors for postoperative complications following trigger finger release.* **They reported** an overall 16% complication rate. Risk factors associated with higher complication rates included longer symptom duration, three or more corticosteroid injections, steroid injection within three months of surgery, and treatment of the dominant hand, index finger, or middle finger. **They concluded** that three or more preoperative steroid injections and those within 90 days of surgery had the greatest risk of postoperative complications.

*Matzon et al. sought to determine the risk of infection with preoperative corticosteroid injections for trigger fingers.* The median time from a preoperative steroid injection to surgery in the patients who developed a postoperative deep infection was 63 days compared to those without infection (183 days). Preoperative steroid injections resulted in a statistically significant increase in postoperative deep infections, especially in the 31- to 90-day time period.

*Kortlever et al. performed a randomized controlled study comparing nylon and chromic gut sutures after minor hand surgery.* Overall, patient satisfaction and the appearance of wound healing were not different between the different suture types. **They conclude** that chromic gut sutures are a viable alternative to nylon sutures after minor hand surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choices 1-4: These answer choices would schedule surgery within 90 days from the last corticosteroid injection. These answers are incorrect, as the studies above have shown a statistically significant increase in the risk of postoperative infection when steroid injections are administered within 90 days of surgery.
