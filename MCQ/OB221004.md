## Question

A 62-year-old male with a BMI of 31 is planned to undergo an elective hindfoot arthrodesis and will be non-weight-bearing for six weeks. Medical history includes well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and prior deep vein thrombosis after a remote ankle fracture open reduction internal fixation. Which of the following is the most appropriate postoperative venous thromboembolism strategy?

## Options

1. No chemoprophylaxis needed as guidelines recommend against it for isolated lower-leg injuries requiring immobilization
2. Routine chemoprophylaxis for all foot and ankle patients who are non-weightbearing
3. Perform a patient-specific risk assessment and consider chemoprophylaxis based on individual risk factors
4. Order scheduled duplex ultrasonography at 2 and 6 weeks to screen for clinically silent deep vein thrombosis
5. Obtain a D-dimer postoperatively and start chemoprophylaxis only if elevated

## Correct option

**3. Perform a patient-specific risk assessment and consider chemoprophylaxis based on individual risk factors**

## Answer explanation

A patient-specific risk assessment is most appropriate because venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk is heterogeneous in foot and ankle patients, and non–weight bearing, increased age, history of diabetes mellitus, and prior DVT are recognized risk factors that may justify individualized prophylaxis decisions (answer choice 3).

Deep vein thrombosis is caused by a blood clot that typically originates in the deep veins in the lower leg, with the propensity to propagate proximally, or rarely lead to pulmonary embolism. It most often presents in middle-aged and elderly adults with calf swelling and pain. Diagnosis is made by combining clinical examination findings, including extremity swelling, calf pain, erythema, warmth, and tenderness to palpation, along with non-invasive imaging. Homan's sign, or the presence of calf pain with passive ankle dorsiflexion, is neither sufficiently sensitive nor specific to meaningfully influence diagnosis. Complete duplex ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality because it is noninvasive and inexpensive, and has high reported sensitivity and specificity. Routine duplex screening is not recommended, and D-dimer testing is not indicated in the perioperative period, as it is often nonspecifically elevated secondary to orthopedic injuries or surgeries. Treatment centers around medical anticoagulation, with direct oral anticoagulants typically employed as first-line options, while thrombolytic therapy or procedural interventions are reserved for select, higher-risk situations.

*Sullivan et al. prospectively evaluated low-risk elective hindfoot and midfoot surgery patients who were non-weight-bearing for at least six weeks using ultrasonographic surveillance at 2 and 6 weeks.* **They reported** a 25.4% prevalence of DVT, with at least 75% of DVTs occurring without clinical symptoms or signs. **They concluded** that clinically silent DVT may be more common than previously appreciated when surveillance ultrasonography is used.

*Weisman et al. reviewed venous thromboembolic prophylaxis in foot and ankle surgery in a narrative review.* **They reported** that ACCP guidelines recommend against chemoprophylaxis for isolated leg injuries requiring immobilization, but also emphasized that there are no clear foot-and-ankle-specific guidelines and that many studies advocate case-by-case risk factor assessment, including the use of risk assessment scoring systems. **They concluded** that practice variability reflects heterogeneity in procedures and patient risk profiles and supports individualized decision-making.

The ICM-VTE Foot & Ankle Delegates provided consensus recommendations on VTE considerations in foot and ankle patients. They reported that non-weight-bearing restrictions are an independent risk factor that should be considered in assessing VTE risk, but stated that no recommendation for additional prophylaxis solely due to non-weight-bearing can be made, given limited evidence. They concluded that surgeons should incorporate postoperative restrictions and patient factors into individualized risk assessment rather than relying on a single uniform approach.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Although most guidelines are against routine chemoprophylaxis for isolated lower leg injuries, there is also general agreement that a case-by-case risk factor assessment is prudent and should be considered. This patient has multiple risk factors for postoperative VTE, which should not be ignored.
Answer choice 2: Although non-weight-bearing is a risk factor for VTE development, routine chemoprophylaxis is not recommended.
Answer choices 4 & 5: Routine postoperative surveillance ultrasonography or D-dimer testing are not indicated in asymptomatic patients. Furthermore, D-dimer testing in the perioperative period is typically unreliable given the rate of false positives secondary to the injury or surgery itself.
