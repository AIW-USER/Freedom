## Question

An orthopedic fellow is critically appraising a randomized controlled trial (RCT) published in a major orthopedic journal comparing operative versus non-operative management of displaced midshaft clavicle fractures. The authors proudly claim Level I evidence. Upon review, the fellow notes that the outcome assessors were completely blinded, sequence generation and allocation concealment were flawless, and a strict intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was performed. However, the study suffered from a 22% loss to follow-up at the final 2-year endpoint. According to the standardized Levels of Evidence criteria adopted by The Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery (JBJS), what is the true level of evidence of this study and what is the primary rationale?

## Options

1. Level III; because surgical trials inherently cannot blind the treating surgeon or the patient to the intervention, making true Level I or Level II therapeutic evidence practically impossible in orthopedics.
2. Level II; because the loss to follow-up exceeded 20%, introducing a significant risk of attrition bias that systematically degrades the initial randomization.
3. Level I; because sequence generation, concealed allocation, and an intention-to-treat analysis were all successfully implemented, which are the absolute requirements for Level I therapeutic studies.
4. Level IV; because performing an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis is statistically invalid when loss to follow-up exceeds 15%, reducing the study's validity to that of a retrospective case series.

## Correct option

**2. Level II; because the loss to follow-up exceeded 20%, introducing a significant risk of attrition bias that systematically degrades the initial randomization.**

## Answer explanation

According to JBJS and Oxford CEBM guidelines, a high-quality RCT can be downgraded to Level II evidence for several reasons, the most common being a follow-up rate of less than 80%. A >20% dropout rate introduces significant attrition bias, as the patients lost to follow-up may have systematically different outcomes (e.g., higher complication rates) than those who stayed.

In medical research, evidence is not treated equally. Frameworks are used to rank research based on its methodological rigor and susceptibility to bias. The two most important concepts to understand are the Traditional Hierarchy (Levels I-V) and the GRADE Framework, which is used to systematically upgrade or downgrade the quality of that evidence.

Part 1: The Traditional Hierarchy (Levels I - V)

Most major journals (like The Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery) categorize studies into five distinct levels based primarily on their study design.

 - Level I (Highest Quality):

 - High-quality Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) with narrow confidence intervals.

 - Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Level I RCTs.

 - Key Feature: Randomization, blinding, and $>80\%$ patient follow-up.

 - Level II:

 - Lesser-quality RCTs (e.g., unblinded, improper randomization, or $>20\%$ loss to follow-up).

 - Prospective Cohort Studies (following a group forward in time, but without randomization).

 - Systematic Reviews of Level II studies.

 - Level III:

 - Retrospective Cohort Studies (looking back at existing records of a group).

 - Case-Control Studies (starting with the outcome/disease and looking back for exposures).

 - Level IV:

 - Case Series (no comparison or control group; just a report on a series of patients with the same intervention).

 - Level V (Lowest Quality):

 - Expert Opinion.

 - Case Reports (single patient).

 - Bench research, in vitro studies, or animal/biomechanical studies.

Part 2: The GRADE Framework

While the traditional hierarchy relies heavily on design, the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach recognizes that a poorly done RCT might actually provide worse evidence than a meticulously done observational study.

Under GRADE, evidence is ranked as High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low.

 - RCTs start as High Quality.

 - Observational Studies start as Low Quality.

From these starting points, reviewers can systematically downgrade or upgrade the evidence based on specific criteria.

Five Factors that DOWNGRADE Evidence Quality

An RCT can be downgraded from High to Moderate, Low, or Very Low if any of the following five flaws are present:

 - Risk of Bias (Methodological Limitations):

 - What it is: Flaws in how the study was designed or executed.

 - Examples: Lack of blinding (performance bias), failure to conceal allocation (selection bias), high loss to follow-up (attrition bias), or stopping a trial early because of perceived benefit.

 - Inconsistency (Heterogeneity):

 - What it is: When multiple studies answering the same question show widely varying, conflicting results.

 - Examples: In a meta-analysis, if one RCT shows a massive benefit and another shows massive harm (often measured statistically by a high $I^2$ value), the overall evidence quality is downgraded because we aren't confident in the true effect.

 - Indirectness:

 - What it is: The study doesn't directly answer the specific clinical question at hand (PICO - Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome).

 - Examples:

 - Population: Applying results from pediatric fractures to osteoporotic geriatric fractures.

 - Outcome: Using a "surrogate" outcome (e.g., measuring bone mineral density via DEXA scan) instead of the clinically important outcome (e.g., actual fracture rates).

 - Imprecision:

 - What it is: The data is too sparse or wide-ranging to be clinically useful.

 - Examples: Small sample sizes, very few "events" (e.g., only 2 infections in a group of 500), or massively wide Confidence Intervals that cross the line of no effect.

 - Publication Bias:

 - What it is: The tendency for journals to only publish studies with positive or statistically significant results, leaving negative trials unpublished.

 - Examples: Often suspected when analyzing heavily industry-sponsored drug or implant trials. Can be detected statistically using a "Funnel Plot."

Three Factors that UPGRADE Evidence Quality

As noted previously, it is rare to upgrade evidence. It is almost exclusively applied to observational studies (which start at "Low") that show remarkably strong data.

 - Large Magnitude of Effect:

 - If the treatment effect is massive and consistent, it overcomes the lack of randomization.

 - Criteria: A Relative Risk (RR) > 2.0 or < 0.5 warrants a 1-level upgrade. An RR > 5.0 or < 0.2 warrants a 2-level upgrade.

 - Dose-Response Gradient:

 - If increasing the exposure directly leads to a proportional increase in the outcome, it strongly implies a causal relationship.

 - Example: Higher doses of a specific post-operative anticoagulant correlating linearly with a higher risk of major GI bleeding.

 - All Plausible Confounders Would Reduce the Effect:

 - If unmeasured biases and confounders in the study actually worked against finding an effect, yet a significant effect was still found, the true effect must be very strong.

 - Example: An observational study comparing a new joint replacement technique to an old one. If surgeons selectively used the new technique only on their most complex, morbidly obese patients, those patients should theoretically have worse outcomes. If the data shows they still had better outcomes than healthy patients with the old technique, the evidence is upgraded.

Answer: 2
