## Question

A minor league baseball player presents to the orthopedic clinic with a chief complaint of right shoulder pain. He reports the pain worsens whenever his throwing arm is maximally externally rotated during his wind-up. He indicates the pain feels deep and posterior. On physical exam pain is reported when the arm is brought into 90° of abduction and maximal external rotation. He has a negative apprehension sign with 1+ passive anterior translation. His throwing arm has 30° less in a total arc of motion than his contralateral shoulder. An MRI is ordered. What MRI findings are expected based on this patient’s history and physical exam?

## Options

1. Supraspinatus leading edge partial thickness bursal sided tear
2. Superior border of the subscapularis tear
3. Increased humeral anteversion
4. Supraspinatus/infraspinatus partial thickness articular sided tear
5. Anterior inferior labral tear

## Correct option

**4. Supraspinatus/infraspinatus partial thickness articular sided tear**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with history and physical exam findings consistent with glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD). GIRD without instability is classically associated with articular-sided partial rotator cuff tears and tightening of the posterior capsule (Answer 4).

Throwing athletes, such as baseball pitchers, place a major strain on the dynamic and static stabilizers of the shoulder joint. When the arm is abducted and externally rotated in the late cocking phase, the posterosuperior rotator cuff and labrum can be pinched between the greater tuberosity and glenoid. An adaptive change that throwers develop is tightness in the posterior inferior glenohumeral ligament, resulting in a posterior superior shift in the glenohumeral rotation. This allows extra clearance between the greater tuberosity and the glenoid. However, excessive hyper-external rotation causes abrasion along the articular portion of the posterosuperior rotator cuff. Even more significantly, hyper-external rotation allows repetitive hyper-twisting and eccentric load along the rotator cuff. This results in the classic articular-sided tears seen in throwers with GIRD.

*Burkhart et al. published a series of review articles in 2003 that was a three-part series on disability in the throwing shoulder.* Part 1 focused on the pathoanatomy and biomechanics of internal impingement. The authors review the adaptive changes that occur, namely the tightening of the posterior capsule with a shift in the rotation point of the glenohumeral joint. **The authors conclude** that the hyper-twisting eccentric loads placed on the cuff are likely the main contributor to articular-sided tears.

*Gelber et al. published a review article in 2018 that discussed adaptive changes and pathoanatomy in the thrower's shoulder.* **The authors note** that several changes including increased proximal humerus retroversion, thickening of the posterior inferior glenohumeral ligament, and attenuation of the anterior capsule can be adaptive changes that do not always result in disability. The authors delineate symptomatic internal impingement into 3 groups: No instability, anterior instability, and multidirectional instability. **The authors conclude** that partial-thickness articular-sided and posterior labral tears are common in internal impingement without instability.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The tear pattern associated with GIRD and internal impingement is an articular-sided tear, typically at the junction of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus.
Answer 2: Superior subscapularis tears are not associated with GIRD.
Answer 3: Overhead-throwing athletes with GIRD typically have increased humeral retroversion.
Answer 5: A GIRD patient with no instability is unlikely to have an anterior inferior labral tear.
