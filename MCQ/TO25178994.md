## Question

Regarding the molecular pathogenesis of conventional osteosarcoma, which of the following statements represents the most accurate characterization of genetic alterations?

## Options

1. The vast majority of sporadic osteosarcomas exhibit pathognomonic balanced reciprocal chromosomal translocations, similar to Ewing sarcoma.
2. Somatic inactivation of both the RB1 and TP53 pathways is nearly universal, often driven by complex structural variations rather than simple point mutations.
3. MDM2 and CDK4 gene amplification via supernumerary ring chromosomes is the primary driver of high-grade conventional intramedullary osteosarcoma.
4. Germline mutations in the RECQL4 helicase gene are frequently found in sporadic high-grade osteosarcomas of elderly patients.

## Correct option

**2. Somatic inactivation of both the RB1 and TP53 pathways is nearly universal, often driven by complex structural variations rather than simple point mutations.**

## Answer explanation

Advanced genomic sequencing shows that concurrent disruption of the TP53 and RB1 pathways via structural rearrangements, chromothripsis, or copy number losses is a hallmark of conventional osteosarcoma pathogenesis.

Molecular and Genetic Pathogenesis of osteosarcoma

The pathogenesis of osteosarcoma involves a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, tumour suppressor gene inactivation, oncogene activation, and dysregulated osteoblast differentiation pathways.

Retinoblastoma (RB1) Gene Inactivation: The RB1 tumour suppressor gene (chromosome 13q14) encodes the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), a critical negative regulator of the cell cycle that prevents uncontrolled progression from G1 to S phase. Loss of both alleles of RB1 (through deletion, mutation, or epigenetic silencing) removes this cell cycle checkpoint, permitting unregulated proliferation. Patients with hereditary retinoblastoma (germline RB1 mutation) have a 500–1000-fold increased risk of developing osteosarcoma, typically in the second decade — representing the most well-established genetic predisposition. Somatic RB1 alterations are found in approximately 60–70% of sporadic osteosarcomas, confirming its central role in pathogenesis.

TP53 Gene Inactivation: The TP53 tumour suppressor gene (chromosome 17p13) encodes p53, the "guardian of the genome" — a transcription factor that responds to DNA damage by inducing cell cycle arrest (allowing DNA repair), apoptosis (eliminating irreparable cells), or senescence. Loss of p53 function permits the accumulation of genomic instability and the survival of cells with oncogenic mutations. Li-Fraumeni syndrome (germline TP53 mutation, autosomal dominant) carries a markedly elevated risk of osteosarcoma (along with breast cancer, brain tumours, adrenocortical carcinoma, and soft tissue sarcomas). Somatic TP53 mutations or deletions are found in 20–50% of sporadic osteosarcomas.

Chromosomal Instability and Genomic Complexity: Unlike many sarcomas (which often harbour specific, pathognomonic translocations — e.g., Ewing sarcoma with t(11;22)), osteosarcoma is characterised by extreme chromosomal instability — complex karyotypes with numerous structural and numerical chromosomal aberrations, chromothripsis (catastrophic chromosomal shattering and rearrangement), gene amplification, and extensive copy number alterations. This genomic chaos reflects the fundamental loss of cell cycle checkpoints (RB1, TP53) that normally maintain chromosomal integrity during cell division. Specific recurrent amplifications include MDM2
(chromosome 12q13 — encodes a protein that degrades p53, functionally inactivating the p53 pathway even when TP53 is intact), CDK4
(cyclin-dependent kinase 4 — drives cell cycle progression past the RB checkpoint), and MYC (oncogenic transcription factor promoting proliferation).

Growth Factor and Signalling Pathway Dysregulation: The peak incidence of osteosarcoma during the pubertal growth spurt is not coincidental — the metaphyseal location and adolescent age predilection suggest that the rapidly proliferating osteoblast progenitor cells in the growth plate region are the cells of origin. During the growth spurt, these cells are undergoing rapid division under the influence of growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and other mitogenic signals. The high proliferative rate increases the probability of acquiring oncogenic mutations, particularly in the setting of compromised cell cycle checkpoints (RB1, TP53). The IGF-1/IGF-1R signalling axis is frequently activated in osteosarcoma and promotes cell survival and proliferation. VEGF
(vascular endothelial growth factor) overexpression promotes tumour angiogenesis. Wnt/β-catenin, Hedgehog, and Notch
signalling pathways — all involved in normal osteoblast differentiation — are dysregulated in osteosarcoma, contributing to the arrested differentiation state of the malignant osteoblasts (they produce osteoid but fail to mature into organised lamellar bone).

Answer: 2
