## Question

Which of the following is true regarding surgery compared to immobilization + a single steroid injection for management of carpal tunnel syndrome?

## Options

1. Equal patient-reported outcomes at 6 months, but improved at 12 months
2. Improved patient-reported outcomes at 6 and 12 months
3. Decreased patient-reported outcomes at 6 months, but improved at 12 months
4. Equal patient-reported outcomes at 6 and 12 months
5. Decreased patient-reported outcomes at 6 and 12 months.

## Correct option

**2. Improved patient-reported outcomes at 6 and 12 months**

## Answer explanation

Carpal tunnel release (CTR) has shown increased patient-reported outcomes compared to conservative management at 6 and 12 months.

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a compressive neuropathy of the median nerve at the wrist. Symptoms primarily involve paresthesias of the radial 3-1/2 digits of the hand, clumsiness, loss of grip strength, and in severe cases thenar muscle atrophy. Conservative management generally involves wrist immobilization, NSAIDs, and corticosteroid injections into the carpal canal. When conservative management fails, the release of the transverse carpal ligament (carpal tunnel release) is the treatment of choice.

*Hui et al. performed a randomized controlled trial comparing carpal tunnel release (CTR) to a single steroid injection for the management of CTS.* *There were 25 patients in each group and all patients were followed up to 20 weeks.* At 20 weeks, patients who underwent surgery had greater symptomatic improvement than those who were injected but the mean grip strength in the surgical group was reduced by 1.7 kg compared with a gain of 2.4 kg in the injection group. **The authors concluded** that compared to a steroid injection, open carpal tunnel release resulted in better symptomatic outcomes but not grip strength in patients with CTS over a 20-week period.

*Jarvik et al. performed a randomized controlled trial to compare surgical versus non-surgical treatment for patients with CTS.* *There were 57 patients in the CTR group and 59 patients in the multi-modal treatment group.* **The authors noted** a significant 12-month adjusted advantage for surgery in both function and symptoms. There were no clinically important adverse events and no surgical complications. **The authors concluded** that surgical treatment led to better outcomes than did non-surgical treatment.

*Gerritsen et al. performed a randomized control trial of 176 patients with clinically and electrophysiologically confirmed idiopathic CTS to compare wrist splinting during the night for at least 6 weeks versus open carpal tunnel release.* **The authors noted** that surgery was more effective than splinting on all outcome measures and that at 18 months, the success rates increased to 90% for the surgery group (61/68 patients) vs 75% for the splinting group (59/79 patients). However, by that time 41% of patients (32/79) in the splint group had also received the surgery treatment. **The authors concluded** that open carpal tunnel release surgery resulted in better outcomes than treatment with wrist splinting for patients with CTS.

*Graham et al. published the AAOS evidence-based guidelines for the treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome.* **They noted** strong evidence which supports high BMI and high hand/wrist repetition rate being associated with the development of CTS; strong evidence that wrist splinting and steroid injections both improve patient-reported outcomes. **They also noted** strong evidence supporting surgical treatment of CTS having greater treatment benefit at 6 and 12 months as compared to splinting, NSAIDs/therapy, and a single steroid injection. Lastly, **they also noted** strong evidence against the use of post-operative immobilization following CTR.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, 4, and 5: Carpal tunnel release has shown improved patient-reported outcomes at 6 and 12 months compared to non-surgical management.
