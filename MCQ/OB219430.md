## Question

A 36-year-old male presents to the clinic with a chief complaint of sporadic numbness in his right hand. On exam, he has a positive tinel sign posterior to the medial epicondyle with reproducible paresthesias over the small and ring fingers with compression over the cubital tunnel. He also has decreased strength with index finger abduction when compared to the contralateral hand. The patient is counseled on treatment options and requests surgical decompression. An intraoperative photograph is shown in Figure A. What anatomic structure is labeled by the black dot in Figure A?

![[q219430-a-epitrochlearis.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Long head of the Triceps
2. Osborne Ligament
3. Flexor Carpi Ulnaris
4. Anconeus epitrochlearis
5. Arcade of Struthers

## Correct option

**4. Anconeus epitrochlearis**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent ulnar nerve decompression secondary to cubital tunnel syndrome with muscle weakness. The muscle tissue that is overlying the ulnar nerve posterior to the medial epicondyle is the anconeus epitrochlearis (Answer 4).

Cubital tunnel syndrome is a compressive neuropathy of the ulnar nerve around the medial elbow. This clinical syndrome presents with paresthesias over the small and ring fingers that are often exacerbated with elbow flexion. Loss of ulnar nerve function can result in intrinsic muscle weakness. This weakness can be reported as decreased pinch and grip strength but can also be isolated with testing of the first dorsal interossei. The ulnar nerve can be compressed at several points around the elbow. Compression at the Osborne ligament is the most common scenario. The anconeus epitrochlearis is an anomalous muscle at the medial elbow that runs between the medial aspect of the olecranon and the medial epicondyle. It replaces the Osborne ligament in approximately 10% of the population and can cause static or dynamic compression of the ulnar nerve.

*Staples et al. presented a review article regarding the anatomy, diagnosis, and clinical management of cubital tunnel syndrome.* *They also discuss potential sites of nerve compression that need to be considered during surgery (Illustration A).* *The authors of this study recommend offering surgery to patients with a decrease in two-point sensibility and/ or presence of muscle atrophy because ongoing nerve compression results in intrinsic nerve damage and loss of muscle that gradually becomes increasingly recalcitrant to treatment.*

*Kim et al. reported on a case series of 16 cases of cubital tunnel syndrome and an associated anconeus epitrochlearis.* Eight patients were treated with myotomy and ulnar nerve decompression while the other 8 were treated with myotomy and submuscular transposition of the ulnar nerve. **They recommend** testing the compression of the ulnar nerve intraoperatively by passively ranging the elbow while observing the tension of the nerve and muscle fibers. However, regardless of these findings, **they recommend** myotomy of the muscle and in situ decompression of the ulnar nerve.

Figure A is an intraoperative picture of the medial approach at the elbow directly over the cubital tunnel. The ulnar nerve is visualized exiting from underneath a muscular tissue that extends from the medial epicondyle to the olecranon. This is an anconeus epitrochlearis. Illustration A is a figure taken from the above-mentioned review article by Staples et al., showing possible sites of ulnar nerve compression.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The long head of the triceps travels in a different orientation and has already traveled midline at this level to insert on the olecranon.
Answer 2: In the presence of an anconeus epitrochlearis, the Osborne ligament is replaced by the muscle itself. The tissue seen in Figure A is muscle.
Answer 3: Flexor Carpi Ulnaris is seen anterior in the surgical wound traveling from its origin on the medial epicondyle.
Answer 5: The arcade of Struthers is proximal to the medial epicondyle.
