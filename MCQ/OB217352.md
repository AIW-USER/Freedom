## Question

A 45-year-old male was arm wrestling his friend at a bar when he felt a pop. He presents today complaining of weakness with elbow flexion and supination and bruising in the antecubital fossa. You obtain an MRI which confirms a complete rupture of the distal biceps tendon. You recommend distal biceps repair and explain to the patient that you will perform this procedure using only one incision. Which of the following statements is true regarding this technique?

## Options

1. Improved post-operative supination strength with one-incision compared to two-incision techniques
2. More anatomic repair with one-incision compared to two-incision technique
3. Utilizes interval between pronator teres and flexor carpi radialis
4. Pronation should be maintained to avoid injury to posterior interosseous nerve
5. Higher rates of sensory nerve injury are seen with one-incision compared to two-incision technique

## Correct option

**5. Higher rates of sensory nerve injury are seen with one-incision compared to two-incision technique**

## Answer explanation

Distal biceps ruptures repaired through a one-incision technique have a higher rate of sensory nerve injury than those repaired through a two incision technique.

Distal biceps ruptures present primarily in middle-aged men and occurs with excessive eccentric tension. Surgical management to reattach the biceps is usually recommended as nonoperative management leads to loss of supination and, to a lesser degree, flexion strength. Both single- and double-incision approaches are utilized, with different risks. The single-incision technique is performed between the brachioradialis and pronator teres and risks sensory nerve injury to both the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABC) and the radial sensory nerve. The posterior interosseous nerve is also at risk and can be protected with maximal supination during the procedure. The double incision technique has a lower risk of PIN injury, but traditionally has been described as having higher rates of heterotopic ossification, presumably due to periosteal stripping of the ulna. While both approaches are acceptable, specific risks must be taken into consideration and discussed with patients before proceeding with surgery.

*Grewal et al. performed an RCT evaluating single vs. double-incision techniques for the repair of distal biceps ruptures.* **They found** no differences in functional outcomes at follow-up and a slight improvement in flexion strength with the double-incision technique. The single-incision technique was associated with a greater number of LABC neuropraxias (19) compared to the double-incision group (3).

*Ford et al. reviewed major complications after distal biceps repairs in 970 cases.* There was a major complication rate of 7.5% overall, most commonly including PIN palsy (1.9%), proximal radioulnar synostosis (1%), rerupture (1.6%) and heterotopic ossification (0.9%). The two-incision technique had a higher rate of radioulnar synostosis, while rates of sensory neuropraxia were higher in the single-incision technique.

*Miyamoto et al. reviewed distal biceps tendon ruptures including the history and examination as well as surgical management.* With regards to operative management, they again noted a higher rate of nerve injury with the single-incision approach, and a higher rate of heterotopic ossification with the two-incision approach. **They noted** there was no evidence to support a specific fixation device in this population, however.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Post-operative supination strength is similar or slightly better with the two-incision technique.
Answer 2: More anatomic repair is thought to be possible with the two-incision technique because of better exposure of the distal biceps footprint.
Answer 3: One-incision approach utilizes the interval between the brachioradialis and the pronator teres.
Answer 4: The arm should be positioned in maximum supination during one-incision repair in order to avoid injury to the posterior interosseous nerve.
