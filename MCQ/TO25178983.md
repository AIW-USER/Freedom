Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Carpal Instability

## Question

In Carpal Instability Dissociative (CID), specifically following a chronic Scapholunate Interosseous Ligament (SLIL) rupture, which structure is considered the primary 'secondary stabilizer' whose failure leads to the transition from dynamic to static DISI deformity?

## Options

1. Dorsal Intercarpal Ligament (DIC)
2. Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) ligament complex
3. Space of Poirier
4. Volar Radiolunate Ligament

## Correct option

**2. Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) ligament complex**

## Answer explanation

In the presence of an SLIL tear, the STT and Scaphocapitate ligaments act as the primary secondary stabilizers; their attenuation leads to a static deformity.

Carpal Instability Dissociative (CID)

Definition

CID refers to instability occurring within a carpal row due to disruption of the intrinsic (interosseous) ligaments that bind bones within the same row. The pathology is between bones of the same row — the proximal row is the most commonly affected. The two principal forms are scapholunate dissociation
(the most common and clinically important carpal instability) and lunotriquetral dissociation.

Scapholunate Dissociation (DISI Pattern)

This results from disruption of the scapholunate interosseous ligament (SLIL), particularly its dorsal component (the strongest portion, providing the primary restraint to scapholunate separation). When the SLIL ruptures, the scaphoid and lunate decouple — the scaphoid flexes (its natural tendency under axial load due to its oblique orientation) while the lunate extends (pulled into extension by the intact lunotriquetral ligament and the influence of the triquetrum, which naturally extends). This produces the dorsal intercalated segment instability (DISI) pattern — the lunate tilts into extension (dorsiflexion) on the lateral radiograph.

Clinical Features: Dorsal wrist pain exacerbated by gripping, weakness of grip, clicking, and a positive Watson (scaphoid shift) test — the examiner applies pressure to the scaphoid tubercle volarly while moving the wrist from ulnar to radial deviation; in SL dissociation, the proximal pole of the scaphoid subluxates dorsally over the rim of the distal radius, producing a palpable clunk and pain that is relieved when pressure is released. Tenderness is localised to the dorsal SL interval (1 cm distal to Lister's tubercle).

Radiographic Findings (Weight-Bearing PA and Lateral): The Terry Thomas sign (widened scapholunate gap >3 mm on PA view, named after the gap-toothed British actor), the scaphoid ring sign (the flexed scaphoid is seen end-on, producing a cortical ring shadow on the PA view), increased scapholunate angle on the lateral view (normal 30–60°; >70° is diagnostic of DISI), and decreased carpal height ratio as collapse progresses. The natural history of untreated SL dissociation follows the predictable SLAC (Scapholunate Advanced Collapse) pattern of progressive arthrosis — Stage I: radial styloid-scaphoid arthritis; Stage II: radioscaphoid arthritis; Stage III: midcarpal (capitolunate) arthritis. The radiolunate joint is characteristically preserved in SLAC because the lunate articulation with the radius remains congruent.

Management by Stage:

Acute SLIL Tear (<6 weeks, reducible): Direct arthroscopic or open SLIL repair with suture anchors through a dorsal approach, augmented with temporary K-wire fixation of the scapholunate interval (wires removed at 8–10 weeks). Dorsal capsulodesis (dorsal intercarpal ligament advancement — the Berger capsulodesis) augments the repair and provides secondary restraint.

Chronic Irreparable SLIL Tear (No Arthritis): The ligament is attenuated and cannot be directly repaired. Options include ligament reconstruction — the modified Brunelli procedure (a strip of the flexor carpi radialis tendon is passed through a drill hole in the scaphoid and secured to the dorsal lunate or dorsal radius, reconstructing the dorsal SL constraint) or the RASL procedure (Reduction and Association of the Scaphoid and Lunate — a headless compression screw across the reduced SL interval, though this sacrifices motion). Three-ligament tenodesis (3LT)
and the SLAM procedure (ScaphoLunate Axis Method) are newer reconstructive options. Bone-ligament-bone grafts using dorsal hamatocapitate or dorsal radius autografts are also described.

SLAC Wrist (Established Arthritis):
Salvage procedures tailored to the stage of arthritis — Stage I/II:
Scaphoid excision and four-corner fusion (capitolunate, lunotriquetral, triquetrohamate, triquetrumcapitate arthrodesis) — removes the arthritic radioscaphoid articulation while preserving the intact radiolunate joint, maintaining approximately 50% of wrist flexion-extension. Alternatively, proximal row carpectomy (PRC) — excision of the entire proximal row (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum), allowing the capitate to articulate directly with the lunate fossa of the radius. PRC is a simpler, motion-preserving procedure but requires an intact capitate head and lunate fossa articular cartilage. Stage III: Four-corner fusion remains effective (the radiolunate joint is preserved even in Stage III). Stage IV (pancarpal arthritis including radiolunate joint): Total wrist arthrodesis.

Lunotriquetral Dissociation (VISI Pattern)

Disruption of the lunotriquetral interosseous ligament (LTIL) decouples the lunate from the triquetrum. Without the extending influence of the triquetrum, the lunate follows the scaphoid into flexion, producing the volar intercalated segment instability (VISI) pattern — the lunate tilts into flexion (volarflexion) on the lateral radiograph. Less common and less clinically significant than SL dissociation.

Clinical Features: Ulnar-sided wrist pain, positive Reagan (lunotriquetral ballottement) test — the examiner stabilises the lunate with one thumb and displaces the triquetrum dorsally and volarly with the other, producing pain and crepitus. Positive Kleinman shear test.

Radiographic Findings: Decreased scapholunate angle (<30°) on the lateral view, VISI configuration. PA view may show disruption of the normal proximal carpal arc (Gilula's arc I).

Management: Acute tears are managed with direct repair and K-wire fixation. Chronic tears may be managed with lunotriquetral arthrodesis (high non-union rate, 20–40%), ulnar shortening osteotomy
(if concurrent ulnar positive variance and TFCC pathology contribute to the instability), or ligament reconstruction.

The scaphoid naturally wants to flex, while the triquetrum (and lunate via its connection) wants to extend. The Scapholunate Interosseous Ligament (SLIL) acts as the tether that balances these opposing forces. When this tether fails, the lunate is "captured" by the triquetrum and tilts into extension, creating a Dorsal Intercalated Segment Instability (DISI).

Dynamic versus static DISI

1. Dynamic DISI Deformity

This represents an early or partial stage of instability where the carpal bones still maintain a normal relationship at rest.

 - Definition: The carpal alignment appears normal on standard, non-stress radiographs, but becomes pathological under mechanical load or specific maneuvers.

 - Ligament Status: The primary SLIL
 is typically torn or significantly attenuated, but the secondary stabilizers (such as the Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) and Scaphocapitate ligaments) are still competent enough to hold the scaphoid in place during rest.

 - Diagnosis: * Standard X-rays are often negative.

 - Instability is revealed through stress views (e.g., clenched fist view) or provocative testing (e.g., Watson Shift Test).

 - On a clenched fist view, the scaphoid flexes and the lunate extends, often revealing the "Terry Thomas sign"
 (widened SL gap).

2. Static DISI Deformity

This represents a more advanced, chronic stage of instability where the deformity is "fixed."

 - Definition: The malalignment is visible on standard, neutral-position radiographs without the need for stress or maneuvers.

 - Ligament Status: Both the SLIL
 and the secondary stabilizers have failed or permanently stretched. The scaphoid has fallen into permanent flexion, and the lunate has tilted into permanent extension (dorsiflexion).

 - Radiographic Hallmarks:

 - SL Angle: Increased to >60°
 (often >80°).

 - Radiolunate Angle: Increased to >15°
 (lunate tilted dorsally).

 - Ring Sign: On the AP view, the flexed scaphoid is seen end-on, creating a cortical "ring."

 - Clinical Significance: Static deformity often indicates that the window for a simple ligament repair has passed, and more complex reconstructions (like capsulodesis, tenodesis, or salvage procedures) may be required.
