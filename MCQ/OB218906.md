## Question

A 63-year-old male presents with a history of Charcot arthropathy of his ankle secondary to diabetes that has now become recalcitrant to brace wear and orthotics. He has no evidence of open wounds, and he wishes to be able to ambulate without pain, as he is very active and enjoys recreational golf. Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the patient’s ankle. What would be the most appropriate management of this patient at this time?

![[q218906-a-screen-shot-2023-06-18-at-4-57-34-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Tibiotalar fusion with an anterior fusion plate
2. Below knee amputation
3. Total contact cast
4. Controlled ankle motion orthotic
5. Tibiotalar and subtalar fusion with a retrograde tibio-talar-calcaneal fusion nail

## Correct option

**5. Tibiotalar and subtalar fusion with a retrograde tibio-talar-calcaneal fusion nail**

## Answer explanation

This patient has failed conservative management and operative intervention is indicated. Given the severity of the patient’s Charcot arthropathy involving the tibiotalar and subtalar joints, a tibio-talar-calcaneal (TTC) fusion with a retrograde nail is the most appropriate treatment of the options provided (Answer 5).

Charcot arthropathy is most commonly caused by diabetes mellitus in Western and developing countries. However, there are many other etiologies for its development, such as alcohol abuse, rheumatoid arthritis, toxicity (e.g. induced by methotrexate), idiopathic, or as a result of leprosy or other diseases (e.g. syphilis). The development of Charcot arthropathy is thought to be secondary to the interaction of several component factors (diabetes, sensory-motor neuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, trauma, and metabolic abnormalities of bone) that result in an acute localized inflammatory condition, eventually leading to varying degrees and patterns of bone destruction, subluxation, dislocation, and deformity. The treatment and reconstruction of Charcot feet remains a surgical challenge. Most cases exhibit atypical, multidirectional destruction and instability, often combined with major bone loss and ulceration with infection. Multiple treatment strategies can be utilized, including internal, external, or combined fixation techniques. Patients with Charcot arthropathy secondary to diabetes are at increased risk of infection, and this heightened risk must be taken into consideration. TTC fusion with a retrograde nail has become a powerful tool for surgeons, as it obviates the need for large incisions needed for classic plating techniques, which are fraught with wound healing complications in these patients with Charcot arthropathy secondary to diabetes.

*Illgner et al. performed a retrograde study of 282 patients with ankle Charcot arthropathy that underwent reconstruction with external fixation.* **They reported** that patient activity improved significantly according to the Hoffer activity score, and that the most common minor complication was persistent or recurrent ulceration (23%). Secondary amputation (after the failure of external fixation) was required in only 12 patients (6.2%). **They concluded** that reconstructive surgery of Charcot feet using external fixation is a safe and economically feasible procedure.

*Pinzur performed a prospective study of 26 consecutive diabetic adults who had operative correction of nonplantigrade Charcot midfoot deformity using a three-level ring external fixator.* **He reported** that at a minimum 1-year follow-up, 24 of 26 patients were ulcer and infection free and able to ambulate with commercially available depth-inlay shoes and custom accommodative foot orthoses. **He concluded** morbidly obese diabetic individuals with multiple co-morbidities complicating severe Charcot foot deformity can achieve correction of midfoot deformity after operative correction of the deformity and maintenance of that correction with a neutrally applied ring external fixator.

*Yammine and Assi performed a meta-analysis comparing retrograde intramedullary nailing (IMN) and external fixation (EF) for ankle Charcot arthropathy.* **They reported** that IMN was found to yield double the fusion rate than EF with 5 weeks less time to heal and that while no significance was noted for revision surgery and amputation rates, EF yielded higher rates of hardware infection and wound infection. **They concluded** that the IMN method seems to offer a quicker and better fusion rate when compared to EF with significantly lower postoperative infection rates.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A tibiotalar fusion with anterior plating would be best utilized in patients without an increased risk for wound healing complications, such as this patient with Charcot arthropathy secondary to diabetes. Furthermore, this would not address the subtalar joint.
Answer 2: This should be reserved as a salvage procedure and would not be appropriate at this time.
Answer 3: Total contact casting is a technique used to address ulcers in patients with Charcot arthropathy. There is no evidence of ulceration in this patient.
Answer 4: This patient has failed orthotics and is indicated for operative intervention.
