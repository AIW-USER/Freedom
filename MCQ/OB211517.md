## Question

A 57-year-old woman presents 2 years after undergoing bunion correction of her left foot with the inability to properly fit in her shoes in the last 4 months, despite shoe modification. On examination, she has severe pain and stiffness of her great toe, with crepitation. Her clinical image is depicted in Figure A and her radiograph is depicted in Figure B. ## Options

1. Deformity correction and first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) fusion
2. Dorsal cheilectomy
3. Keller resection arthroplasty
4. Moberg osteotomy
5. Lapidus fusion

## Correct option

**1. Deformity correction and first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) fusion**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent hallux valgus deformity correction and unfortunately is now presenting with a hallux varus deformity, which is a rare but known complication. In addition, she has arthritic changes in her first MTP joint, necessitating deformity correction and first MTPJ fusion to allow her to have a painless and shoeable foot.

The risk factors for the development of hallux varus after hallux valgus correction include fibular sesamoid removal, detachment of adductor hallucis, intermetatarsal angle overcorrection, excessive resection of the medial eminence, excessive postoperative bandaging, and degenerative changes of the 1st MTP joint. For treatment, taping of the hallux into a valgus position may correct the deformity if recognized early and the MTPJ remains flexible. Osteotomies and/or ligamentoplasty with tendon transfers may be considered for patients who fail taping but remain flexible. When the deformity becomes more rigid, and particularly in the setting of MTP arthritic changes, a fusion of the MTP joint is indicated.

*Perera et al. reviewed the pathogenesis of hallux valgus.* **They reported** that since there are no ligamentous structures binding the distal first and second metatarsals, the first tarsometatarsal joint can be affected by several factors, including pes planus, a long hallux, or functional equinus. **They highlighted** that the hallux valgus foot results from both innate and acquired biomechanical abnormalities, and pathogenesis is often multifactorial.

*Devos et al. reviewed the management of hallux varus.* **They reported** that the condition may be congenital, acquired, or iatrogenic, and highlighted that the deformity is due to an imbalance between osseous, tendonous, and capsuloligamentous structures of the first MTP joint. **They emphasized** that the treatment goals are to obtain a functional, painless, shoeable foot.

Figure A demonstrates a patient with a hallux varus foot deformity. Figure B is a radiograph depicting hallux varus with a medial deviation of the proximal phalanx in the setting of first MTPJ degenerative changes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: This patient has a significant hallux varus deformity with first MTPJ arthritis. A cheilectomy alone would not correct the deformity.
Answer 3: A Keller resection arthroplasty may be considered in the low demand patient with significant joint degeneration and loss of motion, but not in this patient with significant hallux varus.
Answer 4: A Moberg osteotomy would not address the hallux varus deformity and first MTPJ arthritis.
Answer 5: A Lapidus procedure would not address the hallux varus deformity and first MTPJ arthritis.
