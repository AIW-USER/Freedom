## Question

A 72-year-old female presents to clinic for follow up of her right flatfoot deformity. She states her foot deformity has actually worsened and become "more stiff". She describes most of her new pain in the lateral hindfoot while standing or walking. On exam, she has significant pes planovalgus with forefoot abduction. Hindfoot and forefoot alignment are unable to be passively corrected to neutral. She is unable to perform a single-leg heel rise. She has full, painless ankle ROM without coronal plane instability. Despite her continued symptoms, she does not wish to consider surgical intervention at this time. Figures A and B are weight-bearing radiographs of the right foot. She is an avid walker and wants to continue to exercise daily. Which nonoperative treatment option would be best for this patient?

## Options

1. Short articulated ankle-foot-orthosis (AFO)
2. Short leg cast with non-weightbearing
3. Removable CAM boot and therapy with PTT strengthening and achilles stretching
4. University of California Berkley Laboratories (UCBL) orthosis with medial heel wedge
5. Arizona brace

## Correct option

**1. Short articulated ankle-foot-orthosis (AFO)**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with progressive, rigid flatfoot deformity, likely as a result of posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI). When considering her rigid deformity, selection of the proper orthosis should provide adequate support that accommodates her deformity while maintaining motion of her stable ankle joint. Therefore, the best option would be a short articulated AFO.

In stage III PTTI, patients present clinically with significant pes planovalgus deformity. Deformities include hindfoot valgus, forefoot supination, and forefoot abduction. Additionally, more severe lateral sinus tarsi pain may be present from subfibular impingement of the calcaneus or talus with the tip of the fibula. Bracing and orthoses are essential components of nonsurgical treatment for progressive flatfoot deformity. Depending on deformity severity, a variety of orthotic options exist (e.g. AFO, UCBL, Arizona brace). Although there are no comparative studies at this time, it is common practice to utilize articulating AFO, rather than rigid braces such as the Arizona brace, in patients who are active and want to maintain motion.

Stein and Shon discussed the current concepts and evolution of adult posterior tibial tendon dysfunction pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment. They discuss the importance of early surgical intervention to correct flatfoot deformities with joint-sparing procedures to prevent disease progression. For example, they discuss the utility of calcaneal osteotomy to correct hindfoot valgus to prevent the development of rigid deformity.

Deland provided a comprehensive review of adult acquired flatfoot deformity. He discussed conservative and surgical treatment options for all 4 stages of deformity. He concludes that surgical intervention can be considered in all 4 stages of deformity to achieve proper foot alignment and maintain flexibility to optimize long-term patient outcomes.

*Tankson reviewed the indications and surgical technique of the Cotton osteotomy for treatment of forefoot varus deformity in stage II flatfoot deformity.* *He discussed the importance of identifying forefoot varus and medial column instability as a contributor to arch collapse, which may involve the 1st TMT joint or NC joint.* **He concluded** that if instability or arthritis is present, medial column arthrodesis should be considered. If no instability or arthritis is noted, persistent forefoot varus may be addressed by a Cotton osteotomy (opening wedge medial cuneiform osteotomy) to plantarflex the first ray.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Casting is an option during the early stages of flatfoot deformity to provide posterior tibial tendon "rest", thus alleviating inflammation. Casting is less likely to benefit this patient with rigid deformity and a non-functional PTT.
Answer 3: A CAM boot may provide some support to the foot and is appropriate in the early stages of PTTD. However, it would immobilize the ankle joint which is normal in this patient, and would be unlikely to provide sufficient support to the rigid deformity. Stretching the heel cord would provide marginal benefit for patients with rigid deformity.
Answer 4: UCBL orthosis with medial heel wedge would be an appropriate option for stage I or II PTTD, as it is less cumbersome than bracing and can correct flexible hindfoot valgus. It is not appropriate for the patient with rigid flatfoot deformity.
Answer 5: An Arizona brace is a potential non-surgical option for advanced flatfoot deformity. Its firm, leather lace-up design crosses the ankle joint and can provide stability to the medial arch. However, the Arizona brace significantly limits ankle motion, thus affecting gait. An articulated AFO would provide similar support without restricting ankle motion.
