## Question

A 58 years old post-menopausal female had a minor fall at home and sustained a compression fracture in D12 vertebra. Since mode of injury was a minor fall, DEXA scan was done and she was started on Bisphosphonate therapy after her BMD recorded was in osteoporotic range on DEXA Scan by the treating doctor. The fracture was managed conservatively and in about 3 months the pain subsided completely. She has now returned for 1 year follow up. On enquiring drug intake history, she says she took bisphosphonates only on an intermittent basis as she didn’t feel the need owing to the fact, she had no symptoms. Which of the following tools will be of least use in assessing her response to the drug she has taken and knowing regarding the adequacy of treatment she has taken?

## Options

1. Bone turnover markers
2. FRAX tool
3. DEXA Scan
4. Quantitative CT scan

## Correct option

**2. FRAX tool**

## Answer explanation

Ans: B

Osteoporosis is the most common metabolic bone disorder characterized by a structural deterioration of bone tissue leading to an increased risk of fracture

FRAX Tool (Fracture Risk Assessment) is an openly available fracture risk calculator (developed by University of Sheffield, UK) and can be used to estimate the probability of an individual sustaining an osteoporotic fracture over the next 10 years. It is based on assessment of some important clinical risk factors and BMD (T-score) at the femoral neck. The clinical risk factors include seven dichotomous clinical factors viz. prior fragility fracture, parental hip fracture, smoking, systemic glucocorticoid use, excess alcohol intake, rheumatoid arthritis, and other causes of secondary osteoporosis apart from age and sex. However, it does not incorporate all clinical risk factors such as those associated with falls, markers of bone turnover, region-specific risk entities such as lower dietary calcium intake and Vitamin D deficiency.

Conventionally DEXA (BMD measurement) has been used to predict fracture risk. But only 50% of 50% of people who sustain osteoporotic fracture tend to have BMD above the WHO definition of osteoporosis (T score < -2.5 SD) and hence the FRAX tool was developed to better predict risk of osteoporotic fracture over a period of 10 years. However, its direct use in assessing adequacy of osteoporotic treatment being taken is not well established.

Regarding monitoring of patients being treated for osteoporosis, so far dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry has been recognized as the tool to monitor osteoporosis therapy by most clinical practice guidelines. A quantitative CT scan can also very reliably pick up the mineral content in the bone, but owing to cost and radiation issues is practically less used.

Now, recently, the duration of osteoporosis therapy has become a hot research question with the possibility of having an optimal drug holiday to prevent bisphosphonate induced atypical fractures. Hence, the role of Bone turnover markers is being increasingly investigated.

BONE TURNOVER MARKERS (BTMs)

Bone is a metabolically active structure which undergoes continuous remodeling throughout life. After attaining peak bone mass (at 30 years of age), bone undergoes constant remodeling through bone resorption followed by formation sequentially at the basic multicellular unit of bone called “Bone remodeling unit.” Under optimal physiological conditions, bone resorption takes place in around 10 days and bone formation takes about 3 months. Up to 20% of the skeleton may be replaced by remodeling every year.

Now, various biomolecules released into the circulation during bone resorption and formation. These are called BTMs. These molecular markers of bone metabolism are novel tools which detect the dynamics of bone remodeling with respect to bone formation and resorption. Their wider availability, cost-effectiveness, and the fact that they are sensitive, and specific assays for bone turnover make them useful tools, especially in the follow-up of the patients who had been on antiresorptive or bone formation therapies.

The BTMs are grouped into two categories based on the metabolic phase during which they are produced as:

A). Bone formation markers (these are products of active osteoblasts expressed in serum/ plasma during different phases of their development)

Bone formation markers are categorized as:

1. By-products of collagen synthesis: Propeptides of type 1 collagen: (C-terminal: P1CP, N-terminal: P1NP)

2. Osteoblast enzymes: Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (total and bone-specific)

3. Matrix proteins: Osteocalcin (OC).

B). Bone resorption markers (these are by-products of osteoclastic activity released during bone resorption phase into plasma/ serum)

The bone resorption markers are categorized as follows:

- Collagen degradation products:

• Telopeptides of type 1 collagen (C-terminal: CTX-1 and CTX-matrix metalloproteinases [MMP], N-terminal: NTX-1)

• Hydroxyproline

• Pyridinium crosslinks (pyridinoline [PYD], deoxypyridinoline [DPD])

- Non-collagenous proteins:

• Bone sialoprotein

- Osteoclastic enzymes:

• Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase

• Cathepsin K

- Osteocyte activity markers:

• Receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)

• Osteoprotegerin (OPG)

• Dickkopf-related protein 1

• Sclerostin

USES of BTMs:

- Assessment of Fracture Risk

Bone turnover markers increase in proportion to fracture risk, independent of bone mineral density (BMD). Increased bone turnover (high values of BTMs) leads to the deterioration of bone microarchitecture and thus contributes to increased risk of fracture. However, this correlation is not absolute in individuals and other causes that include high turnover states such as hyperparathyroidism or hyperthyroidism, Paget disease, malignancy, or advanced renal failure must be kept in mind.

BTMs best serve as complementary tool to BMD which can only assess the bone mass in fracture risk assessment and cannot alone calculate risk.

- Monitoring effect of Osteoporosis Treatment

Monitoring compliance and adherence to treatment represent a potentially useful application of BTMs, particularly in the case of bone resorption inhibitors. Bone resorption markers typically fall by over 40% within 3 months of starting bisphosphonate therapy. This is followed by a reduction in bone formation markers over the next 6–12 months. Denosumab produces a very rapid fall in resorption markers, which remain suppressed, with a slower fall in formation markers. Hence, if the values indicate bone turnover rate is low, it can be assumed that the osteoporotic therapy is effective.

International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) and International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine has proposed serum CTX-1 (sCTX) and serum P1NP to be used as reference markers of bone resorption and formation, respectively, for the assessment of fracture risk and monitoring therapy in clinical settings.
