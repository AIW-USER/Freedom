## Question

A 57-year-old male presents to the hand clinic with a contracture of the right ring finger. He reports having been treated six years ago for Dupuytren’s disease with a needle aponeurotomy, but the contracture has recurred over the past year. On examination, he demonstrates a 35° flexion contracture at the metacarpophalangeal joint, a 40° contracture of the proximal interphalangeal joint, and a palpable Dupuytren cord. After discussing treatment options, the patient agrees to undergo the procedure with the lowest risk of recurrence. What is the most common complication following this procedure?

## Options

1. Wound dehischence and skin necrosis
2. Hematoma
3. Bowstringing
4. Neurovascular Injury
5. Infection

## Correct option

**1. Wound dehischence and skin necrosis**

## Answer explanation

The patient has flexion contractures of the right ring finger at the PIP and MCP joints due to Dupuytren’s disease. The intervention with the lowest risk of recurrence is a partial fasciectomy, which has a 5-year recurrence rate of 20%. However, like all treatments for Dupuytren's disease, there is a high complication rate, with the most common being wound complications, specifically skin tearing/wound dehiscence and skin necrosis, in 23% of patients.

Dupuytren’s disease is a benign fibroproliferative disorder that affects the fascia of the palms and fingers, leading to fascial nodules and cords, which cause flexion contractures of the digits. Symptoms primarily guide treatment. In mild cases where only fascial nodules are present without functional impairment, observation may suffice, or corticosteroid injections may be used for painful nodules. The most common treatments for more advanced cases are collagenase clostridium histolyticum injection (CCH), percutaneous needle aponeurotomy, and partial or radical palmar fasciectomy. Needle aponeurotomy and CCH injections have higher recurrence rates at 5 years (50% and 35%, respectively) compared to fasciectomy (20%). However, fasciectomy is associated with a high risk of wound complications and flap failure, along with a longer recovery time compared with aponeurotomy and collagenase injections. Wound complications following intervention for Dupyutren's disease include skin tearing, wound dehiscence, and skin necrosis.

*Boe et al. conducted a systematic review in 2021 on the treatment and cost-effectiveness of Dupuytren contractures.* **They found** that the most common complications following interventions included skin tears after needle aponeurotomy (16%), extremity pain after collagenase injection (49%), and wound complications after fasciectomy. **The authors concluded** that while treatment choices should be guided by disease severity and the patient’s tolerance for complications, the literature generally supports fasciectomy due to its more favorable outcomes compared to aponeurotomy and collagenase injections.

*Denkler et al. reviewed the literature in 2010 to investigate complications of fasciectomy for Dupuytren’s disease.* **They reported** major complications in 15.7% of cases, including a 5% incidence of digital artery and nerve injuries, and wound complications (dehiscence, skin necrosis, and skin tearing) in 22.9%. **They concluded** that while fasciectomy is an effective treatment, wound complications are common, and care must be taken to protect the digital arteries and nerves, particularly in revision surgeries.

*Rissen et al. analyzed 5-year follow-up data from a randomized controlled trial comparing percutaneous needle fasciotomy and limited fasciectomy for Dupuytren’s disease.* *Their study included 115 hands in 111 patients and found a 5-year recurrence rate of approximately 85% in the needle fasciotomy group, compared to only 20% in the limited fasciectomy group.* Interestingly, 53% of patients preferred needle fasciotomy despite its higher recurrence rate. **The authors concluded** that the risk of recurrence is significantly lower after limited fasciectomy compared to needle fasciotomy.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Hematomas can occur following treatment for Dupuytren’s disease, especially after fasciectomy, but their overall incidence is low.
Answer 3: Bowstringing is a complication more commonly seen after trigger finger release due to release of both the A1 and A2 pulley.
Answer 4: The risk of neurovascular injury is reported to be 2-3%, which is lower than the risk of wound complications following fasciectomy.
Answer 5: The infection rate after fasciectomy is about 7%, approximately 1% after needle aponeurotomy, and 0% after collagenase injection.
