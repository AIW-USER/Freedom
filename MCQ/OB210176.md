## Question

A 2-year-old female presents to the ED with refusal to bear weight on her left leg. She is accompanied by her mother, who states that she went down the slide with her daughter in her lap two days prior and feels she may have twisted her leg. She is afebrile and non-toxic appearing. Her physical exam is benign other than slight ecchymosis overlying her left proximal tibia and tenderness to palpation in that region. Due to the delayed presentation, the ED staff obtained a skeletal survey, with selected images shown in Figures A-F. Orthopedics is consulted based on the findings. What is the next step in management?

## Options

1. Apply a long leg cast, arrange for the grandparents to take the child home, and call CPS to initiate an investigation
2. Obtain a CBC, ESR, and CRP
3. Apply a long leg cast only, discharge home
4. Apply a long leg cast and admit to pediatrics for non-accidental trauma work-up
5. Obtain Vitamin D levels

## Correct option

**4. Apply a long leg cast and admit to pediatrics for non-accidental trauma work-up**

## Answer explanation

This child has a non-displaced left proximal tibia fracture (toddler's fracture) and a left proximal humerus metaphyseal corner fracture. A toddler's fracture alone is not necessarily indicative of non-accidental trauma (NAT), however, the presence of this injury and the corner fracture requires hospital admission and complete NAT work-up.

As one-third of abused children are evaluated by an orthopedic surgeon, it is imperative to recognize the signs and symptoms of abuse and initiate a NAT work-up when warranted. Toddler's fractures, or spiral tibia fractures, are often seen in ambulatory children (9 months to 3 years of age) as a result of twisting injuries following minor trauma. In contrast, physeal fractures are associated with shear forces rather than bending or tension, raising the suspicion for NAT in young children. Along with corner and bucket handle fractures, long bone fractures in children less than 2 and humerus fractures in pediatric patients should also raise concern for NAT. Physicians are mandatory reporters of suspected child abuse in all 50 states, and failure to report may result in disciplinary action.

*Baldwin et al. provide a review of orthopedic issues in child abuse.* **They emphasize** that no specific fracture is pathognomonic of NAT, but suspicious injuries include posterior rib fractures, metaphyseal corner fractures, and fractures in various stages of healing. **They also emphasize** the importance of knowing injuries associated with NAT beyond fractures, which are the second most common injuries seen in NAT following soft tissue trauma.

*Pandya et al. specifically look at humerus fractures in pediatrics and propose an algorithm to identify abuse versus accidental trauma.* *They performed a retrospective review of their institutional database and identified 39 humerus fractures attributed to NAT and compared them to 95 humerus fractures attributed to accidental trauma.* **They found** that non-accidental trauma was correlated with age above 18 months, evidence of prior injury, and suspicious injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1 and 3: A long leg cast or splint should be applied for the toddler's fracture. However, the child needs to be admitted to the hospital for NAT work-up due to the concomitant metaphyseal corner fracture.
Answer 2: There is no reason to obtain labs at this time without suspicion of infection in an afebrile, non-toxic appearing patient.
Answer 5: Vitamin D labs may be obtained at a later time if needed, but are not of immediate concern in this scenario.
