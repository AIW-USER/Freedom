## Options

1. Decreased serum calcium
2. Decreased urinary N-telopoptide
3. Decreased urinary collagen cross-links
4. Elevated serum alkaline phosphatase
5. Elevated serum calcium

## Correct option

**4. Elevated serum alkaline phosphatase**

## Answer explanation

This patient has radiographic and histologic findings consistent with Paget's disease, an idiopathic condition of abnormal bone remodeling. Secondary to excessive bone resorption and disorganized new bone formation, Paget's disease is associated with elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Answer 4).

Paget's disease typically presents in patients over the age of 40 with asymptomatic lesions discovered incidentally on radiographs, often during the work-up of an unrelated musculoskeletal complaint. The incidence of Paget's peaks in the 5th decade of life and occurs most commonly in Caucasians. It affects males and females equally and may be monostotic or polyostotic. Commonly affected sites include the femur, pelvis, tibia, skull, and spine.

The underlying pathophysiologic mediator of Paget's disease of increased osteoclastic bony resorption. While the exact cause of this derangement is unknown, many hypothesize that it may occur secondary to a slow virus infection. Most cases are spontaneous, although some familial clusters have been identified. The laboratory findings in Paget's disease are representative of increased bony resorption and disorganized bone formation and include:
-Elevated serum ALP
-Elevated urinary collagen cross-links
-Elevated urinary hydroxyproline (a collagen breakdown marker)
-Increased urinary N-telopeptide, alpha-C-telopeptide and deoxypyridinoline
-Normal calcium levels

The treatment of Paget's disease varies. Asymptomatic Paget's disease is observed, while symptomatic Paget's disease is managed with medical therapy aimed at osteoclast inhibition (i.e., bisphosphonates or calcitonin). The prognosis for Paget's disease is favorable, although a small subset of patients (<1%) can develop Paget's sarcoma. These sarcomas are most often osteosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, or chondrosarcoma and, when metastatic, have a five-year survival rate of <10%.

*Singer et al. provide clinical practice guidelines for managing Paget's disease of bone.* **The authors recommend** plain radiographs as a first-line evaluation in patients with suspected Paget's disease. **The authors also note** that measurement of serum ALP is helpful not only to establish a baseline marker of bone turnover but also to assess response to treatment. **The authors recommend** treatment with bisphosphonates for most patients, as these medications can help prevent or slow the progress of complications associated with Paget's disease (i.e., hearing loss, osteoarthritis, spinal cord manifestations).

Frederick Singer provides an expert opinion on best practice guidelines for evaluating and treating Paget's disease of bone. He notes that a family history is present in 15% of patients and that many patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally following routine laboratory studies (ALP) or radiographic workups for other patient concerns. He states that the single most effective therapy in the management of Paget's disease is a single 5mg IV infusion of zoledronic acid, which can normalize ALP levels for up to 6.5 years.
