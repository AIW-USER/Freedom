## Question

A 54-year-old male underwent the surgery depicted in Figure A several years ago. He has had progressively worsening pain. ESR and CRP are obtained and are not elevated. Serum cobalt and chromium levels are 29 and 40 µg/L, respectively. The decision is made to proceed with surgery. Intraoperatively, a pseudotumor is found. Additionally, the abductors are found to be intact. What is the most appropriate surgery for this patient?

![[q220045-a-bhr1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Irrigation and debridement and antibiotic spacer placement
2. Polyethylene liner exchange
3. Revision to a total hip arthroplasty with a metal-on-metal bearing surface
4. Revision to a constrained liner
5. Revision of the femoral component

## Correct option

**5. Revision of the femoral component**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a painful metal-on-metal hip resurfacing implant with elevated serum metal ions and a pseudotumor. The acetabular component appears to be in a reasonable position. The most appropriate surgery for this patient is a revision of the femoral component to a femoral stem, and placement of a non-metal-on-metal bearing surface (e.g. ceramic on polyethylene).

Hip resurfacing arthroplasty is a bone-preserving procedure that is favorable in young male patients and utilizes metal-on-metal articulations. Metallosis is, therefore, a concerning complication of these implants and can result in pseudotumor formation and subsequent destruction of the hip abductors. Patients presenting with a painful prosthesis should be screened with metal ion levels as well as a metal artifact reduction sequence MRI. The treatment for this condition is to revise to a total hip arthroplasty with a non-metal-on-metal bearing surface

*Kwon et al. performed a retrieval study in which they compared implants revised for pseudotumors to those revised for other reasons of failure.* **They found** that the pseudotumor group had a higher rate of linear wear of the femoral component compared to the control group (8.1 microm/year vs 1.179 microm/year, p = 0.002), and acetabular component (7.36 microm/year vs 1.28 microm/year, p = 0.001). Wear of the acetabular component always involved the edge of the implant. **They concluded** that pseudotumor is associated with increased wear at the metal-on-metal articulation.

*Bisschop et al. performed a prospective cohort study of 149 hip resurfacing patients.* **They found** that 5.6% of patients required a revision due to a symptomatic pseudotumor. **They found** 28% of patients had a pseudotumor on CT, with 28% of these patients having complaints of groin pain, a mass, or paresthesia.

Figure A demonstrates a metal-on-metal hip resurfacing implant.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: There is low concern for infection in this case.
Answer 2: The hip resurfacing implant in Figure A has a metal-on-metal bearing surface.
Answer 3: Maintaining a metal-on-metal bearing surface in the presence of metallosis and a pseudotumor would not be appropriate.
Answer 4: The patient does not have instability and their abductors are intact. It is very unlikely they would require a constrained liner.
