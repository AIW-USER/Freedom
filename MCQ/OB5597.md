## Options

1. Unicameral bone cyst
2. Aneurysmal bone cyst
3. Fibrous dysplasia
4. Osteofibrous dysplasia
5. Eosinophilic granuloma

## Correct option

**1. Unicameral bone cyst**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with a unicameral bone cyst (UBC).

UBCs are unilocular bone cysts filled with sero-sanguineous fluid. They usually occur males in the 1st decade of life (3-14 years, mean 9 years). The most common locations are proximal humerus > proximal femur > proximal tibia. These expansile lesions begin in the central metaphysis and extend into the diaphysis. The cortex is thinned. The cyst wall is composed of fibrous tissue and collagen fibers, and no cells.

*Pretell-Mazzini et al. reviewed UBCs.* **They recommend** nonsurgical treatment in incidental, asymptomatic lesions with no decrease in bone strength. Surgery is indicated with obvious/suspected pathological fracture, cyst enlargement, and locations with risk for fracture (proximal femur). Risk for fracture includes transverse diameter >85% that of affected bone, cyst wall <0.5mm thick. Age is a predictor of successful treatment as patients > 10 years heal at a higher rate (90%) than younger patients (60%) regardless of which treatment regimen is used.

*Hou et al. compared treatment of UBC in 40 patients.* *Patients treated with minimally-invasive curettage, ethanol, calcium sulfate grafting and cannulated screw placement (Group 4) had highest rate of radiographic healing and shortest time to union, compared with percutaneous serial steroid and bone-marrow injection (Group 1), open curettage and calcium sulfate grafting without instrumentation (Group 2), and with instrumentation (Group 3).*

Figure A is an AP pelvis radiograph showing a latent bone cyst of the proximal femur with moderate thinning of the cortex. Figure B is a coronal T1-weighted pre- and post-contrast TSE MRI showing low signal features of UBC of the proximal femur. Figure C is a coronal gadolinium contrast T1-weighted MRI showing signal voids in the cystic portion of the lesion with peripheral rim enhancement, characteristic of a UBC. Figure D is a coronal T2-weighted TIRM MRI showing high signal characteristics of the cystic portion of the unicameral bone cyst. Figure E is a low power image showing the histology of a UBC. The inner layer of the cyst consists of a thin layer of connective tissue. Hemosiderin is noted. Illustration A shows the Dormans and Pill classification of pathological fractures associated with UBC of the proximal femur. Management depends on (1) whether the physis is open, (2) integrity of the lateral buttress, (3) bone stock between the physis and cyst (for types IA though IIB)

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: ABCs are multiloculated, eccentric expansile lesions with transverse diameter wider than the physis. MRI will show double density fluid levels and septations. Cyst walls have plump uniform fibroblasts and multinucleated giant cells and thin bone trabeculae.
Answer 3: Fibrous dysplasia can be mono- or polyostotic. The latter affect the femur, pelvis and tibia. Radiographs show a geographic lesion with ground glass appearance and a rim of sclerotic bone. Histology shows fibrous tissue around trabeculae ("Greek letters/Chinese letters/alphabet soup").
Answer 4: Osteofibrous dysplasia usually involves the anterior mid-shaft of the tibia. Radiographs show bubbly, multiloculated intra-cortical lucency and surrounding sclerosis. Histology shows woven bone rimmed by osteoclasts and osteoblasts.
Answer 5: Eosinophilic granulomas are lytic on radiographs. Histology shows round Langerhans histiocyte-like cells with "coffee bean" nuclei and nuclear grooves. Electron microscopy shows tennis-racket shaped Birbeck granules.
