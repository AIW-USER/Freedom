## Answer explanation

The term Pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) was coined by Jaffe et al. who described it as a disease affecting joints, tendon sheath and bursae.

is a rare benign disease of the synovial membrane which is characterised by hypervascular neoplastic proliferation of the synovium with deposition of macrophages, multinucleated giant cells and hemosiderin. Haemosiderin deposition gives the typical brown appearance of the synovium seen at arthroscopy (See Fig).

TYPES

Depending on the extent of synovial involvement two growth patterns of PVNS have been described, a localised form affecting a specific region of the joint and a diffuse form which affects a large portion of the synovial membrane. Usually, it is a monoarticular disease of people in their third and fourth decade. The knee joint is the most commonly involved joint followed by hip and ankle joints. A yearly incidence of 1.8 cases per million population with arthroscopic incidence of one in 250 cases has been reported

ETIOLOGY

The aetiology of this disease is uncertain but the presence of aneuploidy and trisomy 7 in many diseased synovial cells indicates a neoplastic origin. Centrifugal growth patterns and ability to erode surroundings and DNA flow cytometry also support this view. Around 20 cases of malignant transformation and metastases have been reported so far in the English literature. Extensive involvement of surrounding structures including muscles, bone and skin and a multifocal variety involving several joints have also been reported. Perka et al. have suggested localised PVNS as reactive granuloma and generalised PVNS as neoplastic disease.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Patients usually present with swelling and decreased range of motion. A mechanical symptom like locking and catching mimicking a meniscal tear are common in the localised variety. Pain is not usually a complaint. Patients may not seek treatment for years due to vague symptoms.

IMAGING

X-ray of the knee frequently shows features of advanced osteoarthritis with soft tissue swelling about the joint. Erosion and subchondral cysts are more common in PVNS of hip and ankle joints in comparison to the more spacious knee joint. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is helpful in differentiating PVNS from other synovial diseases. On MRI it is typically seen as a synovial mass that displays low signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted pulse sequences. Foci of brighter T1 and T2 signals indicate a relatively low concentration of hemosiderin.

HISTOPATHOLOGY

The diagnosis of PVNS is confirmed by histopathology. On the basis of common histopathological features of the localised nodular and diffuse varieties, Rao and Vigorita have proposed a common histogenesis. However, invasiveness, aneuploidy, high recurrence rate, chromosomal abnormalities, malignant transformation and metastases suggest generalised PVNS as a distinct variety.

TREATMENT

Many treatment modalities have been tried with variable success rates for treatment of PVNS including arthroscopic and open excision, synovectomy and arthroplasty, low-dose external beam radiotherapy and imatinib therapy (tyrosine kinase inhibitor used in leukaemia) etc. Long-term side effects of radiotherapy for soft tissue tumours including the secondary cancer are well known. Hence, Synovectomy is the mainstay of the treatment. External beam radiotherapy or intra-articular yttrium-90 is usually combined with surgical excision to prevent recurrences. Many authors have proved the efficacy of arthroscopic synovectomy in both localised and diffuse PVNS.

To reduce the recurrence rate, excision of diseased synovium from the posterior compartment is important. A routine posteromedial portal should be made to address it. Synovial shavers and radiofrequency ablation are very useful in removal of diseased synovium. The main benefit of arthroscopic synovectomy over open synovectomy is low post-operative morbidity. Open synovectomy produces pain and stiffness.
