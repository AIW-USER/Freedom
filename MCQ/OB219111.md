## Question

A 56-year-old male presents to the ambulatory surgical center for treatment of his posterior tibial tendon insufficiency recalcitrant to nonoperative means. He subsequently undergoes a flexor digitorum longus transfer, medializing calcaneal osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, and gastrocnemius lengthening. Which of the following exam findings correlates with the aforementioned treatment?

## Options

1. Flexible hindfoot valgus deformity, gastrocnemius contracture
2. Flexible forefoot abduction and hindfoot valgus deformities, gastrocnemius contracture
3. Isolated posterior tibial tendon tenderness
4. Rigid forefoot and hindfoot valgus deformities, gastrocnemius contracture
5. Rigid forefoot and hindfoot valgus deformities, tibiotalar arthritis

## Correct option

**2. Flexible forefoot abduction and hindfoot valgus deformities, gastrocnemius contracture**

## Answer explanation

This 56-year-old undergoes a flexor digitorum longus (FDL) transfer, medializing calcaneal osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, and gastrocnemius lengthening, which would correlate with a flexible hindfoot and forefoot with gastrocnemius contracture (Stage 2, Answer 2).

Posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) typically follows a predictable pathological sequence. Pain stemming from tenosynovitis generally first occurs, and then flexible hindfoot valgus and forefoot abduction deformities follow, which persist until becoming rigid. For flexible deformities, exam findings dictate treatment, which often necessitates a combination of tendon transfers (FDL vs. flexor hallucis longus to navicular) and osteotomies (calcaneal osteotomy [medial displacement vs. lateral column opening osteotomies], plantarflexion medial cuneiform osteotomies). While rigid deformities typically require a hindfoot fusion (isolated vs. triple vs. tibiotalocalcaneal fusions).

*Myerson et al. performed a retrospective review examining the radiographic follow-up of 18 patients who underwent FDL transfer and medializing calcaneal osteotomy for flexible pes planovalgus.* Radiographs demonstrated a reduction in the magnitude of deformity, which included talar-first metatarsal and talonavicular coverage angles on the anteroposterior weight-bearing radiographs, as well as talar-first metatarsal angle and the distance from the medial cuneiform to the floor on lateral weight-bearing films. **The authors conclude** that bony reconstruction with the calcaneal osteotomy offsets the weaker dynamic forces of the FDL following transfer.

*Moseir-LaClair et al. performed a retrospective review of 26 patients (with an average five-year follow-up) with Stage II PTTI treated with flexor digitorum longus tendon transfer, lateral column lengthening, medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy, and heel cord lengthening.* **They reported** a mean postoperative ankle-hindfoot score of 90 and no nonunions. Four patients (14%) displayed radiographic signs of calcaneocuboid arthritis at follow-up, with only one that was symptomatic and required calcaneocuboid joint fusion. **The authors conclude** that the above treatment is appropriate for treating Stage II PTTI with lasting results.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This constellation of examination findings would warrant a calcaneal medial displacement osteotomy with gastrocnemius lengthening
Answer 3: Isolated posterior tibial tendonitis would warrant tenosynovectomy only after immobilization fails
Answer 4: This presentation would warrant solely bony operations with likely triple arthrodesis and gastrocnemius lengthening
Answer 5: The presence of tibiotalar arthritis would require a tibiotalar fusion at a minimum, if not in association with a triple arthrodesis
