## Question

A 61-year-old active male presents for evaluation of progressive hand weakness over the past month without antecedent trauma. They note symptoms have begun to limit them during exercise, noting difficulty with grasping handles of exercise machines. Sensory exam demonstrates two-point discrimination of 4-5 mm along the volar aspect of the distal digits. Motor exam reveals normal thumb extension, weakness with thumb interphalangeal joint flexion, and normal index finger metacarpophalangeal joint abduction strength. MRI of the upper extremity is obtained without any notable abnormalities. You diagnose them with a compressive neuropathy of a peripheral nerve. What is the anticipated disease progression for this patient’s most likely pathology?

## Options

1. They will ultimately require corticosteroid injections to alleviate symptoms
2. They will have progressive weakness necessitating tendon transfers
3. They will have persistent symptoms for under two years before spontaneous resolution
4. They will have persistent symptoms necessitating surgical decompression
5. They will develop sensory abnormalities requiring nerve transfer

## Correct option

**3. They will have persistent symptoms for under two years before spontaneous resolution**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s physical exam demonstrated motor weakness of the flexor pollicis longus (FPL) without sensory abnormalities, consistent with anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) compressive neuropathy. The vast majority of cases of AIN neuropathy resolve with conservative management with observation (Answer 3).

AIN compressive neuropathy, also known as Kiloh-Nevin's syndrome, is a relatively rare condition affecting the forearm, characterized by motor deficits without sensory involvement. Patients typically present with weakness in flexion of the thumb, index, and middle fingers, leading to an inability to make the "OK" sign. The presence of antecedent forearm pain is variable and often lasts 2–3 weeks when present. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, relying on a careful neurological examination, although imaging and electrodiagnostic studies can help rule out other conditions. The primary management for most patients is non-operative (observation and rest), as symptoms often improve over a period of 3 to 12 months, although some cases may take up to 18 months to fully recover. Surgical decompression may be considered if conservative treatment fails or if a space-occupying lesion is present.

*Miller-Breslow et al. retrospectively reviewed 10 cases of spontaneous AIN palsy, focusing on the nonoperative management of the condition.* **They found** that 8 patients treated conservatively showed signs of recovery within 6 months and full recovery within a year, and two surgically explored patients did not demonstrate faster recovery times. **They concluded** that spontaneous, partial AIN paralysis is likely a form of neuritis and can be safely treated without surgery, with the expectation of complete recovery.

*Sood and Burke reviewed 16 patients with AIN palsy in 19 limbs, seen at a single center, with a mean follow-up of 6.4 years.* **They found** a high incidence of incomplete lesions and associated neurological signs in other areas, noting no difference in recovery time between patients treated surgically and those managed conservatively, with good recovery in incomplete lesions regardless of treatment type. **They concluded** that incomplete AIN lesions and those with other neurological signs generally have a good prognosis with conservative management and that surgery should be considered in cases with penetrating injuries, as well as those with complete lesions without recovery after at least 6 months.

*Chi et al. reviewed AIN syndrome, discussing its presentation, diagnosis, and management based on available literature and their experience.* **They found** that AIN palsy often presents with a history of pain followed by motor weakness, particularly affecting the flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus to the index finger and that while electrodiagnostic studies can be helpful, they may also show abnormalities in muscles not clinically affected. **They concluded** that spontaneous recovery is common, especially in incomplete or partial palsies, and that conservative management for at least 6 months is often warranted before considering surgery, although surgical exploration might be considered earlier if a compressive lesion is suspected.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Systemic corticosteroids may be of benefit in the case of inflammatory neuritis, but there is currently no data to support the use of injection for AIN neuropathy.
Answers 2 and 5: Given the large majority of cases of AIN neuropathy spontaneously resolve, tendon transfers and even more so nerve transfers are hardly ever required.
Answer 4: Early surgical decompression may be indicated in cases of AIN neuropathy with evidence of a compressive lesion; however, an MRI of this patient effectively ruled this out. In cases without a compressive lesion, it is more common for patients to experience spontaneous resolution rather than require surgical release.
