## Question

A 25-year-old female athlete presents to your office with chronic right patellar instability. She is here today following a right patella dislocation that occurred yesterday during a recreational soccer game. She notes a history of 5 similar patellar dislocations in the past. Figure A is a plain radiograph obtained in the office today. You order an MRI which demonstrates a medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) tear with asymmetry of the trochlear facets including a steep (cliff pattern) vertical slope and a supratrochlear spur. Based on these findings, what procedure is indicated to reduce residual patellar instability and allow for more anatomic patellar tracking?

![[q220538-a-screenshot-2025-04-10-at-11-04-33-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Arthroscopic primary repair of the MPFL and VMO strengthening postoperatively
2. Open direct repair of the MPFL
3. MPFL reconstruction with trochleoplasty
4. Tibial tubercle osteotomy
5. MPFL reconstruction utilizing a semitendinosus autograft

## Correct option

**3. MPFL reconstruction with trochleoplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with chronic patellar instability secondary to severe (Dejour type D) trochlear dysplasia. Addressing the MPFL tear and severe trochlear dysplasia is important to restore anatomic patellar tracking and reduce continued patellar instability postoperatively, therefore, an MPFL reconstruction with a trochleoplasty is indicated (Answer 3).

Patellar instability is characterized by patellar subluxation or dislocation episodes as a result of injury, ligamentous laxity, or an increased Q-angle of the knee. This most commonly occurs in the 2nd or 3rd decade of life with general and anatomical risk factors associated. General risk factors include a previous patella dislocation, ligamentous laxity (e.g. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome), and malalignment of the knee leading to an increased Q-angle (e.g. femoral anteversion, genu valgum, and external tibial torsion/pronated feet). Anatomical risk factors include patella alta, trochlear dysplasia, excessive lateral patellar tilt, hypoplasia of the lateral femoral condyle, weak vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) muscle belly, and tight lateral structures (e.g. IT band and vastus lateralis).

Trochlear dysplasia has been described in the literature as an important factor to consider in patellar instability, especially with severe dysplasia, as it can lead to residual patellar instability if the MPFL is solely tended to in the primary procedure. Trochlear dysplasia has been described by the Dejour classification system, which has shown to be useful in predicting treatment. Type A is defined by a crossing sign on lateral imaging and a shallow or symmetric concave trochlea on axial imaging. Type B is defined by a crossing sign and supratrochlear spur on lateral imaging with a flat or convex trochlea on axial imaging. Type C is defined by a crossing sign and double contour on lateral imaging with asymmetric trochlear facets and a hypoplastic medial facet. Type D is defined by a crossing sign, supratrochlear spur, and double contour on lateral imaging with asymmetric trochlear facets and a steep vertical slope on axial imaging. It has been accepted that Dejour A and C are more mild types and Dejour B and D are more severe types. Since types B and D are more severe, many believe these are the types most amenable to trochleoplasty.

*Nolan et al. reviewed the indications and techniques for trochleoplasty in the setting of trochlear dysplasia.* **They report** patellar instability in the setting of severe trochlear dysplasia is the primary indication for a trochleoplasty. The most commonly reported techniques include sulcus deepening and recession wedge trochleoplasty to address severe trochlear dysplasia (Dejour types B and D). The results of these procedures demonstrate a significant improvement in Kujala, IKDC, and VAS pain scores without any reported patellar re-dislocations. **They conclude** that these techniques demonstrate promising results when properly indicated in Dejour types B and D knees without patellofemoral arthritis.

*Lippacher et al. evaluated the efficacy of the Dejour classification.* *4 surgeons (2 senior and 2 junior) reviewed 50 patients in this study with both lateral radiographs and MRIs.* Each image was read twice independently by the surgeons who determined the specific grade of trochlear dysplasia based upon the Dejour classification and if it was low grade (A) or high grade (B-D). The 4-grade analysis demonstrated fair intra- and inter-observer agreements (24-78%) whereas the 2-grade analysis had good to excellent agreements (56-96%). **They conclude** that the Dejour classification helps determine the type of trochlear dysplasia, but is most useful when separating low-grade from high-grade dysplasia.

*Kazley et al. reviewed the Dejour classification of trochlear dysplasia.* **They report** that the Dejour classification system for trochlear dysplasia has several limitations including poor reliability and reproducibility in the clinical setting due to the need for near-perfect lateral radiographs of the knee to diagnose the crossing sign and the lack of objective severity grading for treatment modalities. **They conclude** that the usefulness of this classification system can be enhanced with more advanced imaging modalities such as MRI, for quantitative grading and improving its clinical relevance.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answers 1 and 2: MPFL repair with or without VMO strengthening postoperatively would not address the severe trochlear dysplasia in this patient.
Answer 4: A tibial tubercle osteotomy could assist with restoring patellar tracking, however this would not address the severe trochlear dysplasia.
Answer 5: Residual patellar instability is a well-reported postoperative complication when MPFL reconstruction is performed alone in a patient with severe trochlear dysplasia.
