Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Tennis Elbow

## Question

A middle aged women comes to OPD with complaints of right elbow pain which has been troubling her for last 3 months. She feels pain and unable to do her household chores. On examination wrist extension is painful and tenderness is present at around lateral epicondyle. She has been taking NSAIDS for last 7 days and using tennis elbow band but didn’t get enough relief. What is the next best treatment?

## Options

1. PRP injection
2. Corticosteroid injection
3. Eccentric exercises
4. ECSW therapy

## Correct option

**3. Eccentric exercises**

## Answer explanation

Regardless of the underlying cause, nonoperative treatment is successful in 95% of patients with tennis elbow. Despite widespread use, corticosteroid injection has repeatedly been shown to have no long-term benefit. Local corticosteroid injection has short-term (two to six weeks) benefits in pain reduction, global improvement, and grip strength compared with placebo and other conservative treatments. However, these benefits do not persist beyond six weeks.

PRP injection: results of studies are conflicting. Cochrane review found moderate certainty evidence that PRP does not provide patient-important benefits compared with placebo in people with tennis elbow.

ECSWT: Early studies were promising for extracorporeal shock wave therapy, but more recent studies have shown conflicting results. Recent studies did not find a meaningful difference between treatment of lateral epicondylitis with extracorporeal shock wave therapy combined with forearm stretching and treatment with forearm stretching alone.

Orthoses: Despite the widespread use of orthoses, multiple systematic reviews have been unable to provide conclusions about the benefits of orthoses for lateral epicondylitis. Use of an inelastic, nonarticular, proximal forearm strap may decrease pain and increase grip strength after three weeks. Bracing for up to six weeks also may improve the patient's ability to perform daily activities. However, conflicting evidence suggests that straps are no better than sham bracing or other conservative therapies for lateral epicondylitis and may be inferior in the short term to corticosteroid injection and topical NSAIDs.

This patient has tennis elbow or lateral epicondylitis. First line treatment is conservative. Evidence suggests that exercise programs can reduce pain, but the improvement in grip strength is less clear. Regimens should focus on eccentric instead of concentric phases.

Answer: 3
