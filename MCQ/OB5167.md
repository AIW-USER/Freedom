## Options

1. Physeal growth acceleration
2. Recurrent fractures
3. Limb angular deformity
4. Arterial injury
5. Compartment syndrome

## Correct option

**3. Limb angular deformity**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a displaced distal femoral physeal fracture that was treated with closed reduction and casting. The most common complication would be physeal growth arrest causing limb deformity.

The rate of limb growth at the distal femur is approximately 9-10 mm/year. Any fracture involving the distal femoral physis has a 50-55% chance of causing growth arrest. However, displaced fractures have a greater risk of growth arrest than undisplaced fractures. As a result, there is common thought that all displaced fractures should be operatively stabilized as the undulating physis remains unstable following reduction and it cannot be fully stabilized with casting alone.

*Basener et al. performed a meta analysis of 16 articles that published the incidence of growth disturbance after distal femoral growth plate fractures in children.* *Of the 564 fractures, growth disturbance occurred in 36% of SH 1 fractures, 58% in SH 2, 49% in SH 3, and 64% in SH 4 fractures.* Displaced fracture had growth disturbances in 65% of patients compared to 31% of the fractures with no displacement. Growth disturbances included limb length discrepancies and angular deformities, of which angular deformities were more frequent.

Figure A - D shows a displaced SH 1 distal femur fracture that has been reduced and placed in a circumferential cast. Based on the fracture pattern and resting position of the fragments, the mechanism of injury was likely a hyperextension injury.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Physeal growth acceleration would not occur. Limb lengthening can occur following femoral shaft fractures in children.
Answer 2: Recurrent physeal fractures are rare.
Answer 4: Hyperextension injury with anterior displacement of the epiphysis have a risk of vascular complication. In some studies, the rate of arterial injury has been reported as high as 20%, but remain less common than limb angular deformity
Answer 5: Compartment syndrome occurs at a rate of <1-3% in most studies, less common than limb angular deformity.
