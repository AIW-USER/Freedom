## Question

A 13-year-old male competitive gymnast presents to your clinic with a 3-month history of low back pain that is exacerbated by extension and gymnastics activities. Physical examination reveals a palpable step-off at L5-S1 and hamstring tightness. Standing lateral lumbar spine radiographs confirm a Grade I isthmic spondylolisthesis at L5-S1. The patient has no neurological deficits and no radicular symptoms. What is the MOST appropriate initial management strategy for this patient?

## Options

1. Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) at L5-S1.
2. Bracing with a lumbosacral orthosis (LSO) for 6-12 months.
3. L5 pars interarticularis repair.
4. Nonoperative management with a short period of activity modification followed by a structured physical therapy program.
5. Posterior lumbar fusion from L5 to S1.

## Correct option

**4. Nonoperative management with a short period of activity modification followed by a structured physical therapy program.**

## Answer explanation

A low-grade isthmic spondylolisthesis in a pediatric patient is typically best managed nonoperatively with rest and physical therapy, as most patients respond well and can return to their normal activities (answer choice 4).

Low-grade isthmic spondylolisthesis, which is a common cause of low back pain in young athletes, is typically a stable condition. The initial management strategy is overwhelmingly nonoperative. This approach focuses on a short period of activity modification to allow symptoms to subside, followed by a structured physical therapy program. The goal of physical therapy is to strengthen the core musculature, particularly the abdominal and paraspinal muscles, and to address hamstring tightness, which can help unload the lumbar spine and improve stability. Surgical intervention is rarely needed and is reserved for cases that fail to respond to a comprehensive nonoperative protocol, have progressive neurological deficits, or have significant slippage.

*Nielsen et al. evaluated the timeline for the diagnosis of spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis in children.* **They noted** that the diagnosis was frequently delayed by several months after the initial presentation to a non-orthopaedic provider. **They concluded** that greater awareness and a higher index of suspicion are needed to ensure timely diagnosis and appropriate management of this common condition in pediatric patients.

*Crawford et al. provided a comprehensive evidence-based review on the treatment of pediatric lumbar spondylolisthesis.* *They summarized the literature on both nonoperative and operative treatments for various grades of slippage.* **They concluded** that nonoperative management is the recommended first-line treatment for low-grade slips, with surgical options reserved for more severe or refractory cases.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: ALIF is an aggressive surgical procedure that is not indicated as the initial treatment for a low-grade spondylolisthesis.
Answer 2: While bracing can be considered for some spondylolysis cases, it is a prolonged and often unnecessary intervention for a low-grade spondylolisthesis and does not represent the standard initial approach.
Answer 3: Pars repair is a surgical option, but it is not the first-line treatment and is considered only after a failed course of conservative care, particularly for painful pars defects without significant slippage.
Answer 5: This is a major surgical procedure and is not appropriate for the initial management of a Grade I spondylolisthesis.
