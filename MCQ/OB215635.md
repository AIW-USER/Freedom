## Question

A 71-year-old female presents to the clinic for evaluation of low back/buttock pain and numbness and tingling in her bilateral lower extremities. She has noticed progressive difficulty walking due to gait instability. The numbness and tingling are relatively constant, but she feels symptoms slightly improve when sitting. After 4 months of physical therapy, she received an interlaminar epidural steroid injection with no improvement in symptoms. ## Options

1. Repeat epidural steroid injection
2. Continued physical therapy for an additional 2 months
3. L3-4 decompressive laminectomy
4. L3-4 decompressive laminectomy with fusion
5. L4-5 posterior lumbar fusion

## Correct option

**3. L3-4 decompressive laminectomy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has central lumbar spinal stenosis at L3-4 with evidence of a stable grade I spondylolisthesis at L4-L5 and has failed an appropriate trial of conservative management (3-6 months). Due to the stenosis in the setting of a stable spine, L3-4 laminectomy is the best treatment option. (Answer choice 3).

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a degenerative condition in which changes in the discs, ligamentum flavum, and facet joints with aging cause narrowing of the spaces around the neurovascular structures of the spine. Narrowing of bony and/or soft tissue structures leads to compression of neural elements. Compression located centrally manifests as neurogenic claudication and is often caused by hypertrophied/buckling ligamentum flavum (as seen at L3-4 in the patient above). Patients will often complain of low back and buttock pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness in the legs, and difficulty walking or standing which can be relieved with lumbar flexion. Lateral stenosis manifests as radiculopathy and may be caused by foraminal (far lateral, extraforaminal) disc herniation, facet arthropathy and osteophyte formation. Central stenosis secondary to ligamentum flavum pathology can be addressed with a pedicle-to-pedicle decompression. Fusion procedures in the setting of lumbar spinal stenosis are often reserved for when there is concomitant instability (i.e., spondylolisthesis).

*Pearson et al.* *2012 performed a combined prospective randomized controlled trial and observational cohort study of intervertebral disc herniation (IDH) in association with the SPORT trial.* IDH patients underwent either discectomy or nonoperative care and were analyzed according to the treatment received. **They reported** that all analyzed subgroups improved significantly more with surgery than with nonoperative treatment. **They concluded** IDH patients who met their strict inclusion criteria improved more with surgery than with nonoperative treatment, regardless of specific characteristics. They do note, however, that being married, without joint problems, and worsening symptom trends at baseline were associated with a greater treatment effect.

Lurie and Tomkins-Lane (2016) provide a review on the non-surgical and surgical management of lumbar spinal stenosis. The report options for non-surgical management include drugs, physiotherapy, spinal injections, lifestyle modification, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation. They note that a systematic review concluded that there is insufficient evidence to recommend any specific type of non-surgical treatment. They also report that outcomes (leg pain and disability) seem to be better for surgery than for non-operative treatment, but the evidence is heterogeneous and often of limited quality.

Försth et al. performed a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of fusion surgery in addition to decompression surgery in patients who have lumbar spinal stenosis, with or without degenerative spondylolisthesis. They reported no significant difference in clinical outcome scores between the groups at 2 and 5 years postoperatively. They further reported that operating time was longer, the amount of bleeding was greater, and surgical costs were higher in the fusion group than in the decompression-alone group. They concluded that among patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, with or without degenerative spondylolisthesis, decompression surgery plus fusion surgery did not result in better clinical outcomes at 2 years and 5 years than did decompression surgery alone.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: This patient has trialed nonoperative modalities, including epidural steroid injection, and failed to find relief. Surgical intervention is indicated at this point.
Answer choice 2: The first line treatment for spinal stenosis includes 3-6 months of physical therapy. This patient has trialed 4 months of physical therapy and therefore further therapy is not indicated as next line of treatment.
Answer choice 4: Although L3-4 laminectomy is the correct treatment for this patient, the combination of fusion with this procedure is unindicated as this patient's spine is stable.
Anser choice 5: Due to the central stenosis, a decompression procedure is indicated in this patient, not a fusion procedure. The spondylolisthesis in Figure A is stable, as demonstrated by the flexion and extension radiographs.
