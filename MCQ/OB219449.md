## Question

A 74-year-old woman presents with a recurrent periprosthetic joint infection after being treated at an outside facility. After prolonged discussion with the patient and her family, she decides to undergo 2-stage revision arthroplasty over other options. She undergoes removal of current stemmed revision implants with spacer placement. Her infection is confirmed to be eradicated after 6 weeks of targeted IV antibiotics based on serum labs and synovial fluid analysis. Which of the following would serve as the strongest indication to utilize a rotating hinged implant during stage 2 revision total knee arthroplasty?

## Options

1. Significant loss of distal femur cancellous bone with retained condylar cortical bone
2. Medial distal femoral cortical bone loss involving the medial epicondyle
3. Significant loss of proximal tibial cancellous bone with retained plateau cortical bone
4. Moderate anterior tibial plateau cortical bone loss with preservation of tibial tubercle
5. Posterior tibial plateau cortical bone loss with preservation of collateral ligament insertions

## Correct option

**2. Medial distal femoral cortical bone loss involving the medial epicondyle**

## Answer explanation

In the setting of revision total knee arthroplasty, bone loss to critical areas that result in ligamentous instability (i.e. medial collateral ligament origin along the medial epicondyle) requires the utilization of rotating hinged implants for long-term implant survivorship (Answer 2).

In the setting of revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA), the residual bone stock has important implications on the appropriate revision implant utilized. Significant cancellous bone loss on either the distal femoral or proximal tibial can be addressed by utilizing tantalum cones or metaphyseal sleeves. Conversely, significant cortical bone loss can be addressed by utilizing metal augments. When bone loss involves the origin or insertion of the collateral ligaments (particularly the medial collateral ligament), a rotating hinged implant must be utilized to provide long-term coronal plane stability. The Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) serves as one classification system to guide addressing bone loss in the setting of revision TKA (Illustration A).

*Melnic and colleagues provided a comprehensive review regarding the use of megaprostheses in non-oncologic hip and knee revision arthroplasty.* In the setting of revision TKA, **they note** excessive distal femoral bone loss including the epicondyles require megaprosthesis to replace the entire distal femur because augments, cones, and sleeves are not feasible to provide adequate coronal stability. **They further emphasize** that careful determination of construct length, maintenance of the joint line, and preservation of rotational alignment are critical technical considerations when using a distal femur replacement implant. **The authors conclude** that the continued improvements in megaprosthesis designs have aided in their expanded indications for use.

*Cottino and colleagues retrospectively reviewed the long-term outcomes of their institution’s utilization of rotating-hinged components for nononcologic indications.* **They identified** 408 patients with 18% indicated for complex primary cases and 82% for revision cases. All patients had significantly improved Knee Society scores with a cumulative incidence of all-cause revisions being 9.7% at 2 years and 22.5% at 10 years. **They further found** lower revision rates for aseptic loosening when metaphyseal cones were utilized. **The authors concluded** that contemporary rotating-hinge TKA implants are durable constructs with good long-term outcomes.

Illustration A demonstrates the AORI classification of bone loss in revision TKA. Type 1 defects involve isolated cancellous bone loss without significant cortical bone loss. Type II defects involve non-critical cortical bone loss. Type III defects involve critical bone loss with resultant compromised collateral ligamentous or patellar ligament attachments.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1&3: In the setting of revision TKA, significant cancellous bone loss on either the femoral or tibial side can be addressed utilizing tantalum cones or metaphyseal sleeves. In either case, the utilization of a rotating hinged implant design is not required, and both cones and sleeves may be utilized with less constrained implant designs.
Answer 4&5: In the setting of revision TKA, significant cortical bone loss on either the femoral or tibial side can be addressed utilizing metal augments. However, if cortical bone loss occurs in critical areas resulting in collateral ligamentous instability, then rotating hinged implants should be employed.
