## Question

A 42-year-old male martial artist presents to your clinic with a history of an elbow injury 5 weeks ago where he felt a pop while participating in jiu jitsu. He reports that since the injury he has lacked strength in his elbow and on exam you note weakness in supination and flexion. His primary care provider ordered an MRI which demonstrates a distal biceps rupture with 6 cm of retraction. Which of the following is true regarding management this injury?

## Options

1. Allograft augmentation is necessary given retraction
2. Nonoperative management provides equivalent outcomes
3. Primary fixation in hyperflexion is well tolerated
4. Given chronicity, two incision technique is required
5. Interference screw should be used in addition to cortical button fixation

## Correct option

**3. Primary fixation in hyperflexion is well tolerated**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a subacute distal biceps rupture which requires fixation. Primary fixation is initially preferred even if elbow hyperflexion is required, as these patients have good outcomes without flexion contracture.

Distal biceps ruptures are relatively rare overall but tend to occur in the dominant elbows of middle aged men. They typically occur during eccentric activities where the arm is forced from a flexed to extended position. In young, active patients who wish to return to activities, anatomic repair of the distal biceps to the radial tuberosity is preferred. Acute ruptures without scarring or retraction are typically easier to repair, therefore repair within the first few weeks after injury is optimal. In cases of more chronic ruptures, there is usually an increase in adhesions and tendon retraction and excursion of the tendon can be more difficult. Intraoperatively, hyperflexion of the elbow may be required to primarily fixate the tendon back in the appropriate position. Luckily, in these cases, hyperflexion at the time of surgery does not lead to long term deficits in range of motion or function. Therefore, if at all possible, this method of primary fixation is preferred to allograft augmentation which tends to have less consistent results.

*Schmidt et al. provided a review of distal biceps tendon injuries.* **They noted** that as knowledge has advanced, understanding of the tendon structure and anatomic footprint has improved, allowing surgeons to better treat these injuries. They go on to describe both nonoperative and operative management strategies based on the degree and chronicity of the tear.

*Bosman et al. reviewed the repair of chronic distal biceps ruptures without using an allograft.* *In this study, patients were operated on an average of 79 days from their injury and at 20 months postoperatively, the cohort had an average of 95% flexion and 95% pronosupination strength compared to the contralateral side.* Additionally, **the authors reported** no long term strength deficits or complications which allowed them to conclude that primary fixation of these chronic defects was appropriate, even if fixation in hyperflexion was required.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: In chronic distal biceps ruptures with an inability to reach the biceps tuberosity, even in hyperflexion, allograft augmentation may be used. However, primary fixation in hyperflexion is preferred if possible and should be attempted first.
Answer 2: Nonoperative management leads to weakness in supination and elbow flexion and in a martial artist who wishes to return to activity would not be the most appropriate choice.
Answer 4: Chronicity of the biceps rupture does not require a different approach, although at times it may require a more extensile approach.
Answer 5: If the distal biceps tendon has the excursion to reach the radial tuberosity, cortical button fixation alone is sufficiently strong without the need for additional screw augmentation.
