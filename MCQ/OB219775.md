## Question

A 44-year-old obtunded male with a history of drug abuse presents to the emergency department for evaluation of a swollen right hand. The patient was found down on his right side in his home and was estimated to have been there for 12 to 18 hours. On examination, his hand is palpably taut. Passive extension of the fingers elicits some agitation. His current blood pressure is 88/53 mmHg. Intracompartmental measurements are obtained, with greater than 25 mmHg pressure observed in multiple interossei compartments. He subsequently undergoes surgical release of his hand compartments. Which of the following is considered a normal compartment pressure of the hand?

## Options

1. 10 mmHg
2. 20 mmHg
3. 30 mmHg
4. 40 mmHg
5. 50 mmHg

## Correct option

**1. 10 mmHg**

## Answer explanation

This 44-year-old drug-using male exhibits findings of hand compartment syndrome and subsequently undergoes surgical release. Normal compartment pressures of the hand range from 0-15 mmHg (Answer 1).

Hand compartment syndrome is a rare entity primarily associated with infection, intravenous/intra-arterial extravasation, trauma (crush injuries or multiple fractures), and drug use. It is important to assess the hand thoroughly, focusing on pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, paresthesias, paralysis, palpable swelling, and/or loss of pulses. In the setting of patient obtundation, manometry can serve as a useful adjunct to diagnosing/ruling out compartment syndrome. However, sizeable debate surrounds what constitutes 'pathological' pressures within the hand, with some postulating as little as 15 mmHg should be considered abnormal. Moreover, studies have illustrated the incompetence of the fascial layer between volar and dorsal interossei, further obfuscating the prospect of compartment syndrome in these compartments. Nonetheless, a compartment pressure within 30 mmHg of diastolic blood pressure remains the gold standard for diagnosis with manometry in obtunded patients, while hand compartment syndrome in particular largely remains a clinical diagnosis.

*Guyton et al. performed a cadaver study (n=3) examining the compartmental anatomy of the hand utilizing radio-opaque dye-laden continuous infusions into the second dorsal interosseous.* **The authors noted** a sharp rise to 10 mmHg with the first 5 mL of fluid, slowly rising over the next 40 mL. No compartment pressure exceeded 34 mmHg. **The authors found** contrast egression into adjacent interossei compartments before 15 mmHg in all hands, suggesting the tenuous integrity of the dorsal interossei fascia. **The authors conclude** that while the interossei compartments themselves can support higher pressures, the fascial planes between volar and dorsal interossei may be more tenuous than previously believed.

*Wong et al. performed a cadaver study (n=3) aiming to assess the clinical accuracy of diagnosing hand compartment syndrome through palpation and manometry.* Following intra-compartmental injection into the thenar and hypothenar compartments of the hand, 17 assessors examined the specimens. Sensitivity and specificity of 49% and 79%, respectively, were found with palpation of the thenar eminence, and utilizing a manometer increased the sensitivity and specificity to 97% and 86%. Regarding the hypothenar eminence, the sensitivity and specificity of palpation were found to be 62% and 83%, respectively, which improved to 100% in both with the aid of a handheld manometer. **The authors recommend** utilizing manometry as an adjunctive diagnostic tool to aid in diagnosing compartment syndrome of the hand.

Ouellette and Kelly perform a retrospective review of 19 patients (ten adults, nine children) experiencing hand compartment syndrome of varying etiologies. 78.9% of patients were obtunded upon initial examination, with 68.4% experiencing the condition due to extravasation of an intravenous or intra-arterial line. Following decompression of the compartments with concomitant carpal tunnel release, 76.4% went on to experience satisfactory results. The authors conclude physicians should maintain a high level of suspicion for hand compartment syndrome in obtunded patients receiving repeated intravenous or intra-arterial injections.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-5: compartment pressures exceeding 15 mmHg are considered pathologic.
