## Question

All are true about pseudoarthrosis of tibia except (pediatric orthopedics- congenital pseudarthrosis of tibia)

## Options

1. Anterior angulation is most common
2. Neurofibromatosis is most common association
3. 20% of neurofibromatosis patients have pseudoarthrosis
4. Dysplastic fibula

## Correct option

**3. 20% of neurofibromatosis patients have pseudoarthrosis**

## Answer explanation

Congenital unilateral bowing of the tibia is an uncommon orthopedic condition. It is classified according to the direction of the angulation: anterolateral, anteromedial and posteromedial. Anterolateral tibial bowing is commonly but not exclusively associated with NF1 and presents with a high risk of pathologic fracture and pseudarthrosis.

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia is rare, with an incidence of approximately one in 250,000 live births. Most large series report 50% to 90% association of this disorder with the stigmata of neurofibromatosis, including skin and osseous lesions.

So three types of tibial bowing exist in children

- anterolateral bowing (may be associated with pseudarthrosis of tibia)

- posteromedial bowing (physiologic)

- anteromedial bowing (fibular hemimelia)

boyd classification:

Type I pseudarthrosis occurs with anterior bowing and a defect in the tibia present at birth.

Type II pseudarthrosis occurs with anterior bowing and an hourglass constriction of the tibia present at birth. Spontaneous fracture, or fracture after minor trauma, commonly occurs before 2 years of age. This is the so-called high-risk tibia. The tibia is tapered, rounded, and sclerotic, and the medullary canal is obliterated. This type is the most common, is often associated with neurofibromatosis, and has the poorest prognosis. Recurrence of the fracture is common during the growth period but decreases in frequency with age and generally ceases to occur after skeletal maturation.

Type III pseudarthrosis develops in a congenital cyst, usually near the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the tibia. Anterior bowing may precede or follow the development of a fracture. Recurrence of the fracture after treatment is less common than in type II.

Type IV pseudarthrosis originates in a sclerotic segment of bone in the classic location without narrowing of the tibia. The medullary canal is partially or completely obliterated. An “insufficiency” or “stress” fracture develops in the cortex of the tibia and gradually extends through the sclerotic bone. With completion of the fracture, healing fails to occur, and the fracture widens and becomes a pseudarthrosis. The prognosis for this type generally is good.

 Type V pseudarthrosis of the tibia occurs with a dysplastic fibula. A pseudarthrosis of the fibula or tibia or both may develop. The prognosis is good if the lesion is confined to the fibula.

Type VI pseudarthrosis occurs as an intraosseous neurofibroma or schwannoma that results in a pseudarthrosis. This is extremely rare

association with neurofibromatosis

- neurofibromatosis type I
- is found in 50-55% of patients with anterolateral bowing/ congenital pseudarthrosis of tibia
- only 6-10% of patients with neurofibromatosis will have anterolateral tibial bowing

- 15% patients with anterolateral bowing/ congenital pseudarthrosis of tibia are associated with Fibrous Dysplasia

 Answer: c
