## Options

1. Physical therapy utilizing a continuous passive motion device
2. Oral pexidartinib with tapered dosing as necessary
3. Intra-articular injection of high-dose corticosteroids
4. Intra-articular injection of a high-dose beta-emitting radiocolloid
5. Primary total knee replacement with a posterior stabilized implant design

## Correct option

**2. Oral pexidartinib with tapered dosing as necessary**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with recurrent atraumatic effusions and hyperproliferative synovium on arthroscopic and open evaluation, consistent with pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS). Given his aggressive recurrent disease despite repeated surgical intervention, he is indicated to undergo a trial of oral pexidartinib therapy (Answer 2).

PVNS, now classified by the World Health Organization as a subtype of tenosynovial giant cell tumor, is caused by overexpression of colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1). The resultant downstream effect involves hyperproliferation of the synovium lining joints. Most reported cases involve isolated monoarticular involvement within the knee or hip with initial presentation of recurrent atraumatic joint effusions, limited range of motion, and early cartilage wear (Illustration A). MRI findings include hypointense signaling on T1- and T2-weighted imaging secondary to hemosiderin within the hyperplasic cells (Illustration B). Marginal excision via partial synovectomy (for small areas of localized disease) or total synovectomy (for diffuse joint involvement) remains the mainstay of treatment. Unfortunately, recurrences (~50%) are common and often require repeated marginal excision procedures. Pexidartinib (CSF-1 receptor inhibitor) may be used for aggressive recalcitrant disease cases. This novel medication was recently approved by the FDA in 2019 for treating PVNS patients in whom surgery would be associated with potentially worsening functional limitation or severe morbidity. Providers should be aware of Pexidartinib’s adverse profile, including hair color changes (loss of pigmentation) and potential significant hepatotoxicity (requires regular monitoring of liver enzymes). Pexidartinib’s dosing can be tapered in response to these adverse events as necessary.

*Tap and colleagues performed the randomized control trial (phase 3) ENLIVEN study comparing pexidartinib to a placebo for the treatment of PVNS in patients where surgery was not recommended (i.e. would result in worsening functional limitation or morbidity).* **The authors found** an overall decreased tumor burden (based on RECIST), significantly improved range of motion, and significantly improved PROMIS scores in the pexidartinib group at 25 weeks. **They also found** that the most frequent side effects were hair color changes (loss of pigmentation), liver enzyme increase, fatigue, and dysgeusia. **The authors concluded** that pexidartinib is the first systematic therapy to demonstrate a robust tumor response and improved patient symptoms/functional scores in PVNS patients deemed unfit/unideal for surgery.

*Casp and colleagues performed a retrospective propensity-matched cohort analysis utilizing the PearlDiver administrative database to identify TKA complications associated with PVNS.* *The authors compared 485 PVNS primary TKA patients to 1812 matched controls and found that at a 2-year follow-up, post-operative stiffness and deep infection were reported at a significantly higher rate in the PVNS cohort.* The revision rate, ER visits, readmission, and death did not differ. **The authors concluded** potential manipulation under anesthesia and periprosthetic joint infections may be higher in PVNS patients undergoing primary TKA compared to standard osteoarthritic indications.

*Bickels and colleagues performed a retrospective case series review of their utilization of the beta-emitting radiocolloid (i.e., Yttrium-90) intra-articular injection in patients with diffuse PVNS of the ankle joint.* In their 7-patient series, two experienced full-thickness skin necrosis requiring free flap coverage, and one patient developed a persistent sinus tract and debilitating pain. Therefore, **the authors recommend** against its use as an intra-articular injection for treating PVNS.

Figures A and B demonstrate arthroscopic findings of hyperproliferative synovial tissue throughout this patient’s knee joint compartments, consistent with diffuse PVNS. Figure C shows a clinical photograph from this patient’s subsequent open synovectomy via a medial parapatellar approach. Figure D demonstrates the synovial tissue removed during the case. Figure E shows intraoperative findings of his early cartilage degeneration. Illustration A shows the characteristic hemosiderin-laden macrophages (red arrow) found under microscopic evaluation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Engaging in a physical therapy program with a continuous passive motion device would be considered an appropriate treatment modality for this patient’s knee stiffness. However, it would not prevent PVNS recurrence.
Answer 3: An intra-articular injection of high-dose corticosteroids would provide temporary pain relief but would not address disease recurrence.
Answer 4: Intra-articular injections of beta-emitting radiocolloid (i.e., Yttrium-90) were once considered beneficial; however, subsequent studies demonstrated an unacceptably high complication rate. Therefore, other adjunctive treatments should be sought.
Answer 5: Given his young age and lack of end-stage cartilage destruction, it would not be appropriate to perform a total knee arthroplasty.
