## Question

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a frequently used imaging modality when evaluating patients for shoulder pathology. While most scans are performed with the patient's arm in the anatomic position, modifications of arm position can be useful to more accurately identify pathology, if present. In which position should the patient's arm be placed in order to best visualize the humerus in the coronal plane and the glenoid in the axial plane?

## Options

1. Abduction and internal rotation
2. Abduction and external rotation
3. Abduction and neutral rotation
4. Adduction and internal rotation
5. Adduction and external rotation

## Correct option

**2. Abduction and external rotation**

## Answer explanation

With the arm in abduction and external rotation, the humerus is viewed in the coronal plane, and the glenoid is viewed in the axial plane (Answer 2).

MRI is a useful imaging modality for a variety of shoulder pathology. Although most scans are performed with the patient in the anatomic position, modifications to the arm position can be helpful in teasing out difficult-to-visualize anatomic areas or patient pathology. When the patient's arm is placed into abduction and external rotation, the humerus is sequenced in the coronal plane, while the glenoid remains fixed in the axial plane (Illustration A). This position allows for much better visualization of a variety of structures including, but not limited to, the articular surface of the supraspinatus tendon, the anteroinferior portion of the glenoid labrum, and the anterior band of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL). While a definitive diagnosis and creation of a surgical plan typically depend on the results of a diagnostic arthroscopy, better preoperative detection of these injuries can greatly aid surgeons in preoperative counseling, implant selection, and, if applicable, patient position for surgery.

*Borrero et al. investigated the magnetic resonance appearance of the posterosuperior labral peelback mechanism during humeral abduction and external rotation (ABER).* *Their study included 34 patients with both appropriate pre-operative imaging and an operative report documenting the presence of labral peelback in the ABER position.* **The authors found** that MRI in the ABER position demonstrated a sensitivity of 73%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, and negative predictive value of 78% for detecting the presence of superior labral peel-back during diagnostic arthroscopy. Overall, **the authors conclude** that this imaging modality is a highly precise method for the detection of posterosuperior labral peelback, and can be used to alert surgeons to the likely presence of superior labral instability.

*Aydingoz et al. provide a review of the abduction and external rotation (ABER) sequence in magnetic resonance arthrography of the shoulder.* **The authors note** that it is useful in better visualizing the articular surface of the supraspinatus tendon, the anteroinferior labrum, the anterior band of the IGHL, and the posterosuperior labrum. The authors comment that despite these benefits, this sequence remains underutilized, typically secondary to unfamiliarity with the positioning and sequencing details. The authors, however, provide a guide on obtaining appropriately positioned and sequenced images and also provide an outline on interpreting these images.

Illustration A is a T1-weighted ABER image. The white arrow shows the posterior portion of the supraspinatus as it attaches onto the greater tuberosity, and the yellow arrow indicates the antero-inferior glenoid labrum.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,3,4,5: in order to view the humerus in the coronal plane and the glenoid in the axial plane the arm should be placed in abduction and external rotation.
