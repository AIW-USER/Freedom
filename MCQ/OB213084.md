## Question

A 72 year-old-female presents with low back pain and right hip pain that has progressively worsened over the last year. Her primary complaint is that she just can't walk the distance that she use to. Symptoms are worsened with prolonged walking and improve with sitting. The pain is located in her right buttock, right groin, and anterior thigh. Neurologic exam shows no motor deficits, normal sensation, and normal reflexes in the bilateral lower extremity. Physical examination of the spine shows a negative straight leg test, and increased pain in buttocks with prolonged erect standing. Physical exam of the right hip shows 0° of internal rotation and 10° of external rotation. Physical exam of the left hip shows 20° of internal rotation and 40° of external rotation. Range of motion testing of the right hip elicits pain. Current imaging is shown in Figures A & B. What would be the most appropriate step?

## Options

1. Obtain Electromyography and Nerve Conduction Studies of the Bilateral Lower Extremities
2. Perform Fluoroscopically-guided lidocaine and corticosteroid injection of the right hip
3. Prescribe a Methylprednisone Taper
4. Proceed with right Total Hip Arthroplasty
5. Proceed with surgical decompression of L2-3

## Correct option

**2. Perform Fluoroscopically-guided lidocaine and corticosteroid injection of the right hip**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting symptoms and physical exam finding consistent with both hip arthritis and lumbar spinal stenosis. In order to better understand the distribution of symptoms originating from the hip versus the lumbar spine the most appropriate next step is a corticosteroid/lidocaine injection in either the hip or the L2-3 epidural space.

Hip-spine syndrome is the result of coexisting spine and hip pathology resulting in lower extremity pain. The most common combination involves hip osteoarthritis and lumbar spinal stenosis. In many cases, the primary pain generator may not be obvious based on the presenting symptoms and physical exam findings. In these cases, diagnostic injections can help elucidate the primary pain generator. Surgical treatment should be directed at the identified primary generator. Patients should be counseled that there is a chance that pain from the secondary pain generator may worsen after surgery due to increased activity levels. In cases of severe lumbar stenosis, decompressive laminectomy should be performed regardless of the primary pain generator to prevent the risk of postoperative motor weakness.

*Buckland et al. performed a review of the identification and treatment of lumbar spine and hip pathology.* **They found** in the literature there was improvement in lower back pain after total hip arthroplasty when patients reported preoperative lower back pain. However, there are reported cases of patients developing worsening lower back pain or foot drop necessitating decompressive laminectomy following total hip arthroplasty. **They recommend** a thorough history and physical with a critical assessment of imaging studies and counseling patients regarding potential worsening symptoms from the secondary pain generator.

*Devin et al. performed a review of the treatment and management of patients presenting with concomitant hip and spine pathology.* **The authors proposed** taking a very thorough history and physical examination in patients presenting with hip-spine syndrome. Surgical treatment should be directed at the primary pain generator, which may require extensive physical examination testing, imaging, and diagnostic injections. Studies have suggested that correctly addressing the primary pain generator may improve the coexisting symptoms from the secondary pain generator. However, there are instances where symptoms from the secondary pain generator are worsened with surgical treatment of the primary generator due to increased activity levels.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Electromyography (EMG) on this patient would be unlikely to provide added diagnostic utility. If there were documented neurologic abnormalities confirming L2-3 spinal stenosis, there would still be uncertainty as to whether the hip or the spine are the primary pain generators.
Answer 3: The patient is describing symptoms of claudication, however in the context of normal dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses as well as no lower extremity skin changes and loss of hair, vascular causes are unlikely. An ankle-brachial index is unlikely to reveal the etiology of the patient's symptoms.
Answer 4: The patient has end-stage osteoarthritis of the right hip, however, based on the patient's symptoms it is not clear whether the primary pain generator is the hip or the spine. Further work-up should be performed before scheduling for total hip arthroplasty.
Answer 5: The patient does have physical exam findings suggestive of both hip osteoarthritis and spinal stenosis. Before proceeding with definitive surgical treatment of spinal stenosis, confirmation that the spinal stenosis is the primary pain generator in this patient.
