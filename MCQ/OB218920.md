## Answer explanation

This adolescent hurdler is at risk for the development of an avulsion fracture. Figure B, which demonstrates a minimally-displaced lesser trochanter avulsion fracture, is best managed with crutches, protected weight-bearing and surveillance radiographs (Figure B). The other injuries are best managed through other means.

Apophyseal avulsion fractures are injuries that typically affect adolescent patients. These injuries occur secondary to the disruption of the tendon origin on the pelvis during "explosive" type activities and often occur in young athletes. The most common avulsion fractures, and the tendon(s) of origin, include ischial (hamstrings/adductors), anterior inferior iliac spine (rectus femoris), anterior superior iliac spine (sartorius), pubic symphysis (abdominal muscles), iliac crest (external oblique abdominal muscles) and the lesser trochanter (iliopsoas). An overview of these injuries is depicted in Illustration A. The initial treatment for these injuries is almost always nonoperative through protected weight-bearing with crutches and surveillance radiographs to assess for healing. When markedly displaced ( > 3 cm, historically) or in the case of failed nonoperative management, treatment includes open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF).

*Cho et al. provide a case series of six adult patients treated for isolated lesser trochanter fractures.* **The authors note** that in the adult population, these injuries are rare and typically pathologic secondary to an underlying malignancy. **The authors conclude** that when faced with these injuries in an adult patient, especially in the absence of trauma, an underlying malignancy must be suspected, and that a stepwise diagnostic approach can help to prevent misdiagnosis.

*Ruffing et al. performed a retrospective case series investigating lesser trochanter avulsion injuries in adolescent patients.* *Their study included 5 patients (4 male, and 1 female) with a mean age of 13.8 years.* *All patients received similar nonoperative management including early protected weight-bearing and surveillance radiographs.* *The mean follow-up was 4.9 years.* *All patients were able to return to competitive athletic activity and the average Harris Hip Score was 100/100.* **The authors conclude** that nonoperative treatment of these injuries is able to provide an excellent return to sports rate, and should be the initial treatment modality for these fractures.

*Tahir et al. provide a case series and literature review of lesser trochanter avulsion fractures in adolescent patients.* Their case series reported on three patients, all of who were injured following sprinting or after sustaining a fall. All patients were managed non-operatively and all returned to athletic activity. Based on a review of the available literature, and secondary to poorly defined surgical indications, **the authors recommend** initial nonoperative management for all isolated lesser trochanter avulsion injuries in adolescent patients.

Figure A demonstrates a left-sided slipped capital femoral epiphysis. Figure C demonstrates CAM-type FAI. Figure D demonstrates a compression-sided femoral neck stress fracture. Figure E demonstrates a tension-sided femoral neck stress fracture. Illustration A is a graphical representation of the adolescent pelvic ring and the possible avulsion injuries.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This SCFE would be best managed with in-situ screw fixation.
Answer 3: The management of FAI typically begins with a course of physical therapy and unprotected full weight bearing. There is no indication to put the patient on crutches and/or obtain close follow-up surveillance radiographs.
Answer 4: This compression-sided femoral neck stress fracture would be best managed with either non-weight bearing or, if found to span >50% of the femoral neck, prophylactic screw fixation.
Answer 5: This tension-sided femoral neck stress fracture would be best managed with cannulated screw fixation, with the patient being made non-weight bearing until surgery is completed.
