## Question

A 5-year-old boy develops a painful “head tilt” 5 days after a tonsillectomy. On examination, you note right-sided neck bending, with left-sided head turn and sternocleidomastoid spasm. AP, lateral, and open-mouth odontoid radiographs show no fracture or congenital anomaly. In addition to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and an antispasmodic, which of the following is the most appropriate management?

## Options

1. Halter traction with inpatient monitoring
2. Cervical collar
3. Halo-vest immobilization
4. Posterior C1–C2 fusion
5. Continued observation

## Correct option

**2. Cervical collar**

## Answer explanation

A soft cervical collar is the most appropriate next step in an acutely presenting child (within <2 weeks) with suspected atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation (AARS), in conjunction with analgesics and antispasmodics; escalation is reserved for more persistent or refractory cases (answer choice 2).

Atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation presents with acute torticollis in children and spans a spectrum from mild subluxation to a fixed mechanical block from facet dislocation. History is critical in diagnosis, as most cases occur secondary to recent infection (e.g., otitis media, upper respiratory tract infection), trauma, or head and neck surgery. Physical exam may also help differentiate from congenital muscular torticollis (CPM), as the chin tilt and sternocleidomastoid spasm are typically contralateral to each other in cases of CPM, whereas they are typically ipsilateral in cases of AARS (theorized to become spasmodic as a protective mechanism from further subluxation). Diagnosis is primarily clinical, although initial plain radiographs are often obtained to rule out congenital or traumatic etiologies; CT scan and MRI may be helpful in diagnosis, although these are typically reserved for cases of persistent or refractory disease. Early cases are typically treated nonoperatively with a cervical collar and medications, progressing to halter traction if symptoms persist or the deformity fails to reduce. For failures of halter traction, skeletal traction and halo immobilization may be used to achieve and maintain reduction; surgical fusion is reserved for recalcitrant or recurrent cases. Chronicity strongly influences the need to escalate care, with longer symptom duration correlating with greater resistance to conservative measures.

*Neal and Mohamed reviewed AARS in children and* **emphasized** that time from symptom onset is the key determinant of conservative success. **They noted** that treatment ranges from observation and a cervical collar with analgesics for acute presentations to halter/skeletal traction, halo immobilization, and ultimately posterior C1–C2 fusion for refractory or chronic cases. **They concluded** that most acute cases respond to nonsurgical management, with escalation guided by failure to improve and by duration of symptoms.

*Park et al. reported a chronic pediatric AARF (>3 months after onset) successfully reduced using long-term halter traction followed by bracing and a collar.* *The child recovered normal alignment and full neck motion at follow-up.* **They concluded** that prolonged traction can be a viable, surgery-sparing option for selected chronic cases in children.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Halter traction with inpatient monitoring is typically utilized when symptoms persist beyond the acute window or when a collar/medications fail to improve symptoms after two weeks.
Answer choice 3: Halo-vest immobilization is used to maintain reduction after successful skeletal traction or for more chronic/resistant cases, not as first-line therapy in a 5-day presentation.
Answer choice 4: Posterior C1–C2 fusion is reserved for failed traction/halo or recurrent deformity; it is not indicated initially in an acute presentation.
Answer choice 5: Observation alone may allow some acute cases to improve, but standard early management includes a cervical collar with analgesics when AARS is suspected.
