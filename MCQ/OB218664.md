## Question

An 11-year-old gymnast has been having significant pain in her left wrist while practicing her floor routine over the last month. Radiographs of her wrist are shown in Figures A and B. She has swelling and predominantly radial-sided wrist tenderness on palpation with slight loss of active wrist extension on active range of motion. ## Options

1. Compression-sided pseudofractures
2. Distal radial overgrowth
3. Madelung deformity
4. Radiocapitellar instability
5. Ulnar longitudinal deficiency

## Correct option

**3. Madelung deformity**

## Answer explanation

The patient has distal radial physeal stress syndrome, also known as "gymnast's wrist." If she fails conservative treatment, premature physeal closure can occur, resulting in relative ulnar overgrowth and a Madelung deformity (Illustration A).

Gymnast's wrist occurs due to overuse of the wrist with repetitive, supra-physiologic loading moments and is most frequently seen in young gymnasts who use their wrists as weight-bearing joints. The diagnosis can be made clinically with radial-sided wrist pain and tenderness to palpation about the distal radius, with or without swelling, and a reduction in wrist flexion or extension due to pain. Radiographs often reveal widened distal radial growth plates with ill-defined, sclerotic borders (Figures A and B), while MRI can show para-physeal edema and a CT scan can show early physeal bridging (Figure C). Initial treatment is non-operative with NSAIDs, rest, and immobilization for 3-6 weeks. However, once physeal bridging occurs, the patient is at risk for growth deformities that can vary from slight ulnar overgrowth and ulnocarpal abutment to a Madelung deformity depending on the length of time remaining to skeletal maturity. Though a Madelung deformity can arise de novo as a congenital deformity, it can also occur in the setting of traumatic premature closure of the ulnar third of the distal physis of the radius (Figure C), resulting in bowing of the radial shaft and ulnar overgrowth with increased interosseous space and dorsal subluxation of the distal radioulnar joint (Illustration A). Early treatments for small physeal closures include surgical resection of the physeal bridging bone, while ulnar epiphysiodesis +/- shortening with radial osteotomy may be needed for more severe deformities.

Hart, et al. provide a literature review and discussion of the young injured gymnast. The authors note that the most common skills causing injury to a gymnast are front/back handsprings and saltos/flips, with two very specific injuries found almost exclusively in gymnasts including gymnast’s wrist and grip lock. Given that the distal radius typically bears 80% of the axial load imparted on the extended wrist, the distal radius is at increased risk; thus they recommend prevention strategies including working on proper technique for weight-bearing upper extremity skills, proper equipment usage, the use of wrist guards and/or supportive
braces, and decreasing repetition of elements on the wrist during periods of significant growth. They ultimately conclude that working with a physical therapist or athletic trainer to increase shoulder flexibility and thoracic spine mobility also may help decrease the forces on the wrist.

Benjamin, et al. review wrist pain in gymnasts, citing common overuse pathology associated with the gymnastics athlete. They note that general return to play principles are similar for all gymnast-related wrist injuries, including resolution of pain, restoration of normal wrist joint function, completion of a progressive rehabilitation program, and use of proper technique. In terms of prevention, the authors ultimately recommend the addition of directed therapy for the injured wrist, with physical therapy aimed at incorporating core stabilization exercises as well as mobility and stabilization exercises of the shoulder and elbow, as this is felt to potentially minimize the load on the wrists during upper extremity activities.

Figures A and B are PA and lateral radiographs of a skeletally immature wrist showing physeal widening and sclerosis about the distal radius. Figure C represents an axial cut from the CT scan of the patient's wrist at the level of the distal radial physis showing the development of a physeal bar in the ulnar aspect of the distal radial physis. Illustration A shows PA and lateral radiographs comparing a normal wrist to one with the characteristic findings of a Madelung deformity.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Compression-sided pseudofractures, or "looser zones," represent cortical infractions most frequently associated with osteomalacia and rickets. They are not characteristic of distal radial physeal stress syndrome.
Answer 2: Though a distal radial deformity does occur, it is due to shortening and premature physeal closure, not distal radial overgrowth.
Answer 4: Radiocapitellar instability or dislocation occurs in a Nursemaid's elbow with subluxation of the annular ligament after a sudden longitudinal traction moment is applied to the arm. Distal radial ulnar joint (DRUJ) instability would be expected with a Madelung deformity, but the proximal radial ulnar joint (PRUJ) and radiocapitellar joints are not affected.
Answer 5: Ulnar longitudinal deficiency, or "ulnar club hand," is a congenital anomaly associated with the deficiency of the ulna and/or ulnar-sided carpal structures, not ulnar overgrowth.
