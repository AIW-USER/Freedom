## Question

A 28-year-old female is playing recreational indoor soccer when she collides with an opposing player and feels her knee twist awkwardly. She limps to the bench and is unable to continue playing. The following day her knee is painful and swollen medially but there is no appreciable effusion. Based on your examination, you are suspicious of injury to the structure labeled "A," in Figure A. What are the primary functions of this structure as they relate to knee biomechanics?

![[q218218-a-posteromedial-corner.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Resist anterior tibial translation and tibial internal rotation near full extension
2. Resist varus and tibial external rotation near full extension
3. Resist valgus and tibial internal rotation near full extension
4. Resist valgus and tibial internal rotation at 60° flexion
5. Resist valgus and tibial external rotation at 60° flexion

## Correct option

**3. Resist valgus and tibial internal rotation near full extension**

## Answer explanation

The structure detailed in Figure A is the posterior oblique ligament (POL), and injury typically occurs with valgus stress and excess tibial internal rotation near full extension.

The importance of the POL as a medial-sided knee structure has recently become increasingly appreciated. The POL has three arms: the superficial, central, and capsular, with the central portion thought to be most important in reinforcing the posteromedial joint capsule. The POL attaches just distal and posterior to the adductor tubercle on the femur, giving it an attachment slightly posterior and proximal to the medial collateral ligament. The POL is thought to be tensioned near full extension and increasingly lax with more knee flexion. Therefore, injury normally occurs with the knee close to full extension with a valgus load. The degree of internal vs. external rotation also associated with injury has been debated but many sources agree that the POL primarily resists internal rotation. In most cases, injury to the POL and posteromedial corner can be treated non-operatively but with concomitant cruciate or multiligament injury, there should be a concern for increased cruciate reconstruction graft strain.

*Tibor et al. looked at the management of posteromedial corner knee injuries.* **They note** that the role of the posteromedial corner is becoming emphasized in the setting of concomitant cruciate ligament injury and multiligament knee injury as they believe the risk for increasing graft strain and failure is higher if the posteromedial corner is not addressed. They go on to discuss specific reconstruction strategies for the posteromedial corner in this setting.

*Dold et al. published a JAAOS article looking at the anatomy, pathology, and management of posteromedial corner injuries.* *They detail the components of the posteromedial corner including the POL, the semimembranosus and its capsular attachments, the oblique popliteal ligament, the posteromedial joint capsule, and the posterior horn of the medial meniscus.* *They go on to talk specifically about injury patterns and modes of reconstruction, particularly in unstable injuries that are associated with other cruciate injuries.*

Figure A depicts the posteromedial corner of the knee with the structure labeled "A," being the superficial arm of the POL. Illustration A is a labeled diagram of the posteromedial corner.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The anterior cruciate ligament is responsible for resisting anterior tibial translation and tibial internal rotation near full extension.
Answer 2: The posterolateral corner functions to resist varus loads and tibial external rotation.
Answer 4: While the POL does resist valgus and tibial internal rotation forces, it is most active near full extension and is relatively lax as the degree of knee flexion increases.
Answer 5: The POL is most active near full extension and most studies agree its function relates more to resisting internal tibial rotation than external rotation.
