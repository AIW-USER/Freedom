## Question

A 50-year-old man undergoes an arthroscopic shoulder surgery to repair a large right supraspinatus tear. Preoperatively, he received a supraclavicular nerve block by the anesthesiologist with bupivacaine, and also underwent general endotracheal anesthesia with video laryngoscopy. During the case, his vital signs remained stable. Postoperatively, he complains of moderate dyspnea in the recovery unit and is started on supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula for decreased blood oxygen saturation. Chest X-ray reveals no abnormal findings. What is the most likely etiology of his dyspnea?

## Options

1. Pneumothorax
2. Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis
3. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) syndrome
4. Tracheal injury
5. Arthroscopic fluid extravasation

## Correct option

**2. Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis**

## Answer explanation

This patient received a supraclavicular nerve block and is likely experiencing hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (HDP) due to accidental involvement of the phrenic nerve (Answer 2). Although a chest x-ray sometimes demonstrates an elevated hemidiaphragm, a dynamic fluoroscopic "sniff test" is often required to visualize this condition with imaging.

Brachial plexus regional blocks are effective modalities to control surgical pain intraoperatively and postoperatively, in addition to regional anesthesia techniques. Supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and costoclavicular approaches can be utilized to approach the brachial plexus. The phrenic nerve, comprised of the anterior branches of the C3-C5 nerve roots, lies on the surface of the anterior scalene muscle underneath the sternocleidomastoid muscle and is near the brachial plexus. Inadvertent blockade of the phrenic nerve leads to transient HDP, which in most cases can be treated expectantly; however, frail, elderly, or obese patients may experience more significant respiratory compromise. The supraclavicular approach to brachial plexus blockade carries a higher risk of phrenic nerve involvement than the infraclavicular or costoclavicular approaches. Before the regular utilization of ultrasound, the risk of block complications was much higher than contemporary methods regardless of the approach.

*Petrar et al. performed a randomized controlled trial investigating the risk of HDP in contemporary ultrasound-guided blocks with 30ccs of 0.5% ropivacaine.* *They compared the supraclavicular and infraclavicular approaches.* *Eleven of 32 patients receiving supraclavicular blocks and only 1 of 32 patients receiving infraclavicular blocks sustained HDP.* **The authors concluded** that infraclavicular approaches reduce the risk of phrenic nerve involvement but do not eliminate it.

*Hong et al. conducted a randomized prospective trial with 80 patients investigating the incidence of HDP in patients undergoing supraclavicular versus costoclavicular blocks.* *They defined HDP as less than 20% of fractional change in the diaphragm thickness on ultrasound M-mode.* Almost 50% of patients with supraclavicular blocks met their criterion for HDP, while only 11% of the costoclavicular group met this same criterion. **They recommended** the use of costoclavicular blocks to reduce the risk of HDP.

*Aliste et al. performed another randomized prospective trial investigating the efficacy of analgesia and the incidence of complications between interscalene blocks and costoclavicular blocks.* Both blocks had equivalent analgesic effects for up to 24 hours. There was a higher incidence of HDP in the interscalene group. **They concluded** that costoclavicular blocks are safe and effective in upper extremity surgery with minimal risks of HDP.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Pneumothorax is a risk of brachial plexus blocks, but has an exceedingly low incidence. Additionally, a clinically significant pneumothorax will be more likely to be visible on chest x-ray as a lucency around the edge of the lung in the upper costal margin.
Answer 3: LAST syndrome presents with perioral numbness, metallic taste, confusion, and more serious CNS derangements including hypertension and tachycardia and subsequently bradycardia and hypotension.
Answer 4: Tracheal injury from endotracheal anesthesia is possible, but rare, and unlikely to present with isolated dyspnea.
Answer 5: Arthroscopic fluid extravasation is more likely to present with extremity swelling, pain, and neurologic symptoms in the affected extremity.
