## Question

A 7-year-old boy presents for evaluation of low back pain. The pain has been persistent for 2 weeks and has gradually gotten worse. He admits to fevers and chills. Labs reveal an ESR of 56 mm/hr, WBC of 8.5K and CRP of 4 mg/dl. Imaging studies are shown in Figures A and B. Which of the following is true of the most likely diagnosis in this patient?

## Options

1. Group B strep is the most likely common inciting organism
2. Likely caused by direct inoculation of bacteria
3. ESR is the best indicator for response to treatment
4. His current CRP levels place him at increased risk for venous thromboembolism
5. WBC counts are often within normal limits

## Correct option

**5. WBC counts are often within normal limits**

## Answer explanation

The patient's clinical course and imaging are consistent with vertebral osteomyelitis. White blood cell (WBC) counts are elevated in less than 25% of cases.

Osteomyelitis in the pediatric population occurs more often in boys (2.5x higher rate than in girls) and at a mean age of 6.6 years. The most common mechanism is hematogenous seeding to the bone from bacteremia caused by staph aureus. Diagnosis is based on clinical exam, laboratory studies, and diagnostic imaging. MRI studies will show decreased T1 signal with an accompanying increase in T2 signal. WBC counts are generally within normal limits. ESR and CRP are often elevated and the latter is often used for response to treatment which generally involves intravenous antibiotics with or without surgery.

*Kaplan performed a review of the diagnosis and treatment of osteomyelitis in children.* *The author cites osteomyelitis in children as a common cause of hospitalization and prolonged antibiotic administration.* **The author concludes** that increasing microbial virulence, diminishing antibiotic susceptibility, and advances in diagnostic molecular microbiology and imaging techniques have led to changes in the clinical management of osteomyelitis in children.

Song and Sloboda review the role of staph aureus in acute hematogenous osteomyelitis in children. The authors note that s.aureus, which has the ability to bind to cartilage, produce a protective glycocalyx, and stimulate the release of endotoxins, accounts for 90% of infections in all age groups. The authors also cite that sequential intravenous and high-dose oral antibiotic therapy is now an accepted modality with response to treatment being monitored by CRP levels. The authors conclude that long-term sequelae and morbidity in pediatric osteomyelitis are primarily due to delays in diagnosis and inadequate treatment.

*Dormans and Moroz performed an overview of infections and tumors in the pediatric spine.* **The authors note** that while spinal tumors and infection are uncommon in children, a high index of suspicion should be maintained when evaluating a child who presents with back pain. **The authors conclude** that a thorough understanding of the characteristic clinical and radiographic presentations of these conditions in children can facilitate a
timely diagnosis and lead to early intervention, better outcomes, and decreased mortality for children with spinal tumors and infection

Figures A and B are the sagittal T2 and T1-weighted MRI image w/ gadolinium that show diffuse enhancement in the L2–L3 vertebral bodies and intervening disk space consistent with vertebral osteomyelitis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Staph aureus is the most common inciting organism in pediatric osteomyelitis.
Answer 2: The most common cause of osteomyelitis is due to hematogenous seeding, not direct inoculation.
Answer 3: CRP, not ESR, is the most useful indicator for the response to treatment of pediatric osteomyelitis.
Answer 4: CRP levels > 6 mg/dl place a patient with pediatric osteomyelitis at increased risk for DVT
