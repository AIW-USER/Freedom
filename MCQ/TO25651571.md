Chapter: Metabolic Bone Disorder MCQs — Osteoporosis and Fragility Fractures

## Question

A 74-year-old female with severe osteoporosis and a history of a T11 vertebral fragility fracture is being evaluated for the long-term sequencing of her pharmacological regimen. Which of the following statements regarding the discontinuation and sequential transitioning of her targeted osteoporosis therapy is most accurate according to current evidence?

## Options

1. If she completes a 24-month course of teriparatide, she can safely enter a 12-month drug holiday, as the newly formed osteoid is structurally stable and will maintain its density.
2. If she discontinues denosumab after 4 years of continuous therapy, immediately initiating teriparatide is the optimal strategy to prevent rebound vertebral fractures and further increase cortical bone mass.
3. If she completes a 24-month course of teriparatide, transitioning immediately to denosumab will result in continued, robust increases in bone mineral density at both the lumbar spine and total hip.
4. If she discontinues denosumab after 3 years of continuous therapy, a single infusion of intravenous zoledronic acid should be administered 12 months after her last denosumab dose to suppress the anticipated bone turnover.

## Correct option

**3. If she completes a 24-month course of teriparatide, transitioning immediately to denosumab will result in continued, robust increases in bone mineral density at both the lumbar spine and total hip.**

## Answer explanation

Option C is CORRECT: This represents the highly effective "anabolic-to-antiresorptive" sequence. The DATA-Switch trial demonstrated that patients transitioning from 24 months of teriparatide directly to denosumab experienced continued, significant increases in bone mineral density (BMD) at the lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck. Denosumab effectively consolidates the newly formed, highly cellular woven bone generated by the parathyroid hormone analog.

Option A is INCORRECT: Discontinuing teriparatide without an immediate follow-up antiresorptive agent (bisphosphonate or denosumab) leads to rapid, progressive loss of the newly gained bone mass within the first year. A drug holiday immediately following anabolic therapy is absolutely contraindicated.

Option B is INCORRECT: The "antiresorptive-to-anabolic" sequence using denosumab followed by teriparatide is highly detrimental. Denosumab discontinuation triggers a massive surge in osteoclastogenesis. Teriparatide, which also stimulates bone remodeling, cannot suppress this rebound osteoclast activity. Clinical trials show that transitioning from denosumab to teriparatide results in a transient but significant decrease in BMD, particularly at cortical sites like the total hip and distal radius, and fails to protect against rebound vertebral fractures.

Option D is INCORRECT: The timing of the bisphosphonate relay after stopping denosumab is critical. Because the rebound effect of denosumab begins precisely as the effect of the last dose wears off, intravenous zoledronic acid must be administered exactly 6 months after the last denosumab injection (or up to 8 months if strictly guided by rising serum CTX levels). Waiting 12 months leaves the patient completely unprotected during the peak of the rebound phenomenon, exposing them to a high risk of multiple rebound-associated vertebral fractures. Furthermore, long-term denosumab users (>2.5 years) often require more than a single infusion of zoledronic acid to fully suppress the rebound.

Principles of stopping denosumab and teriparatide:

Teriparatide (PTH 1-34)

Maximum Duration: The standard course of Teriparatide is 24 months (2 years)
over a patient’s lifetime. While the FDA recently removed the absolute lifetime limit warning (the osteosarcoma black-box warning), a single continuous course remains standardized at 24 months due to a plateau in anabolic efficacy after this period.

What happens if stopped without follow-up? The new, highly cellular woven bone laid down by Teriparatide is rapidly resorbed. Within 12 months of stopping without an antiresorptive, the patient will lose almost all the bone mineral density (BMD) gained during the 2 years of daily injections.

Transition Strategy (What to give and how long): switch the patient to an antiresorptive agent immediately (within 1 to 3 months) after the last dose of Teriparatide to consolidate and mineralize the newly formed osteoid.

 - Option A: Bisphosphonates (First-line relay)

 - Drug: IV Zoledronic acid (5 mg annually) or Oral Alendronate (70 mg weekly).

 - Duration: Typically 1 to 2 years of IV Zoledronic acid, or 3 to 5 years of oral Alendronate. This allows for adequate mineralization of the teriparatide-induced matrix and maintains the BMD gains.

 - Option B: Denosumab

 - Drug: Denosumab (60 mg subcutaneously every 6 months).

 - Duration: Indefinite/long-term (often 5–10 years). The DATA-Switch trial demonstrated that transitioning from Teriparatide to Denosumab results in robust, progressive increases in BMD across all skeletal sites.

2. Denosumab (RANKL Inhibitor)

Maximum Duration: Unlike Teriparatide, Denosumab has no defined maximum duration. The FREEDOM extension trial has proven its safety, continued efficacy, and cumulative BMD gains for up to 10 continuous years. For patients with severe osteoporosis at high risk of fracture, guidelines support long-term, indefinite use.

What happens if stopped without follow-up? Stopping Denosumab triggers a severe, rapid rebound phenomenon. Because it does not incorporate into the bone matrix like bisphosphonates, its clearance removes the inhibition on osteoclasts. Within 6 to 12 months of a missed dose, patients experience a massive surge in bone turnover, accelerated bone loss, and a dramatically increased risk of Multiple Rebound-Associated Vertebral Fractures.

Transition Strategy (What to give and how long): If Denosumab must be stopped (e.g., patient preference, reaching a specific BMD target, or adverse events), a carefully timed transition to a bisphosphonate is mandatory. (Note: Teriparatide should NOT be used directly after Denosumab, as it induces transient, rapid bone loss at the hip and radius).

The European Calcified Tissue Society (ECTS) provides risk-stratified guidelines for this relay, depending on how long the patient was on Denosumab:

 - Short-Term Denosumab Use (< 2.5 years):

 - Drug: IV Zoledronic acid (ZA) or Oral Alendronate.

 - Timing: Administer ZA exactly 6 months after the last Denosumab injection (do not wait for the 7th month).

 - Duration/Monitoring: 1 year of oral Alendronate, or a single dose of ZA. Monitor bone turnover markers (specifically serum CTX) at 3 and 6 months post-infusion. If CTX rises significantly, a second ZA infusion is required.

 - Long-Term Denosumab Use (> 2.5 years):

 - Drug: IV Zoledronic acid or Oral Alendronate.

 - Timing: The rebound is much more aggressive in these patients. Administer ZA exactly 6 months after the last Denosumab dose.

 - Duration/Monitoring: A single dose of ZA is rarely sufficient to block the rebound in long-term users. They typically require 1 to 2 years of Oral Alendronate, or two or more infusions of ZA spaced 6–12 months apart, strictly guided by serial CTX monitoring.

Answer: 3
