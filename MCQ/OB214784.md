## Question

A 20-year-old minor league baseball pitcher is diagnosed with a mid-substance ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) tear in his pitching elbow. Nonsurgical management consisting of rest and physical therapy aimed at elbow strengthening, scapular stabilization, and pitching mechanics optimization has failed to provide relief. He has concomitant cubital tunnel symptoms that worsen while throwing. Physical examination reveals ulnar nerve subluxation with elbow flexion. What is his best surgical option?

## Options

1. UCL repair and nighttime elbow extension splinting
2. Autograft UCL reconstruction with ulnar nerve transposition
3. Allograft UCL reconstruction using a docking technique
4. UCL repair with ulnar nerve in-situ decompression
5. Autograft UCL reconstruction using a docking technique

## Correct option

**2. Autograft UCL reconstruction with ulnar nerve transposition**

## Answer explanation

High-level pitchers with symptomatic mid-substance UCL tears with associated cubital tunnel symptoms (and ulnar nerve subluxation) require UCL reconstruction with ulnar nerve transposition.

Attenuation or rupture of the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of the elbow is the leading cause of valgus instability in the overhead throwing athlete. Diagnosis is usually made by a combination of physical exam and MRI. Treatment for most individuals is rest and physical therapy, with surgery reserved for high-level overhead athletes such as pitchers. Outcomes and return to sport following surgical UCL reconstruction (Tommy John surgery) depends on precise recreation of the UCL and diligent rehabilitation with autograft being the best-studied graft selection. Newer techniques have shown excellent outcomes with 90% of athletes returning to pre-injury levels of throwing. In patients with concomitant pre-operative ulnar nerve symptoms, a simultaneous ulnar nerve decompression with or without transposition provides good results. In patients with ulnar nerve subluxation pre-op, a transposition is the best option.

*Azar et al. performed a cohort study of 91 UCL reconstructions (n =78) or repairs (n = 13) with an average follow-up of 35 months.* Subcutaneous ulnar nerve transposition with stabilization of the nerve with fascial slings of the flexor-pronator mass was performed in all patients. Ten patients had preoperative ulnar nerve symptoms, nine of whom had complete resolution of symptoms after surgery. Seventy nine percent (79%) of patients had returned to their previous levels of competition or to a higher level at final followup. **The authors conclude** that reconstruction of the UCL, with transposition and stabilization of the ulnar nerve and appropriate rehabilitation, was found to be effective in correcting medial instability of the elbow and allowed most athletes to return to previous levels of play in less than 1 year.

*Vitale and Ahmad performed a systematic review of UCL reconstruction in overhead athletes.* **The authors found** 8 level III studies with a minimum of 1-year follow-up and noted that 83% of patients in all studies had an excellent result. There was an overall 10% complication rate, with the most common complication being postoperative ulnar neuropathy, which occurred in 6% of patients. **The authors also noted** that abandoning obligatory ulnar nerve transposition was associated with better outcomes, as there was only a 75% rate of excellent results and a 9% rate of postoperative ulnar neuropathy in patients treated with obligatory ulnar nerve transposition compared with 89% excellent results and a 4% rate of postoperative ulnar neuropathy in patients who did not have obligatory ulnar nerve transposition.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1 and 4: UCL reconstruction has been shown to be superior to repair for the management of mid-substance UCL injuries in the overhead athlete.
Answer 3 and 5: While allograft/autograft UCL reconstruction may be appropriate for this patient, the ulnar nerve symptoms need to also be addressed with an ulnar nerve decompression with transposition (due to pre-op subluxation)
