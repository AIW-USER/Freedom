## Question

A 35-year-old recreational runner presents for evaluation of plantar heel pain. They deny any antecedent trauma or changes in footwear. After extensive nonoperative treatment fails to resolve symptoms, you perform a surgical release of the abductor hallucis fascia, and symptoms resolve. Decompression of a branch of which of the following nerves was most likely performed?

## Options

1. Deep peroneal nerve
2. Medial plantar nerve
3. Lateral plantar nerve
4. Superficial peroneal nerve
5. Posterior tibial nerve

## Correct option

**3. Lateral plantar nerve**

## Answer explanation

Release of the abductor hallucis fascia may be employed in cases of persistent symptoms arising from compression of the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve, also known as Baxter’s nerve (answer choice 3).

Entrapment of the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve, often termed Baxter's neuropathy, is a common but frequently misdiagnosed cause of chronic heel pain, often presenting with symptoms akin to plantar fasciitis. This nerve compression typically manifests as burning pain, tingling, or numbness along the medial plantar aspect of the foot, worsened by direct pressure or tapping to the area. The location of the pain and worsening with tapping along the plantar foot help distinguish it from the more truly plantar location characteristic of plantar fasciitis, which typically worsens with direct pressure over the calcaneal insertion as well as with dorsiflexion of the great toe. Although these conditions may coexist and may respond initially to conservative measures such as orthotics and anti-inflammatory agents, accurate differentiation through a comprehensive physical examination is critical when considering surgical management. In the case of refractory Baxter’s neuropathy, surgical release of the abductor hallucis fascia is necessary, contrasting with the partial plantar fasciotomy or gastrocnemius recession often considered for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis.

*Ferkel et al. reviewed the characteristics, diagnoses, and treatments of various entrapment neuropathies in the foot and ankle, including posterior and anterior tarsal tunnel syndromes, medial plantar nerve compression, Morton neuroma, and Baxter’s neuropathy, also known as distal tarsal tunnel syndrome.* **They emphasized** that distal tarsal tunnel syndrome is frequently encountered but often initially misdiagnosed as plantar fasciitis, while other neuropathies present with distinct symptoms and anatomical compression sites. **They concluded** that accurate clinical differentiation of these conditions is essential for appropriate management, which typically involves conservative measures like rest and splinting, with surgical decompression reserved for refractory cases.

*Latt et al. reviewed the evaluation and treatment of chronic plantar fasciitis, a prevalent cause of heel pain in adults attributed to repetitive stress and often associated with gastrocnemius tightness.* **They found** that CPF typically presents as aching heel pain, worse with initial steps or after rest, with diagnosis confirmed by point tenderness, but noted the importance of differentiating it from neurological etiologies like Baxter's neuropathy, often identifiable by abductor digiti minimi atrophy on MRI. **They concluded** that while initial nonoperative treatments achieve symptomatic relief in most patients, recalcitrant cases may necessitate minimally invasive interventions or surgery, with careful consideration of potential complications from interventions like corticosteroid injections.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: Entrapment of the deep peroneal nerve, known as anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome, typically occurs due to compression at the inferior extensor retinaculum.
Answer choice 3: Compression of the medial plantar nerve, known as Jogger’s Foot, typically occurs in runners with hyperpronation and excessive heel valgus. Compression is typically at the intersection of the flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus tendons (Knot of Henry), or externally from the medial arch of orthotics.
Answer choice 4: Compression of the superficial peroneal nerve may occur at multiple sites. In the lower leg, this is most commonly at due to entrapment as the nerve exits the lateral compartment at the deep crural fascia.
Answer choice 5: Entrapment of the posterior tibial nerve in referred to as tarsal tunnel syndrome. Compression occurs within the tarsal tunnel proper, a fibro-osseous tunnel directly inferior to the medial malleolus, and is typically the result of local fascial septae, the flexor retinaculum, or the abductor hallucis, which forms the plantar border of the tarsal tunnel.
