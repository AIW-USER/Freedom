## Question

A 19-year-old collegiate baseball pitcher presents to your office for a second opinion regarding an ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) tear. You review all the treatment options and he asks what difference, if any, there is between UCL reconstruction using the Jobe and Docking techniques. What is your advice?

## Options

1. Jobe technique has a higher return to play but higher rate of complications
2. Jobe technique has a lower return to play but lower rate of complications
3. No difference in outcomes or rate of complications
4. Docking technique has a higher return to play with lower rates of complications
5. Postoperative ulnar neuropathy rate is 20% regardless of technique

## Correct option

**4. Docking technique has a higher return to play with lower rates of complications**

## Answer explanation

The docking technique for ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction (UCLR) has been shown to have higher rates of return to play and a lower risk of complications when compared to the original Jobe technique.

UCLR techniques have undergone several modifications since originally described by Frank Jobe. The original Jobe technique utilized a flexor-pronator tenotomy, figure-of-8 autograft through ulnar and medial epicondyle drill tunnels, and routine submuscular ulnar nerve transposition. The modified Jobe technique aimed to decrease morbidity by implementing a muscle-splitting approach and avoiding routine ulnar nerve transposition. The Docking technique uses two converging ulnar tunnels and a single humeral docking tunnel. Advantages of the Docking technique include less ulnar nerve manipulation and flexor-pronator morbidity, as well as greater control of graft tensioning, with studies demonstrating higher rates of excellent results (by about 15%), a threefold decrease in all complications, and a 3 to 5% decrease in the rate of ulnar neuropathy post-operatively.

*Vitale et al performed a systematic review of outcomes of UCL reconstructions in overhead athletes.* Overall there was a 6% rate of postoperative ulnar neuropathy. The Jobe and Docking techniques had a 20% and 3% rate of postoperative ulnar neuropathy, respectively. **They noted** that the muscle-splitting approach, as opposed to detaching the flexor-pronator mass, decreased handling of the ulnar nerve, and the use of the docking technique resulted in better outcomes and lower complications.

*Watson et al conducted a systematic review comparing clinical outcomes and biomechanical results of various UCLR techniques.* *A total of 21 studies, including 7 biomechanical and 14 clinical studies were reviewed.* The Docking technique was associated with a higher rate of return to play and a lower complication rate compared to the modified Jobe technique.

*Gehrman et al provided an overview of the diagnosis and management of elbow UCL injuries.* Such injuries should be suspected in overhead athletes with medial elbow pain and decreased throwing performance. History, the physical exam and MRI are key diagnostic tools. Nonoperative treatment is the first line of treatment for most cases and partial tears. Operative treatment is indicated for high-level athletes and those who have failed nonoperative treatment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3: Jobe technique is associated with lower outcomes scores and higher rates of complication.
Answer 5: Postoperative ulnar neuropathy rates are less than 10%. The Docking technique is associated with lower rates of postoperative ulnar neuropathy compared to the Jobe technique.
