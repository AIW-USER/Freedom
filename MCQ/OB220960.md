## Question

A 6-year-old male presents to the emergency department with an elbow injury after a fall from the monkey bars. A radiograph of his elbow is shown in Figure A. On presentation, his hand is warm and pink, but you are unable to palpate a radial pulse. He is otherwise neurologically intact. You take the patient back to the operating room for closed reduction and percutaneous pinning. After pinning, you note the hand is now cold with sluggish capillary refill. After removing the pins, the hand remains cold and pale. What is the next best step?

![[q220960-a-g3.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Cast and admit the patient for observation
2. Re-attempt closed reduction and pinning
3. Apply a bair hugger and nitropaste to the patient's hand and wait for reperfusion
4. Open exploration and call vascular for possible repair/reconstruction
5. Transfer the patient to a pediatric trauma center; you are not equipped to deal with this

## Correct option

**4. Open exploration and call vascular for possible repair/reconstruction**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a Gartland Type III supracondylar humerus fracture. On initial presentation, he has a well-perfused hand but an absent radial pulse. As long as the hand clinically appears perfused during reduction and fixation, an open vascular exploration is not always necessary. However, if the hand begins to show signs of vascular compromise, particularly after reduction and pinning that persists after the pins are removed, vascular exploration is indicated (Answer 4).

Supracondylar humerus fractures are the most common traumatic fractures in children. They can be classified by the degree of displacement using the Gartland classification. A Type I fracture is non-displaced, and a subtle fat pad sign (radiolucency around the distal humerus) may be the only radiographic sign. A Type II fracture is displaced, but the posterior hinge remains intact; this type can be subdivided into Type IIA (no rotational component) and IIB (rotational component present). Type III is completely displaced, often with a rotational component. A type IV is rare and is characterized by intraoperative instability with both flexion and extension. The treatment for Type I and some Type IIA fractures is a long arm cast. Type IIB and above fractures are usually treated with either closed or open reduction and percutaneous pinning. Type IV fractures are more likely to require an open reduction. Assessment of vascular status is critical for determining the timing of surgery. In surgical patients with a palpable pulse and perfused hand, surgery can wait until the next morning, or the patient can even be discharged after a period of observation and scheduled for outpatient surgery. Patients with a perfused hand but a non-palpable radial pulse should be taken to the operating room either overnight or early the next morning. They are also typically observed postoperatively for signs of ischemia. If the patient develops signs of vascular compromise, emergent surgery is indicated, and consideration should be given to open vascular exploration if the hand does not regain perfusion after closed reduction. Indications for open vascular exploration include a pulseless white hand on presentation that does not improve with reduction and pinning, or if a previously perfused hand becomes poorly perfused after pinning.

*Badkoobehi et al. authored a review of the management of the pulseless hand in patients with supracondylar fractures.* *They describe the appropriate evaluation and management strategies of patients who present without a radial pulse in the setting of a supracondylar fracture.* **They note** that patients with poor perfusion should be taken emergently for reduction, and if perfusion does not improve, then vascular exploration with possible repair/reconstruction is indicated. **They also recommend** that in patients with a perfused hand, but no radial pulse after surgery, a period of 24-48 hours of observation should be performed to monitor for signs of ischemia before discharge. If ischemia develops, the vascular exploration is also indicated.

*Weller et al. conducted a retrospective review of 1297 patients who presented with a supracondylar fracture.* *Of these patients, 54 (4%) presented without a radial pulse.* *Of these, 9% underwent open exploration due to clinical findings of ischemia, and all of these patients ultimately required vascular intervention to restore blood flow.* In addition, 20/54 (37%) of the patients who presented without a radial pulse still did not have a radial pulse after reduction and fixation, though clinically the hands remained grossly perfused. These patients were observed postoperatively, and only one patient required a return to the operating room for open exploration, while the remainder regained pulses over time. **The authors concluded** that the lack of a radial pulse in a perfused hand is not an absolute indication for open exploration, but a brief period of observation should be performed postoperatively to monitor for signs of ischemia.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has signs of ischemia and needs emergent exploration. He will still require observation postoperatively.
Answer 2: A repeat reduction has a low rate of restoration of blood flow at this point and would delay exploration.
Answer 3: This is unlikely to be successful and only delays exploration.
Answer 5: This is inappropriate and will delay reperfusion to this patient’s hand.
