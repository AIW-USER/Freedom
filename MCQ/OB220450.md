## Question

A 55-year-old male presents 8 months after index total knee arthroplasty (TKA). He notes progressive anterior knee pain with difficulty descending stairs due to instability. The patient recovered without any complications and denies any wound healing issues. He regained his baseline range of motion rapidly in physical therapy after surgery. His inflammatory markers, including ESR and CRP, are mildly elevated, prompting aspiration. Aspiration yields 300 polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) with 60% neutrophils and many red blood cells. Radiographs are shown in Figures A-C with relevant radiographic measurements. A revision procedure is planned with conversion to a posterior-stabilized TKA design. Which of the following additional revision strategies is most likely to improve the patient's symptoms?

## Options

1. Reducing the size of the femoral component, use of distal femoral augments, and downsizing the polyethylene component
2. External rotation of the tibial tray and upsizing the polyethylene component
3. Isolated increase in size of polyethylene component
4. Additional distal femur resection, use of posterior femoral augments, and upsizing the polyethylene component
5. Internal rotation of the tibial tray and increase of tibial slope

## Correct option

**4. Additional distal femur resection, use of posterior femoral augments, and upsizing the polyethylene component**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with flexion instability due to multiple etiologies, including a distalized joint line (leading to flexion-extension gap mismatch), reduced posterior condylar offset, excessive tibial slope, and potential incompetence of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL). Several interventions can be used to address flexion instability at the time of revision. The flexion gap can be tightened with posterior femoral augments and upsizing of the polyethylene, while the extension gap is balanced with additional distal femur resection (Answer 4).

Instability after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the major reasons for revision surgery. However, diagnosis of the underlying cause is critical for a successful revision, as instability may be multifactorial. Furthermore, instability can be global or affect the patient at a specific range of motion. The most common causes of instability include ligamentous dysfunction/incompetence, component malpositioning, or polyethylene wear. After infection has been ruled out, careful examination and radiographic evaluation can aid in diagnosis and plan for revision. Patients with flexion instability may be asymptomatic, but will often note anterior knee pain (peripatellar or pes anserine bursitis) due to excessive strain on the anterior soft tissues, along with difficulty descending stairs and recurrent joint effusions. Radiographs aid in determining whether the femur (lack of posterior condylar offset, distalized joint line), tibia (excessive tibial slope), or both contribute to an increased flexion gap.

*Abdel et al. published on flexion instability after TKA with a retrospective review of 60 patients undergoing revision with well-fixed components and a mean follow-up of 3.6 years.* **The authors note** that distalization of the joint line, increased tibial slope, and decreased posterior condylar offset (all p < 0.001) contributed to mismatch of flexion and extension gaps with flexion instability. After revision, patients obtained a combined mean correction of 9.5 mm posterior condylar offset and joint line resection along with 5 degrees tibial slope reduction. A stepwise approach to revision is recommended as follows: (1) neutralization of tibial slope, (2) correction of component malalignment/sizing, (3) increase condylar offset, and (4) elevation of the joint line.

*Kannan et al. analyzed 37 patients retrospectively undergoing revision TKA for flexion instability.* *The authors addressed the lack of posterior condylar offset by using the largest femoral component possible, with the addition of posterior augments if necessary.* Additionally, excessive tibial slope was reduced if present. After intervention, the patients had significant improvement in Knee Society Scores as well as satisfaction on a 7-point Likert scale. In conclusion, **the authors state** that revision for flexion instability can be successful in improving patient-reported outcomes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Reducing the size of the femoral component will reduce the anteroposterior dimension of the implant, further widening the flexion gap. Reducing the size of the polyethylene will loosen both the flexion and extension gaps. Finally, the addition of distal femoral augments will serve to tighten the flexion gap, rather than the extension gap.
Answer 2: External rotation of the tibial tray may be helpful in cases of patellar maltracking, but it will not affect the flexion gap. Upsizing the polyethylene will tighten the flexion gap, but may also excessively tighten the extension gap, leading to further gap mismatch.
Answer 3: Isolated increase in polyethylene will tighten the flexion gap, but may excessively tighten the extension gap. Over time, there may be attritional injury/stretching of the periarticular soft tissues due to an overly tight extension gap, creating additional flexion instability.
Answer 5: Internal rotation of the tibial tray will not address the flexion gap and may lead to patellar maltracking. Furthermore, an increase in tibial slope may further loosen the flexion gap.
