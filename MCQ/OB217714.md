## Question

An 18-year-old male presents to the ED after a football injury where he sustained a hit directly to the knee. Radiographs in the trauma bay demonstrate an anterior knee dislocation without obvious associated fracture. On examination of the patient, he has 2+ DP/PT pulses and 2 second capillary refill on the injured extremity. You then perform an expedient closed reduction of the knee under sedation and confirm concentricity of the knee joint on radiographs. The patient remains neurovascularly intact after this reduction. Ankle-brachial index is obtained and measures 0.8. What is the next most appropriate step in evaluating this patient?

## Options

1. Serial pulse checks
2. CT Angiography
3. Serial ankle-brachial index (ABI) measurements
4. Obtain MRI
5. Check pulse oximeter measurement of toes

## Correct option

**2. CT Angiography**

## Answer explanation

When the ankle-brachial index (ABI) is abnormal (<0.9) after knee dislocation reduction, CT angiography should be performed.

Knee dislocations are typically high energy injuries that have a relatively large percentage of associated neurovascular injury. In fact, the incidence of vascular injury and nerve injury are both around 25% in the setting of a knee dislocation. On presentation, a thorough neurovascular examination should be performed and radiographs should be scrutinized for associated fractures (occur in 60%). After closed reduction, neurovascular examination should again be undertaken. If pulses remain normal and equal to the contralateral side, the next step is to obtain an ABI. This is because, given the significant collateral circulation at the knee, vascular injury may not cause a change in pulse quality. If ABI is <0.9, emergent CT angiography should be performed. If pulses are absent or diminished immediately after reduction, immediate surgical exploration should be strongly considered given the short amount of time before the leg becomes ischemic.

*Weinberg et al. studied whether vascular injury was able to be accurately assessed by means of physical examination after knee dislocation.* *They denoted a "normal physical examination," to mean that both pulses and ABI were normal and found 100% sensitivity for ruling out vascular injury when pulses were palpable and ABI was >0.9.* **They concluded** that the combination of these exam findings can be used accurately in ruling out vascular injury after knee dislocation.

*Mills et al. prospectively reviewed the use of ABIs in diagnosing arterial injury after a knee dislocation.* *They studied 38 patients,* **noting** that the sensitivity and negative predictive value of ABIs in demonstrating vascular injury were both 100%. **They concluded** that given these findings, ABI is a quick and reliable tool for assisting in the diagnosis of vascular injury after knee dislocation and should be used routinely before other more invasive studies.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Serial pulse checks may be performed, but given abnormal ABIs, angiography would be recommended.
Answer 3: Serial ABIs do have a high negative predictive value if normal (>0.9), but if abnormal, further testing should be employed.
Answer 4: An MRI may be useful to evaluate the ligamentous injury associated with the knee dislocation, but would not be helpful in the setting of vascular injury and may delay appropriate care.
Answer 5: Normal pulse oximetry readings do not rule out a vascular injury given the large degree of collateral circulation about the knee. Consideration of invasive testing or surgical exploration is recommended in the setting of abnormal ABI.
