## Question

A 20-year-old male college football player presents to your clinic with complaints of acute right knee pain. It has been present for the last 2 weeks after he awkwardly twisted his knee in a game. No prior issues with the knee. He notes his pain is primarily over the anteromedial joint line and he feels a "catching," sensation when he extends the knee. On exam, he is tender over the medial joint line and has a moderate size effusion. His lachman and posterior drawer are negative and he has no increase in varus/valgus laxity at 0/30°. Radiographs and MRI demonstrate an unstable osteochondral lesion measuring 1.5 x 3 cm^2 with no chronic bony changes. What is the best management strategy for this patient?

## Options

1. Physical therapy and corticosteroid injection
2. Arthroscopic assisted internal fixation of lesion
3. Debridement and abrasion arthroplasty
4. Matrix autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI)
5. Osteochondral autograft transplantation (OATS)

## Correct option

**2. Arthroscopic assisted internal fixation of lesion**

## Answer explanation

He has a large, unstable osteochondral lesion (OCD) for which arthroscopic assisted internal fixation of the lesion should be attempted.

Osteochondral lesions can occur in skeletally immature or adult patients, with those occurring in adults more frequently requiring intervention. The most common location for an OCD is the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle. Symptoms include persistent effusion, joint line tenderness, and in some cases, mechanical symptoms. MRI is helpful in characterizing the size of the lesion, the status of the subchondral bone, and the stability of the lesion. If there is good bone still attached to the lesion and it is acute, every effort should be made to fixate the fragment back to its native bed using either metal or biocomposite screws. In more chronic cases or those where the tissue quality is poor or no bone is attached, loose body removal and debridement with plans for later cartilage restoration are appropriate.

*Kocher reviewed the management for knee OCDs,* **noting** that lesions in skeletally mature patients are more likely to be unstable than juvenile lesions. **They note** that the majority of adult OCD cases and all cases with unstable fragments require an attempt at fixation of the lesion. In the setting of more chronic, loose fragments, healing potential is more questionable and removal with employment of other cartilage techniques may be recommended.

*Schulz reviewed juvenile OCDs noting that nonsurgical management is still appropriate for young patients with stable lesions.* However, surgical management is gaining popularity in certain scenarios in order to encourage vascularity and healing in the area of the lesion. They also agree that unstable and displaced lesions usually represent a difficult, surgical issue.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: With an unstable lesion causing mechanical symptoms, physical therapy and injections are not appropriate. Surgical management should be employed, with an effort to fixate the fragment.
Answer 3: In an acutely injured osteochondral lesion, every effort should be made to fixate the fragment, rather than removal and debridement.
Answers 4: MACI is an option for large chondral defects with a supportive subchondral bone bed, not often seen in OCD lesions
Answer 5: Osteochondral autograft is an option in the setting of osteochondral lesions less than 2x2cm that are unable to be salvaged or chronic cartilage wear but in this setting, fixation would be preferred.
