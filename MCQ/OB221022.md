## Question

## Options

1. Non-weight-bearing cast for 6-8 weeks
2. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning
3. Open reduction and lag screw fixation
4. External fixation to maintain the current position of the fracture and prevent lateral column shortening
5. Primary talonavicular joint fusion

## Correct option

**3. Open reduction and lag screw fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an acute, displaced tarsal navicular fracture. With this amount of displacement, open reduction and lag screw fixation will provide the best construct to provide proper fracture healing (Answer 3).

Tarsal navicular fractures are relatively rare fractures in the midfoot that may occur secondary to repetitive microstress or acute trauma. Fracture characteristics are related to the mechanism of injury and can present as an avulsion fracture, tuberosity fracture, or body fracture in traumatic injuries. Diagnosis is made clinically in addition to plain radiographs or advanced imaging in nondisplaced fractures or stress reactions. Nondisplaced fractures and stress reactions are initially treated with cast immobilization for 6-8 weeks without weight-bearing. Open reduction and internal fixation is indicated with avulsion fractures involving >25% of the articular surface, tuberosity fractures with >5mm of diastasis or a large intra-articular fragment, and displaced or intra-articular body fractures.

*Attia et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis including 11 studies with 315 (97.5% athletes) navicular stress fractures (NSF).* *34.3% of the NSFs were treated operatively with a mean success rate of 97.9%, and 65.7% were treated conservatively with a mean success rate of 78.1% (OR=5.52, p=0.004).* Rates of return to play were not significantly different between the operative or conservative groups (98.98% vs 73.43%, respectively; OR=2.789, p=0.142). Refracture rate was significantly lower in the operative group than in the conservative group (1.28% vs 23.53%, respectively; OR=0.083, p=0.047). **They conclude** that operative management of NSFs results in a higher success rate, a lower refracture rate, and a lower nonunion rate than conservative treatment modalities.

*Patel et al. published a JAAOS review article on navicular stress fractures (NSF).* **They report** that NSFs occur secondary to repetitive microstresses and chronic overload on the navicular bone, particularly in young athletes. The navicular bone has a unique blood supply, which predisposes this tarsal bone to stress reactions and delayed union/nonunion. **They report** that advanced imaging can help diagnose these injuries more quickly, preventing delays in treatment and, therefore, poor outcomes. **The authors conclude** that it is important to properly counsel these patients on expectations for these challenging injuries.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the right foot demonstrating a navicular body fracture with diastasis of the fracture fragments in the midportion of the body. Figure B is a lateral radiograph of the right foot demonstrating a navicular body fracture without dorsal displacement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Cast immobilization for 6–8 weeks is appropriate for nondisplaced navicular body fractures or stress reactions. This patient has a displaced navicular body fracture with diastasis, which requires operative stabilization to restore medial column length and articular congruity.
Answer 2: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning is insufficient in the setting of significant fracture displacement and diastasis. Open reduction allows direct visualization to achieve anatomic reduction, and lag screw fixation provides interfragmentary compression, which is critical for promoting union in navicular body fractures.
Answer 4: Definitive external fixation can be considered for highly comminuted navicular fractures or cases with substantial medial column shortening where maintaining column length is challenging. However, in an isolated displaced body fracture without severe comminution, internal fixation provides more stable and anatomic restoration.
Answer 5: Primary talonavicular arthrodesis is generally reserved for cases with preexisting talonavicular arthritis or nonreconstructable articular destruction. In this patient, there is no radiographic or clinical evidence of talonavicular osteoarthritis, making joint-preserving fixation the preferred treatment.
