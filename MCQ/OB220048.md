## Question

A 12-year-old female soccer player is brought to the office by her parents with atraumatic, vague left knee pain that is activity-related and started about 3 months ago. The patient has not tried anything up to this point, and her pain has slowly worsened, leading to an antalgic gait with recurrent knee effusions. Plain radiographs demonstrate age-appropriate, open physes, and an MRI scan shows a stable 0.5 cm x 0.5 cm OCD lesion in the left medial femoral condyle. She begins conservative treatment, including restricted weight-bearing and bracing. What characteristics warrant initial nonoperative management?

## Options

1. High T2 signal deep to the fragment on MRI with closed physes
2. Lesion with a fracture through the subchondral plate and closed physes
3. Absence of increased T2 signal deep to lesion with open physes
4. 5-mm fluid-filled cyst deep to a 2cm x 2cm lesion and closed physes
5. >2cm x 2cm lesion with displacement of fragment and open physes

## Correct option

**3. Absence of increased T2 signal deep to lesion with open physes**

## Answer explanation

Restricted weight-bearing and bracing is recommended for stable, juvenile osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) lesions, which Answer 3 describes best.

OCD is a lesion affecting the articular cartilage and subchondral bone presenting with various clinical patterns. These lesions are most commonly found in the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle and typically cause activity-related symptoms. Stability is an important factor when deciding treatment options for OCD lesions. Stability has been defined by the absence of increased T2 signal deep to the fragment on MRI, an articular fracture through the subchondral plate, a focal osteochondral defect, or a 5-mm fluid-filled cyst deep to the lesion. Workup typically includes plain radiographs and an MRI to assess the size and stability of the lesion. Most importantly, the presence of an open physis is the best predictor of success in conservative management.

*Bruns et al. published a literature review regarding the etiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of OCD lesions in the knee.* **They found** that spontaneous healing is expected in low-grade stable lesions, especially in those with open physis. Treatment involves rest and immobilization until the lesions have healed.

*Yang et al. reviewed the current literature on nonoperative treatment of knee OCD lesions.* **They noted** that lesion size, location, and stability along with symptoms, should be considered before initiating treatment. Conservative management ranges from full activity modification to complete immobilization and close follow-up is recommended to monitor healing progression.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, 4, and 5: Stable lesions are defined by the absence of increased T2 signal deep to the fragment on MRI, an articular fracture through the subchondral plate, or a 5-mm fluid-filled cyst deep to the lesion. Also, large OCD lesions (>2cm in size) with displacement, are better fixed surgically.
