## Question

A 43-year-old female presents to the office with complaints of numbness and tingling in her right hand. She states that it often wakes her in the middle of the night and her job performance is starting to take a toll due to her lack of sleep. Her abnormal physical exam finding is shown in Figure A. An EMG study reveals the involved nerve is being compressed proximal to the wrist. Release of which of the following structures would provide her with symptomatic relief?

![[q218953-a-real-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Fibrous bands superficial to the radial head
2. A radial recurrent artery and its vena comitans
3. Fibrous edge of the extensor carpi radialis brevis
4. Fascial band along the supinator musculature
5. The border of the flexor pronator mass musculature

## Correct option

**5. The border of the flexor pronator mass musculature**

## Answer explanation

A common site of compression of the ulnar nerve in cubital tunnel syndrome is between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris. The humeral head originates from the medial epicondyle, forming the ulnar border of the flexor pronator mass, while the ulnar head originates from the posterior border of the ulna.

Cubital tunnel syndrome is a compressive neuropathy of the ulnar nerve along the medial aspect of the elbow. Patients will complain of numbness and tingling in their ring finger and small finger, which can disrupt typical sleep patterns. Physical examination may reveal the aforementioned sensory deficits, weakness in pinch strength, positive Froment sign (i.e. Figure A/Illustration A), and even loss of intrinsic musculature (Illustration B). Common sites of anatomic compression, in order from proximal to distal, include the Arcade of Struthers (i.e. fibrous band as the ulnar nerve exits adjacent to tricep muscles), between Osborne’s fascia and the medial collateral ligament (i.e. the cubital tunnel), and the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris (Illustration C). Release of these structures resolves the vast majority of cases of ulnar neuropathy. There are other, far less common, sites of compression and also non-anatomic causes for compression which should be taken into consideration prior to ulnar neurolysis (i.e. heterotopic ossification, ganglion cysts, post-traumatic structural changes).

*Thakker and colleagues present a review article regarding anatomy review and treatment options for cubital tunnel syndrome.* **They discuss** that treatment for mild-moderate cases involves activity modifications and elbow extension splinting. Patients who fail to respond to conservative treatment and severe cases with chronic changes (i.e. muscle wasting, Illustration B) should be treated with cubital tunnel release. They further discuss risk factors for failed cubital tunnel release and considerations during revision cases. **The authors conclude** that early recognition and treatment are paramount to preventing chronic ulnar neuropathy which often incurs permanent deficits.

*Andrews and colleagues present a similar review article regarding the anatomy, clinical presentation, and management of cubital tunnel syndrome.* *They discuss the major sites of compression and stress their importance of release during ulnar nerve neurolysis along the medial aspect of the elbow.* **They similarly conclude** that timely recognition and treatment are paramount to good clinical outcomes and avoiding irreversible muscle atrophy and functional deficits.

Figure A/Illustration A shows a positive Froment sign (i.e. engagement of flexor pollicis longus due to the weak, ulnar-innervated adductor pollicis muscle). Illustration B shows 1st webspace atrophy, a sign of chronic ulnar neuropathy. The radial border of the second metacarpal can be easily visualized due to the lack of adjacent intrinsic musculature. Illustration C highlights the common areas of compression of the ulnar nerve along the medial aspect of the elbow. Illustration D displays the radial tunnel and the most common areas of the posterior interosseous nerve which lies just a few centimeters lateral to the cubital tunnel within the proximal forearm.

Incorrect Answer Choices:
Answers 1,2,3,4: The radial nerve can similarly be compressed (i.e. posterior interosseous nerve syndrome) along its anatomic course within the radial tunnel. The most common sites of compression within this tunnel can be recalled using the mnemonic “FLEAS.” (1) Fibrous bands just superficial to radial head (2) Leash of Henry, a radial recurrent artery and its vena comitans (3) Edge of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (4) Arcade of Frohse, a fascial band along the edge of the superficial supinator and (5) Supinator muscle itself (Illustration D).
