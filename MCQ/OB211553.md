## Options

## Correct option

**2. Figure B**

## Answer explanation

Figure B is an axial CT scan demonstrating bilateral facet dislocations, which is associated with more severe initial neurologic injury and inferior outcomes compared to patients with cervical spine injuries without facet dislocations.

Facet dislocations are typically caused by a flexion-distraction force, progressing in severity from subluxation, to unilateral, bilateral, and finally complete dislocation. CT is essential to defining the bony injury, while MRI may demonstrate associated disc herniations, degree of posterior ligamentous injury, and potential injury to the spinal cord. External immobilization is often insufficient due to the inherently unstable nature of these injuries, necessitating anterior or posterior surgical stabilization in most cases.

*Wilson et al. evaluated the outcomes following cervical spinal cord injury with an without associated facet dislocation.* **The authors found** that patients with facet dislocations not only had more severe neurologic injury on presentation but demonstrated significantly less potential for motor recovery at final follow-up. Furthermore, bilateral facet dislocations were more severe in this regard than unilateral facet dislocations.

*Kwon et al. review the presentation, management, and outcomes of subaxial cervical spine trauma.* **The authors note** that facet fracture and dislocation represents a spectrum of osteoligamentous pathology and that the radiographic appearance of facet injuries corresponds with the varying degree of bony and soft tissue disruption. **They conclude** that due to the degree of instability afforded by these injuries, facet dislocations are most commonly managed with arthrodesis.

Incorrect answers:
Figure A is an axial CT scan showing a unilateral right-sided facet dislocation with the characteristic "reverse hamburger bun" sign. The left facet joint has a normal appearance. This has been shown to be associated with improved outcomes as compared to bilateral facet dislocations.
Figure C demonstrates a minimally displaced "floating lateral mass" fracture. While this is an unstable injury and may be associated with radiculopathy, this has not been shown to lead to inferior clinical outcomes.
Figure D shows a unilateral pedicle fracture. This is generally a stable injury and not associated with neurologic injury or poor long-term outcomes.
Figure E reveals a spinous process fracture, or "clay-shoveler" fracture. This is usually an isolated, stable injury and is often not associated with any significant morbidity.
