## Question

A 33-year-old right-hand dominant male guitarist presents to your office with difficulty flexing the DIP joint of his right ring finger. He has just returned from a six-month mission trip abroad and recalls an injury at the beginning of his trip while playing soccer where the DIP was forcefully extended. On examination, he is able to flex the MCP and PIP joints of his ring finger when making a fist, but his DIP joint remains extended. There is a prominent lump palpated proximal to the MCP joint of his ring finger. You are able to passively range the DIP joint into 55º of flexion and full extension and the MCP/PIP joints have a full range of motion. Given this patient's injury and occupation, what is the best treatment strategy?

## Options

1. Extension blocking splint for 6-8 weeks
2. Direct end to end repair
3. Tendon advancement to distal phalanx using button
4. Two stage flexor tendon reconstruction
5. DIP arthrodesis

## Correct option

**4. Two stage flexor tendon reconstruction**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a chronic injury (>3 months) to the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon and two-stage flexor tendon reconstruction would be the most appropriate treatment option.

FDP avulsion injuries, better known as jersey fingers, occur most commonly to the ring finger when the digits are in a resisted flexion position. After an injury, patients are unable to flex the DIP joint when making a fist. The level of retraction and chronicity of the injury are important factors when indicating patients for the appropriate procedure. Ideally, these injuries should acutely (<1 wk) be repaired either end to end or reinserted to the distal phalanx with a button. However, with increasing chronicity, this method of repair is less reliable and flexor tendon reconstruction or DIP arthrodesis should be considered. Flexor tendon reconstruction is indicated in patients with tears > 3 months old in which patients have a full passive range of motion of the DIP and surrounding joints. Flexor tendon reconstruction can be successful, but compared to acute repair, these patients have poorer outcomes with high rates of stiffness.

*Imbriglia et al. report on FDP ruptures or the small finger in 10 patients,* **noting** all injuries occurred in resisted flexion. Seven of the patients were treated surgically and achieved satisfactory results. They did note that the patients who had an early direct repair had better results than those who underwent late reconstruction.

Leddy and Packer discuss FDP avulsion injuries in 36 athletes, noting the ring finger to be the most common culprit. They classified these injuries three ways, (1) presence of bony fragment, (2) level of retraction, and (3) status of blood supply of tendon. They note that the more proximally retracted the tendon is at the time of injury, the quicker the repair should be performed in order to achieve the best results.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: FDP ruptures will not heal appropriately with nonoperative management and the patient will not regain function in his DIP joint.
Answer 2: Direct end-to-end tendon repair is likely to be under too much tension in a chronic flexor tendon injury to provide a successful, stable repair.
Answer 3: Given the chronicity of the injury, it is unlikely that the tendon will be able to be advanced from the palm to the distal phalanx and would have an unacceptably high failure rate regardless.
Answer 5: DIP joint arthrodesis is a viable option for patients with chronic FDP avulsion injuries, however in a young patient who is a guitarist and who has a supple finger, two-stage flexor tendon reconstruction would be a more appropriate option.
