## Question

You are a junior resident on call in the emergency department. You receive a call consulting you with concern for rupture of the extensor mechanism in a patient with a total knee arthroplasty (TKA). In which of the following clinical scenarios after TKA would nonoperative treatment be appropriate?

## Options

1. Acute rupture of the patellar tendon with large wound dehiscence after a fall
2. Rupture of the quadriceps tendon with 45 degrees extensor lag
3. Partial quadriceps rupture, also having coronal instability that has made it difficult to ambulate for the past six months
4. Rupture of the patellar tendon with 30 degrees extensor lag
5. Chronic widely displaced patella fracture nonunion with 5 degrees extensor lag

## Correct option

**5. Chronic widely displaced patella fracture nonunion with 5 degrees extensor lag**

## Answer explanation

Though a widely displaced patella fracture is concerning, the patient in Answer 5 has minimal extensor lag. Fracture fixation (with or without extensor mechanism reconstruction) in this scenario would be of questionable benefit for the patient and has the potential for significant harm with regard to complications related to surgery.

Extensor mechanism disruptions after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are rare, devastating complications. Disruption can come about through tears of the quadriceps and patellar tendons, as well as fractures of the patella. Disruption to any portion of the extensor mechanism after TKA has been attributed to trauma, unrecognized iatrogenic injury during initial TKA, malpositioned implants, cutting the patella too thin (<12mm), and/or local vascular disruption in the multiply-revised patient. In cases of tendinous disruptions, primary repair has consistently been shown to result in high rates of failure due to re-rupture or persistent extension lag. Therefore, reconstruction serves as the current standard of care for these patients. Both allograft and synthetic mesh serve as acceptable surgical options for tendinous extensor mechanism disruptions after TKA. Unfortunately, even with reconstruction, there remains a considerable risk for failure or functionally limiting, persistent extensor lag (~25% of cases), and this should be discussed with patients before surgery.

*Ortiguera et al. reviewed 85 patella fractures at their institution following 12,464 consecutive TKAs.* **They found** that 38 of the 85 patella fractures had a stable patellar component and an intact extensor mechanism. Of these, all but one were treated nonoperatively, and there was only a single failure that required operative treatment. Twelve fractures were associated with extensor mechanism disruption; 11 of these were treated operatively, of which five required reoperation. Twenty-eight fractures occurred in association with a loose patellar component; 20 of these were treated operatively of which four had a complication requiring reoperation. **They concluded** that treatment of patella fractures can be guided by three main criteria: integrity of the extensor mechanism, fixation status of the patellar implant, and quality of the remaining bone.

*Parvizi et al. presented a case series of 12 peri-prosthetic patella fractures after TKA.* Seven of the fractures were non-displaced and treated nonoperatively. Surgical treatment was selected for the remaining 5 cases which included resection arthroplasty combined with open reduction and internal fixation of the fracture (3 knees), partial patellectomy (1 knee), and total patellectomy (1 knee). The outcome was excellent in 1 knee, good in 8 knees, and fair in the remaining 3 knees at the latest follow-up. There were 2 reoperations; 1 for disruption of the extensor mechanism and 1 for refracture. One patient developed a superficial wound infection. **They concluded** that surgical intervention has a high complication rate and preventing a fracture is the most effective management technique.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This case would be best served with irrigation and debridement, polyethylene liner exchange, and repair of the patellar tendon (given the acute nature of the injury).
Answer 2 and 4: These cases should be treated with extensor mechanism reconstruction.
Answer 3: A partial tear of the quadriceps tendon may be treated with nonoperative treatment, but the patient also has significant coronal instability which should not be ignored.
