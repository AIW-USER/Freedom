## Options

1. Revision rates are higher with laminoplasty
2. Laminectomy and fusion has a lower rate of wound complications
3. Local kyphosis >13 degrees is a risk factor for poor outcome after laminoplasty
4. There are no differences in cervical motion postoperatively between the two techniques
5. Laminoplasty has a higher total cost than laminectomy and fusion

## Correct option

**3. Local kyphosis >13 degrees is a risk factor for poor outcome after laminoplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with classic symptoms of myelopathy. Given her progressive symptoms, operative management is indicated to prevent further neurological decline. When considering laminoplasty, assessing alignment is critical, as local kyphosis > 13 degrees has been shown to be a risk factor for a poor surgical outcome with this technique (Answer 3).

Cervical myelopathy is a clinical syndrome characterized by progressive neurological decline, upper extremity clumsiness, gait abnormalities, and later in the course, bladder and bowel issues. Imaging will reveal stenosis causing cord compression, and stenosis can occur at multiple levels, as is the case with this patient. Operative management is indicated to prevent further functional decline, and patients should be preoperatively counseled that full neurologic recovery is not guaranteed postoperatively. Operative options include anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), or posterior-based approaches to include laminoplasty or laminectomy with fusion.

*Suda et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 114 patients who underwent laminoplasty for cervical myelopathy with a minimum follow-up of 2 years.* *In their multivariate analysis,* **they found** that cord signal changes and a local kyphosis angle of > 13 degrees were risk factors for a poor outcome after laminoplasty, which they defined as a <50% improvement in Japanese Orthopedic Association scores at final follow-up. The authors speculated that the loss of lordosis may limit the ability of the cord to migrate posteriorly after laminoplasty, leading to persistent anterior cord compression. In these cases, **they recommended** correction of kyphosis with anterior-based procedures or laminectomy and fusion rather than laminoplasty.

*Blizzard et al. retrospectively compared laminoplasty with laminectomy and fusion.* **They also found** a slightly higher rate of overall complications to include wound and hardware issues with laminectomy and fusion. Patients who underwent laminoplasty had a higher degree of cervical motion postoperatively compared to patients who underwent fusion. **The authors concluded** that laminoplasty may offer advantages in terms of range of motion and lower complication rate but that clinical outcomes largely were equivalent between the two techniques.

*Adogwa et al. compared laminoplasty and laminectomy and fusion and included 3185 total patients.* **They found** a lower rate of complication with laminoplasty versus laminectomy at 90 days (7.61% vs 11.78%), as well as a lower overall cost with laminoplasty at 2 years. The 2-year reoperation rates did not significantly differ in their study between the two techniques.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Revision rates have been shown to be comparable between the two techniques at 2 years.
Answer 2: Laminoplasty has a lower rate of wound complications than laminectomy and fusion.
Answer 3: Laminoplasty maintains cervical motion, while laminectomy and fusion does not.
Answer 5: Laminectomy and fusion is more expensive at 2 years postoperatively compared to laminoplasty
