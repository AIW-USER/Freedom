## Question

A 52-year-old male presents to the orthopedic clinic for a follow-up of right shoulder pain. The patient was previously diagnosed with a supraspinatus rotator cuff tear as well as biceps tendinosis. He has participated in 6 months of physical therapy with rotator cuff and scapular stabilizing exercises. However, he has persistent anterior shoulder pain. On physical exam, he has weakness in elevation and a positive empty can test. He also endorses tenderness over the bicipital groove and a positive Speed's test. The patient is indicated for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair and biceps tenodesis. As part of the preoperative counseling, you inform the patient that a subpectoral biceps tenodesis has a difference in which outcome when compared to a suprapectoral biceps tenodesis?

## Options

1. Higher patient reported outcomes at 1 year follow-up
2. Improved strength at 1 year follow-up
3. Lower rate of revision surgery
4. Higher rate of nerve injury
5. Improved shoulder range of motion

## Correct option

**4. Higher rate of nerve injury**

## Answer explanation

This patient has failed nonoperative management and plans to undergo rotator cuff repair with biceps tenodesis. Subpectoral biceps tenodesis has a higher rate of transient neuropraxia when compared to suprapectoral biceps tenodesis (Answer 4).

The long head of the biceps tendon is a common pain generator in the anterior shoulder. The proper management of this anterior shoulder pain has been the topic of much debate and literature. The topic of bicipital groove pain has been contested in the literature. However, recent prospective studies have shown no difference in the rate of anterior shoulder pain at final follow-up when comparing suprapectoral and subpectoral biceps tenodesis. However, multiple studies show that subpectoral biceps tenodesis has a higher rate of transient neuropraxia. There is a potential risk to the axillary nerve in subpectoral tenodesis with bicortical drilling, while risk is mitigated with unicortical drilling for placement of interference screw or suture anchor in suprapectoral tenodesis. There is also a risk to medial neurovascular structures with retraction during an open subpectoral tenodesis.

*Yi et al published a retrospective review of 66 patients who underwent a biceps tenodesis with arthroscopic repair of rotator cuff tear.* *The median follow-up was 26.8 months.* **The authors report** that at the final follow-up, there was no difference in rates of rotator cuff re-tears, patient-reported outcomes, range of motion, or pain at the bicipital groove. The authors did not report or comment on nerve injury. **The authors conclude** that either approach is appropriate based on surgeon preference.

*Ergun et al published a systematic review in 2022 that evaluated clinical outcomes of biceps tenodesis with concomitant rotator cuff repair.* In total, 1,194 subpectoral and 2,520 suprapectoral tenodesis cases were included. **The authors note** a pooled increased risk of persistent bicipital groove pain in the suprapectoral group. **They also reported** a higher transient nerve injury rate in the subpectoral group. The authors acknowledge significant limitations in their study based on the heterogeneity of operative and fixation techniques. They fail to provide recommendations for which technique should be used.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Patients have the equivalent self-reported outcomes at 1 year in several studies.
Answer 2: There is no reported difference in strength at 1 year.
Answer 3: There is an equivalently incredibly low rate of revision with either approach.
Answer 5: Patients have the equivalent range of motion with either approach.
