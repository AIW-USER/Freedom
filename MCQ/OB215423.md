## Question

A 57-year-old man presents to your clinic with nonspecific dorsal foot pain and intermittent tingling on the medial aspect of the 2nd toe. Pain is worse with plantarflexion and inversion of the ankle. The patient has failed non-operative management and you recommend surgery. In addition to the nerve being compressed, which of the following structures resides in the same fibro-osseous tunnel and must be protected?

## Options

1. Superficial peroneal nerve
2. Posterior tibial artery
3. Intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerve
4. Dorsalis pedis artery
5. Medial calcaneal nerve

## Correct option

**4. Dorsalis pedis artery**

## Answer explanation

This patient has deep peroneal nerve (DPN) entrapment syndrome which is treated with release of the inferior extensor retinaculum, in which both the DPN and dorsalis pedis artery are encountered together.

The anterior tarsal tunnel can cause symptomatic impingement ranging from 1 cm proximal to the ankle to the talonavicular joint distally. There are many different causes of deep peroneal nerve compression, both intrinsic and extrinsic. Surgical management typically consists of inferior extensor retinaculum release and removal of dorsal talonavicular osteophytes, in addition to addressing other specific causes of nerve compression. Additional contents of the anterior tarsal tunnel include the extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus, tibialis anterior, peroneus tertius, and the dorsalis pedis artery/vein. The surgeon must be careful to avoid damaging these at-risk structures during release, particularly the vascular bundle.

*Pomeroy et al. provide a comprehensive review of nerve entrapment syndromes about the leg, foot, and ankle.* **They note** that any of the five major nerves can be entrapped and history/physical exam are key to an accurate diagnosis in addition to newer advanced imaging techniques. Once the point of compression is localized, decreasing compressive forces surgically or non-surgically is important to successful management.

*Dellon et al. review deep peroneal nerve entrapment syndromes, the associated anatomy, and nonoperative/operative techniques for treatment.* **They recommend** at least 3 months of nonoperative treatment including orthotics, NSAIDS. and management of contributing systemic diseases like diabetes and vasculitis. **They also note** they found the extensor hallucis brevis (EHB) to be a primary contributor to DPN compression as it crosses superficially over the inferior extensor retinaculum.
