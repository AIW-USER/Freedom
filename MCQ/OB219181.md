## Question

A 48-year-old woman undergoes the procedure shown in Figure A. Post-operatively, the anesthesiologist asks if she can give the patient Toradol as part of her multimodal pain control. By which of the following mechanisms do nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) work to affect bone healing in a negative manner?

![[q219181-a-screenshot-2023-10-17-at-4-50-12-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Decrease cyclic-AMP (cAMP) production and cause increased differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoclasts
2. Decrease production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE-2) and COX-mediated osteoblastic mesenchymal differentiation
3. Increase the production of COX-2 causing RANK-mediated upregulation of osteoclasts
4. Increase the negative feedback inhibition of osteoclastogenesis
5. Upregulate cyclic-AMP (cAMP) production and cause increased osteoclastic resorption

## Correct option

**2. Decrease production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE-2) and COX-mediated osteoblastic mesenchymal differentiation**

## Answer explanation

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ketorolac (Toradol), decrease the production of COX-2, which is known to be a stimulus for the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts, indirectly affecting the anabolic bone healing process in a negative manner.

In settings of fracture healing or surgeries that depend on stable fusion mass formation, NSAIDs have the potential to disrupt or delay bone formation by inhibiting COX-2 formation, which has been shown to indirectly inhibit mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into anabolic cell lines. More specifically, the concentration of Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which is regulated through the enzymes COX-1 and COX-2, is thought to positively control osteoblast behavior through the relative expression of the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANK-L) and osteoprotegerin. Thus, when COX enzymes are inhibited by NSAIDs, PGE2 levels decrease, and subsequent cell signaling that causes mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts is reduced. To date, the studies in humans appear to demonstrate a dose- and time-dependent association of NSAID effect on bone healing, with the recommendation for NSAIDs being for use of the lowest effective dose and for no more than 7 days postoperatively.

*Wheatley et al. published a JAAOS Research Article on the effects of NSAIDs on bone healing.* *The authors systematically reviewed the literature, reviewing 19 studies that included 15,242 healing bones and found 512 cases of delayed union or non-union, of which 226 had been exposed to NSAIDs.* **The authors concluded** that NSAID exposure increased delayed union or nonunion risk by an odds ratio of 2.07, also suggesting that the effect may be dose or time-dependent because low-dose/short-duration exposure did not affect union rates.

*Einhorn et al. reviewed fracture healing mechanisms and interventions.* **The authors note** that the repair of bone fractures is a postnatal regenerative process that recapitulates many of the ontological events of embryonic skeletal development. Specifically, throughout the early stages of endochondral bone formation during bone healing, cytokines with inflammatory and immune functions, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17F, IL-23, and TNF, are variably expressed. **They conclude** that during the period of acute inflammation immediately after injury, TNF and IL-6 recruit cells needed for tissue regeneration, and their complete absence has been shown to delay skeletogenic mesenchymal stem cell differentiation and the subsequent bone healing response.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 3-5: NSAIDs do not upregulate osteoclastogenesis, and decrease rather than increase COX-2 activity, resulting in reduced PGE2 and decreased osteoblastic cell signaling.
