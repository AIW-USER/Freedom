## Question

Which is not associated with increased risk of infection after knee replacement

## Options

1. Old age above 65 years
2. BMI of 30 kg/m2 or higher
3. Blood transfusion
4. Male sex

## Correct option

**1. Old age above 65 years**

## Answer explanation

answer-a

Factors increasing chances of infection after arthroplasty

The risk of developing infection after any form of arthroplasty is affected by both modifiable and non-modifiable patient, surgical, and health-care system factors. Notable important factors that increase chances of infection after hip and knee arthroplasty include male sex, smoking, increasing body-mass index (BMI), steroid use, previous joint surgery, and comorbidities such as diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and depression.

- BMI of 30 kg/m2 or higher was associated with an increased risk of revision for prosthetic joint infection compared with BMI lower than 25 kg/m2
- Patients with a pre-existing history of chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, liver disease, connective tissue or rheumatic disease, or peripheral vascular disease have a higher risk of infection and revision than those without pre-existing histories of these diseases.
- Patients operated on for osteoarthritis are less likely to be revised for prosthetic joint infection. Those operated on for trauma, history of previous infection in the operated joint, or an inflammation arthropathy are at increased risk of revision for prosthetic joint infection.
- Compared with cemented total knee replacement, patients who have an uncemented total knee replacement, a patellofemoral replacement, or an unicondylar replacement are at lower risk of revision for prosthetic joint infection.
- Anticoagulation is an indirect risk factor for infection as it increases the risk of bleeding, anemia, transfusion, hematoma, wound dehiscence, and thereby infection.
- Allogeneic Blood Transfusion Is a Significant Risk Factor for Surgical-Site Infection Following Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty.
- A low socioeconomic status and poor hygiene is associated with increased infection risk.
- The risk of revision was increased for patients with a posterior stabilised fixed-bearing implant or a constrained condylar implant compare with those with an unconstrained (or cruciate-retaining) fixed-bearing implant.
- The presence of a progressive skin, urinary, digestive, lung or dental infection at another site at the time of, and after, prosthesis implantation is a risk factor for infection. Hematogenous spread of infection is found in about one-third of PJIs. These are mainly Staphylococcus and Streptococcus infections, and are more rarely due to Gram-negative bacilli. The portal of entry is often cutaneous. The chambers of implantable catheters are a potential infection site; they must be removed when not needed.
- Active cutaneous infectious sites (erysipelas, ulcer or folliculitis) are a contraindication to arthroplasty for as long as these wounds have not healed, because of the possibility of hematogenous or contiguity infection. Dermatological treatment before the surgery is required. It appears that eczema and intertrigo are not a risk factor if they are dry and not infectious; the risk related to psoriasis is controversial.
- An increasingly larger number of elderly patients are undergoing arthroplasty. Kaye's study showed that the infection risk increased by 1.1% between 17 and 65 years of age, then decreased by 1.2% per year after 65 years of age. Paradoxically, being older is a protective factor although these patients are more fragile and have multiple comorbidities.
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria is a well-established risk factor for PJI, but urine is not the source of the PJI. Asymptomatic bacteriuria appears to be a marker of PJI risk. As a consequence, neither antibiotics treatment nor delaying the surgery is indicated in cases of preoperative asymptomatic bacteriuria. No screening is required for asymptomatic bacteriuria before orthopedic surgery procedures. International guidelines consist of screening and treating urinary infections only in patients who have a symptomatic infection or recurring urinary infections
- Nasal carriage of Staphylococcus aureus- nasal carriage of S. aureus (SA) increases the risk of SA PJIs in orthopedic surgery, but there is no proof that eradication in patients candidate for a joint prosthesis is effective. Currently routine screening and antibiotic treatment is not recommended for it.
- Stem cell transplant: arthroplasty can be performed at least 1 year after the transplant, after the immune status, on-going treatments and transplant tolerance have been assessed.
- The presence of a cancer or metastasis increases the infection risk.

Answer : a
