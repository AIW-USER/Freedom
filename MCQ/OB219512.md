## Question

A 20-year-old male presents approximately 2 years after undergoing a right-sided anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction by a provider in the community. He states his knee never felt quite right after surgery and continues to endorse instability with pivoting movements while playing football. He has a 1A Lachman on examination with stable anterior and posterior drawer tests. However, he appears to have an abnormal pivot shift test. His radiographic imaging is shown in Figure A. Which of the following would best explain the causality of this patient’s instability?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Insufficiency in the anteromedial bundle’s biomechanical contribution to his ACL reconstruction
2. Insufficiency in the anterolateral bundle’s biomechanical contribution to his ACL reconstruction
3. Insufficiency in the posteromedial bundle’s biomechanical contribution to his ACL reconstruction
4. Insufficiency in the posterolateral bundle’s biomechanical contribution to his ACL reconstruction
5. A missed extra-articular injury resulting in persistent instability

## Correct option

**4. Insufficiency in the posterolateral bundle’s biomechanical contribution to his ACL reconstruction**

## Answer explanation

This patient has symptomatic rotational instability after ACL reconstruction despite normal Lachman and anterior drawer tests on physical examination. His radiographic images demonstrate vertical tunnel placement, which results in adequate anterior-posterior restraint however with compromised rotatory stability normally provided by the posterolateral bundle of the native ACL (Answer 4).

The ACL is comprised of two native bundles, as defined by their tibial insertion: (1) anteromedial and (2) posterolateral. The anteromedial bundle is longer and primarily responsible for tibial anterior-posterior stability and is assessed via the anterior drawer test. The posterolateral bundle is primarily responsible for tibial rotational stability and is assessed with the pivot shift test. Antiquated ACL reconstruction techniques involved drilling a single, transtibial tunnel which resulted in numerous cases of vertical femoral tunnel placement (Figure A). While this reliably recreates the anterior-posterior stability of the ACL (i.e. anteromedial bundle’s biomechanical contribution), it often fails to restore the rotational stability (i.e. the posterolateral bundle’s biomechanical contribution). For this reason, independent femoral drilling is now performed by most surgeons to better recreate the native ACL biomechanics.

*Dhawan and colleagues provided a comprehensive review regarding anatomic tunnel placement in ACL reconstruction.* **They note** a paradigm change from the use of transtibial femoral tunnel drilling to contemporary techniques involving independent femoral drilling by most surgeons. **They note** that independent femoral tunnel drilling techniques allow for a better re-creation of native ACL kinematics and biomechanics. The authors proceed to describe these anatomic tunnel placements based on consistent anatomic structures (Illustration A) and conclude that the identification and placement of these graft apertures are fundamental to anatomic ACL reconstruction.

*Miller and colleagues provided a comprehensive review article regarding ACL revision reconstruction.* **They note** that the most common technical error in ACL reconstruction is malpositioned tunnels, specifically an anteriorly placed femoral tunnel (resultant loss of knee flexion with supraphysiologic stress resulting in graft failure with physiologic knee flexion). **They also note** that vertical femoral tunnel placement leads to adequate anterior-posterior restraint; however with compromised rotatory stability. **The authors conclude** by discussing other risk factors for primary ACL reconstruction failure, revision graft options, and extraarticular ligamentous supplementation techniques.

Tang and Zhao provided a technique review article on their “condyle-pinching double bundle” ACL ligament reconstruction technique. The authors note that anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction has been known to provide added rotational stability to ACL reconstruction. However, they argue that the ALL is not naturally colinear to the ACL, and therefore their ACL-mimicking anterolateral structure reconstruction technique serves as a superior reconstructive option over standard ALL reconstruction. They conclude that, anecdotally, this technique has obtained improved outcomes and should be considered for use by surgeons.

Figure A demonstrates radiographic imaging of this patient’s right knee with vertical tunnel placement. Illustration A highlights the native ACL anatomy and landmarks along the lateral femoral condyle to consider when performing independent femoral tunnel drilling (i.e the tunnel should be posterior to the lateral intercondylar ridge and just proximal to the bifurcate ridge/~1cm anterior to the posterior articular margin of the lateral femoral condyle).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The anteromedial bundle of the ACL primarily provides restraint against anterior tibial translation relative to the femur. However, it is the posterolateral bundle of the ACL that provides rotational stability and is thus primarily responsible for this patient’s endorsed instability with performing pivoting moments during exercise.
Answers 2&3: The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) has anterolateral and posteromedial bundles which prevent posterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur. The PCL works in concert with the posterolateral corner (PLC) to resist posterior translation and posterolateral rotatory instability.
Answer 5: Given this patient’s obvious vertical femoral tunnel, it is most likely that they are experiencing rotational instability due to the vertical tunnel placement. This tunnel has the biomechanical functional equivalence of an insufficient ACL posterolateral bundle.
