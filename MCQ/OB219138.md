## Question

A 35-year-old female presents to the emergency department after suffering a right humeral shaft fracture after a fall from a bike. On exam, she has a gross deformity of her arm but her skin is intact. She is also noted to have the most common nerve palsy associated with this injury pattern. The patient is concerned about the function of her hand given that she is a general surgeon. Which of the following is true regarding the first muscle expected to recover that also crosses the wrist joint?

## Options

1. Inserts on the extensor expansion of the 2nd digit
2. Inserts on the base of 2nd metacarpal
3. Tendon contained in the 3rd extensor compartment
4. Originates from lateral epicondyle and posterior border of ulna
5. Only tendon in corresponding extensor compartment

## Correct option

**2. Inserts on the base of 2nd metacarpal**

## Answer explanation

Humeral shaft fractures are most commonly associated with radial nerve palsy. The extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) is the first muscle to recover that crosses the wrist joint and inserts on the base of the 2nd metacarpal (Answer 2).

Humeral shaft fractures consist of 3-5% of all fractures and generally occur in a bimodal distribution. Radial nerve palsy is the most common associated nerve injury following humeral shaft fractures. Radial nerve palsy occurs in 10-22% of fractures, with distal one-third spiral fractures having the highest risk. Closed fractures most commonly result in neuropraxic injuries. In such cases, 85-90% will improve with observation over 3 months, and full recovery is expected around 6 months. Nerve recovery generally occurs in a predictable fashion with reinnervation of muscles with more proximal origins occurring first, as these nerves have a shorter distance to travel to reinnervate the motor endplate. In the case of radial nerve palsies following humeral shaft fractures, the brachioradialis has the most proximal origin and is the first muscle to be reinnervated; however, it inserts on the radial styloid and, thus, does not cross the wrist joint. ECRL has the most proximal origin of the extensor tendons that insert on the hand and is the next to be reinnervated. It originates from the lateral supracondylar ridge of the humerus and inserts on the base of the 2nd metacarpal, so wrist extension in radial deviation would be expected to be regained first. Conversely, the extensor indicis proprius (EIP) originates from the posterior surface of the distal third of the ulna and interosseous membrane, and so its function would be expected to be regained last. Open fractures most commonly result in neurotmesis, or complete transection of the nerve. These injuries are unlikely to recover spontaneously and are more likely to require surgical treatment.

*Carroll et al. provided a review of the management of humeral shaft fractures.* **The authors note** that there is good evidence to support nonoperative treatment using functional bracing for most humeral shaft fractures, particularly in fractures that meet acceptable alignment: <3cm shortening, <30 degrees of coronal angulation, and <20 degrees of sagittal alignment. Surgical management is indicated for polytraumatic injuries, open fractures, vascular injuries, floating elbow injuries, and fractures that fail nonsurgical management. Surgical options include open reduction and internal fixation, minimally invasive percutaneous osteosynthesis, intramedullary nailing, and external fixation.

*Claessen et al. performed a retrospective study to analyze the factors associated with radial nerve palsy in patients treated operatively.* *They reviewed 259 patients and found a 7% incidence of radial nerve palsy.* **They found** that a lateral surgical approach had the highest rate of iatrogenic radial nerve palsy (1 in 5), followed by posterior (1 in 9), and anterolateral (1 in 25). **They also found** that open fractures, location, and high-energy mechanisms were associated with radial nerve palsy of any type.

*Seiler et al. reviewed upper extremity tendon transfers following nerve palsy.* Typically tendon transfers are indicated for restoration of function after peripheral nerve injury or irreparable muscle/tendon injury by transferring an expendable functional muscle to a non-functional unit. For radial nerve palsy, restoring wrist extension can be achieved by transferring pronator teres to ECRB. Restoring finger extension can be achieved by transferring FDS, FCR, or FCU to EDC. Thumb extension can be restored by transferring palmaris longus or FDS to EPL.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: EIP inserts on the extensor expansion of the 2nd digit. It has the most distal origin and, thus, its function would be expected to be regained last.
Answer 3: EPL, not ECRL, is found in the 3rd extensor compartment.
Answer 4: ECU originates from the lateral epicondyle and posterior border of the ulna. ECRL originates from the lateral supracondylar ridge of the humerus.
Answer 5: ECRL and ECRB are found in the 2nd extensor compartment.
