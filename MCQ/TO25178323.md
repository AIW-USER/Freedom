Chapter: General Orthopedics MCQs — Bone Cement

## Question

Maximum amount of antibiotics that can be mixed in 40 gm bone cement in cement spacers.

## Options

1. 2 gm
2. 4 gm
3. 1 gm
4. 8 gm

## Correct option

**4. 8 gm**

## Answer explanation

In orthopedic surgery, the amount of antibiotic added to Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) is strictly governed by the intended goal: mechanical stability vs. antimicrobial efficacy.

1. Prophylaxis (Primary Arthroplasty)

 - Maximum Dose: 1g to 2g of antibiotic per 40g bag of cement.

 - Most Common: 1g of Gentamicin or 1g of Vancomycin.

 - Rationale:

 - Mechanical Integrity: At this low concentration (approx. 2.5–5% by weight), the cement maintains its compressive and fatigue strength, meeting ISO 5833 standards. This is critical because the cement must last 15–20+ years as a permanent fixation.

 - Prevention vs. Treatment: The goal is to inhibit the adhesion of a small number of bacteria introduced during surgery, not to treat an established deep infection or biofilm.

2. Established Infection (Stable Hardware / Retention)

 - Maximum Dose: 2g to 3.6g of antibiotic per 40g bag.

 - Most Common: Dual loading, e.g., 1g Gentamicin + 2g Vancomycin.

 - Rationale:

 - The "Structural Limit":
 Concentrations above 10% (4g per 40g bag) lead to a catastrophic drop in mechanical strength. If the hardware is being retained (e.g., in a DAIR procedure for an acute infection), the cement must still be strong enough to prevent the implant from loosening.

 - Increased Elution: Using two different antibiotic powders creates a "spacer effect," where the dissolution of one antibiotic increases the porosity of the cement, allowing the second antibiotic to elute more effectively.

3. Antibiotic Spacers (Stage 1 of Revision)

 - Maximum Dose: 4g to 8g of antibiotic per 40g bag (some extreme protocols go up to 10g).

 - Most Common: "The 4+4 Cocktail" (e.g., 4g Vancomycin + 4g Tobramycin/Gentamicin).

 - Rationale:

 - Therapeutic Delivery System: In this scenario, the cement is no longer a "glue"—it is a local drug delivery device. Mechanical strength is a secondary concern because the spacer is temporary and the patient is usually restricted to touch-down weight-bearing.

 - Surface Area and Porosity:
 High-dose loading makes the cement highly porous. This is intentional. The more "holes" created by the dissolving antibiotic, the more deep-seated drug can reach the tissue to combat established biofilms and resistant organisms.

 - Dead Space Management: The high volume of antibiotic ensures the fluid in the joint space remains above the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) for weeks.

Clinical considerations:

Systemic Toxicity: While the delivery is "local," high-dose spacers (especially with aminoglycosides like Gentamicin/Tobramycin) can lead to systemic absorption. In patients with pre-existing renal issues, an 8g spacer can potentially trigger Acute Kidney Injury (AKI).

Mixing Technique: For prophylaxis, vacuum mixing is preferred to reduce air bubbles and increase strength. For spacers, hand mixing is often preferred because the resulting air bubbles increase surface area and enhance antibiotic elution.

The "2-Gram" Rule: A general rule of thumb in many centers is that as long as you stay under 2g per bag, you are safe for permanent load-bearing. Once you cross 4g, you must treat the construct as "non-load-bearing."
