## Question

. A gymnast suddenly became unconscious while practicing. ECG was done and depicted a long QT interval (>480 ms). Most dangerous among various types of long QT syndromes is [FNB 2022]

## Options

1. Type 1
2. Type 2
3. Type 3
4. Type 4

## Correct option

**2. Type 2**

## Answer explanation

Ans 2: B

Congenital long QT syndromes

The congenital long QT syndromes (LQTS) are inherited disorders (inherited as autosomal dominant or recessive traits) with genetic mutations and characterized by abnormalities in cardiac sodium or potassium ion channels. Abnormal shifts in electrical currents result in membrane instability during cardiac repolarization and predispose to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia resulting in ventricular fibrillation (an arrythmia also called as Torsades de pointes), culminating in sudden cardiac death (SCD), mostly in females under the age of 40 years.

The prevalence of Congenital LQTS in the general population is approximately I in 2500. However, based on the findings from screening programs in athletes, a long QT interval is identified in 0.42% of elite athletes. Acquired LQTS can also occur but its usually secondary to QT prolongation due to electrolyte disturbance or some drugs such as sotalol and amiodarone, certain antibiotics such as macrolides and fluoroquinolones, antipsychotics such as haloperidol and olanzapine, and certain gastric motility agents such as Cisapride.

Concerning Congenital LQTS, there are currently 12 different identified types in this group; termed LQTS-1 to LQTS-12 respectively. LQTS-1 (potassium ion channel defect due to KCNQ1 gene mutation on chromosome 11), LQTS-2 (potassium ion channel) defect due to KCNH2 gene mutation on chromosome 7), and LQTS-3 (sodium ion channel) account for approximately 95% of all known cases of the disorder. LQTS-1 is commonest and most SCDs occur in females with LQTS type 2.

Clinical features: Adrenergic surges provoke ventricular arrhythmias, in individuals with the LQTS (particularly genotype 1). Many deaths in sport occur in young females during swimming and are attributed to the adrenergic surge associated with diving suddenly into cold water.

Affected sports persons may present with palpitations, dizziness, and “unheralded syncope” secondary to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. The latter may be associated with brief myoclonic movements, resulting in a misdiagnosis of epilepsy in recurrent cases.

Diagnosis: In symptomatic athletes, Long QT syndrome is usually diagnosed on a resting ECG, which reveals a long QT interval in approximately 60% of cases (QTc >440 ms in males and QTc >460 ms in females). Holter monitoring is recommended to evaluate the possibility of associated episodes of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. In the absence of symptoms or a family history (which is positive in 40% cases) of LQTS, clear diagnosis of the disorder can be considered in athletes with a QTc >500 msec, or in athletes with a shorter QT interval in the presence of exercise-related paradoxical QT prolongation, the demonstration of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, or the identification of a long QT interval in a first-degree relative. Genetic testing is recommended in all cases as it may facilitate clinical management; however. the diagnostic yield from genetic analysis is only 70%.

Management: Athletes diagnosed with LQTS are recommended to avoid all sports associated with adrenergic surges. All affected individuals should be treated with a beta blocker unless contraindicated. An implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) is recommended in patients with Long QT syndrome who were resuscitated from a cardiac arrest. It is also indicated in those whom have beta-blocker resistant symptoms or have contraindications to beta-blockers. It also may be indicated in asymptomatic individuals who are suspected to be at high risk for ventricular arrhythmias.
