## Question

A 25-year-old male is snowboarding in Colorado during his Christmas holiday and sustains a twisting injury to his knee where he feels a pop. He is unable to ambulate and his knee is quite swollen. He flies home immediately to see his local orthopedic surgeon who obtains an MRI. He is diagnosed with a complete rupture of his anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and given his near full range of motion, he is scheduled for ACL reconstruction several days later. If present, which of the following additional injuries would you want to address during his acute ACL reconstruction?

## Options

1. Lateral femoral condyle impaction fracture
2. Minimally displaced fibular head fracture
3. Grade II posterior cruciate ligament sprain
4. Grade II proximal medial collateral ligament sprain
5. Grade III distal medial collateral ligament tear

## Correct option

**5. Grade III distal medial collateral ligament tear**

## Answer explanation

One of the main indications for addressing a medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury acutely at the time of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is in the setting of a grade III distal MCL avulsion.

Injuries to the MCL are among the most common knee ligament injuries and often occur concomitantly with injuries to the ACL. Most proximal MCL injuries are treated non-operatively with a period of bracing and physical therapy prior to ACL reconstruction as managing the ACL acutely in the setting of MCL injury can lead to post-operative arthrofibrosis. However, distal MCL avulsions have a propensity to lack normal healing capabilities due to entrapment around the pes tendons, a so-called "stener lesion" of the knee. Therefore, in the setting of grade III distal MCL injury, consideration should be given to acute repair of the MCL at the time of ACL reconstruction.

Encinas-Ullán et al. published regarding the management of MCL tears, noting that most can be managed non-operatively. The primary indication for repair or reconstruction of the MCL is in the setting of a grade III injury with valgus instability or distal MCL entrapment with the pes tendons. They note that primary repair, if undertaken, should be performed within 7-10 days while reconstruction is useful in the setting of failed non-operative management or more chronic instability.

*Desai et al. reviewed midterm outcomes following acute repair of grade III distal MCL avulsions in the setting of multi-ligamentous knee injuries.* *Twenty patients were included, with most having concomitant ACL injuries and all demonstrating valgus stability and good range of motion at an average of 20 months of follow-up.* *Tegner and IKDC outcome scores remained high at a 5.7-year follow-up in the setting of grade III distal MCL repair.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Impaction fractures of the lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibial plateau are common in the setting of ACL injury due to the pivot-shift mechanism and usually do not need to be addressed at the time of ACL reconstruction.
Answer 2: A minimally displaced fracture of the fibular head indicates some degree of injury to the posterolateral corner (PLC) but usually heals uneventfully without the need for surgical fixation.
Answer 3: Grade II sprains of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) are normally treated non-operatively in the setting of ACL reconstruction.
Answer 4: Grade II proximal sprains of the MCL are typically treated non-operatively with a period of bracing for 4-6 weeks to allow healing and maintenance of motion before the ACL reconstruction is performed.
