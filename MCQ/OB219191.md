## Question

A 36-year-old male is brought to the trauma bay after being involved in a head-on motor vehicle accident. Radiographs are obtained and demonstrate a comminuted calcaneus fracture. After applying a bulky Jones splint, a CT scan is obtained, demonstrating marked displacement of the posterior facet of the calcaneus. The patient is neurovascularly intact, but you note limited motion of the hallux when compared to the uninjured extremity. What is the origin of the tendon which often becomes entrapped between the posterior facet of the talus and the posterior facet fragment of the fractured calcaneus?

## Options

1. Posterior surface of tibia distal to popliteal line
2. Anterior surface of the fibula and the adjacent interosseous membrane
3. Inferior 2/3 of posterior surface of fibula, lower part of interosseous membrane
4. Head of the fibula, upper 1/2 - 2/3 of lateral fibular shaft surface; also anterior and posterior intermuscular septa of leg
5. Lateral condyle of tibia, proximal 1/2 - 2/3 or lateral surface of tibial shaft, interosseous membrane, and the deep surface of the fascia cruris

## Correct option

**3. Inferior 2/3 of posterior surface of fibula, lower part of interosseous membrane**

## Answer explanation

The flexor hallucis longus (FHL) is medial to the posterior facet of the calcaneus. Displacement of this facet can lead to interposition of the tendon in the fracture site. The origin of the FHL is the inferior 2/3 of the posterior surface of the fibula/lower part of the interosseous membrane (Answer 3).

The calcaneus is the most commonly fractured tarsal bone, and calcaneus fractures are associated with high degrees of morbidity and disability. Approximately 60-75% of these fractures are intra-articular, and 17% are open fractures. Intra-articular fractures of the calcaneus typically occur secondary to a high-energy axial load, a mechanism that commonly occurs in motor vehicle accidents or following a fall from height.

The calcaneus has three articular facets. The posterior facet is the largest and represents the primary weight-bearing surface of the calcaneus. The middle facet is located anteromedially on the sustentaculum tali. The anterior facet is often confluent with the middle facet. The FHL runs medial to the posterior facet. The FHL tendon is medial to the posterior facet and inferior to the medial facet and can be injured with drillbit overpenetration or the placement of overly-long screws. The FHL can also become entrapped when there is marked displacement of the posterior facet. It is important to examine toe range of motion, as limited hallux motion is typically observed with FHL entrapment.

Komiya and Terada provide a case report of FHL tendon entrapment in the setting of an intra-articular calcaneus fracture with displacement of the posterior facet. The authors note that despite adequate CT imaging, they did not recognize this complication preoperatively. The authors highlight that limited hallux motion was the key physical examination finding in the setting of FHL entrapment.

*Della Rocca et al. provide a case series reporting on the injury characteristics and surgical technique of 19 patients with fractures of the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus.* Of note, none of these patients had posterior facet involvement. The authors utilized a medial approach, which allowed for retraction of the neurovascular bundle and toe flexors. The authors do note that this approach allows for visualization of the medial tendons, and can be utilized in cases of FHL entrapment.

*Albert et al. performed a cadaveric study to investigate the structures at risk during ORIF of calcaneal fractures.* *The authors placed multiple reference K-wires to determine which structures were at risk with both surgical fixation or fracture displacement.* Overall, **the authors concluded** that the FHL tendon was near the posterior facet, and at risk with either errant screw placement, or with marked fracture displacement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the flexor digitorum longus (FDL). This tendon is more medial than FHL at the level of the calcaneus, and less commonly entrapped. Furthermore, you would not expect limited hallux motion with FDL entrapment.
Answer 2: This describes the extensor hallucis longus (EHL). While EHL entrapment could cause limited hallux motion, it is an anterior structure and is not at risk of entrapment with posterior facet displacement.
Answer 4: This describes the peroneus longus, a lateral structure, which is not at risk with posterior facet displacement.
Answer 5: This describes tibialis anterior, an anterior structure, which is not at risk with posterior facet displacement.
