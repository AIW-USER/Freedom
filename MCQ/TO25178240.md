## Question

Case: During a Turco-style posteromedial release for a 14-month-old child, the surgeon successfully reduces the talonavicular joint and stabilizes it with a K-wire. Postoperative radiographs are taken in the operating room.

Question:
Which radiographic finding on the AP view of the foot would most reliably indicate that the rotational deformity of the clubfoot has been adequately corrected and the TN joint is properly reduced?

## Options

1. The long axis of the talus points directly at the base of the first metatarsal.
2. The long axis of the talus passes through the center of the ossified navicular and the first metatarsal shaft.
3. The talocalcaneal (Kite’s) angle is reduced to less than 15 degrees.
4. The calcaneus is positioned in a "parallel" relationship with the talus on the lateral view.

## Correct option

**2. The long axis of the talus passes through the center of the ossified navicular and the first metatarsal shaft.**

## Answer explanation

Detailed Explanation and Rationale:

To ensure a stable reduction during a Posteromedial Release (PMR), the stabilization of the Talonavicular (TN) joint is the single most critical step. If this joint is not accurately pinned, the "rotational" deformity of the clubfoot will recur immediately, regardless of how well the Achilles tendon was lengthened.

Surgical Principles of K-Wire Fixation in PMR

1. The TN Joint Pinning (The Key Step)

After the comprehensive release of the Master Knot of Henry, the spring ligament, and the medial capsule, the navicular must be "reduced" onto the head of the talus.

 - Trajectory: A 1.2mm or 1.5mm K-wire is passed from the dorsomedial aspect of the navicular into the center of the talar head, extending into the talar body.

 - The Maneuver: You must physically lift the navicular out of its medially displaced position and "center" it in front of the talus.

 - Objective: This restores the "acetabulum pedis" (the socket formed by the navicular, calcaneus, and spring ligament) around the talar head.

2. The Subtalar / Calcaneocuboid Pinning

While less common than TN pinning, in rigid neglected cases, a second wire may be used to stabilize the calcaneocuboid joint or the subtalar joint.

 - Trajectory: Usually passed from the heel (calcaneus) across the subtalar joint into the talus to maintain the "divergence" between the talus and calcaneus.

 - Clinical Pearl: On a lateral X-ray, the goal is to increase the Talocalcaneal angle, which is nearly zero (parallel) in a clubfoot.

 - Choice B (The Goal): In a normal foot, a line drawn through the long axis of the talus should pass directly through the navicular and follow the shaft of the first metatarsal. In a clubfoot, the navicular is medially displaced, so the talar axis passes lateral to the navicular. Restoring this alignment confirms that the "medial rotation" of the midfoot has been corrected.

 - Choice A: This is a sign of residual adduction. If the talar axis points to the first metatarsal base but the navicular is not centered, the TN joint is still subluxated.

 - Choice C: This is incorrect. Kite’s angle (the angle between the talus and calcaneus) is low in a clubfoot (parallelism). A successful correction should increase the angle (typically to 20°–40°), reflecting the divergence of the talus and calcaneus as the hindfoot varus is corrected.

 - Choice D: This describes the deformity itself (parallelism). Proper correction requires the talus and calcaneus to diverge on the lateral view, creating a larger talocalcaneal angle.

Postoperative Protocol:

 - Pin Maintenance: K-wires are typically left in situ for 6 weeks.

 - Cast Changes: The first cast change is usually at 2 weeks under sedation to check the wound and remove sutures, followed by a fresh cast in the corrected position.

 - Bracing: Once pins are removed at 6 weeks, the child must transition to a Foot Abduction Orthosis (FAO)
 or a Denis Browne splint to maintain the correction during the high-remodeling phase.

Answer: 2
