## Question

A 37-year-old man presents with a 2-year history of a left foot equinovarus contracture after he sustained the injury depicted in Figure A with significant neurologic deficits to the anterior and lateral compartment of the leg. On examination, he exhibits full active plantarflexion and inversion strength but is very weak in dorsiflexion and eversion. You are able to passively correct his deformity. He would like to regain his active motion back after multiple trials of therapy and bracing. You recommend a tendon transfer along with Achilles tendon lengthening. Which of the following is true of the tendon transfer likely being recommended?

![[q3855-a-screen-shot-2020-01-30-at-6-25-38-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. It is innervated by the deep peroneal nerve
2. Its muscle belly eccentrically contracts after heel strike
3. Its antagonist tendon is the peroneus brevis
4. Its antagonist tendon is the peroneus longus
5. It is innervated by the superficial peroneal nerve

## Correct option

**3. Its antagonist tendon is the peroneus brevis**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an open segmental tibial shaft fracture, secondary to a high-energy mechanism with subsequent neurological deficits to the anterior and lateral compartments of the leg. He has decreased motor function to ankle dorsiflexion and subtalar joint eversion. The tendon transfer likely being recommended is the tibialis posterior tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot to allow active ankle dorsiflexion and subtalar joint eversion. The antagonistic tendon to the tibialis posterior is the peroneus brevis.

Injury to the deep branch of the peroneal nerve, superficial peroneal nerve, or L5 nerve root results in anterior (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallicus longus, peroneus tertius) and lateral (peroneus longus and brevis) leg musculature weakness, leaving the action of the tibialis posterior and long toe flexor muscles unopposed, causing hindfoot and forefoot varus. After trauma and/or compartment syndrome, foot deformity correction may require tendon releases, which necessitate specific requirements. The tendons being transferred require appropriate strength and excursion, insertion close to the substituted tendon and routed in a direct line, an intact nerve and blood supply, and the joints on which the tendon is to act must be functional.

*Krause et al. reviewed neuromuscular issues in the cavovarus foot.* **They reported** that the donor morbidity after tibialis posterior transfer for the equinovarus foot is only minor since the bones and ligaments of the foot prevent arch collapse. **They highlighted** that soft tissue contractures need to be released, bone deformity corrected, and muscles balanced to optimize their strength and prevent recurrence of the deformity when correcting the cavovarus foot.

*Younger et al. reviewed the adult cavovarus foot deformity.* **They reported** that in this deformity, the relatively strong peroneus longus and tibialis posterior muscles cause a hindfoot varus and forefoot valgus position. **They recommended** tendon rebalancing and transfers as an early intervention to prevent unsalvageable deterioration of the foot when the deformity is flexible.

Figure A demonstrates a segmental tibial shaft fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The tibialis posterior is innervated by the tibial nerve.
Answer 2: It is the tibialis anterior that eccentrically contracts after heel strike.
Answer 4: The antagonist of the tibialis anterior is peroneus longus.
Answer 5: The peroneal muscles are innervated by the superficial peroneal nerve, while the tibialis posterior is innervated by the tibial nerve.
