## Question

A 34-year-old thrill seeker falls from a zipline in New Zealand and sustains the fracture seen in Figure A. He is treated with an antegrade reconstruction nail. The treating surgeon attempts to stabilize the butterfly segment with a cerclage cable and notes profuse bleeding that is difficult to initially localize. Which of the labeled structures in Figure B is most likely injured?

## Options

1. Structure A
2. Structure B
3. Structure C
4. Structure D
5. Structure E

## Correct option

**4. Structure D**

## Answer explanation

Structure D represents the deep branch of the femoral artery (DFA), or "profunda femoris." When femur fractures are cabled in the proximal segment, the deep femoral artery and its branches are more often injured than the superficial femoral artery and its branches (Structure A).

Cerclage wiring can be a useful adjunct in the reduction and fixation of femur fractures, but it has been associated with the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures, namely the perforator and main branches of the deep femoral artery (DFA) and the superficial femoral artery (SFA). These vascular injuries can be devastating and pose a risk to both the life and limb of the patient. Though they can present with acute intra-operative bleeding, they can also present more innocuously post-operatively with progressive or recurrent swelling, persistent pain, and/or a decrease in hematocrit due to pseudoaneurysm. The DFA is more vulnerable in the proximal femur because of its proximity to the femoral diaphysis and reduction in mobility due to its perforators that pierce the intermuscular septum. The SFA, on the other hand, is further away from the bone in the proximal femur and is more mobile, but moves closer to the femur in the distal shaft and has a fixation point in the adductor canal that limits its mobility (Illustration B). The safest area is the midshaft of the femur, where the SFA and DFA lie at safe distances from the bone.

*Devendra et al. provide a review of vascular injuries that occur due to the use of cerclage passers, delineating the relevant anatomy and providing words of caution.* *The authors reported on four cases of arterial injury and ultimately found a 1.59% rate of DFA injury and a 7.14% rate of SFA injury, with all DFA injuries occurring in the proximal femur.* **They concluded** that caution and accuracy are imperative to avoid vascular injuries while using the cerclage passer in femur fractures, and strict vigilance is essential for early identification and prompt management.

*Apivatthakakul et al. provide a technical guide for cerclage wiring in the femur based on a study of CTAs.* *The CTAs of 80 patients were reviewed to delineate the course and proximity to the femur of the DFA and SFA.* **They found** that the the safe area represents the proximal half (midshaft) of the femur where the SFA and DFA lie at a safe distance from the femur. **They concluded** that cerclage wiring should be started from the posterior intermuscular septum at the linea aspera with the tip kept close to the posteromedial cortex to avoid injury to the SFA and sciatic nerve, especially below the distal fourth of the femur.

*Won et al. provide a case report of an amputated limb after errant cerclage wiring of a spiral femoral diaphyseal fracture in an 82-year-old patient.* The patient was treated with an antegrade intramedullary nailing and multiple cable augmentation, after which ipsilateral leg necrosis and amputation resulted due to direct belting of the superficial femoral artery. **The authors concluded** that though minimally invasive cerclage wiring has reported good results, its increase in popularity should provide caution that increased care for the major vessels must be taken to prevent potentially catastrophic iatrogenic injury.

Figure A is an AP radiograph demonstrating a comminuted proximal femur fracture with a large butterfly segment. Figure B demonstrates the unlabeled femoral artery anatomy, while Illustration A provides labels for the important femoral artery branches. Illustration B demonstrates the points of fixation, namely the proximal perforators and the adductor canal, that limit the mobility of the DFA and SFA, respectively, increasing their risk for injury at these levels.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Structure A is the superficial femoral artery, which is more commonly injured in the distal femur, as above.
Answers 2 and 3: Structure B is the lateral circumflex artery and Structure C is its transverse branch, neither of which are commonly injured directly with cerclage cabling of the proximal femur.
Answer 5: Structure E represents the descending genicular branch of the superficial femoral, which for the reasons stated above, would not be commonly injured with cabling of a proximal femoral segment.
