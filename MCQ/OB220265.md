## Question

A 32-year-old male presents to the clinic for evaluation of right ankle pain and snapping following an ankle injury five months ago. On examination, snapping is noted overlying the lateral ankle with resisted ankle dorsiflexion and eversion. Radiographs are obtained, as illustrated in Figures A-C. He ultimately undergoes surgical repair of the superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR), retromalleolar groove deepening, and tenodesis of the peroneus brevis to the peroneus longus. ## Options

1. Peroneus brevis tendonitis
2. Longitudinal peroneus brevis tear comprising 30% of the tendon
3. Complex peroneus brevis tear comprising 65% of the tendon
4. Complex peroneus brevis and longus tears comprising 70% of the tendons
5. Complex peroneus longus tear comprising 70% of the tendon

## Correct option

**3. Complex peroneus brevis tear comprising 65% of the tendon**

## Answer explanation

This 32-year-old male sustained a superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) tear as evidenced by the 'fleck sign' on radiographs. A complex peroneus brevis tendon tear comprising greater than 60% of the tendon is an indication for tenodesis to the peroneus longus (Answer 3).

Peroneal tendon tears commonly stem from inversion ankle injuries, with forced dorsiflexion on an inverted foot/ankle imparting significant stress onto the SPR, leading to its disruption. Associated tendon injuries can occur at the time of initial injury or can result in repeated subluxation episodes following SPR disruption. When patients experience pain and instability recalcitrant to immobilization and physical therapy, surgery is indicated. Both the size and type of tear dictate how the pathology is treated. In conjunction with SPR repair and retromalleolar groove deepening, symptomatic tendon tears comprising less than 50-60% undergo tubularization, while those greater than 60% are tenodesed to the adjacent tendon. Moreover, longitudinal tears are more amenable to tubularization, while complex tears often require debridement.

*Philbin et al. reviewed peroneal tendon injuries, detailing the anatomy, physical examination, imaging, pathophysiology, and treatment.* The type of treatment should be individualized and based on the chronicity of the injury, concomitant pathologies, age, and patient activity level. **The authors note** high failure rates associated with nonoperative treatment, exceeding greater than 50%. Nonetheless, **the authors conclude** nonoperative treatment is most amenable for low-demand individuals, while surgery is reserved for younger, more active patients, as they experience more significant loss of function.

*Porter et al. reported on their case series of 14 ankles with recurrent peroneal tendon instability, which was ultimately treated with fibular groove deepening and SPR reconstruction.* *At an average follow-up of 35 months, no patients experienced recurrent instability, and radiographs were devoid of any abnormalities.* Moreover, no peroneal tendon discomfort or weakness was reported, and all had returned to their sport at an average of three months. **The authors conclude** fibular groove deepening with an SPR repair provides adequate, lasting stability while allowing an accelerated return to sport.

Figures A, B, and C represent AP, mortise, and lateral views of the right ankle, demonstrating the 'fleck sign' representative of an SPR tear with an otherwise congruent tibiotalar joint. Of note, there is also an os subfibulare present.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: chronic tendonitis warrants tenosynovectomy with possible debridement
Answer 2: longitudinal tears of 30% would be best treated with tendon debridement and tubularization
Answer 4: tears of both tendons, amounting to 70% of each tendon, would likely require allograft reconstruction vs. tendon transfer, depending on the degree of muscle excursion present
Answer 5: a peroneus longus tear comprising 70% of the tendon requires tenodesis to the peroneus brevis
