## Question

A 45-year-old male presents with a vague, dull ache in the proximal forearm and episodes of tingling and numbness radiating into the thenar eminence, as well as the volar thumb, index, and middle fingers. The patient notes a lack of pain at night or early morning. On examination, you can recreate his pain by both continued compression as well as repeated tapping at the volar forearm, but not at the wrist. With their elbow extended, you ask the patient to flip their hand from a palm up to a palm down position, similarly increasing their pain. Neither resisted supination and elbow flexion, nor resisted finger flexion recreate his symptoms. The anatomic structure most likely implicated in this patient’s compressive neuropathy has which of the following attachment sites?

## Options

1. Medial epicondyle of the humerus and volar middle phalanges of digits 2-5
2. Biceps tendon and deep volar forearm fascia
3. Lateral supracondylar ridge and styloid process of the radius
4. Medial epicondyle of the humerus, coronoid process of ulna, and radial shaft
5. Scaphoid tubercle, trapezium, pisiform, hook of the hamate

## Correct option

**4. Medial epicondyle of the humerus, coronoid process of ulna, and radial shaft**

## Answer explanation

Due to the characteristic symptoms and physical examination findings, the pronator teres is the most likely source of compression in this patient. The pronator teres originates from the medial epicondyle (humeral head) and coronoid process (ulnar head) and inserts at the middle of the lateral surface of the proximal radius shaft.

Pronator syndrome arises from median nerve compression at the proximal forearm, presenting with vague forearm pain and paresthesias in the median nerve distribution, often made worse with repeated pronosupination. Differentiation from carpal tunnel syndrome, which is significantly more common, requires careful history-taking and physical examination. There are multiple sites of possible proximal compression and electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction velocity (NCV) tests are often negative in the setting of isolated pronator teres syndrome. Given the rarity of this syndrome and vague symptoms, they are still often obtained to assist in establishing the diagnosis, as well as helping exclude other concurrent compression sites. Treatment involves conservative measures such as rest, anti-inflammatory medications, and splinting, followed by surgical decompression when conservative measures fail to provide relief.

Adler and Wolf review the anatomy, pathoanatomy, clinical presentation, workup, and treatment of pronator syndrome. They highlight the commonly implicated compression sites, including the pronator teres, lacertus fibrosus, fibrous arch of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), supracondylar process/ligament of Struthers, and accessory head of the flexor pollicis longus (FPL; also known as the Gantzer muscle). Given the vague symptoms and lack of gold-standard testing, they emphasize the importance of combining history, physical exam, and EMG/NCV studies to help support the diagnosis.

Xing and Tang provide a detailed review of the various entrapment neuropathies of the forearm and wrist, including pronator syndrome. They outline the common sites of compression, clinical presentations, and management strategies, providing insights into how differential diagnoses are established based on symptomatology and physical exam findings.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1. These sites describe the origin and insertion of the flexor digitorum superficialis. Although the fibrous arch of this muscle is a known site of possible median nerve compression, it is less commonly the primary source, and pain exacerbation with resisted finger flexion would be expected.
Answer 2. These sites describe the origin and insertion of the lacertus fibrosis. Although another commonly implicated structure in pronator syndrome, symptoms would be expected to be exacerbated with resisted elbow flexion and wrist supination.
Answer 3. These sites describe the origin and insertion of the brachioradialis. This may be implicated in either radial tunnel syndrome or Wartenberg syndrome; however, it does not play any role in proximal median nerve compression.
Answer 5. These sites describe the osseous attachments of the transverse carpal ligament, which is a critical structure implicated in carpal tunnel syndrome. The patient in this stem, however, has a negative Tinel sign (i.e., "repeated tapping") as well as a negative Durkan compression test (i.e., "continued compression") at the wrist, making this diagnosis less likely.
