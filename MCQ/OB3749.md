## Question

A 55-year-old male reports a 6-month history of neck and arm pain. He reports a shooting sensation from his neck to his left long finger. He also reports numbness of his left long and index fingers. Physical exam shows weakness to wrist flexion. Figure A is an axial image from his affected level. He has tried physical therapy, NSAIDS, and gabapentin during that time and remains symptomatic. The patient wishes to avoid surgery and would like to proceed with a selective nerve root injection. What cervical level should be targeted, and what is the likelihood it will lead to improvement at one year.

![[q3749-a-disc-herniation.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. C4/5: 25-50%
2. C5/6: 25-50%
3. C5/6: 50-70%
4. C6/7: 25-50%
5. C6/7: 50-70%

## Correct option

**5. C6/7: 50-70%**

## Answer explanation

This patient has cervical radiculopathy at the C6/7 level. Long-term success after epidural steroid injection has been found to range from 50% to 70%.

A disc herniation at the C6/7 level presents with a C7 radiculopathy. This is due to a mismatch in the cervical spine where the nerve root of interest exits above its named pedicle. Patient symptoms of a C7 radiculopathy are numbness and tingling in the middle finger with unilateral weakness with grip strength and overhead activities. C7 radiculopathy also presents with weakness of the ipsilateral triceps and wrist flexors. The triceps reflex is also diminished. Non-operative treatment modalities include physical therapy, NSAIDs, corticosteroids, muscle relaxants, anti-depressants, and epidural steroid injections.

*Rao et al. performed a review of degenerative cervical spondylosis.* **They report** that long-term success has been reported in 50% to 70% of patients who received translaminar or transforaminal epidural corticosteroid injections for the treatment of cervical radiculopathy. **They conclude** that though rare, catastrophic complications may be associated with injections.

*Rhee et al. performed a review of cervical radiculopathy.* **They report** steroids have an anti-inflammatory effect with inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis, interruption of nociceptive input, direct membrane stabilizing effects, sympathetic blockade, blockade of neuropeptide synthesis, and blockade of C-fiber activity in the dorsal root ganglion. The complications of cervical injections include dural puncture, meningitis, epidural abscess, intraocular hemorrhage, adrenocortical suppression, epidural hematoma, and root or cord injury.

*Carragee et al. conducted a critical review of the literature of assessment tools and screening protocols for traumatic and non-traumatic neck pain.* **They report** that injections are only supported in cases with radiculopathy and not neck pain alone. **They conclude** that cervical foraminal or epidural injections can be very effective and are associated with minor adverse events (5%-20%). Serious adverse events are very uncommon occurring in around 1% of cases.

Figure A is a T2-weighted axial MRI slice demonstrating a left-sided disc herniation in the cervical spine. Illustration A is a table from the Rhee et al. article detailing the most common cervical radiculopathy patterns.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: A herniation at the C4/5 level will affect the C5 nerve root which presents with lateral arm pain and weakness in the deltoid (shoulder abduction). The biceps reflex is diminished when compared to the contralateral side.
Answers 2 & 3: A herniation at the C5/6 level will affect the C6 nerve root which presents with radial forearm pain and pain in the thumb and index fingers. Weakness in elbow flexion and wrist extension are present as well. The brachioradialis reflex is diminished when compared to the contralateral side.
Answer 4: Though this patient likely has a herniation at the C6/7 level, long-term success after epidural steroid injection has been found to range from 50% to 70%.
