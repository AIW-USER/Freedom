## Question

A 12-year-old male presents to your pediatric sports medicine clinic with complaints of shoulder pain that has been persistent now for several weeks. His pain is primarily over the lateral aspect of the shoulder and is worse during the late cocking phase of the throwing motion. He is right handed and his right shoulder radiograph is seen in Figure A. What represents the most appropriate treatment plan for this young athlete?

![[q217858-a-ll-shoulder.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Decrease pitch count and breaking balls, but OK to continue playing
2. Refrain from throwing for 2-3 months with physical therapy and progressive return to activity
3. Sling for 4 weeks and non-weight bearing restrictions to right upper extremity
4. Open reduction internal fixation with tension band construct
5. Closed reduction and percutaneous fixation

## Correct option

**2. Refrain from throwing for 2-3 months with physical therapy and progressive return to activity**

## Answer explanation

The primary treatment for little leaguer's shoulder (LLS) is rest, abstinence from throwing and physical therapy.

LLS is an overuse shoulder condition seen primarily in youth baseball players where the proximal humeral physis is affected. Radiographic changes, including physeal widening and sclerosis, reflect these changes. LLS affects the hypertrophic zone of the physis. Elevated pitch counts have been implicated as the primary factor in causing LLS. This includes the number of pitches thrown per game as well as the number of games pitched per week. Treatment is almost always nonoperative, involving cessation of throwing and physical therapy. After symptoms have improved, a graduated throwing plan is restarted to progress back to baseball activities.

*Norton et al. reviewed risk factors for shoulder/elbow injuries for adolescent baseball players.* **They found** that several variables in their systematic review increased the risk for a throwing arm injury in baseball players. These risk factors included age, height, playing for multiple teams, throwing velocity, and arm fatigue.

*Heyworth et al. discussed trends in management and outcomes of LLS.* *They reviewed 95 patients over a 14 year span who were diagnosed with LLS, with the number of cases increasing each year in the study.* *Most patients were pitchers, and the average time to full resolution was 2.6 months with internal rotation deficit being a significant risk factor for the development of LLS.*

*Saper et al. reviewed epidemiologic trends in shoulder/elbow injuries in high school baseball players, also noting that majority of both shoulder and elbow injuries occurred in pitchers.* A high portion of injured pitchers return to play within three weeks but severe elbow injuries resulting in long-term downtime were more common in pitchers. Overall, a vast majority (>90%) of the shoulder and elbow injuries in this population were successfully treated nonoperatively.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the right shoulder depicting proximal humerus physeal widening and sclerosis indicative of LLS.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Treatment for LLS is centered around rest and refraining from throwing for an extended period of time to allow healing of the physis.
Answer 3: Immobilization and non-weight bearing restriction is unnecessary, as the patient should still be able to perform basic activities of daily living.
Answer 4 & 5: Operative intervention is not recommended in LLS, especially in a situation that would risk the integrity of the physis.
