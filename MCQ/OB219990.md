## Question

A 32-year-old homeless male presents to the emergency department at 4 AM after being found unconscious in a snow bank. He awakens shortly after arrival and reports swelling, numbness, and tingling in his fingers. On examination, the digits are cold and blue, capillary refill is absent, and two-point discrimination is greater than 15 mm. No other injuries are identified. Which medication is most appropriate at this time?

## Options

1. Diltiazem
2. Clostridium histolyticum injections
3. Tissue plasminogen activator
4. Topical diclofenac
5. Amlodipine

## Correct option

**3. Tissue plasminogen activator**

## Answer explanation

This 32-year-old homeless male sustained frostbite to his bilateral hands after falling unconscious in a snowbank. In acute scenarios (within 24 hours) tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) can be employed to restore blood flow to the digits and reduce the need for amputation.

Frostbite has been increasing in frequency and is most commonly seen in the northern United States, with a predilection for males (10:1) aged 30-50. Several risk factors have been associated with the condition, with homelessness and alcohol abuse being the most notorious risk factors. The diagnosis can be initially made by observing central pallor with surrounding erythema and possible blister formation of the digits. The degree of frostbite (first through fourth; Illustration A) can only be determined after the extremity has been adequately rewarmed. For those who present acutely following cold exposure (within 24 hours of cold exposure), intravenous tPA is an adjunctive medication that has been shown to decrease the risk of future amputation. The decision to administer the medication should be weighed carefully, as the presence of concomitant trauma, potential for internal bleeding, recent history of major surgery, and a myriad of other factors preclude its use.

*Golant et al. reviewed cold exposure to the extremities, examining the epidemiology, risk factors, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of limbs after prolonged exposure to excessively cold temperatures.* **The authors noted** alcohol use was involved in 63% of patients admitted for frostbite. Initial management of frostbite involves rewarming, and only after this therapy can the degree of frostbite be determined. **The authors note** that intravenous or intra-arterial tPA administered within 24 hours significantly reduces the need for amputation, however, this is not seen in instances of cold exposure >24 hours or warm ischemia times > 6 hours.

Dr. Hutchinson similarly reviews hand frostbite, detailing its pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment. Regarding intravenous tPA, the author notes recent studies have illustrated its utility if given within 24 hours of thawing. However, the author believes the therapy should be avoided in instances of concurrent trauma, neurological impairment, recent surgery, or hemorrhage. Ultimately, the author concludes more evidence is required to better delineate the therapy's ability to prevent amputation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Diltiazem is a calcium channel blocker primarily utilized as an antiarrhythmic medication.
Answer 2: Clostridium histolyticum injections are most commonly used for Dupuytren's disease, and selectively degrades collagen types I and III, but does not affect blood vessels.
Answer 4: Topical diclofenac is a common over-the-counter drug not typically administered in these scenarios.
Answer 5: Amlodipine is a calcium channel blocker often used to treat Raynaud's syndrome, which can be sequelae of milder frostbite, but is not indicated at this time.
