## Question

## Options

2. Early functional rehabilitation
3. Proceed with acute Brostrom repair
4. Short leg cast for eight weeks
5. Lisfranc open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**5. Lisfranc open reduction and internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has plantar ecchymosis and foot radiographs indicative of a Lisfranc injury. In this young, active soldier, surgery to address his Lisfranc injury is indicated, which can be accomplished with either open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or primary arthrodesis (Answer 5).

Lisfranc injuries represent a disruption of the tarsometatarsal joint between the medial cuneiform and base of the second metatarsal. These injuries present in various ways, including medial column disruptions, associated metatarsal base fractures, or intercuneiform disruptions. Surgical management and fixation constructs vary widely depending on the specific fracture pattern. The majority of Lisfranc injuries are managed surgically, and nonoperative management is typically reserved for patients medically unfit for surgery or nondisplaced injuries that are stable with weightbearing. Computed tomography (CT) can be helpful to assess for subtle fractures or a fleck sign (a bony avulsion of the Lisfranc ligament), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be helpful in purely ligamentous injuries. Surgery typically involves ORIF or arthrodesis, though there is some controversy about which patients should undergo one procedure rather than the other. Open reduction and internal fixation is favored in fracture dislocations and acute injuries, while arthrodesis is favored in purely ligamentous injuries or in the case of delayed presentation. Both procedures offer comparable functional outcomes. However, the reoperation rate is higher with ORIF, as many surgeons elect to remove hardware once bony healing is achieved.

*Watson et al. authored a review article on the treatment of Lisfranc injuries.* *They provide a comprehensive review of the diagnosis and management of these injuries.* **They also highlight** the nuances required to identify and manage subtle Lisfranc injuries.

*Cassinelli et al. conducted a retrospective case series of eight patients who underwent delayed ORIF for Lisfranc injuries.* *Patients in the series had a mean time to surgery of 15.1 weeks after injury, and a minimum follow-up of two years.* *Patients showed improvement in pain and function, and* **the authors noted** no postoperative diastasis, suggesting that ORIF can be considered in appropriate patients up to 4 months after injury.

*Rammelt et al. authored a review paper describing Lisfranc arthrodesis as a treatment option for chronic, post-traumatic midfoot deformities.* *They discuss anatomic considerations and describe surgical techniques based on unique injury patterns.*

Figure A is a mortise radiograph of this patient’s ankle. There is no evidence of a fracture. Figure B shows an AP radiograph of the foot, which demonstrates widening of the Lisfranc interval with an associated fracture of the second metatarsal base.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: MRI is typically not indicated. It is usually reserved for patients with chronic ankle instability or when a syndesmotic injury is suspected. An MRI of the foot may be useful in evaluating the integrity of the Lisfranc ligament in patients with more subtle radiographic findings.
Answer 2: Radiographs show a displaced Lisfranc injury which is an indication for surgery.
Answer 3: A lateral ligament repair is utilized to address chronic lateral ankle instability, not a Lisfranc injury
Answer 4: Nonoperative management of Lisfranc injuries is reserved for patients medically unfit for surgery or with stable injuries.
