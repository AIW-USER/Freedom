## Question

A 32-year-old male presents with right ankle pain after rolling his ankle 18 months prior while hiking. He denies trauma since the index event and his primary complaint is a "pins and needles" sensation in the distribution indicated by Figure A. On physical exam, there are no lesions, sensation is intact to light touch, pedal and posterior tibial pulses are 2+ palpable, strength is 5/5, and there is no appreciable subluxation of the peroneal tendons. With percussion about the anterolateral ankle, he has shooting pain up his leg and down his foot. Conservative management, including corticosteroid injections, bracing, anti-inflammatories, and physiotherapy have not improved his symptoms over the course of 1 year. What is the next step in management?

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Semmes-Weinstein monofilament test
2. Platelet-rich plasma injection
3. Neuroma excision and nerve grafting
4. Neurotomy and transposition to bone
5. Broström-Gould reconstruction

## Correct option

**4. Neurotomy and transposition to bone**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a chronic superficial peroneal nerve injury following an ankle inversion injury. After failure of conservative management, the correct answer is neurotomy with transposition into bone.

The superficial peroneal nerve (SPN) is a branch of the common peroneal nerve that supplies most of the dorsum of the foot. The first web space is supplied by the deep peroneal nerve. Proximally, it wraps around the fibular head and exits the anterior compartment of the leg approximately 10-12 centimeters proximal to the lateral malleolus. As the nerve crosses the ankle joint, it is a risk for injury during hyper-inversion injuries. Most of these injuries resolve with prolonged conservative management. For those that fail treatment neurotomy is indicated.

*Chiodo et al. compared 27 patients with symptomatic superficial peroneal neuromas undergoing neurotomy with burial of the proximal stump into muscle (group A) and neurotomy with burial into bone (group B).* Improvement in mean pain score was significantly higher with burial into bone, and **the authors conclude** that this treatment offers superior results compared with burial in muscle.

*Acus et al. described a case report in 1991 of perineural fibrosis of the superficial peroneal nerve at the level of the ankle following an ankle inversion injury.* This traction injury was previously described only at the level of the popliteal fossa and fibular neck.

Figure A shows the expected pattern paresthesias following an injury to the SPN at the level of the ankle. Illustration A shows the anatomy of the superficial peroneal nerve. Illustration B shows the dermatomes of the foot including the SPN.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Semmes-Weinstein monofilament testing is used in screening for diabetic neuropathy.
Answer 2: There is no evidence to support the use of platelet-rich plasma in this instance.
Answer 3: Neuroma excision and nerve grafting has not been established as the best management for a chronic superficial peroneal nerve traction injury.
Answer 5: The Broström-Gould procedure is performed for lateral ligamentous instability, which is not evident on this patient's physical exam.
