## Question

A 7-year-old girl presents with a 3-day history of worsening fever and left hip pain. She recalls falling onto her left hip during a soccer game earlier in the week but brushed it off and kept playing. She has a temperature of 39 degrees Celsius and is only able to bear minimal weight on the left leg due to pain. CRP is 13.2 mg/dL (normal <2.0), ESR 52 mm/h (normal <20), and WBC 15.1 (normal <10.0). An ultrasound of the left hip reveals an effusion and the hip is aspirated. ## Answer explanation

Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative organism in pediatric septic arthritis, and the micrograph in Figure D shows Gram-positive cocci in clusters, characteristic of a Staphylococcus species.

This patient is presenting with 4 of the 5 modified Kocher criteria, putting her likelihood of having a septic hip at 93.1% (Caird, JBJS 2006). Septic arthritis is one of the few surgical emergencies in orthopedics - chondral damage can occur within as soon as 8 hours due to the hydrolytic enzymes, so prompt irrigation and drainage are the standard of care for the vast majority of cases. Concerning findings on history and physical is typically followed by some imaging study (ultrasound or rapid-sequence MRI, as long as it does not delay treatment) and aspiration to confirm the diagnosis before operative intervention. In cases of gonococcal arthritis, however, high-dose penicillin with an initial joint aspiration for synovial fluid sample collection can be the definitive treatment. However, in cases of persistent effusion, the affected joint may require drainage.

*Erkilinc et al. published a 2021 JAAOS review on pediatric septic arthritis, where the authors reviewed the etiology, workup, diagnosis, and treatment.* Causative pathogens can vary depending on the child’s age, comorbidities or immunodeficiency, socioeconomic factors, and vaccination status. Overall, S. aureus is the primary causative organism; however, recent studies suggest that Kingella kingae may be the most common cause in children younger than 5 years of age. In adolescents, Neisseria gonorrhoeae must be considered. Additionally, although Haemophilus influenzae type B was the most common cause historically, fortunately immunization has all but eradicated it as an etiology of septic arthritis.

Figure A is a micrograph of the spirochete Treponema pallidum, the organism responsible for syphilis. Figure B is a micrograph of Neisseria gonorrhoeae, classically a facultatively intracellular Gram-negative diplococcus. Figure C is a micrograph of E. coli, a Gram-negative bacillus. Figure D is a micrograph of S. aureus, a Gram-positive coccus. Figure E is a micrograph of Kingella kingae, a Gram-negative coccobacillus.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 2, 3, 5: None of these are the most common organism responsible for pediatric septic arthritis of the hip.
