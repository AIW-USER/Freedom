## Question

Your first case of the day involves shoulder arthroscopy. After establishing the posterior viewing portal, you perform the posterior lever push test and are subsequently concerned about a tear of the corresponding structure. Which of the following intraoperative findings would most support this finding?

## Options

1. Absent middle glenohumeral ligament
2. Humeral avulsion of inferior glenohumeral ligaments
3. Partial thickness articular sided supraspinatus tear
4. Detachment of biceps anchor with tear extension into posterior labrum
5. Intrasubstance biceps tendon tear

## Correct option

**1. Absent middle glenohumeral ligament**

## Answer explanation

This patient's arthroscopic findings are concerning for a tear of the subscapularis and absence of the middle glenohumeral ligament (MGHL) further supports these findings.

Subscapularis tears are being increasingly recognized in the setting of supra/infraspinatus pathology. The function of the subscapularis is important in the anterior/posterior force coupling of the shoulder. Preoperatively, physical exam findings consisting of decreased internal rotation, weakness with belly press/lift-off maneuvers and excessive passive external rotation can be indicative of subscapularis tearing. Intraoperatively, the posterior lever push maneuver can be helpful to better visualize the subscapularis insertion on the lesser tuberosity of the humeral head. Still, subscapularis tears can sometimes be difficult to visualize. The MGHL is normally a fairly consistent landmark adjacent to the subscapularis and anterior labrum, that when absent, may suggest abnormality of the subscapularis. It is thought that as the subscapularis tears and retracts, the MGHL similarly translates medially over the anterior glenoid and is no longer easily visualized from the posterior portal. Subscapularis repair is recommended when possible to restore the the normal force coupling mechanism of the shoulder.

*Lee et al. review subscapularis tears,* **noting** they have long been the "forgotten," tendon of rotator cuff pathology. They go through physical examination maneuvers (bear hug, lift off, and belly press tests), and discuss the Lafosse classification (Illustration A) which is helpful with treatment. **They note** that Lafosse I/II tears are typically able to be addressed viewing from a posterior portal, while larger Lafosse III/IV tears are best repaired visualizing anteriorly from the subacromial or subdeltoid space. Lafosse V tears typically require tendon transfers or reverse shoulder arthroplasty.

*Chauvet et al. review the "invisible MGHL test," noting that some subscapularis tears, even when full-thickness, are difficult to identify arthroscopically.* Therefore, having intra-operative clues to identify these tears can ensure subscapularis tears are appropriately repaired. They reviewed 100 arthroscopic videos looking for the absence of the MGHL when entering the joint. The "invisible MGHL," was present in 62% of cases with a retracted subscapularis tear and its presence was associated with greater tear width and retraction. The restoration of the normal MGHL appearance was also helpful to ensure that appropriate tension had been restored to the tendon after repair.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2-5: While all of these shoulder pathologies are significant, none have been reliably shown to predict associated tears in the subscapularis.
