## Question

A 66-year-old female presents to the ER with acute right hip pain after a mechanical ground-level fall. Imaging studies are shown in Figures A and B. She has a history of metastatic breast cancer with her Oncologist noting a favorable 5-year prognosis. She is scheduled for radiation therapy next week. No functional limitations at baseline and she is a community ambulator without assistive devices. What surgical option would be most appropriate in the management of her fracture?

## Options

1. Hemiarthroplasty
2. Percutaneous pinning
3. Total hip arthroplasty
4. Cephalomedullary nail
5. Sliding hip screw

## Correct option

**3. Total hip arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

A total hip arthroplasty (THA) would be most appropriate in this patient with metastatic disease, a favorable prognosis, and a pathologic femoral neck fracture with acetabular lesions on imaging.

The hip is a common location for the development of bony metastatic disease. Once a pathologic fracture develops, the central goal of treatment is to provide pain relief and unrestricted weight-bearing while ideally outlasting the patient. For this reason, prosthetic replacement is typically the treatment of choice for pathologic femoral neck fractures. Internal fixation with plates and screws, or cannulated screws have been shown to have a high failure rate in such cases. Controversy exists in the current literature on whether cementing components is necessary in the setting of pathologic femoral neck fractures. A proposed concern with the use of uncemented stems is the growth of tumor in the cortices supporting the stems resulting in prosthetic loosening. Furthermore, cementing the femoral component imparts immediate stability, while bony ingrowth is unreliable in uncemented tumor cases especially when radiation therapy is warranted. Nevertheless, there are a number of studies demonstrating good outcomes with uncemented reconstruction. It is relatively unknown whether cementing the acetabular component is beneficial in these situations, but most case series and case reports support uncemented acetabular fixation with additional screws for improved stability.

*Wedin et al. evaluated pathological fractures of the proximal femur and the various treatment options available to manage them.* The most important risk factor for failure of treatment was the length of survival after the operation. In addition, reconstruction with prosthetic devices, as opposed to osteosynthetic devices, appeared to be safer as endoprostheses are not dependent on the healing of the fracture.

*Issack et al. reviewed surgical management of metastatic disease of the proximal part of the femur, and specific to femoral neck fractures* **they recommend** prosthetic replacements as the treatment of choice.

*Macaulay et al. compared THA to hemiarthroplasty in the treatment of femoral neck fractures in a prospective, randomized multicenter clinical trial.* **They found** significantly improved outcomes in the THA group compared to the hemiarthroplasty group with regard to pain relief and function at 2 years.

Figures A and B are CT images demonstrating a nondisplaced pathologic femoral neck fracture with large cystic acetabular lesions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A hemiarthroplasty can be considered in certain pathologic femoral neck fractures, however this patient’s good prognosis with cystic lesions in the acetabulum would be best addressed with a THA.
Answers 2,4,5: Given the intracapsular nature of this fracture in combination with the poor healing environment associated with diseased bone, reconstruction is superior to fixation.
