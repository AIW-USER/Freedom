## Question

Her presenting radiograph is shown in Figure A. She is prescribed physical therapy with vitamin D/calcium supplementation and is discharged home. ## Options

1. Closed reduction and cephalomedullary nail fracture fixation
2. Closed reduction and dynamic hip screw fracture fixation
3. Open reduction via anterior approach with internal fixation
4. Hip resurfacing
5. Hemiarthroplasty

## Correct option

**5. Hemiarthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a transcervical femoral neck insufficiency fracture. Due to the fracture location and displacement, the most appropriate operative treatment is arthroplasty.

Femoral neck insufficiency fractures in the elderly are associated with a high mortality rate among the elderly. In general, the goal of treatment is to allow early mobilization and limit complications associated with fracture disease (i.e. bed sores, pneumonia, narcotic-related delirium, etc). For this reason, patients generally have better outcomes when treated within the first 24-48 hours after injury. Femoral neck insufficiency fractures are often classified based on the Garden Classification (Illustration A). Valgus-impacted and nondisplaced femoral neck fractures may be considered for operative fixation. However, displaced femoral neck fractures have an unacceptably high rate of revision due to nonunion after operative fixation. Therefore, displaced femoral neck fractures are best treated with arthroplasty procedures (hemiarthroplasty versus total hip arthroplasty).

*Pincus and colleagues performed a population-based, retrospective cohort study of adults undergoing hip fracture surgery over 5 years.* **They found** that among the 42,230 patients, the overall mortality at 30 days was 7.0% and at 1-year was ~20%. The authors then utilized propensity-score matching to account for covariates (comorbidities, age, surgeon volume, etc) and separated these matched groups into (1) those who received surgery in <24 hours and (2) those who received surgery in >24 hours. **They found** that patients who underwent surgery within 24 hours after injury had significantly lower mortality at all time points and lower all-cause complication rates. **The authors concluded** that increased wait time is associated with greater health risks in this patient population.

*Bhandari and colleagues performed a systematic review of level-1 randomized controlled trials comparing internal fixation to arthroplasty for the treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures in the elderly (65+ years).* **They found** that arthroplasty had an overall 10.5% revision rate compared to the 35% revision rate of internal fixation (p<0.05). However, arthroplasty had significantly higher complication rates. **The authors concluded** that while arthroplasty had higher infection rates, blood loss, and operative time, it incurred a much lower risk for revision surgery.

*Leonardsson and colleagues reported their long-term (10-year) follow-up of their original randomized controlled trial comparing internal fixation to arthroplasty for displaced femoral neck insufficiency fractures (age 70+).* **They reported** internal fixation had a 45.6% failure rate compared to arthroplasty which had an 8.8% failure rate (p<0.05). Both groups had similar patient-reported pain, functional outcomes, and mortality rates. **The authors concluded** primary replacement allows reliable long-term results in displaced femoral neck insufficiency fractures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1&2: Extracapsular fractures, such as basicervical femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures (Illustration B) are most often treated with a cephalomedullary nail or dynamic hip screw construct. Due to their extracapsular anatomic location, the blood supply to the fracture site is maintained and therefore these injuries are much more likely to go onto union with operative fixation.
Answer 3: In younger patients with traumatic femoral neck fractures, open reduction internal fixation serves as the gold standard surgical treatment. Outcomes are predicated on the quality of reduction. Therefore, many surgeons prefer to directly visualize the fracture reduction via an anterior hip approach when placing internal fixation.
Answer 4: Hip resurfacing is indicated for younger patients with advanced arthritis and good proximal femoral bone stock. Femoral neck fractures are considered an absolute contraindication to hip resurfacing.
