## Question

A 24-year-old female with chronic midfoot pain presents to your office with progressively worsening pain for the last six weeks. She notes that she is currently training for her third marathon and has been unable to run the past two weeks due to pain. Before her first marathon two years ago, she had similar pain and treated it with a trial of immobilization, which slightly improved her symptoms. ## Options

1. Weight bearing as tolerated in a hard soled shoe
2. Non weight bearing cast immobilization
3. Fragment excision and posterior tibial tendon advancement
4. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning
5. Open reduction and internal fixation with autologous calcaneus bone graft

## Correct option

**5. Open reduction and internal fixation with autologous calcaneus bone graft**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with symptoms and imaging studies consistent with a chronic navicular stress fracture and associated diastasis. Initial management of these injuries typically consists of non-weight-bearing cast immobilization, which she previously tried two years ago. Now that this is a chronic issue that did not heal with immobilization, and the fracture is intra-articular, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with autologous bone grafting is warranted (Answer 5).

Tarsal navicular fractures are rare midfoot injuries that may occur with acute trauma or due to repetitive microstress. These present with vague midfoot pain and swelling. Diagnosis is established on physical examination, plain radiographs, and, often, advanced imaging, including CT or MRI. Initial treatment consists of conservative management with cast immobilization. Surgical treatment indications include failing conservative management, avulsion fractures involving >25% of the articular surface, tuberosity fractures with >5mm of diastasis or a large intra-articular fragment, and displaced and/or intra-articular Type 1 or 2 navicular body fractures. Surgical treatment includes fragment excision, closed versus open reduction and internal fixation, or fusion.

*Khan et al* **found** that patients managed with a minimum of 6 weeks of non-weight-bearing (NWB) had significantly improved rates of return to sport (86%) compared with patients who were allowed to weight bear as tolerated (26%). After failure of weight-bearing management, 6/7 patients who were then NWB in a cast were able to return to sports.

*Lee et al reviewed the clinical presentation, evaluation, and treatment of navicular stress fractures.* **The authors advocate** for initial management with NWB immobilization and note that CT and MRI may be useful to determine the chronicity of the injury.

*Torg et al performed a meta-analysis of the existing literature regarding the treatment of navicular stress fractures.* **The authors note** that no difference has been found regarding patient outcomes between NWB and operative management, and they state that NWB cast immobilization should be the initial treatment for navicular stress fractures.

Figure A is an AP plain radiograph of the foot demonstrating an intra-articular navicular body fracture. Figure B is an axial CT scan demonstrating mild diastasis of the navicular body fracture with intra-articular involvement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Weight bearing as tolerated in a hard-soled shoe is less effective than NWB cast immobilization for navicular stress fractures, and the patient has already tried this. Therefore, surgical treatment is the next step in management.
Answer 2: NWB with cast immobilization is the first step in managing these injuries; however, the patient previously tried this and the injury recurred. Therefore, surgical treatment is the next step in management.
Answer 3: Fragment excision and tendon advancement is not a described technique to manage these injuries.
Answer 4: Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning is not a described technique to manage these injuries.
