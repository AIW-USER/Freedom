## Question

A 39-year-old male presents to the clinic for evaluation of wrist pain. One year ago, he recalls participating in a mosh pit and injured his wrist while falling onto an outstretched hand. He immediately felt vague pain, which decreased over the ensuing days but never completely resolved. Radiographs obtained in the clinic demonstrate a scapholunate angle of 80°. Which of the following ligament disruptions would most likely result in this finding?

## Options

1. Volar scapholunate ligament
2. Interosseous scapholunate ligament
3. Dorsal scapholunate ligament
4. Volar lunotriquetral ligament
5. Dorsal lunotriquetral ligament

## Correct option

**3. Dorsal scapholunate ligament**

## Answer explanation

This 39-year-old exhibits an increased scapholunate angle, suggesting a scapholunate injury with subsequent dorsal intercalated segment instability (DISI). The most important stabilizer for the scapholunate joint is the dorsal scapholunate ligament (Answer 3).

Scapholunate injuries are common conditions following low-energy trauma and are often seen with concomitant injuries, such as distal radius fractures (30%). A thorough evaluation is required, as subtle injuries are often missed and, if neglected, can lead to significant disability secondary to altered wrist kinematics (DISI) and eventual osteoarthritis (scapholunate advanced collapse). The scapholunate ligament (SLL) is C-shaped and comprised of three parts: volar, interosseous (or proximal), and dorsal. The latter-most has been shown to be the most important component of the ligamentous complex and is most stout in terms of thickness, measuring 3 mm on average (vs. 1 mm in the volar component). If the injury is left untreated, DISI develops and is characterized by an extended lunate secondary to an intact lunotriquetral ligament and pull from the triquetrum, which manifests as an increased scapholunate angle, typically greater than 70° (normal: 30 to 70°; Illustration A).

Rajan and Day provide a review of the scapholunate ligament anatomy and wrist biomechanics. They note the dorsal part of the SLL is the most important, and disruption of this ligament leads to altered wrist mechanics. Normally, in instances of wrist flexion and/or radial deviation, the scaphoid flexes and pulls the lunate into flexion by way of the SLL. In contrast, ulnar deviation forces the scaphoid and lunate into extension through increased forces transmitted through the triquetral-hamate articulation (Illustration B). With SLL disruptions, significant increases in scaphoid flexion and lunate extension occur, with pathologic rotatory subluxation of the scaphoid also being demonstrated. Collectively, the injury leads to altered radiocarpal and ulnocarpal pressure distributions, with continued pathologic pressures resulting in osteoarthritis. The authors note that increased subluxation of the scaphoid may contribute to DISI deformity.

*Short et al. performed a cadaver study examining 24 cadaver forearms placed in a wrist stimulator and subjected to 1,000 cycles of motion after sectioning each of the three components of the SLL.* **The authors noted** sectioning of the SLL altered scaphoid and lunate kinematics, whereas sectioning of the scaphotrapezium and radioscaphocapitate ligaments conferred minimal angular changes. **The authors determined** the SLL functions as the primary stabilizer of the scapholunate joint, while the scaphotrapezium and radioscaphocapitate ligaments function as secondary stabilizers.

*Short et al. performed an additional study with 24 cadaver forearms, further sectioning the dorsal radiocarpal ligament and dorsal intercarpal ligaments to determine their function.* **They noted** that dividing the dorsal intercarpal ligament failed to alter scaphoid or lunate kinematics, while sectioning the dorsal radiocarpal ligament did result in a slight increase in the lunate's radial deviation. Sectioning of the SLL following sectioning of the dorsal intercarpal, dorsal radiocarpal, or scaphotrapezial ligaments resulted in significant scaphoid flexion and lunate extension. The findings of the study reaffirmed the importance of the SLL's role in scapholunate kinematics.

Illustration A demonstrates the scapholunate angle, which is the angle between the longitudinal line through the axis of the lunate and another line through the axis of the scaphoid. Illustration B is a diagram representing the kinematics of the wrist (retrieved from the Rajan and Day review).

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 & 3: the volar and interosseous parts of the scapholunate ligament do not play as integral role in scapholunate joint stability as the dorsal ligament.
Answers 4 & 5: disruption of the lunotriquetral ligament leads to volar intercalated segment instability, which would demonstrate a scapholunate angle less than 30°. The volar portion of the lunotriquetral ligament plays the most important role in lunotriquetral stability.
