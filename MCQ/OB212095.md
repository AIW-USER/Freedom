## Question

A 47-year-old male patient presents to your clinic stating he is concerned he may have PIN syndrome after researching his symptoms on the internet. Which physical exam findings would corroborate with this diagnosis?

## Options

1. Intact sensation throughout the hand, minimal pain present, inability to actively extend fingers from a clenched fist, but can maintain active finger extension
2. Intact sensation throughout hand and forearm, pain present in mobile wad and tracking distally along radial aspect of forearm, 5/5 wrist and finger extension
3. Decreased sensation in right upper extremity, pain radiating from shoulder to long finger, decreased triceps and wrist flexor muscle strength
4. Intact sensation in the upper extremity, pain distal to the lateral epicondyle overlying the mobile wad, weakness of the wrist and finger extensors
5. Decreased sensation and pain over the dorsal aspect of the thumb and wrist, normal motor strength in the upper extremity muscle groups

## Correct option

**4. Intact sensation in the upper extremity, pain distal to the lateral epicondyle overlying the mobile wad, weakness of the wrist and finger extensors**

## Answer explanation

Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) syndrome is characterized by localized pain with motor weakness affecting the wrist and finger extensors in the setting of intact sensation (Answer 4).

PIN syndrome is a compression neuropathy of the deep motor branch of the radial nerve in the forearm. Symptoms consist of localized elbow or forearm pain (dependent on the site of compression), decreased motor function to the wrist and finger extensors (except the extensor carpi radialis longus), and intact sensation. While lipomas are the most common lesion responsible for PIN syndrome, other sites of compression have been described, including the vascular leash of Henry, Arcade of Fröshe (leading edge of the supinator), anomalous fibrous connections between the capsule of the radiocapitellar joint, and the extensor carpi radialis brevis muscle edge. Surgical decompression is indicated in patients who remain symptomatic despite three months of activity modifications, splinting, and NSAIDs.

*Dang and Rodner reviewed compressive neuropathies of the radial nerve in the forearm.* *They distinguished PIN syndrome from radial tunnel syndrome by the isolated presence of motor deficits and mobile wad pain, respectively.* *They cited literature supporting lipomas as being the most common cause of PIN syndrome.*

*Konjengbam and Elangbam performed a cadaveric study of 46 upper extremities, documenting the anatomy of the radial nerve in the forearm.* **They reported** fibrous adhesions between the deep branch of the radial nerve and elbow joint capsule in 50% of specimens, vascular arcades between radial recurrent vessels in 72% of specimens, a tendinous superomedial border of the ECRB in 78% of specimens, and a tendinous arcade of Fröshe in 87% of specimens. **The authors concluded** that these anatomic variants contribute to the development of radial nerve entrapment neuropathy.

*Lister et al. performed a retrospective study of 20 patients who underwent surgical decompression for radial tunnel syndrome.* **They reported** most patients to have symptoms for ~21 months before diagnosis and were often initially treated for lateral epicondylitis. There was a 95% symptomatic improvement following surgical release of the radial tunnel.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Sagittal band rupture results in subluxation of the extensor tendons from the dorsal aspect of the MCP joint leading to a flexion moment arm rather than an extension moment. This manifests as the inability to actively extend the fingers from a clenched fist, but the ability to maintain finger extension.
Answer 2: Radial tunnel syndrome is characterized by pain in the mobile wad that is distal to the lateral epicondyle and not associated with motor deficits.
Answer 3: C7 radiculopathy can manifest as upper extremity pain and weakness that radiates into the long finger and is associated with triceps and wrist flexion weakness.
Answer 5: Wartenberg's syndrome is a compression neuropathy of the superficial radial nerve and manifests with numbness and pain in the dorsal thumb, index, and long finger proximal to the PIP joints.
