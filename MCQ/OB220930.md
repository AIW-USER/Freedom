## Question

A 15-year-old male presents to your clinic with hip pain for the past few months. He just started his high school cross-country season, but began to notice pain while practicing. Radiographs of his pelvis show moderate hip dysplasia bilaterally. Notable exam findings include a foot deformity, which is demonstrated in Figure A. What is this patient’s most likely diagnosis?

![[q220930-a-pes-cavus.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Osteogenesis Imperfecta
2. Down Syndrome
3. Charcot-Marie-Tooth
4. Achondroplasia
5. Cleidocranial dysplasia

## Correct option

**3. Charcot-Marie-Tooth**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a cavovarus foot deformity and evidence of hip dysplasia, raising concern for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (Answer 3). The diagnosis can be supported by electrodynamic nerve studies, which will show decreased nerve conduction velocity.

Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) is an inherited neuromuscular disease characterized by a variety of sensory, motor, or sensorimotor neuropathies. It is the most common inherited neuromuscular disorder. Generally, CMT is caused by dysfunctional myelin sheath proteins, which cause progressive demyelination and neuropathy in peripheral nerves (though CMT Type 2 is unique in that it involves axonal damage and Wallerian degeneration with an intact myelin sheath). As a result, nerve conduction velocities are decreased on nerve studies. The most common inheritance pattern is autosomal dominant and involves the duplication of chromosome 17, which codes for peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP 22). The orthopedic conditions involved in CMT are broad and include pes cavovarus foot deformity, toe clawing, scoliosis, and hip dysplasia. In patients with CMT, it is important to screen for hip dysplasia, as even in severe cases, the hips may be asymptomatic. The prevalence of hip dysplasia in patients with CMT ranges from 6-8%. Surgical treatment of hip dysplasia has comparable outcomes to patients with developmental dysplasia, underscoring that early identification and management of hip dysplasia are important to optimizing the quality of life in these patients.

*Novais et al. conducted a cohort study comparing the severity of hip dysplasia in patients with CMT and patients with developmental dysplasia.* *They included 14 patients in the CMT cohort and matched these to 45 patients with developmental dysplasia.* Overall, the severity of hip dysplasia was significantly more severe in the CMT cohort, with a smaller lateral center edge angle, smaller overall acetabular volume, and larger Tonnis angle. Femoral head under-coverage was also more severe in the CMT cohort, as was the prevalence of arthritis. **The authors concluded** that patients with CMT hip dysplasia typically have a higher degree of acetabular deformity, worse hip subluxation, and more severe arthritis than patients with developmental dysplasia.

*Stover et al. performed a retrospective review of patients with CMT hip dysplasia who underwent periacetabular osteotomy (PAO).* *They included 14 patients who underwent PAO in 19 hips with a mean age of 16.2 years and an average follow-up of 3.4 years.* Radiographic parameters were significantly improved postoperatively. Patient-reported outcome measures significantly improved postoperatively in a majority of patients, but complications such as avascular necrosis, perioperative ramus fractures, and heterotopic ossification were common. **The authors concluded** that while complications were common following PAO, a majority of patients did significantly improve in terms of patient-reported outcomes.

Figure A demonstrates a foot with a pes cavovarus deformity commonly seen in patients with CMT.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Osteogenesis Imperfecta can present with extremity deformities, but is not classically associated with hip dysplasia or a cavovarus foot deformity.
Answer 2: Down syndrome can present with hip dysplasia. However, pes planovalgus is seen rather than a cavovarus foot.
Answer 4: Achondroplasia classically presents with rhizomelic dwarfism, spine abnormalities, and genu varum.
Answer 5: Cleidocranial dysplasia is not typically associated with a cavovarus foot deformity and usually presents with clavicle aplasia/dysplasia, dental abnormalities, and a degree of proportionate dwarfism.
