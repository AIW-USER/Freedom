## Question

A 35-year-old jiu-jitsu instructor presents after sustaining a knee injury at the gym. His presenting radiographs are shown in Figure A, which do not demonstrate any acute fractures. He is found to have a posterior cruciate ligament tear and undergoes reconstruction. One year later he presents to the office with a new injury to the same knee sustained while performing burpees at CrossFit. He has a 1A Lachman test and his dial test results are as shown below:

At 30° of knee flexion:
Left knee: 28° external rotation
Right knee: 15° external rotation

At 90° of knee flexion:
Left knee: 26° external rotation
Right knee: 12° external rotation

Which of the following would be the most appropriate surgical management for this patient?
(ACL = anterior cruciate ligament, HTO = high tibial osteotomy, PCL = posterior cruciate ligament, PLC = posterolateral corner)

![[q217822-a-varus-alignment-radiographs.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. ACL, PCL and PLC soft tissue reconstruction alone
2. Isolated PLC reconstruction, HTO that also increases tibial slope
3. Isolated PLC reconstruction, HTO that also decreases tibial slope
4. PLC and PCL reconstruction, HTO that also increases tibial slope
5. PLC and PCL reconstruction, HTO that also decreases tibial slope

## Correct option

**4. PLC and PCL reconstruction, HTO that also increases tibial slope**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a repeated PCL tear with additional PLC injury, as indicated by >10° external rotation asymmetry when performing a dial test with the knee at 30° and 90° of flexion. He has a baseline genu varum malalignment deformity and performing a high tibial osteotomy (HTO) focused on increasing the tibial slope will improve his mechanical alignment and protect his PCL/PLC reconstruction from reinjury.

*Savarese and colleagues reviewed the role of HTO in the setting of chronic PCL/PLC injuries.* **The authors discuss** that performing an HTO not only corrects coronal alignment but also may correct sagittal alignment as well. In the setting of PCL/PLC injuries, an HTO can be performed to improve varus coronal alignment and increase the tibial slope, which will effectively decrease the posterior tibial translation through range of motion. **The authors conclude** this surgery ultimately protects the PCL/PLC soft tissue reconstruction surgeries with improved long-term outcomes.

*Rossi and colleagues performed a comprehensive review of the surgical technique for HTOs and how to change the sagittal alignment.* **They discussed** that medial opening wedge HTOs have significantly lower complication rates and is generally preferred over lateral closing wedge HTOs. In particular, the peroneal nerves make the lateral closing wedge HTO a more technically difficult procedure. **They conclude** that cruciate ligament reconstruction injuries often have predisposing coronal and sagittal malalignment which can be corrected at the time of ligamentous reconstructive surgery.

*Herman and colleagues reviewed the role of HTO in the setting of chronic anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) deficiency.* **They discussed** that an HTO that decreases the posterior tibial slope will lead to decreased anterior tibial translation through range of motion, which protects any ACL reconstructive surgery patients with genu varum. **The authors conclude** that when indicated appropriately, patients undergoing ACL reconstruction with HTO can expect to return to their everyday activities and recreational sports without debilitating pain.

Figure A shows a lower extremity alignment radiograph in a patient with significant genu varum. The red line indicated the overall mechanical axis which passes medial to each patella, indicative of genu varum.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient had a 1A Lachman, indicating that his ACL is intact. Therefore, an ACL reconstructive surgery would not be indicated.
Answers 2&3: This patient has an abnormal dial test at both 30° and 90° of flexion, indicating both PLC and PCL injuries. Both should be addressed at the time of surgery.
Answer 5: When performing an HTO in the setting of PCL/PLC injuries and coronal malalignment, the posterior tibial slope should be INCREASED to prevent posterior tibial translation through range of motion. This protects the soft tissue reconstructive surgery and addresses the coronal plane malalignment as well.
