## Question

You evaluate a 35-year-old male in the trauma bay with multiple injuries after a high-speed motor vehicle accident. Radiographs demonstrate multiple rib fractures and a shortened right subtrochanteric femur fracture as seen in Figure A. On exam, the patient has a significant amount of bruising, fluctuance, and blistering with skin hypermobility present over the fracture in the right proximal lateral thigh, as shown in Figure B. A CT scan distal to the fracture is shown in Figure C with a notable fluid collection. Where is this patient's fluid likely accumulating in his thigh?

## Options

1. Dermal layer
2. Deep to subcutaneous fat and superficial to deep fascia
3. Subcutaneous fat
4. Intramuscular
5. Deep to muscle

## Correct option

**2. Deep to subcutaneous fat and superficial to deep fascia**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained polytraumatic injuries after an MVC and developed a Morel-Lavallee lesion (MLL) in the right proximal lateral thigh around his subtrochanteric femur fracture. This results from a shearing force, separating the subcutaneous fat from the deep fascia (answer 2).

MLLs are soft tissue degloving injuries that are typically caused by high-energy mechanisms. They result from a shearing force, separating the dermis and subcutaneous fat from the underlying deep fascia. This subsequently injures lymphatics and vessels in the subdermal plexus, leading to a significant collection of fluid between these layers. These lesions are typically associated with pelvic, acetabular, and proximal femur injuries. A large fluctuance with hypermobility of the skin on examination, in addition to advanced imaging showing a fluid collection between these layers, are both used to diagnose MLLs. Nonoperative treatment, such as compressive therapies, can be used in smaller, isolated lesions (<50 cm³). Operative intervention is indicated for lesions exceeding 50 cm³ and typically involves thorough (and often serial) irrigation and debridement of the region, with or without drain placement.

*Scolaro et al. reviewed the diagnosis and management of MLLs.* **They report** that it is crucial to diagnose MLLs promptly because distracting injuries in a polytraumatized patient can lead to a missed or delayed diagnosis. **They found** that the incidence of bacterial colonization in these lesions reaches 46% in some studies, regardless of the time from injury to debridement. Therefore, **they conclude** that it is important to diagnose and treat MLLs promptly due to their high association with perioperative infection.

*Powers et al. wrote a case report on MLLs.* **They report** that these lesions most commonly occur in the proximal thigh and trochanter, rarely causing bilateral injuries. MLLs may mimic a pseudotumor when not diagnosed early on. **They conclude** that MLLs have a high association with perioperative infection, and timely diagnosis, with MRI as the modality of choice, is paramount.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: A seroma can collect in the dermis layer, but MLLs do not.
Answer 3: Generalized peripheral edema most commonly occurs when fluid collects in the subcutaneous or hypodermis layer. This is spread throughout the entire limb and does not cause a large localized fluctuance on exam.
Answer 4: Intramuscular fluid collections (hematoma) can occur following trauma and result from trauma to the blood vessels supplying the muscle. This does not cause hypermobility of skin.
Answer 5: Fluid collections deep to muscle can occur from bleeding bone or vascular injuries after high-energy trauma; however, this will not cause the skin to be hypermobile.
