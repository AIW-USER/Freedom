## Question

A 42-year-old patient presents to clinic with complaints of isolated medial-sided right knee pain. The patient is active and participates in recreational sports frequently. X-rays demonstrate isolated moderate medial compartment osteoarthritis. The decision is made to proceed with a high tibial osteotomy (HTO). When considering an HTO in the setting of knee instability, what is the effect of posterior slope with regards to the integrity of the ACL and PCL?

## Options

1. A decreased posterior slope would lead to more stability for a PCL deficient knee
2. An increased posterior slope would lead to more stability for a PCL deficient knee
3. A neutral posterior slope would provide the greatest stability for a PCL deficient knee
4. An increased posterior slope would lead to more stability for an ACL deficient knee
5. A decreased posterior slope would lead to less stability for an ACL deficient knee

## Correct option

**2. An increased posterior slope would lead to more stability for a PCL deficient knee**

## Answer explanation

In a PCL-deficient knee, an increased tibial slope would lead to greater stability. With PCL deficiency, the tibia will translate posteriorly with superior directed force such as weight bearing activity (which is why the integrity of the PCL is evaluated with the posterior drawer). By increasing the posterior tibial slope, a superiorly directed force will decrease the posterior translation of the tibia, thereby increasing the stability of the knee.

In the native knee, the ACL and PCL are essential to maintaining the kinematics and stability in knee joints. During neutral tibial rotation, the ACL and PCL are the primary restraints for the anterior and posterior drawers, respectively. During mid-flexion, the ACL and PCL provide knee joint stability, and during high flexion the PCL is partially responsible for posterior femoral rollback. In the setting of isolated medial or lateral compartment osteoarthritis, surgical options include an HTO or a unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA). HTOs are used to change the mechanical weight-bearing axis and alter the loads carried through the knee. Conventional indications for HTO are isolated compartment osteoarthritis and coronal plane malalignment of the knee causing pain and dysfunction. The indications for an HTO include patients with chronic ligament deficiencies and malalignment, because an HTO procedure can change not only the coronal but also the sagittal plane of the knee.

*Savarese et al. reviewed the effects of chronic posterior instability and tibial slope alteration on knee and soft tissues, in addition to planning and surgical technique for chronic posterior and posterolateral instability with HTO.* **They report** that decreased posterior tibial slope causes posterior tibia translation and helps the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-deficient knee. Vice versa, increased tibial slope causes anterior tibia translation and helps the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)-deficient knee. **They further state** that a review of literature shows that soft tissue procedures alone are often unsatisfactory for chronic posterior instability if alignment is not corrected.

*Lee et al. investigated the biomechanical effects on PCL-deficient knees in medial UKA.* **They reported** that the posterior stability for low flexion activities in PCL-deficient UKA remained unaffected; however, the posterior stability for high flexion activities was negatively affected. **They further reported** that posterior stability may reduce the overall risk of progressive OA which is achieved by increasing the posterior tibial slope (and that excessive posterior tibial slope must be avoided).

*Plancher et al. evaluated the relationship between preoperative posterior tibial slope and postoperative slope of the implant (PSI) on outcomes in patients with ACL-intact and ACL-deficient knees after fixed-bearing medial UKA.* **They reported** that there were no significant differences in outcome scores between the ACL-intact and the ACL-deficient group so the data was combined for analysis of slope. **They noted** that for all patients, at 8 years followup, functional outcome scores were better in patients with PSI ≤7°. **They concluded** that for patients undergoing fixed-bearing medial UKA, the posterior slope should be less than 7°, as a slope >7° leads to significantly worse postoperative pain.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 3: An increased, not decreased (or neutral) posterior tibial slope would lead to more stability in a PCL-deficient knee.
Answer 4: Increasing the posterior tibial slope increases the anterior translation of the tibia which would lead to DECREASED stability in an ACL-deficient knee.
Answer 5: Decreasing the posterior slope would cause less of an anterior-displacing force on the tibia with weight bearing, thereby INCREASING stability in an ACL-deficient knee.
