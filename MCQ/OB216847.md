## Question

A 79-year-old male presents to your office with shoulder pain that has been present for many years. He states he had a previous rotator cuff tear that had been treated with injections and physical therapy by another surgeon. However, his pain is no longer controlled and he is ready to move forward with surgery. Radiographs demonstrate arthropathy of the glenohumeral joint and biconcavity of the superior glenoid. Given the most likely surgery performed in this patient, what position should he be counseled to avoid post-operatively to prevent dislocation?

## Options

1. Flexion, abduction
2. Flexion, internal rotation, adduction
3. Flexion, internal rotation, abduction
4. External rotation, abduction
5. Extension, internal rotation, adduction

## Correct option

**5. Extension, internal rotation, adduction**

## Answer explanation

This patient is best suited for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) and should avoid positions where the arm is extended, adducted and internally rotated, such as pushing out of a chair, as this may lead to dislocation of the prosthesis.

RTSA is a type of shoulder arthroplasty that utilizes a center of rotation that is moved inferiorly and medially compared to the native shoulder, such that the mechanical advantage of the deltoid is increased and able to power the shoulder. RTSA is particularly useful in cases of rotator cuff tear arthropathy, where there is concomitant glenohumeral arthritis and insufficiency of the rotator cuff. Dislocation is an uncommon complication, as there is inherent stability of the RTSA implants. However, dislocation is reported to occur in around 2-4% of cases and patients are most at risk with the arm positioned in extension, internal rotation and adduction. Other risk factors for dislocation include an unrepairable subscapularis, revision surgery, and proximal humeral bone loss. Reduction can be attempted under sedation/anesthesia, but may ultimately require open reduction or revision.

*Gerber et al. review the indications and technical considerations with regards to RTSA.* **They note** that while RTSA has allowed for salvage of previous injuries that were difficult to treat, it also has a high rate of complication. Specifically, they discuss a 3.4% rate of instability, with dislocations occurring anteriorly, with the arm in extension and internal rotation.

*Cheung et al. specifically reviewed complications in RTSA, alluding to the higher rate of complication with RTSA compared to anatomic shoulder arthroplasty.* *They discuss common complications including neurologic injury, periprosthetic fracture, infection, scapular notching, dislocation, loosening and acromial fracture.* *They further discuss the presentation of each conditions and management strategies.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Active flexion and abduction after a rotator cuff repair may place the repair at risk, but would not be the position of highest risk after RTSA.
Answer 2: Flexion, internal rotation and adduction can place a posterior labral repair at risk.
Answer 3: Active flexion, internal rotation and abduction after a rotator cuff repair may place the repair at risk, but would not be the position of highest risk after RTSA.
Answer 4: External rotation and abduction can place an anterior labral repair at risk.
