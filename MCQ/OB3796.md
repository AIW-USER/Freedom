## Question

A 48-year-old woman presents to your clinic with 3 weeks of worsening leg heaviness and difficulty with balance. She denies any bladder or bowel issues. On examination, she has 3/5 strength in the bilateral iliopsoas and quadriceps. Sagittal and axial T2 MRI sequences are shown in Figures A and B, respectively. Cervical MRI is unremarkable. What is the next most appropriate step in management?

## Options

1. NSAIDs and physical therapy
2. Epidural steroid injection
3. Thoracic laminectomy
4. Thoracic laminectomy and instrumented fusion
5. Thoracic laminectomy with transpedicular discectomy and instrumented fusion

## Correct option

**5. Thoracic laminectomy with transpedicular discectomy and instrumented fusion**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with signs and symptoms consistent with thoracic myelopathy with imaging showing cord compression and signal change secondary to a thoracic disk herniation. The most appropriate treatment is discectomy and decompression using a transpedicular approach and instrumented fusion.

Thoracic disc herniations account for less than 1% of disc herniations and typically occur from T8-T12, with T11-12 being the most involved segment by far. Patients can present with a wide variety of symptoms, ranging from simple back pain to band-like radicular symptoms, and, in severe cases, myelopathy. Treatment is based on decompressing the involved segment to remove pressure on the neural elements. Given the kyphosis in the thoracic spine, laminectomy alone typically does not provide adequate decompression for an offending disc. Decompression requires access to the disc space through a transpedicular, costotransversectomy, lateral thoracotomy, or transthoracic approach.

*Bohlman and Zdeblick reported on 22 thoracic disc herniations treated with either a transthoracic or costotransversectomy approach.* **They found** excellent or good results in 16 patients and an improvement in motor deficit in 12 of 14 patients with preoperative weakness. However, they do caution that anterior spinal cord decompression is a technically demanding procedure with a risk of spinal cord injury.

*Nahhas et al. evaluated the non-neurological complication and outcome profile of 432 patients with thoracic disc herniation treated with an anterior (19.7%) versus posterior (80.3%) approach.* **They found** no significant difference in non-neurological complications, but the anterior approach patients did have a slightly longer length of stay.

Figures A and B are T2 sagittal and axial MRI images, respectively, showing a thoracic disc herniation at T6-7 with cord compression and spinal cord signal change. Illustrations A and B are postoperative images of the same patient after lateral thoracotomy for decompression and iliac crest allograft placement in the interbody space (which is difficult to visualize) and posterior instrumented fusion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1,2: The patient has thoracic myelopathy with progressive neurological deficits and imaging showing cord compression with signal change. Nonoperative management is not appropriate in this patient.
Answer 3,4: The kyphotic alignment of the thoracic spine and the presence of the spinal cord (rather than the roots, as in the lumbar spine) limit the ability of laminectomy alone to provide adequate decompression. The disc herniation must be accessed using one of the approaches mentioned above.
