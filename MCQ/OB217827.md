## Question

A 19-year-old male who plays wide receiver for his high school football team presents to your office with complaints of recurrent anterior shoulder dislocations. He has had 3 episodes of instability over the prior 2 football seasons. He has been doing therapy exercises with the athletic trainers at his school. On examination, he is quite apprehensive in a position of abduction and external rotation and has a grade II load and shift test. His Beighton score is 2. Radiographs demonstrate evidence of anterior glenoid bone loss and a concomitant Hill Sachs lesion. CT scan is obtained and characterizes his anterior glenoid bone loss at 30%. Given these findings, what is the best next step for this patient?

## Options

1. Continued physical therapy for at least 6 months
2. Arthroscopic bankart repair
3. Arthroscopic bankart repair + remplissage
4. Open bankart repair
5. Bony glenoid augmentation (i.e. Latarjet, distal tibia allograft, etc)

## Correct option

**5. Bony glenoid augmentation (i.e. Latarjet, distal tibia allograft, etc)**

## Answer explanation

Given this patient's significant degree of anterior glenoid bone loss (>25%), a bony augmentation procedure would be the best next step to prevent recurrent instability.

Anterior shoulder instability is much more common in younger males who are participating in contact sports. Recurrence rates are quite high in this population and it is thought that each time the humeral head dislocates more damage is done to the anterior glenoid and surrounding capsulolabral complex. In the same respect, the posterosuperior humeral head can also be damaged, termed a Hill Sachs lesion. Several options exist for management of these complex instability issues. Most surgeons consider arthroscopic labral (bankart) repair to be the first line surgery for patients with capsulolabral disruption and little bone loss. Open bankart repairs can also be performed to address these abnormalities but it is thought that bone loss >20-25% represents a critical degree of deficiency, by which repair of the soft tissues can no longer prevent recurrent instability. In cases with this kind of significant bone loss, bony augmentation with a Latarjet procedure or distal tibial allograft may be effective in re-establishing the bony anatomy of the glenohumeral joint and preventing anterior subluxation/dislocation of the humeral head.

*Boileau et al. reviewed risk factors for recurrent instability after arthroscopic bankart repair.* **They noted** that bony defects, either on the glenoid or humeral side increased the risk for recurrence. Other predictors of instability included shoulder hyperlaxity, and those for which 3 or fewer bony anchors were used.

*Beran et al. reviewed the management of chronic glenoid defects in cases of anterior shoulder instability.* *They discuss the different techniques for managing glenoid bone loss including coracoid transfer as well as allograft or autograft reconstruction.* However, **they note** that there was a lack of evidence to suggest superiority of one of these procedures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Given this patient's recurrent instability and the degree of bone loss present, continued physical therapy is not a good option.
Answer 2-4: While bankart repair (arthroscopic & open) can be utilized for fixing labral pathology, it is noted to have a higher rate of failure in cases of critical bone loss; in fact, there is newer data suggesting that even smaller degrees of bone loss may increase recurrence rates.
