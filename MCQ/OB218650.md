## Question

A 33-year-old female gives birth to her sixth child, which was complicated by macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, and a difficult vaginal delivery. At three months of age, the patient is examined by a pediatrician who notes a significant internal rotation contracture of the right upper extremity, associated triceps and biceps weakness, and an asymmetric pupillary reflex with a notable droop of the right eyelid when compared to the contralateral side. When counseling the mother, which of the following should the treating physician note is likely to develop at long-term follow-up?

## Options

1. Complete spontaneous recovery
2. Glenohumeral dysplasia
3. Radiocapitellar instability
4. Recovery of pre-ganglionic deficits only
5. Snapping scapula syndrome

## Correct option

**2. Glenohumeral dysplasia**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with an obstetric brachial plexopathy with pre-ganglionic symptoms of miosis and ptosis, which portends the worst prognosis for spontaneous recovery. With persistent deficits, the patient is likely to develop glenohumeral dysplasia with age.

Obstetric brachial plexopathy involves injury to the brachial plexus that occurs during birth, usually as a result of a stretching injury from a difficult vaginal delivery. The infant typically presents with a lack of active hand and arm motion, with the arm hanging limp at the side, adducted, and internally rotated. The affected shoulder typically subluxates posteriorly, with a notable decrease in shoulder external rotation. An upper trunk injury (C5-C6), colloquially known as Erb's palsy, is the most common presenting form of obstetric brachial plexopathy. Treatment starts with observation and daily passive range of motion exercises, with most patients recovering anti-gravity function of the biceps by 3 months. Lack of biceps functional recovery by 3 months and co-existing Horner's syndrome symptoms (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) indicating a pre-ganglionic lesion are poor prognostic indicators. In the setting of failed recovery, chronic internal rotation contracture leads to increased glenoid retroversion, humeral head flattening, and posterior humeral head subluxation, the constellation of which is known as glenohumeral dysplasia and will affect functional outcomes if future reinnervation procedures are planned.

Waters, et al. provide a review of the evaluation and management of obstetric brachial plexus injuries. The authors note that most infants who show signs of functional recovery within the first 2 months of life will go on to have normal function. However, those that fail to recover by 3 months have a considerable risk of long-term limited strength and range of motion. They conclude that those with total plexus lesions, lesions with loss of C5-C7, and those with Horner's syndrome have the worst prognosis for functional recovery.

Kirjavainen, et al. published long-term results of surgery for brachial plexus birth palsy in a nationwide, minimum 5-year-follow-up study out of Finland. Of 1706 obstetric brachial plexopathies registered from 1971-1997, 7.3% underwent surgery at a mean of 2.8 months. Incongruence of the glenohumeral joint was noted in over 16% of patients, and a significant number of post-operative patients continued to require help performing activities of daily living out to a mean of 13 years postoperatively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Complete spontaneous recovery is less likely at three months of age if appreciable biceps function has not returned, especially in the setting of persistent Horner's syndrome symptoms, as in this patient.
Answer 3: Though elbow contractures can occur due to relative triceps weakness, radiocapitellar instability is not a common association with obstetric brachial plexopathy.
Answer 4: Pre-ganglionic deficits are a poor prognosticator of functional recovery, and do not typically improve before post-ganglionic deficits.
Answer 5: Snapping scapula often occurs in the setting of scapulothoracic bursitis, and is not commonly associated specifically with obstetric brachial plexopathy.
