## Question

A 9-year-old girl presents to the emergency department after falling from her bike. She reports severe right knee pain and an inability to bear weight on the right lower extremity. Physical exam is remarkable for swelling of the right knee. Her radiographs are shown in Figure A. What type of tibial eminence fracture does she have, and what is the next best step in treatment?

![[q213369-a-typeii.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Type II; closed reduction and long leg casting in 90° of flexion
2. Type II; closed reduction and long leg casting in extension
3. Type III; closed reduction and long leg casting in 90° of flexion
4. Type III; closed reduction and long leg casting in extension
5. Type III; open or arthroscopic reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**2. Type II; closed reduction and long leg casting in extension**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a Type II tibial eminence fracture, which should be initially treated with closed reduction and long leg casting in extension.

Tibial eminence fractures are intra-articular fractures of the bony attachment of the ACL on the tibia that is most commonly seen in children from age 8 to 14 years. Initial treatment of tibial eminence fractures includes closed reduction and long leg casting in extension. Failed reductions may be due to interposition of the anterior horn of the medial meniscus and can be treated with open or arthroscopic reduction and internal fixation. In Type II tibial eminence fractures, suture fixation has been found to be biomechanically superior than screw fixation. Many patients have some objective anterior cruciate ligament laxity after a tibial spine avulsion fracture; however, with adequate treatment most patients do not have symptomatic laxity.

*Mah et al. published a follow-up study of arthroscopic reduction and fixation of Type III tibial eminence fractures.* **They found** that subjective knee function was excellent in all cases, and no clinical or objective evidence of knee laxity or instability was detected in any patient. Additionally, all patients demonstrated full range of motion of the affected knee compared with the contralateral side.

*McLennan et al. published their findings after second-look arthroscopy in Type III fractures of the tibial spine that were initially treated with closed reduction, arthroscopic reduction, or arthroscopic reduction with internal fixation.* **They found** that loss of reduction is common after closed treatment of tibial spine fractures and should be closely monitored. **They concluded** that Type III fractures that are unreducible or displaced require reduction, appropriate tensioning, internal fixation, and aggressive rehabilitation.

*Meyers et al. proposed a classification system for fractures of the intercondylar eminence of the tibia.* Type I injuries are non-displaced (< 3mm); Type II injuries are displaced but have an intact posterior hinge; Type III injuries are completely displaced; and Type IV injuries are completely displaced, rotated, and comminuted.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the right knee demonstrating a displaced tibial eminence fracture with an intact posterior hinge. Illustration A is a diagram depicting the Modified Meyers and McKeever classification system.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Type II fractures should be initially treated with closed reduction and long leg casting in extension, not in 90° of flexion.
Answers 3, 4, and 5: The patient has a Type II tibial eminence fracture.
