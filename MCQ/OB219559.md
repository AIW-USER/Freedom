## Question

A 62-year-old male presents with left hip and groin pain that was recalcitrant to conservative measures. He undergoes uncomplicated left total hip replacement and does well post-operatively. He returns to the clinic ten years later and would like to proceed with a right hip replacement. His updated radiographs are shown in Figure A. The patient notes he had a posterior spinal fusion procedure (L3-5) two years ago without any complications. He has a normal overall spine alignment but a stiff lumbar spine, as denoted from the lack of change in his sacral slope when compared between standing and seated positions (Figure B). Compared to his left total hip arthroplasty, how can the positioning of the final implants used for right total hip arthroplasty be adjusted to minimize complications associated with this patient’s stiff spine?

## Options

1. Increased cup anteversion to decrease potential impingement
2. Increase cup abduction to allow an improved hip range of motion
3. Place the femoral stem in more retroversion to decrease potential impingement
4. Decrease the femoral stem offset to allow an improved hip range of motion
5. Use of a constrained liner to prevent recurrent dislocations

## Correct option

**1. Increased cup anteversion to decrease potential impingement**

## Answer explanation

Patients with a history of spinal fusion often have abnormal spinopelvic motion associated with inferior outcomes, increased rates of posterior instability, and higher rates of revision after total hip arthroplasty (THA). These patients require some degree of increased cup anteversion (Answer 1), increased femoral offset, and/or dual mobility articulations to prevent instability and dislocation.

The spine's overall alignment and mobility determine the pelvis' orientation in various positions of everyday life. This spinopelvic relationship has significant implications for the appropriate placement of THA components. Patients with a history of spinal fusion or degenerative spondylosis demonstrate abnormally restricted spinopelvic motion that can result in necessary femoral hypermobility for normal ambulation. Collectively, this predisposes these patients to THA instability and dislocation. Therefore, these patients should undergo lateral seated and lateral standing spine-pelvis-hip radiographs to evaluate their (1) degree of spinal stiffness and (2) overall spinal deformity.

Spinal Stiffness: With normal spinopelvic motion, the pelvis tilts (i.e., rotates) posteriorly when a patient moves from standing to seated position (Illustration A). Thereby, the acetabulum tilts posteriorly (increasing anteversion, Illustration B) to accommodate the femur as it flexes forward. If a patient demonstrates decreased spinopelvic motion from a stiff spine (i.e., the pelvis fails to tilt posteriorly when sitting), the femoral neck can impinge on the anterior acetabular rim and cantilever the head towards a posterior dislocation. Therefore, in patients with spinal stiffness (determined by a < 10-degree change in sacral slope between standing and seated positions), surgeons may opt to place the acetabular cup with increased anteversion to avoid this impingement (Illustration C).

Overall Spinal Deformity: The patient’s overall spinal deformity also drives the natural position of the pelvis and acetabulum. With the loss of overall lumbar lordosis (i.e., “flatback deformity”), the pelvis is baseline posteriorly tilted relative to normal patients without any spinal deformity. A patient can be deemed to have a flatback deformity if their standing anterior pelvic plane (APP) is -10 degrees relative to neutral (Illustration D). If the radiographs include the entire lumbar spine, the same deformity may be inferred if their pelvic incidence-lumbar lordosis difference is >10 degrees. Patients with combined flatback deformity and stiff spines require hip extension hypermobility in standing positions, predisposing them to unique anterior hip dislocations.

Collectively, patients with spinal stiffness or spinal deformities can be managed with varied amounts of increased cup anteversion (Illustration E). Given the precise and technically demanding amount of added anteversion, intraoperative radiographs, computer navigation, or robotic-assisted surgery can be considered to ensure appropriate component placement according to the surgeon’s preoperative plan. Conversely, many surgeons will manage these patients using their standard operative techniques with the small change of using a dual mobility articulation for added stability.

Heckman and Lieberman provided a comprehensive review regarding spinopelvic biomechanics and total hip arthroplasty. They review the various terms used to describe the spine-hip relationship and the four categories of the Hip-Spine classification (Illustration E) with the targeted final acetabular position for each. The authors highlight that altering cup position, optimizing offset, removing all sources of impingement, and utilizing dual mobility articulations help decrease complications in this patient population.

*Heckman and colleagues performed a retrospective review of 20 patients with late dislocation (>1 year from index surgery).* *Of the total cohort (n=20), 9 (45%) had anterior dislocations and 11 (55%) had posterior dislocations.* **The authors found** that 18 (90%) patients had developed spinopelvic abnormalities after the index surgery (i.e., spinal fusion or developed stiffness) that contributed to the instability. 8 of those patients had concomitant soft tissue compromise (i.e., loss of capsular integrity or abductor insufficiency identified during surgery). **The authors conclude** that spinopelvic imbalance should be considered a fourth category for dislocation causality following THA, adding to the classic three, including patient positioning, component malposition, and soft-tissue compromise.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The left-sided cup position was appropriately placed at approximately 40-45 degrees of abduction. Increasing the abduction angle would result in a vertical cup and the associated consequences of eccentric polyethylene wear and posterior/superior instability.
Answer 3: Placing the femoral stem in more retroversion would increase the risk of impingement and posterior hip dislocation, particularly in the seated position. In general, retroverted stems should always be avoided.
Answer 4: Decreasing the femoral stem offset may increase the final hip range of motion. However, this would also decrease the abductors’ tension and thus reduce the overall implant stability. This would further predispose the patient to instability and dislocation.
Answer 5: Constrained liners are highly successful at preventing recurrent dislocations in patients with poor soft tissue constraints (i.e., multiple revision patients) or other non-modifiable causes (i.e., neuromuscular disorders). However, constrained liners have worse functional outcomes from a restricted range of motion and can be avoided in this patient by making appropriate adjustments to the primary implants’ positioning.
