## Question

An 38-year-old patient is brought to a trauma center after a diving accident. After trauma workup, his only injury is found to be an occipital condyle fracture. The decision is made to treat him definitively with halo immobilization. Which of the following is true of this treatment modality?

## Options

1. Better used for lower cervical injuries to create a longer working length
2. Lateral bending is the most controlled motion
3. Pins should be tightened to 8 in-lbs of force
4. Safe zone is just superior to the middle third of the orbit
5. Trochlear nerve (CN IV) is the most commonly injured cranial nerve following halo application

## Correct option

**3. Pins should be tightened to 8 in-lbs of force**

## Answer explanation

When placing a halo orthosis in a adult patients, 4 pins should be used and tightened to 8-in-lbs of force.

The halo fixator may be used for the definitive treatment of cervical spine trauma, preoperative reduction in the patient with spinal deformity, and adjunctive postoperative stabilization following cervical spine surgery. In adults, 4 pins are used, with the first two anterior pins being placed just superior to the lateral one third of the orbit. This placement is anterior and medial to the temporalis muscle and lateral to the supraorbital nerve. Two additional posterior pins should be placed 180 degrees from the anterior pins. As surgeons, it's of paramount importance to not only know the amount of torque that can safely be used to tighten these pins, but also how to read each manufacturers torque wrench. Typically a colored ring is applied to the torque wrench which has a number engraved on it, lets say "2" as an example. The wrench is then tightened until it reaches the reading, we'll use "6" as an example. The two numbers are added to determine the total torque applied to the pin (6 on the main read out, and 2 on the colored ring; 6+2=8 in lbs of total torque).

*Copley et al. review the accuracy and reliability of torque wrenches used for cervical traction.* **They report** that the measured torque value was within 10% of the target value in 69.2% of trials. **They conclude** that there were differences in the accuracy of the torque wrenches from different manufactures and that surgeons should be very familiar with the instruments they are using.

*Bono et al. review the halo fixator.* **They report** that halo fixation decreases cervical motion by 30% to 96%. There are several absolute contraindications that should be respected including cranial fracture, infection, and severe soft-tissue injury at the proposed pin sites. **They conclude** that, when used correctly, halo fixators are an excellent treatment to have in your armamentarium.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Halo fixators are better utilized for upper cervical injuries secondary to paradoxical spinal motion that shows midcervical kyphosis in recumbent lateral radiographs.
Answer 2: Lateral bending is the least controlled motion utilizing this treatment.
Answer 4: The safe zone is just superior to the lateral third of the orbit. A more medial placement would injury the supraorbital nerve.
Answer 5: The Abducens nerve (CN VI) is the most commonly injured CN with halo traction and presents with diplopia and eventual loss of lateral gaze.
