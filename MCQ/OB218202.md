## Question

A 20-year-old college volleyball player presents to your clinic with pain in her anterolateral bilateral legs that gets worse throughout her workouts and competitions. She states her legs feel "tight and fatigued." She also occasionally notices some numbness of the dorsum of her foot and describes difficulty with dorsiflexing the foot late in her workouts. You refer her to a colleague to have the compartments of her legs measured with a suspicion of exertional compartment syndrome. Which of the following sets of measurements would confirm this diagnosis?

## Options

1. Resting = 11 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 25 mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 15 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 12 mmHg
2. Resting = 15 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 27mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 12 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 10 mmHg
3. Resting = 8mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 29 mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 20 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 14 mmHg
4. Resting = 15 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 23 mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 19 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 11 mmHg
5. Resting = 17 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 32 mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 21 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 16 mmHg

## Correct option

**5. Resting = 17 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise = 32 mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise = 21 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise = 16 mmHg**

## Answer explanation

A diagnosis of exertional compartment syndrome can be made with the following criteria: resting pressure > 15 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise pressure > 30mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise pressure >20 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise > 15 mmHg (Answer 5).

Exertional compartment is often seen in athletes whose sports require activity or running of longer duration and is most common in younger athletes. The anterior leg compartment is most commonly affected but all compartments can be involved in particular cases. Symptoms typically include aching pain that begins after a predictable duration of exercise and is relieved with rest, paresthesias, and a drop foot in some cases. If concerned, compartment pressures should be measured, with the pressures above the aforementioned values being diagnostic. Nonoperative management is maximized with activity modification and anti-inflammatories but in true cases of exertional compartment syndrome, this strategy is rarely successful.

*Fraipont et al. published a review on chronic exertional compartment syndrome,* **noting** it is often overlooked as a cause of extremity pain in people who participate in repetitive physical activity. **They note** that compartment measurements are essential in formally diagnosing exertional compartment syndrome. In discussing treatment, **they note** that nonoperative management is often unsuccessful and surgical intervention entails fasciotomies of the involved compartments which usually produce satisfactory results.

*Campano et al. reviewed surgical management for chronic exertional compartment syndrome including 24 studies.* The most commonly involved compartment was the anterior compartment (51%), followed by lateral (33%), deep posterior (13%), and superficial posterior (3%). **They note** that 84% were satisfied with their surgical outcome after fasciotomy but there was a 13% complication rate mostly related to neurologic complication or infection.

*Burrus et al. looked at chronic leg pain in athletes, including medial tibial stress syndrome, chronic exertional compartment syndrome, stress fracture, popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, nerve entrapment, Achilles tightness, DVT and CRPS.* **They note** findings can be overlapping, making imaging and diagnostic testing important in establishing a diagnosis. Most of these problems can be treated nonsurgically, but some benefit from invasive intervention.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: Values diagnostic of exertional compartment syndrome are as follows: resting pressure > 15 mmHg, 1 minute post-exercise pressure > 30mmHg, 5 minute post-exercise pressure >20 mmHg, 15 minute post-exercise > 15 mmHg.
