## Options

1. Ankylosing spondylitis, immobilize in hard collar in existing kyphotic position, admit for addition imaging and observation
2. Ankylosing spondylitis, immobilize in hard collar in existing kyphotic position, discharge with followup with surgeon within 5 days
3. Ankylosing spondylitis, immobilize in hard collar in neutral kyphosis, discharge with followup with surgeon within 5 days
4. DISH, immobilize in hard collar in existing kyphotic position, admit for addition imaging and observation
5. DISH, immobilize in hard collar in neutral kyphosis, discharge with followup with surgeon within 5 days

## Correct option

**1. Ankylosing spondylitis, immobilize in hard collar in existing kyphotic position, admit for addition imaging and observation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has ankylosing spondylitis (AS) as evidenced by the chin-on-chest deformity, bilateral sacroiliitis, and lumbar radiographs with a classic bamboo spine. Due to the high risk of fracture and spinal instability, the patient should be immobilized in a hard collar in his existing kyphotic position and admitted for additional imaging and observation for progressive neurological deficits, despite his normal neurological exam.

Patients with AS who sustain spinal column trauma are at a high risk for neurologic injury, epidural hemorrhage and mortality. The kyphotic position is considered the native position of the ankylosed spine in the setting of trauma and this position should be maintained during a fracture work up. Iatrogenic injury can occur if the cervical spine is forced into extension in order to apply immobilization. Neurologic symptoms may be delayed as epidural hematoma can expand over time. Therefore these patients should be admitted for observation. Radiographic findings can be subtle and advanced imaging should be obtained in all patients with a spine-stiffening disease and trauma. Given the stiff nature of the spine, fractures typically occur from an extension moment and result in an unstable three-column injury that requires surgical stabilization.

*Caron et al. present a retrospective review of patients with ankylosed spines (due to AS or DISH) and characterized their fractures and outcomes of treatment.* Cervical fractures were most common (55%) and rates of occurrence decreased as they progressed down the spine, with lumbar fractures seen only 8% of the time. **They found** the most common successful surgical intervention was multilevel posterior instrumentation and fusion (with decompression when necessary for neurologic compromise).

*Thumbikat et al. reviewed spinal cord injuries in patients with AS over a ten-year period.* **They identified** 18 patients, 15 with traumatic injuries, 3 from surgery. Two of the patients were paralyzed during radiologic examinations by cervical extension. **They recommend** maintenance of a flexed position to avoid further extension injury. If the patient is awake they can assist in finding a comfortable position.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2. The patient should not be discharged until advanced imaging is obtained.
Answer 3. A soft collar would not provide adequate immobilization, and advanced imaging is necessary.
Answer 4 & 5. DISH is a common disorder of unknown etiology that is characterized by back pain and spinal stiffness. While it is a unique clinical condition from AS, it is similar in that in leads to a brittle spine with long lever arms that are prone to fracture, bleeding, and subsequent neurological deficits. Patients with DISH should be treated with the same precautions as patients with AS following low energy falls. The factors that allow one to diagnose DISH as opposed to AS include preservation of the disc space (AS involves the disc space), flowing osteophytes (as opposed to the marginal osteophytes seen in AS), absence of bilateral sacroiliitis and BLA-27 (AS is characterized by both of these).
