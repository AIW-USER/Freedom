## Question

A 22-year-old recreational basketball player sustains an inversion injury to his ankle and presents two weeks later with persistent ankle pain, swelling, and a sensation of catching despite immobilization in a walking boot. Ankle radiographs demonstrate an osteochondral lesion of the lateral talar dome with a visible free fragment in the tibiotalar joint. Which radiographic finding or lesion characteristic most strongly favors early operative management over an initial trial of continued immobilization and restricted weightbearing?

## Options

1. A lateral talar dome lesion associated with an acute ankle sprain mechanism
2. A lesion measuring less than 1 cm in diameter on plain radiographs
3. A symptomatic lesion identified within 2 weeks of injury
4. A displaced osteochondral fragment that is no longer congruent with the talar dome
5. A lesion with surrounding subchondral sclerosis but preserved joint alignment

## Correct option

**4. A displaced osteochondral fragment that is no longer congruent with the talar dome**

## Answer explanation

Although many osteochondral lesions of the talus (OCLT) are initially treated conservatively, early surgical management is favored in acute cases with fragment displacement, as nonoperative treatment is unlikely to succeed in these instances (answer choice 4).

Osteochondral lesions of the talus can be broadly divided into atraumatic lesions (i.e., osteochondritis dissecans) and traumatic lesions. Traumatic lesions occur commonly after inversion injuries and may present with persistent pain, swelling, and mechanical symptoms when lesion instability is present. Radiographs may identify displaced fragments, while advanced imaging can further characterize fragment size and stability, helping guide treatment decisions. Stable, nondisplaced lesions may be treated initially with immobilization and activity modification, whereas unstable or displaced fragments are more likely to require surgical management. Lesion acuity and patient age are additional considerations in dictating the appropriate initial management strategy.

*Bruns et al. reviewed osteochondral lesions of the talus, detailing the pathoanatomy, diagnostics, and management strategies for osteochondral injuries as well as osteochondritis dissecans lesions.* **They reported** that lesion stability and fragment displacement are central factors in treatment selection, with stable lesions often treated with a myriad of nonoperative modalities and unstable lesions often requiring surgery. **They concluded** that no operative technique has demonstrated consistent superiority, and studies evaluating these techniques are typically of lower levels of evidence with small sample sizes and short follow-ups, highlighting the need for higher-quality evidence in this space.

*Dunlap et al. reviewed a retrospective case series of acute lateral inverted osteochondral fractures of the talus ("LIFT" lesion) following inversion injuries in a group of 10 patients with an average age of 17.2 years old.* **They reported** their treatment strategy of ankle arthroscopy followed by open reattachment versus fragment excision in eight and two of these patients, respectively. **They concluded** that at an average follow-up of approximately 9 years, most patients had positive functional outcome scores, with minimal range-of-motion deficits; however, all had some osteophyte formation on radiographs.

Illustration A is a coronal CT scan (Dunlap et al.) that demonstrates the appearance of the classic "LIFT" lesion. Note the inverted nature of the joint surface of the fragment, which is facing the talus rather than the tibia.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Although lateral talar dome lesions are more commonly traumatic in etiology compared to medial lesions, laterality alone does not mandate surgery.
Answer choice 2: Lesion size can influence prognosis and surgical technique, but small lesions may still be managed nonoperatively if they are stable and nondisplaced.
Answer choice 3: Early presentation after injury does not by itself indicate the need for surgery, as many acute, stable lesions are initially treated conservatively.
Answer choice 5: Subchondral sclerosis suggests a more chronic or contained process and does not necessarily indicate instability requiring urgent operative intervention.
