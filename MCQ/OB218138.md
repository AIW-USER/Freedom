## Question

You are seeing a referral from a local primary care physician in your office with suspicion for a meniscal tear. Based on your history and physical examination, you are concerned the patient may have a meniscal root tear of their medial meniscus. Which of the following is accurate regarding the work-up and diagnosis of a meniscal root tear?

## Options

1. Anterior meniscal root tears are seen more commonly than posterior root tears
2. "Ghost sign" may be seen on MRI
3. Occur most commonly in males <50 years old
4. Never associated with "pop," or traumatic event
5. Physical examination is the gold-standard in diagnosis

## Correct option

**2. "Ghost sign" may be seen on MRI**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a meniscal root tear, which can be identified on MRI by noting a "ghost sign" on the sagittal sequences of the notch (Illustration A).

Meniscal root tears are becoming increasingly recognized, especially in middle-aged and elderly females. Many affected patients complain of hearing a pop in their knee which is not associated with any specific fall or traumatic event. On physical examination, it can be difficult to differentiate meniscal root and horn tears, as both tend to have joint line tenderness. However, many patients with meniscal root tears don't describe the same degree of catching and locking sensations as those with large flap tears of their meniscus because root tears tend to involve extrusion of the affected meniscus. If suspicion is high for meniscal pathology in this population, an MRI is worthwhile and increases the diagnostic capabilities, as the physical exam lacks specificity. The "ghost sign" may be seen on sagittal images of the notch and represents the absence of identifiable meniscus at the root attachment.

*Johannsen et al. reviewed the qualitative and quantitative aspects of the posterior root attachments of the medial and lateral menisci in 12 cadavers.* *They reviewed the structures attaching adjacent to the roots, including medially where the medial meniscus posterior root attachment was 9.6mm posterior and 0.7mm lateral to the medial tibial eminence.* The lateral root attachment was 1.5mm posterior and 4.2 mm medial to the lateral tibial eminence. The data in this study may help surgeons place their tunnels in the appropriate position to repair the native anatomy when performing arthroscopic root repairs.

*Bhatia et al. reviewed meniscal root tears,* **noting** that they remain less common than tears of the meniscal body and are difficult to diagnose. Further, they discuss the biomechanics of root tears, **noting** that meniscal extrusion diminishes the ability of the meniscus to provide a shock-absorbing function for the knee. They go on to discuss the importance of early diagnosis, the helpfulness of MRI, as well as new techniques to obtain anatomic repairs.

Illustration A demonstrates a T2 MRI sequence of the knee showing a meniscal root tear with the absence of the meniscal root attachment, known as the "ghost sign."

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Posterior root tears are more common, specifically medially, as this attachment is the least mobile.
Answer 3: Meniscal root tears often occur in middle-aged and elderly females.
Answer 4: Most patients complain of hearing a "pop," usually with episodes of deep flexion like rising from a seated position or using stairs.
Answer 5: Physical examination alone lacks specificity in the diagnosis of meniscal root tears.
