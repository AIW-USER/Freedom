## Question

A 62-year-old male presents with a right anterior shoulder dislocation after slipping and falling down 2 stairs. He was successfully treated with a closed reduction and instructed to follow-up as an outpatient. Four weeks later he complains of persistent shoulder weakness and pain. What would be the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Subacromial steroid injection
2. MRI cervical spine
3. MRI right shoulder
4. EMG
5. Physical therapy

## Correct option

**3. MRI right shoulder**

## Answer explanation

The patient likely has a rotator cuff tear following a traumatic anterior shoulder dislocation, which is common in older patients following glenohumeral dislocations.

Persistent shoulder pain and weakness following a traumatic anterior shoulder dislocation may can be the result of concomitant injuries to the shoulder. In elderly patients, this is commonly from a rotator cuff tear, in which the incidence is directly correlated with age (30% at 30 years compared to 80% at 60 years). This should be evaluated with an MRI of the shoulder to evaluate the integrity of the rotator cuff tendon. Other causes include axillary or brachial plexus nerve palsy, which occurs at a lesser rate (5% of patients).

*Robinson et al. performed a prospective database study of 3633 patients with traumatic anterior shoulder dislocations.* **They reported** 492 patients (13.5%) sustained a neurologic injury, 1215 patients (33.4%) developed a rotator cuff tear and that cuff tears were found more commonly in female patients >60 years of age after low-energy fall mechanisms. **The authors concluded** that shoulder dislocations with combined rotator cuff tears and neurologic deficits are common in patients >60 years of age and require careful evaluation.

*Atef et al. performed a prospective study of 240 patients that presented with traumatic anterior shoulder dislocations.* **The authors found** 144 patients (60%) to have associated shoulder injuries with rotator cuff tears being the most common in 67 patients (28%), followed by axillary nerve injury in 38 patients (11.6%). **The authors concluded** that associated lesions are common in traumatic shoulder dislocations, which necessitates close evaluation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient likely has a massive rotator cuff tear as a result of the shoulder dislocation, which would not be addressed with a subacromial steroid injection.
Answer 2: Cervical radiculopathy can be a cause of shoulder weakness and pain, but is unlikely to be the cause of shoulder pain and weakness acutely after a traumatic shoulder dislocation in a patient >60 years of age.
Answer 4: Neurologic injuries, such as an axillary nerve palsy, are possible in patients with a traumatic shoulder dislocation. However, rotator cuff tears occur at a higher rate, and these are best evaluated with an MRI of the shoulder.
Answer 5: Physical therapy would be an appropriate course if there is no evidence of an associated injury and the weakness is associated with deconditioning. Given the patient's age and mechanism, an MRI should be performed to evaluate for a massive rotator cuff tear.
