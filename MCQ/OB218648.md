## Question

A 19-month-old female is seen in the ER for 2 days of irritability and left hip pain. The patient's parents deny any antecedent trauma and note that the patient is fully vaccinated. She refuses to bear weight on her left leg. The patient's temperature is 38.0°C (100.4°F). On physical examination, the hip is held in resting flexion and external rotation. Any movement of the hip elicits pain. Laboratory studies show a white blood cell count of 10,200/mm3, ESR of 21 mm/hr, and CRP of 14 mg/L. Ultrasound shows a small effusion compared to the contralateral hip, and synovial fluid analysis shows white blood cells of 76,000/mm3 with 90% polymorphonuclear leukocytes. Immediate operative irrigation and debridement is performed. Cultures from both the initial aspiration and intraoperative tissue cultures remain negative at 14 days. Which of the following describes the most likely organism?

## Options

1. Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic coccobacillus
2. Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic coccus in chains
3. Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic coccus in clusters
4. Gram-negative, aerobic intracellular diplococcus
5. Gram-negative, aerobic-facultatively anaerobic bacillus

## Correct option

**1. Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic coccobacillus**

## Answer explanation

Given the patient’s age and the moderately elevated laboratory findings combined with a concerning physical exam, this patient has most likely developed septic arthritis from Kingella kingae, which is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic coccobacillus (Answer 1).

While Staphylococcus aureus remains the most common organism responsible for pediatric septic arthritis, these infections typically present with a more severe clinical course and significant elevations in inflammatory markers. Kingella kingae most commonly affects children under 4 years, oftentimes presents with less severe elevations in CRP and ESR, and is now recognized as a major causative organism in pediatric joint infections. It carries a less pronounced clinical picture compared with other causative organisms, and as a result of its indolent nature and comparatively milder signs and symptoms, Kingella-associated septic arthritis lends itself to underdiagnosis. Therefore, Kingella must always be on the differential in the setting of suspected pediatric septic arthritis. Kingella-specific genetic targets such as the rtxA toxin gene have allowed for increased detection rates via nucleic amplification assays (PCR/RT-PCR), demonstrating that Kingella is a much more prevalent etiological agent than was previously suspected. Urgent surgical debridement is critical to timely source control and preservation of the articular cartilage.

*Hagedoorn et al. published a 2023 study describing the performance of various clinical prediction tools such as the Kocher criteria in preschool children with septic hip arthritis due to different pathogens, hypothesizing that the number of Kocher criteria or modified Kocher criteria would be lower in children with septic arthritis caused by Kingella versus Staphylococcus.* *They performed a secondary analysis of the Pre-school Osteoarticular Infection (POI) study (a retrospective multicenter study) including children between 3 months to 5 years of age admitted to 11 different hospitals across Australia and New Zealand in 2012-2016.* **The authors found** that while the number of Kocher and modified Kocher criteria did not differ significantly between the Kingella and Staphylococcus groups, the Kingella group more often had lower temperatures (4/20 patients with temperature >38.5ºC, p=0.02) and CRP levels (mean 34 mg/L, p<0.01) compared to the Staphylococcus group (16/29 with temperature >38.5ºC; mean CRP level 42 mg/L).

*Erkilinc et al. published a 2021 JAAOS review on pediatric septic arthritis, detailing the epidemiology, history, diagnostic tools, treatment, and complications.* *In their review, several studies identified Kingella kingae as a major etiological agent in pediatric septic arthritis, with one study suggesting that Kingella is the most common organism in children under 5 years of age.* To effectively detect Kingella, **the authors state** that synovial fluid should be cultured in aerobic blood culture bottles in addition to agar plates, and PCR/RT-PCR can increase the detection of bacteria not isolated by culture. Kingella is one of the common etiologic agents in young patients and is difficult to culture, and in culture-negative cases, Kingella is the most frequently identified bacteria via PCR.

*Khattak et al. performed a 2021 prospective case series determining the extent to which patient demographics, clinical presentation, and blood parameters vary in Kingella kingae septic arthritis when compared with those of other organisms, and what clinical differences must be considered when evaluating pediatric patients suspected of having septic arthritis.* *They reviewed all cases of suspected septic arthritis in children aged between 0 and 16 years of age involving any joint requiring aspiration or surgical drainage with microbiological culture or PCR analysis of synovial fluid over a 6-year span at a single institution.* *Of the 99 patients in whom an organism was identified, K. kingae was the most common and made up 42% of cases.* Kingella was found most commonly in the knee, then the shoulder, whereas the hip was the most commonly affected location in septic arthritis caused by other organisms. All patients with Kingella septic arthritis were under 4 years of age, and those affected by Kingella were significantly younger (mean 16.1 months) compared to those with septic arthritis caused by other organisms (mean 50.4 months). Additionally, the CRP levels in the Kingella group were significantly lower (mean 37.5 mmol/L) compared to the “other organisms” group (mean 83.7 mmol/L).

*Villani et al. published a 10-year retrospective cohort study that evaluated primary septic arthritis in children aged 6 to 48 months in order to differentiate those with infection caused by Kingella kingae from those with other confirmed pathogens and those with no identified pathogen.* Those who met inclusion criteria were divided into 3 cohorts: (1) confirmed K. kingae;(2)confirmed other pathogens; and (3) presumed [no pathogen identified by blood culture, joint fluid culture, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR)]. **The authors found** that Kingella was the most predominant organism in younger children and that children in the Kingella cohort had lower initial CRP levels, peak CRP, absolute neutrophil count, band percentage, febrile days on antibiotics, bacteremia, and joint fluid positivity. Additionally, children in the Kingella group had a significantly shorter length of hospital stay than children from the other cohorts.
