## Question

An 85-year-old man presents with newly-recognized neck pain after an uneventful fall 4 weeks ago in the dementia unit at the nursing home where he resides. He is on apixaban for atrial fibrillation and Dilantin for seizures. Additionally, he has a history of pulmonary lobectomy for small cell lung carcinoma, pulmonary hypertension, and aortic stenosis. There are no neurologic deficits noted on examination but he does have clear discomfort with neck motion. Current imaging is shown in Figures A-C. What is the best next step in treatment?

## Options

1. Observation
2. Halo vest immobilization
3. Anterior osteosynthesis
4. Posterior cervical arthrodesis
5. Cervical collar

## Correct option

**5. Cervical collar**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained a displaced, unstable Type II odontoid fracture which is unstable and requires immobilization. Given his significant medical co-morbidities, a cervical collar would be the best option.

Possible treatment of Anderson-D'Alonzo Type II fractures includes cervical collars, halo vest immobilization, and surgical fixation. Surgical options generally consist of either anterior screw fixation or various posterior-based fixation constructs. The latter includes more historical less-rigid instrumentation (Brooks and Gallie cables, sublaminar wiring) as well as more modern rigid instrumentation (C1-C2 transarticular screws, or segmental fixation with C1 lateral mass screws and C2 screws either in the pars, pedicle, or lamina). The less-rigid fixation techniques were often combined with supplemental post-operative halo vest immobilization. Surgical outcomes are generally good but the risk of complications increases with patient comorbidities, which may preclude patients from being surgical candidates. The risk of non-union is much greater in elderly patients, fractures displaced >6mm, those with >10 degrees angulation, and in the setting of delayed presentation and comminution. Nonunion is treated expectantly if the patient is asymptomatic.

*Tashjian et al. reviewed the outcomes following the treatment of odontoid fractures in 78 elderly patients (>65 years).* **The authors found** that of the patients managed with halo vest (HV) immobilization, 42% died within the post-operative period, compared to 20% in the non-HV rigid cervical collar group. Major complications occurred in 66% of HV and 36% of non-HV patients. **They concluded** that outcomes after HV are inferior to hard cervical collars and surgery, and cautioned strongly against HV immobilization in the elderly.

*Patel et al. reviewed the treatment of unstable Type II odontoid fractures.* **The authors note** that anterior screw fixation is ideally used when the fracture line lies perpendicular to the compression vector of the screw (anterior oblique fractures), there is minimal comminution, adequate bone stock, and favorable body habitus. There was no difference in outcomes when 1 versus 2 screws was used, though the risk of complications was greater with 2 screws.

Figures A through C are the lateral and closed-mouth radiographs, and sagittal CT scans showing an unstable Type II odontoid fracture. There is a spontaneous C2-3 fusion of lateral masses bilaterally. Illustration A is the lateral radiograph with the orange arrow denoting the fracture and Illustration B is the sagittal CT scan with the orange arrow denoting the odontoid fraction (with the red arrow showing the lateral mass fusion).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This is an unstable fracture configuration and warrants some degree of immobilization.
Answer 2: Halo vest immobilization increases the risk of cardiopulmonary complications and increases morbidity and mortality, especially in elderly patients with preexisting pulmonary compromise. As there are minimal symptoms, this is not indicated.
Answers 3 and 4: Given the co-morbidities, this patient is not a surgical candidate
