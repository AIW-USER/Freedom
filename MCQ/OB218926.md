## Options

1. A stable fusion construct protective against proximal junctional kyphosis
2. A fusion construct that is protective against distal junctional kyphosis but exhibits excessive stiffness
3. An appropriately long fusion construct instrumented to the lowest instrumented vertebral level (LIV)
4. An inappropriately long fusion construct that increases the risk of distal junctional kyphosis
5. An unstable fusion construct with increased risk of distal junctional kyphosis

## Correct option

**2. A fusion construct that is protective against distal junctional kyphosis but exhibits excessive stiffness**

## Answer explanation

Extending the fusion construct beyond the lowest instrumented vertebrae (L3) shown in Figure B would decrease the risk of distal junctional kyphosis, but would result in a fusion construct that is excessively stiff. This unnecessarily places the young patient at risk for early disc degeneration, arthrosis, and pain.

Scheuermann's Kyphosis (SK) is the most common form of structural kyphosis in adolescents. It typically presents between the ages of 10 and 12, is more common in the thoracic than the thoracolumbar spine, and has recently been associated with an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. The exact pathophysiology is unknown but is thought to originate from a developmental error in collagen aggregation, resulting in abnormal vertebral endplate formation and anterior vertebral wedging > 5 degrees across greater than three subsequent vertebrae, ultimately causing a rigid thoracic kyphotic deformity. Deformities < 70 degrees are typically managed successfully with non-operative intervention. When the kyphosis reaches greater than 70-75 degrees (or > 25-30 degrees for the less common thoracolumbar variant), surgical intervention is typically indicated, though cutoffs for these values vary within the literature. Regardless, when intervening surgically, it is essential to identify the most appropriate lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) in order to know where to end the construct. Traditionally this was done using the first lordotic disk (FLD) method; however, given the compensatory hyperlordosis often seen in SK, the sagittal stable vertebra (SSV) method was created. This is done by drawing the posterior sacral vertical line (Figure B) and identifying the most proximal vertebral body that maintains at least 50% of its width anterior to the line drawn. This is the LIV and indicates where the fusion construct should be stopped to protect against distal junctional kyphosis (DJK), which can occur if a more proximal level is inappropriately chosen as the LIV. Junctional kyphosis (JK) is defined as a Cobb angle measurement greater than 10 degrees between the fused and unfused segments. Proximally, this is known as proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK), and though it is less common than DJK, the risk of occurrence significantly increases with overcorrection of kyphosis, due to greater negative sagittal balance.

Tribus, et al. provide a review of Scheuermann's Kyphosis, stating that posterior correction of the kyphotic deformity can be achieved using one of two instrumentation techniques: the four-rod compression technique or the two-rod leverage technique. Regardless of which technique is used, the authors assert that great care should be taken in choosing fusion levels, being sure to fuse down to the FLD to prevent DJK and not to overcorrect the deformity more than 50% of the initial deformity in order to avoid exacerbating negative sagittal imbalance and predisposing to PJK.

Sardar, et al. also provide a comprehensive review of treatment options for Scheuermann's Kyphosis and again highlight the challenging problem of junctional kyphosis. The authors highlight the discrepancy in the literature between using the FLD and the SSV to decide the LIV, with proponents of the SSV noting a markedly reduced incidence of distal junctional failure while only incorporating typically one additional motion segment into the fusion construct when compared to the use of the FLD.

Figure A demonstrates the pathologic kyphosis of a patient with Scheuermann's Kyphosis, which on physical exam is noted to have a sharper angulation with forward bending. Figure B demonstrates the use of the posterior sacral vertical line to identify the sagittal stable vertebra (SSV).

Incorrect Answers:
1. Though fusing to L5 would provide an excessively stiff and likely stable fusion, it would be more protective against DJK than PJK. Unnecessarily lengthening the construct may portend deformity overcorrection, which is a known risk factor for PJK.
3. The most appropriate LIV for this patient based off of the SSV would be L3, not L5. Fusing to L5 would be considered an excessively long fusion construct.
4. Though this would be considered an inappropriately long fusion construct, it would be protective against DJK, not the reverse as this answer choice indicates.
5. This would be considered a stable, not unstable, fusion construct and would not increase but would rather decrease the risk of DJK, though it would be excessively stiff.
