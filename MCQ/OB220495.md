## Question

A 50-year-old male presents to your office with a chief complaint of chronic, non-radiating right hip pain. He further describes the pain as located in the buttock region, occasionally wrapping around the lateral hip and groin. He notes pain with activity and only finds relief while lying supine. On exam, he notes tenderness over the right sacroiliac (SI) joint. Which physical exam finding would you expect if the etiology of this pain was from SI joint pathology?

## Options

1. Pain with hip flexion, adduction, and internal rotation
2. Pain with straight leg raise from 0-50 degrees
3. Pain while extending the right hip off exam table and flexing the left hip and knee
4. Pain with flexion of neck and hip with extension of leg
5. Pain reproduced with coughing

## Correct option

**3. Pain while extending the right hip off exam table and flexing the left hip and knee**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with right sacroiliac (SI) joint pain. On physical exam, this patient would likely present with right SI joint pain while extending the right hip off the exam table and flexing the left hip and knee (answer choice 3).

SI joint dysfunction is a degenerative condition of the SI joint resulting in lower back pain. This is a commonly overlooked condition that may explain up to 15-30% of cases of low back pain in the outpatient setting. An idiopathic mechanism is the most common and likely due to repetitive trauma to the SI joint. Diagnosis is made clinically with pain just inferior to the posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) and made worse with hip flexion, abduction, and external rotation (FABER). Other physical exam findings found in this condition include pain with extending the affected hip off the table while flexing the unaffected hip and knee (Gaenslen's test), SI joint compression test, SI joint distraction test, and anterior sacral thrust. When more than 3 of these diagnostic tests are positive, it is highly suggestive of SI joint pathology. For further diagnostic accuracy, a diagnostic SI joint injection performed under fluoroscopy can be performed. Treatment is usually conservative with pain management, physical therapy, and corticosteroid injections. Operative management is employed if patients experience progressive symptoms and fail conservative management.

*Morgan wrote an editorial commentary regarding the similarities between hip and SI joint pathologies.* **They note** that imaging of the SI joint can be unreliable, and degenerative changes can be found in a majority of asymptomatic patients. The diagnostic accuracy is greatly increased if 3 or more diagnostic tests are positive on the exam and relief is noted from an SI joint injection. **They conclude** that radiologic changes should be interpreted with caution by both hip and spine surgeons.

*Visser et al. reviewed the clinical features of 186 patients, either with sciatica-like symptoms from SI joint pathology or sciatica symptoms from spinal nerve compression.* **They found** that patients with SI joint-related pain 77/186 (41%) were significantly more often female, had shorter stature, shorter duration of symptoms, radiating groin pain, and a history of falls onto their buttocks. **They conclude** that SI joint-related symptoms mimic spinal nerve compression symptoms, and they recommend performing a thorough clinical exam and utilizing imaging modalities to exclude other diagnoses.

*Schmidt et al. wrote a review on the management of SI joint pain.* **They note** it can be difficult to diagnose definitively through physical exam and conventional radiology. A fluoroscopic-guided injection into the SI joint can be both diagnostic and therapeutic. Initial treatment for SI joint pain includes conservative management, including activity modification, NSAIDs, and physical therapy. Surgical treatment is indicated for those who fail conservative management and note a >75% relief after an SI joint injection. Percutaneous SI fusion can be used as a first-line surgical treatment.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Pain with hip flexion, adduction, and internal rotation (FADIR) is present with hip joint pathology, as seen in osteoarthritis.
Answer 2: Pain with a straight leg raise from 0-50 degrees is usually not present. If the leg is elevated more than 60 degrees, pain can occur due to increased motion in the SI joint at this level.
Answers 4-5: Both of these physical exam findings are present with disc herniations, not SI joint pathology.
