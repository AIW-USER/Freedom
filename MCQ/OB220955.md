## Question

A 67-year-old male presents to your clinic to establish care for a mass in his right thigh. The mass has been present for most of his adult life and has not changed in size. He was being followed by an oncologist who the patient recalls said that the mass was “not cancer”. You review his old medical records and note that he did get a biopsy, which revealed a low-grade, well-differentiated fatty neoplasm that amplified for MDM2. Figure A shows the histology slide from the biopsy. The patient asks what is his risk of the mass “turning into cancer”. Which of the following most accurately describes this patient’s risk for his mass dedifferentiating into a malignant tumor?

## Options

1. 0%, this lesion does not undergo malignant transformation
2. Roughly 3-7%
3. Between 20-30%
4. Over 50% undergo malignant transformation
5. Unable to determine; the literature is inconclusive

## Correct option

**2. Roughly 3-7%**

## Answer explanation

This patient likely has an atypical lipomatous tumor of his right lower extremity. These tumors essentially do not metastasize, but they can dedifferentiate in about 3-7% of cases into a malignant tumor (Answer 2).

Lipomatous tumors are common soft tissue sarcomas frequently seen in patients 50-80 years old. The diagnosis is confirmed with biopsy, which will show immature lipoblasts with varying degrees of cellular atypia depending on the subtype. The presence of lipoblasts distinguishes this tumor from a simple lipoma. The terminology to describe low-grade, well-differentiated tumors is controversial; generally, they are referred to as well-differentiated liposarcomas (WDLs) when they occur centrally, or referred to as atypical lipomatous tumors (ALTs) when located in the extremities. ALTs essentially do not metastasize (incidence of <1%), but they can dedifferentiate into higher-grade tumors over time. The risk of an ALT dedifferentiating is roughly 3-7%, though the risk may be slightly higher in WDLs. Other notable liposarcomas include intermediate-grade subtypes (i.e., Myxoid) and high-grade subtypes (round cell, pleomorphic, dedifferentiated, etc.). Dedifferentiated liposarcoma and other high-grade subtypes have a much higher risk of metastasis (>50%) and a five-year survival of 50-80%.

*Fourman et al. conducted a systematic review comparing the prognosis of patients with ALTs (20 studies) versus patients with WDLs (13 studies), as these two terms have been used interchangeably in the past.* *They compared local recurrence and metastatic rates.* **They found** that metastatic rates were 0% vs 2.7% for ALTs and WDLs, respectively. The local recurrence rates were also significantly lower in ALTs than in WDLs (15.1% vs 46% with marginal excision and 3.3% vs 17.4% with wide excision). **The authors concluded** that the term ALT should be reserved specifically for low-grade extremity lipomatous tumors that occur in the extremities, and highlighted that ALTs and WDLs (centrally located tumors) differ slightly in terms of prognosis despite both being low-grade.

*Mavrogenis et al.* **concluded** a retrospective review of 67 patients with ALTs treated with either primary surgical resection or re-resection after recurrence. The mean follow-up time was 81 months. The rate of local recurrence after primary resection was 10.6%. In patients who had a recurrence, 4% had evidence of dedifferentiation on presentation. **The authors concluded** that ALTs have a relatively high rate of local recurrence, but a relatively low rate of dedifferentiation upon recurrence after surgical resection.

Figure A shows the histology classic for ALTs/WDLs. Note the immature lipoblasts surrounded by mature adipocytes. There are no significant pleomorphic, myxoid, or dedifferentiated components.
