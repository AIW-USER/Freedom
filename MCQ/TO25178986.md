## Question

Carpal Instability Complex (CIC) is best represented by which of the following clinical scenarios?

## Options

1. Perilunate dislocations and fracture-dislocations
2. Pure Lunotriquetral dissociation
3. Dorsal tilt of the distal radius following a Colles fracture
4. Radiocarpal translocation in Rheumatoid Arthritis

## Correct option

**1. Perilunate dislocations and fracture-dislocations**

## Answer explanation

CIC includes injuries that have features of both CID (interosseous tears) and CIND (capsular/radiocarpal disruptions), such as PLD.

Carpal Instability Complex (CIC)

Definition

CIC refers to instability patterns that involve a combination of dissociative and non-dissociative components — both intrinsic (within-row) and extrinsic (between-row) ligamentous disruptions coexist. This represents the most severe end of the carpal instability spectrum, typically resulting from high-energy trauma that disrupts multiple ligamentous structures simultaneously.

Pathophysiology

CIC occurs when the injury force is sufficient to cause a cascade of ligamentous disruptions across multiple anatomical planes — intrinsic interosseous ligaments, extrinsic capsular ligaments, and often associated carpal fractures are involved simultaneously. The classic example is the perilunate injury spectrum — a progressive perilunar dislocation sequence described by Mayfield's stages of progressive perilunar instability (1980):

Stage I: Scapholunate ligament rupture (or scaphoid waist fracture — the trans-scaphoid variant). The force, propagating from radial to ulnar, first disrupts the SL linkage.

Stage II: The force propagates through the space of Poirier (the midcarpal joint), disrupting the midcarpal (capitolunate) articulation. The capitate displaces dorsally relative to the lunate.

Stage III: The lunotriquetral ligament ruptures (or the triquetrum fractures), completing the perilunar disruption. The entire carpus (distal row plus scaphoid and triquetrum) displaces dorsally, while the lunate remains in its fossa on the radius.

Stage IV: The lunate itself is extruded volarly from its fossa by the dorsally displaced capitate — this is the complete lunate dislocation, where the lunate lies volarly in the carpal tunnel, potentially compressing the median nerve, while the rest of the carpus collapses dorsally into the space vacated by the lunate.

This spectrum exemplifies CIC because it involves simultaneous disruption of intrinsic ligaments (SL and LT — dissociative components), extrinsic ligaments (midcarpal capsular structures — non-dissociative components), and potentially carpal fractures (scaphoid, capitate, triquetrum — trans-osseous variants).

Trans-scaphoid perilunate dislocation — the most common variant — involves a scaphoid waist fracture (instead of SL ligament rupture) combined with dorsal perilunate dislocation. The combination of a fracture through the proximal row (dissociative) and dislocation between rows (non-dissociative) is the hallmark of CIC.

Other CIC patterns include axial carpal dislocations (longitudinal disruption of the carpus along the intermetacarpal or intercarpal axis — rare, high-energy injuries often associated with crush mechanisms) and complex multi-ligament injuries with combined radiocarpal and midcarpal instability.

Clinical Features

Patients present with a grossly swollen, painful wrist following high-energy trauma (fall from height, motorcycle accident, dashboard injury). Examination reveals a markedly swollen, deformed wrist with severely restricted motion. Median nerve function must be assessed urgently — acute carpal tunnel syndrome occurs in up to 25–45% of perilunate dislocations due to compression by the displaced lunate. The diagnosis is missed in up to 25% of cases on initial emergency department evaluation — a high index of suspicion and careful radiographic assessment are essential.

Radiographic Findings

PA view: Disruption of Gilula's arcs — the three smooth, parallel arcs traced along the proximal carpal row articular margins (Arc I — proximal surfaces; Arc II — distal surfaces; Arc III — proximal hamate and capitate). Any break in these smooth arcs indicates a carpal mislocation or fracture. In perilunate dislocation, the arcs are disrupted at multiple points. The "crowded carpal sign" — the carpals appear overlapped and crowded due to dorsal displacement of the carpus. A triangular or pie-shaped lunate appearance may be seen on the PA view when the lunate is volarly dislocated (it is viewed obliquely). Scaphoid fracture should be specifically sought.

Lateral view: This is the most diagnostic view. Normally, the radius, lunate, capitate, and third metacarpal are co-linear. In perilunate dislocation, the lunate remains aligned with the radius (sitting in its fossa), but the capitate (and the rest of the carpus) is displaced dorsal
to the lunate. In lunate dislocation (Stage IV), the capitate drops back into alignment with the radius (into the lunate fossa), while the lunate is displaced volarly into the carpal tunnel — the classic "spilled teacup" sign on the lateral view.

CT scan with multiplanar reconstruction is essential for detailed assessment of fracture lines (particularly trans-scaphoid and trans-capitate variants), fragment displacement, and pre-surgical planning.

MRI evaluates the extent of ligamentous injury, though in the acute setting CT is more immediately informative.

Management

CIC injuries are surgical emergencies — prompt reduction and stabilisation are essential to prevent avascular necrosis (particularly of the lunate, which derives most of its blood supply from dorsal and volar capsular vessels that are disrupted during dislocation), progressive median nerve compression, and irreversible cartilage damage.

Emergency Closed Reduction:
Performed under adequate anaesthesia (regional or general) or sedation. The classic reduction technique involves longitudinal traction on the hand (restoring length), wrist extension (to disengage the dorsally displaced capitate), and then gradual wrist flexion with volar pressure on the dorsal capitate (to slide the capitate back over the lunate). For lunate dislocation, direct volar pressure on the volarly displaced lunate while applying traction may reduce the lunate back into its fossa. Post-reduction, a splint is applied and repeat radiographs confirm concentric reduction. Median nerve function is reassessed after reduction — improvement suggests the compression was mechanical and reversible.

Definitive Surgical Treatment — Open Reduction and Internal Fixation:

The current standard of care is surgical fixation within 7–10 days of injury. A combined dorsal and volar approach is generally recommended for complete access to all injured structures.

Through a dorsal approach, the capsule is opened (dorsal capsulotomy), the SL and LT intervals are directly visualised and repaired with suture anchors or transosseous sutures, the midcarpal reduction is confirmed, and temporary K-wires are placed across the SL interval, LT interval, and scaphocapitate articulation to maintain reduction during ligament healing. If a scaphoid fracture is present (trans-scaphoid variant), the scaphoid is reduced and fixed with a headless compression screw.

Through a volar approach (extended carpal tunnel approach), the carpal tunnel is decompressed (if median nerve symptoms are present — decompression should not be delayed), the volar capsular ligaments are repaired (the rent in the space of Poirier is closed), and the volar aspect of the lunate is inspected for chondral damage. The volar approach also allows assessment of the volar SL ligament.

K-wires are removed at 8–12 weeks. Supervised rehabilitation follows, focusing on progressive range of motion recovery.

Outcomes and Prognosis: Even with optimal surgical treatment, perilunate injuries carry a guarded long-term prognosis. Post-traumatic arthritis develops in approximately 40–60% of cases at long-term follow-up. Residual stiffness (loss of 30–50% of wrist motion) is common. Grip strength typically recovers to 70–80% of the contralateral side. Trans-scaphoid variants have a risk of scaphoid non-union and AVN. Lunate AVN (Kienböck-like changes) may develop if the lunate vascularity was severely compromised during the dislocation. Delayed diagnosis and treatment (>3 weeks) significantly worsens outcomes — open reduction becomes more difficult due to soft tissue contracture and early fibrosis, and the risk of AVN and arthritis increases substantially.
