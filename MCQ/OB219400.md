## Question

A 37-year-old female presents with a palpable mass along the anterior aspect of her thigh. She states she first noticed it four months ago and has grown since then. She denies pain, fevers, chills, or any other complaints. On exam, her thigh is notably larger than the contralateral side. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveals a 15cm heterogenous soft tissue mass (Figures A and B). A core needle biopsy is performed (Figure C), which tests negative for t(12:16) but positive for p53 mutation. She has a normal CT scan of her thorax. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

## Options

1. Radiofrequency ablation
2. Wide resection alone
3. Neoadjuvant radiation (50Gy) followed by wide resection
4. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and wide resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy
5. Neoadjuvant radiation followed by hip disarticulation

## Correct option

**3. Neoadjuvant radiation (50Gy) followed by wide resection**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s MRI revealed a large, heterogeneous mass, later confirmed to be a pleomorphic liposarcoma based on histologic findings. Without any signs of metastatic disease (negative CT-thorax), this patient should undergo a combination of radiation and wide resection (Answer 3).

Liposarcomas are the second most common soft tissue sarcoma encountered, falling just behind undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas (UPS). Liposarcomas generally contain three subtypes: (1) dedifferentiated, (2) pleomorphic, and (3) myxoid. Dedifferentiated liposarcomas are the most common subtype, appearing like most soft tissue sarcomas (both on MRI imaging and highly mitotic/pleomorphic histology) but demonstrating additional MDM2 and CDK4 amplification. Pleomorphic liposarcomas are the least common and most aggressive subtype, histologically appearing similar to UPS but with large areas of lipoblasts demonstrating complex chromosomal structural changes. These two liposarcomas variants are treated with standard radiation and wide resection. Myxoid liposarcomas demonstrate nodular areas of lipoblasts with surrounding myxoid stroma and are found to have a t(12;16) translocation with CHOP gene fusion. Myxoid liposarcomas are unique compared to most other soft tissue sarcomas. They ARE responsive to chemotherapy (ifosfamide-based) and, therefore, can be treated with triple therapy: chemotherapy, radiation, and wide resection.

*Johnson and colleagues reviewed lipomatous tumors ranging from benign lipomas to very-low malignant potential atypical lipomatous tumors (ALTs) and liposarcomas.* Compared with lipomas, atypical lipomatous tumors (ALTs) have higher local recurrence rates and a potential to dedifferentiate over time (~5-10%). While both are treated with marginal excision, **the authors note** it is important to distinguish between these entities for post-resection surveillance. A straightforward way is to assess for amplification of the MDM2 or CDK4 genes on tissue analysis, which will be positive in ALTs. The authors also stress ALTs in the retroperitoneum are termed well-differentiated liposarcomas (WDLs) and are managed by surgical oncology via wide resection (WDLs have a poor prognosis compared to extremity ALTs). The authors discuss common liposarcoma management, including radiation and wide resection.

*Novais and colleagues retrospectively reviewed their institutional database to establish prognostic factors influencing survival in soft tissue sarcomas.* *Utilizing ~250 patients' medical records,* **they found** that increasing age, male gender, high tumor grade, positive or close (<2mm) margins, tumor response <90%, and developing metastases were all associated with worse survival rates. Importantly, **they found** that local recurrence independently predicted decreased survival. **The authors concluded** that microscopically positive margins and local recurrence should be included as risk factors predicting decreased overall survival (despite prior studies finding these two factors not affecting survival).

Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate MRI imaging of a large (>10cm) heterogeneous soft tissue mass contained in the anterior compartment of the thigh. The mass has components that are heterogeneous to muscle on T1-weighted imaging (i.e., “dark on T1”) that enhances post-contrast and T2 MRI imaging (i.e., “bright on T2”). Figure C is a high-powered image of a high-grade pleomorphic liposarcoma with large lipoblasts, various atypical nuclei, and a high degree of pleomorphism (cells with various shapes and sizes).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Radiofrequency ablation would be an appropriate treatment for an appendicular osteoid osteoma.
Answer 2: Wide resection alone would be an appropriate treatment for an adamantinoma or chondrosarcoma.
Answer 4: Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and wide resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy would be an appropriate treatment for osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma. Myxoid liposarcomas are often treated with a combination of chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery.
Answer 5: Hip disarticulation would be considered an appropriate treatment in thigh tumors deemed non-salvageable. Amputation may be necessary for the management of complex soft tissue sarcomas (i.e., enveloping critical structures) or to treat unintended incomplete resections with gross contamination (i.e., “whoops” procedures). However, the standard of care for most soft tissue sarcomas is radiation and wide resection for intended limb salvage. This patient’s tumor is not impossibly large/invasive, is relegated to the anterior compartment of the thigh, and is therefore indicated for limb salvage.
