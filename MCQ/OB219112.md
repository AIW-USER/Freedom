## Question

A 65-year-old male presents to your office with complaints of persistent foot pain and swelling which has been present now for the last 2 months. He does report a long history of type II diabetes but does not know his most recent hemoglobin A1C. The sensation in his feet is diminished with Semmes-Weinstein monofilament testing bilaterally. He notes that when he wakes up in the morning the swelling and erythema are much better than before he goes to bed. His radiographs are seen in Figure A. No fluctuance is noted clinically and there are no fluid collections seen on the non contrast CT ordered by the ED. He has not had any prior treatment for this issue. What is the next best step in the management of this patient?

## Options

1. Order inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, WBC)
2. MRI of the foot/ankle
3. Total contact casting
4. Midfoot and subtalar arthrodesis
5. Below knee amputation (BKA)

## Correct option

**3. Total contact casting**

## Answer explanation

This patient has diabetic Charcot arthropathy of his foot, for which the first-line treatment is total contact casting (TCC).

Charcot arthropathy is caused when uncontrolled diabetes leads to a loss of protective sensation in the lower extremities. There are multiple theorized etiologies of the pathophysiology (neurotraumatic and neurovascular) by which Charcot arthropathy occurs. Regardless of the cause, diabetic Charcot arthropathy leads to joint destruction, bony fragmentation, soft-tissue edema, and generalized deformity of the foot. Diagnosis can be challenging, as there is often suspicion of infection. MRI and inflammatory markers both have low specificity for diagnostic purposes, and a bone scan or indium WBC scan may be more helpful. First-line management for most patients includes TCC, which is changed every 2-4 weeks for several months. The patient is then transitioned to a Charcot restrained orthotic walker (CROW). In recalcitrant cases, bony exostectomy, reconstruction utilizing osteotomies and arthrodeses, or even amputation serve as potential treatment options.

De Souza, et al. discussed immobilization in a weight-bearing TCC for Charcot arthropathy, particularly for patients in the early stages of the disease process (Eichenholtz I or II). They noted no negative effects from weight-bearing in the TCC with regard to skin ulceration or bony structure. They concluded that weight-bearing in a TCC was an effective treatment for early-stage Charcot arthropathy.

Van der Ven, et al. reviewed Charcot neuroarthropathy of the foot and ankle, noting it will likely become even more common in the future, given the increasing prevalence of diabetes. They note that prevention of deformity and skin breakdown are paramount and that later complications are usually a result of chronic ulceration, infection, or subsequent osteomyelitis. They discuss a diagnostic algorithm as well as options for non-operative and operative management strategies, including newer limb salvage techniques.

Figure A shows a lateral radiograph of the foot that demonstrates Charcot arthropathy primarily involving the midfoot, but also involving the subtalar joint to a lesser degree. Illustration A demonstrates a CROW walker.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Inflammatory markers are likely to be elevated both in cases of Charcot arthropathy and osteomyelitis. Therefore, obtaining these labs does not help obtain a diagnosis, especially given this patient's examination findings where edema and erythema subside upon elevation.
Answer 2: An MRI of the foot/ankle would also demonstrate inflammatory changes on the T2 and STIR sequences, making its diagnostic specificity low between Charcot arthropathy and osteomyelitis.
Answer 4: In cases where nonoperative measures have failed or the existing deformity is not braceable, arthrodesis may be considered.
Answer 5: Below-knee amputation is typically indicated in patients who have severe deformity with soft tissue involvement, or those who have failed prior reconstruction attempts.
