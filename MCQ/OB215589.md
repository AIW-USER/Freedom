## Question

On evaluation you note limited cervical range of motion and a subtle webbed appearance to the neck. Cervical radiographs are ordered as seen in Figures A & B. ## Options

1. Cleared to return to sport after course of physical therapy
2. Cleared for full contact activity immediately
3. Refrain from returning to any contact sports
4. Refrain from contact sports until MRI can confirm there are no associated spinal cord abnormalities
5. Refrain from contact sports until 6 months after posterior spinal fusion

## Correct option

**3. Refrain from returning to any contact sports**

## Answer explanation

This patient has Klippel-Feil syndrome with congenital fusion from C2-4 and should not be allowed to return to contact sports, given the high risk of significant cervical and spinal cord injury.

Klippel-Feil syndrome is a congenital abnormality caused by abnormal segmentation or formation of multiple cervical levels early in fetal development. It is associated with basilar invagination, atlantoaxial instability, cervical stenosis, and adjacent level vertebral degeneration. The most common presentation is a mostly asymptomatic patient with complaints of mild neck stiffness. Type 1 lesions are denoted by a mass fusion with most of the cervical spine involved. Type 2 lesions, as seen in this patient, usually involve one or multiple levels but not the entire cervical spine. In type 2 lesions below C3 in an individual with full neck range of motion and no other degenerative changes, return to play with close observation may be permitted.

*Torg et al. review cervical spine lesions injuries in over 1200 football players and discuss congenital, developmental, and traumatic causes of injury.* *They review Klippel-Feil syndrome specifically,* **noting** that type 1 and type 2 lesions at C3 and above are contraindications for return to contact sports. **They conclude** that, overall, the cervical condition at hand should be considered in combination with age, sport, and other risk factors before deciding if they player should return to competition.

Steyaert discussed Klippel-Feil syndrome and whether it should be a contraindication to sports. He notes certain patterns of cervical abnormality carry higher risk, likely given altered force transfer that goes through adjacent cervical segments, particularly in the setting of pre-existing cervical stenosis. He concludes that despite often being asymptomatic, full workup is important in these patients and certain cervical abnormalities represent absolute contraindication to participation in contact sports.

Figure A is an AP radiograph demonstrating congenital fusion of C2-C4 representative of Klippel-Feil syndrome. Figure B is a lateral radiograph demonstrating C2-C4 fusion with adjacent segment degeneration representative of Klippel-Feil syndrome.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Physical therapy would not offer any improvement given this patient's congenital fusion and she should not be allowed to return to contact activities.
Answer 2: Given this patient's risk factors and current radiographs, she should not be allowed to return to contact sports as the risk of significant injury is present.
Answer 4: Even if MRI does not show spinal cord abnormalities, this patient should not be allowed to return to contact activities, given her radiographic appearance of cervical fusion from C2-4.
Answer 5: This patient may require a posterior spinal fusion at some point given the degenerative appearance of the segments adjacent to the fusion mass, however, she would still not be allowed to return to contact sports after this procedure.
