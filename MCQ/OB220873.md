## Question

A 2-month-old infant is undergoing Pavlik harness treatment after Graf type IV hip dysplasia was diagnosed on ultrasound. He undergoes one week of therapy, and his parents report that he is unable to straighten his knee. On examination, there is decreased quadriceps function and diminished patellar reflex on the affected side. Hip flexion angle in the harness is noted to be 100°. Which of the following statements is most accurate regarding this complication?

## Options

1. A dislocated hip at the time of diagnosis is an independent risk factor for this complication
2. Pavlik harness treatment should be continued without adjustment and spontaneous resolution is expected
3. Younger age and smaller size at initiation of harness therapy places this patient at higher risk for this complication
4. The hip flexion in the harness should be increased with immediate continuation of treatment
5. Pavlik harness treatment should be discontinued indefinitely

## Correct option

**1. A dislocated hip at the time of diagnosis is an independent risk factor for this complication**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with severe developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) and a hip dislocation. Treatment was initiated with a Pavlik harness, after which femoral nerve palsy developed. A dislocated hip at the time of diagnosis and treatment initiation is an independent risk factor for this complication (Answer 1).

DDH is a disorder of abnormal development resulting in shallow acetabular morphology, subluxation, and possible dislocation of the hip secondary to capsular laxity and mechanical instability. Well-established risk factors include female sex, a positive family history, breech presentation, and higher birth weight. Early detection within 3 months is critical, as it allows for effective nonoperative treatment. The most common approach is the Pavlik harness, a dynamic brace that positions the hips in flexion and abduction while still permitting motion. Safe and efficacious use of the Pavlik harness depends on several key principles: (1) starting treatment before 6 months of age, (2) confirming that the hip reduces within the harness, and (3) monitoring closely for complications such as femoral nerve palsy or avascular necrosis.

*Kelley et al. present an expert consensus on Pavlik harness management for DDH,* **emphasizing** early initiation in infants under six months with careful monitoring for maintained hip reduction. The consensus highlights that treatment success is highest in younger infants and less severe dysplasia, and stresses the importance of avoiding excessive hip flexion and abduction to minimize complications such as femoral nerve palsy; harness discontinuation is recommended if reduction is not achieved within 2-3 weeks or if complications arise.

*Ghanem et al. report that femoral nerve palsy during Pavlik harness treatment is most frequently observed in older and larger infants with severe DDH and hip flexion angles above 90°.* *The study demonstrates that femoral nerve palsy typically presents within the first two weeks of treatment and resolves spontaneously before completion of therapy, without the need for harness abandonment or strap release.* The presence of femoral nerve palsy alone is not predictive of treatment failure, and management should focus on close monitoring rather than immediate discontinuation of the harness.

*Murnaghan et al.* **find** that femoral nerve palsy is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of Pavlik harness treatment, with an incidence of 2.5%. It is strongly associated with older age, larger size, and higher severity of DDH. Early presentation (within one week) is typical. **The authors conclude** that the duration of palsy is predictive of treatment outcome: rapid resolution (within three days) is associated with successful harness treatment, while persistent palsy (beyond ten days) correlates with a high rate of treatment failure and need for surgical intervention.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Although some cases may resolve, continuing without adjustment risks permanent nerve injury; hip flexion must be reduced immediately.
Answer 3: Femoral nerve palsy is more likely in older infants (>6 months) and those with severe dislocation.
Answer 4: Additional hip flexion will result in increased mechanical pressure on the femoral nerve, worsening the femoral nerve palsy.
Answer 5: If the palsy does not resolve or the hip remains dislocated, closed versus open reduction with transition to a rigid orthosis/cast should be considered.
