## Options

1. 55-year-old female with left sided radicular back pain, positive straight leg raise, and MRI shown in Figure A
2. 42-year-old male with diffuse lumbar pain, bilateral lower extremity weakness and numbness, with perianal loss of sensation and MRI shown in Figure B
3. 37-year-old female with complaints of superficial low back tenderness, numbness of left thigh, weakness of left foot dorsiflexion, negative straight leg raise and MRI shown in Figure C
4. 74-year-old male with bilateral lower extremity pain and weakness, worse with stairs and MRI seen in Figure D
5. 27-year-old male with history of IV drug use who complains of diffuse low back pain and new onset incontinence, with MRI seen in Figure E

## Correct option

**3. 37-year-old female with complaints of superficial low back tenderness, numbness of left thigh, weakness of left foot dorsiflexion, negative straight leg raise and MRI shown in Figure C**

## Answer explanation

The 37-year-old patient listed above complains of superficial tenderness and leg pain/weakness that do not follow a specific dermatomal pattern. Her MRI shows L4-5 disc degeneration which does not correlate with his symptoms and this combination is indicative of a non-organic cause of back pain.

Waddell's signs are a group of potential clinical signs in a patient with nonorganic low back pain. These signs suggest that the patient's pain is not originating from an anatomic, structural issue. They include superficial or non-anatomic tenderness, complaints which do not follow a dermatomal pattern, pain with axial compression/rotation, negative straight leg raise when distracted, and an overreaction to physical examination. Some have argued that Waddell's signs are not reliable in determining organic vs. nonorganic pain, however, they may still have some relevance in ensuring patients complaints are consistent with spinal pathology.

*Fishbain et al. performed a review of four quality studies with the goal of providing further recommendations on the clinical use of Waddell's signs.* Interestingly, **they found** little evidence that Waddell's signs were linked to psychological issues or cases of secondary gain and noted that Waddell's signs had poor inter-rater reliability. Ultimately, **they concluded** that Waddell's signs were not able to consistently discriminate between organic and nonorganic symptoms of low back pain.

*Craig et al. discuss malingering and pain misrepresentation and its' causes and challenges.* Specifically, with regards to Waddell's signs and other "red flags," **they note** that patients are often inconsistent in symptom recollection and providers do a poor job at discriminating those with organic vs. nonorganic causes of pain. **They conclude** by stating more work needs to be done in measuring pain in order to objectify it so that clinicians can make appropriate treatment decisions.

Figure A depicts a left-sided lumbar disc herniation. Figure B demonstrates a large central lumbar disc herniation causing cauda equina syndrome. Figure C demonstrates L4-5 disc degeneration but no canal stenosis or disc protrusion. Figure D demonstrates an MRI indicative of lumbar stenosis. Figure E shows an epidural abscess from L2-L4.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This patient has left side lumbar radiculopathy as demonstrated by consistent physical examination and imaging findings.
Answer 2: This patient most likely has cauda equina syndrome given their physical exam findings and large lumbar disc protrusion on MRI.
Answer 4: This patient has findings consistent with lumbar stenosis.
Answer 5: This patient has findings consistent with lumbar epidural abscess.
