## Question

As an orthopaedic surgery resident, you arrive late to a medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction procedure in your institution's outpatient surgical center. It is standard practice in your residency program to miss the start of the first case because of didactic requirements on Tuesdays. The surgical team has your gown and gloves ready, and you plan to join the surgical team and assist the attending with the procedure. Which of the following is expected of the resident and is in accordance with standard surgical safety checklists?

## Options

1. Quickly greet the attending surgeon and surgical assistants without repeating a surgical timeout
2. Request to repeat a formal timeout and review of the safety checklist prior to scrubbing
3. Look under the surgical drapes to ensure the contralateral limb was not marked as the prior to scrubbing
4. The use an alcohol-based gel rather than a formal surgical scrub for the first case of the day
5. Not scrubbing the case because you did not personally mark the surgical site in the preoperative holding area

## Correct option

**1. Quickly greet the attending surgeon and surgical assistants without repeating a surgical timeout**

## Answer explanation

When a resident surgeon arrives late to the operating room, it is customary practice for the resident to introduce and greet the operating team. There is currently no official mandate by any governing body that requires the timeout be repeated to confirm the patient and procedure when the operation has already begun.

The World Health Organization (WHO) and nearly every professional surgical subspecialty group advocates for safety checklists prior to operations. Although there are institutional variations to individual items on the checklists, it is important to include a system for marking the surgical site, verifying the patient's identity and procedure planned, identifying all team members in the room and agreement regarding the type of anesthesia, antibiotic prophylaxis, instrumentation used. Simply put, safety checklists have been shown to drastically reduce costly errors in other high-risk industries, and they have been universally adopted by hospitals and surgery centers and the organizations that inspect and validate healthcare settings.

*Kuo et al. reviewed orthopaedic surgeon leadership in healthcare systems to improve orthopaedic surgical patient safety.* **They identified** 6 surgical safety program elements necessary to eliminate preventable surgical harm, including effective surgical team communication, proper informed consent, implementation and regular use of surgical checklists, proper surgical site/procedure identification, reduction of surgical team distractions, and routine surgical data collection and analysis to improve the safety and quality of surgical patient care.

*Haynes et al. investigated the use of a surgical safety checklist to reduce morbidity and mortality in a global population.* **They reported** significant reductions in the complication and mortality rates after the implementation of the surgical safety checklist. **They recommended** universal implementation of this checklist in all institutions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: There is currently no official mandate by the WHO or surgical subspecialty organizations that requires the timeout to be repeated to confirm the patient and procedure.
Answers 3: Looking under the surgical drapes to ensure the contralateral limb was not marked as the prior to scrubbing is not part of the standard surgical safety checklist and would result in surgical team distraction and/or unnecessary delay during the operation.
Answer 4: While it is not a part of the standard patient safety checklist, the use of a formal surgical scrub (not an alcohol-based gel) is recommended for the first case of the day.
Answer 5: The surgical site should always be marked ahead of time when applicable sites are operated on. However, each surgical team member does not have to personally do so prior to the onset of a procedure in order to participate in the case.
