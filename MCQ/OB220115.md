## Question

A 28-year-old male presents to the clinic for evaluation of left elbow pain. Four months ago, the patient experienced a simple elbow dislocation, was immobilized for one week, and was subsequently sent to rehabilitation. Since the injury, the patient has noted worsening pain. On examination, the patient is supine, and then the forearm is brought from full extension to flexion while simultaneously adding valgus, supination, and axial compression. The aforementioned maneuver tests the competency of which of the following structures?

## Options

1. Triceps tendon
2. Lateral ulnar collateral ligament
3. Anterior band of medial ulnar collateral ligament
4. Posterior band of medial ulnar collateral ligament
5. Proximal radioulnar joint

## Correct option

**2. Lateral ulnar collateral ligament**

## Answer explanation

This 28-year-old male sustained a simple elbow dislocation with continued pain and posterolateral rotatory instability. The key structure compromised in this condition is the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL; Answer 2).

Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI) centers around an incompetent LUCL, which normally functions to maintain the reduction of the radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral joints, particularly when the elbow is subjected to axial loads. Patients commonly present with lateral elbow pain, varus instability, and difficulty rising out of a chair. The lateral pivot-shift test is performed with the patient supine and the extremity overhead in full elbow extension. The forearm is then fully supinated, and the elbow is flexed while simultaneously adding valgus, supination, and axial compression. A positive test includes apprehension in the awake patient or a clunk in the anesthetized patient. Of note, rotatory displacement is maximized at 40 degrees of flexion, and dimpling of the skin proximal to the radial head can be seen, which is followed by a clunk.

*Rodriguez et al. performed a retrospective review to characterize the functional and surgical outcomes of 23 military patients undergoing LUCL reconstruction with an average follow-up of 4.6 years.* **The authors noted** an 83% return to prior activity, with four (17%) requiring medical separation. Performance scores suggested good-to-excellent outcomes, however, four (17%) patients experienced complications (cubital tunnel syndrome [3], infection [1]), and three (13%) required reoperations. **The authors conclude** that LUCL reconstruction is a reliable surgery that allows active individuals to return to their prior activity.

*Anakwenze et al. reviewed PLRI of the elbow, detailing the anatomy, causes, presentation, physical exam, workup, and treatment options.* **The authors note** the elbow is a complex joint comprising three articulations and requires complex interplay between static and dynamic stabilizers to maintain stability. The LUCL is part of the lateral collateral ligament complex, originating from the lateral epicondyle and inserting onto the crista supinatorus, and acts as the primary restraint to varus and external rotatory stresses. **The author highlights** the controversy of the LUCL versus the lateral collateral ligament complex as which is the key contributor to PLRI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, 4, and 5: These structures do not play a role in posterolateral rotatory instability. The anterior band of the medial ulnar collateral ligament is the primary stabilizer of valgus elbow stress.
