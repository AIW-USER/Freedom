## Question

A 45-year-old laborer underwent the procedure depicted in Figures A and B after falling off a ladder at work 2 months prior to presentation. He has returned to work but complains of his ability to do his job effectively. On examination, he has a painless flexion-extension arc of 40 to 90 degrees with a near full pronation-supination arc. He has a normal neurovascular examination and is able to demonstrate all cardinal hand motions. What is the most appropriate initial treatment for this patient?

## Options

1. Arthroscopic release of adhesions
2. Limited open capsular release
3. Formal physical therapy and static progressive splinting
4. Removal of hardware due to cortical penetration
5. Cortisone injection followed by aggressive range-of-motion exercises

## Correct option

**3. Formal physical therapy and static progressive splinting**

## Answer explanation

This patient has postoperative elbow stiffness. Initial treatment should be nonoperative, consisting of static progressive splinting and formal physical therapy.

The development of joint contracture is a well-recognized complication of elbow injury. The precise causes of the propensity of this joint for ankylosis are understood poorly. Initial treatment should first be directed at the soft-tissue contracture and begin with formal physical therapy and static progressive splinting. Following failed conservative treatment, surgical interventions should be considered and include limited open procedures, interposition arthroplasty, joint replacement arthroplasty (if >65 years old), and arthroscopic interventions.

*Morrey et al. review the development of joint contracture as a complication of elbow injury.* **They report** that limited open procedures, interposition arthroplasty, joint replacement arthroplasty, and arthroscopic intervention are all viable surgical treatment options for this condition. **They conclude** that these procedures provide improvement in the arc of motion for the vast majority of patients.

*King et al. review the nonoperative and operative treatment options of post-traumatic elbow contracture.* **They report** that this results in the inability to position the hand in space often leading to significant functional disability. **They noted** that while initial treatment should be nonoperative, contracture that interferes with a patient's vocation or avocations can be treated operatively by either open or arthroscopic release. **They concluded** that the surgical release of post-traumatic elbow contracture is rewarding, with a high incidence of success and a reasonable risk of complications.

*Prayson et al. reviewed the placement of K-wires obliquely through the anterior ulnar cortex as a common modification of traditional olecranon tension-band wiring.* **He reports** that wire tip protrusion risks injury to adjacent neurovascular structures and may impede forearm rotation. **They conclude** that to avoid iatrogenic neurovascular injury during tension-band wiring of the olecranon, protrusion of wire tips beyond the anterior ulnar cortex should be no more than 1 cm at a distance of 1.5 cm distal to the coronoid.

*van der Linden et al. reviewed tension-band wiring (TBW) as the treatment of choice for displaced olecranon fractures and its relation to local complications and radiological and clinical long-term outcome.* **They report** that instability of the K-wires and subsequent osteoarthritis were more common in those with intramedullary placement than with transcortical placement. **They concluded** that stiffness is a common complication following these injuries when treated with TBW, with an average final arc of motion of 116 degrees and extension loss of 15 degrees.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: These may be a reasonable option for this patient if he fails conservative management, but a trial of nonoperative modalities should be attempted first. If he continues to be unable to perform his vocation or fails to gain functional improvement in motion, these should be considered.
Answer 4: While the protrusion of the K-wires tips may risk the neurovascular structures and influence forearm rotation, this patient has a normal neurovascular exam and near full forearm rotation. Hardware removal would likely provide little benefit to this patient
Answer 5: Formal physical therapy and static progressive splinting would be preferred over cortisone injection followed by aggressive physical therapy.
