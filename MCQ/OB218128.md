## Question

A 65-year-old homemaker undergoes a total knee arthroplasty with the implant demonstrated in Figure A. Her immediate post-operative films are shown in Figure B and her 5-year-follow-up films are shown in Figure C. In scrutinizing these radiographs, you note that the femoral component is flexed and that too much slope was cut into the tibia. On physical exam, the patient is asymptomatic and has no complaints. Which of the following complications would these technical errors most likely predispose the patient to?

## Options

1. A recurvatum gait with hyperextension instability
2. Coronal plane instability
3. Excessive tibial post wear
4. Patellar clunk
5. Patellar instability

## Correct option

**3. Excessive tibial post wear**

## Answer explanation

Excessive femoral component flexion and tibial slope can lead to tibial post impingement and subsequently accelerated post/poly wear that can contribute to the development of osteolysis (Illustration A).

Posterior-stabilized or substituting (PS) total knee implants represent a level of increased constraint over cruciate-retaining (CR) implants, as they must compensate for the loss of the stabilizing forces and increase in flexion gap when the PCL is sacrificed. To achieve this, the femoral component contains a "cam" that engages a post on the polyethylene insert during flexion. Given the geometry of this articulation, inaccuracies in the placement of the femoral component can result in tibial post impingement during flexion that results in excessive or early polyethylene wear and, ultimately, can contribute to osteolysis. Technical considerations that can be made to minimize this complication include avoiding knee hyperextension by preventing excessively large extension gaps (i.e., excessive distal femoral bone resection). Furthermore, avoiding excessive femoral component flexion and excessive tibial slope can prevent early cam impingement on the anterior tibial post, reducing wear rates.

*Klein et al. published a review on the rotational constraint in posterior-stabilized total knee prostheses.* *The authors constructed a bench model to assess the forces generated by tibiofemoral rotation at various degrees of flexion and found that tibiofemoral conformity is the primary source of rotational constraint, with box-post impingement being a source of additional rotational constraint.* **They concluded** that axial rotation of the knee in vivo can generate substantial torque, which is amplified by implant malpositioning and can result in substantial damage to the tibial post within physiologic ranges of axial motion.

*Callaghan et al. provide a review of tibial post impingement in posterior-stabilized total knee arthroplasty.* **The authors note** that tibial post-femoral cam impingement has been associated with the transmission of rotational forces to the modular tray/polyethylene interface with subsequent backside polyethylene wear and the development of osteolysis. **They conclude** that technical considerations, including the avoidance of excessive femoral component flexion and posterior tibial slope, will minimize anterior tibial post impingement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Though excessive tibial slope is more likely to lead to anterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur and subsequent hyperextension, intra-operatively, one may be inclined to chase the loose flexion gap by increasing polyethylene size and overstuffing in extension. This would predispose you to a loss of full extension. Furthermore, a recurvatum gait status post TKA is associated with global, multiply-operated instability. This would not be expected in an asymptomatic patient such as this, and would indicate the need for revision with more constrained, typically hinged, components.
Answer 2: Coronal plane instability is dictated by the ligamentous balancing; the femoral and tibial osseous cuts contribute primarily to the stability in the sagittal plane (flexion/extension gaps).
Answer 4: Patellar clunk is caused by fibrous scar tissue that forms along the posterior surface of the distal quadriceps tendon/superior pole of the patella that catches on the box of the femoral component during extension. Though risk factors do include small femoral components and excessive femoral component flexion, this patient is asymptomatic and more commonly presents within 12 months of the index surgery and with a painful and often palpable "pop" or "catch" as the knee extends beyond 30-45 degrees of flexion.
Answer 5: Patellar instability and maltracking are more commonly due to malrotated components that iatrogenically increase the patient's Q angle, asymmetric patellar resection or improper patella preparation, and excessive anterior placement of the femoral component.
