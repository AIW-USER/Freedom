## Options

1. Myositis Ossificans; observation
2. Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma; wide resection
3. Chondrosarcoma; wide resection
4. Femoral shaft stress reaction; intramedullary nailing
5. Osteosarcoma; chemotherapy followed by resection and reconstruction

## Correct option

**5. Osteosarcoma; chemotherapy followed by resection and reconstruction**

## Answer explanation

This patient has biopsy-confirmed osteosarcoma of the femur. Osteosarcoma requires chemotherapy, followed by wide resection and reconstruction (Answer 5).

Osteosarcoma is the most common bone-producing malignancy. There is a bimodal distribution in terms of age at presentation, with most patients diagnosed before the age of 20 or after the age of 65. Osteosarcoma typically affects the distal femur, proximal tibia, or proximal humerus as these areas undergo the most growth during adolescence. A biopsy is needed to confirm the diagnosis and will typically show atypical stromal cells, mitotic figures, and irregular lacy osteoid. Patients are treated typically with neoadjuvant chemotherapy for roughly 3 months before surgery. Limb salvage is performed when possible, and requires wide resection of the tumor and reconstruction of the bony defect. Options for reconstruction include allograft, endoprosthesis, or rotationplasty. Amputation is considered if limb salvage is not feasible or would result in a significant decrease in the function of the extremity. Patients typically require a period of adjuvant chemotherapy postoperatively. 5-year survival for patients with localized disease is, on average, 65% and is associated with chemotherapy response. With metastatic disease, the 5-year survival drops to 15-20%.

*Misaghi et al. authored a review of osteosarcoma.* *They focus on the various subtypes and provide an overview of the diagnostic workup, as well as the most common treatment strategies.*

*Messerschmitt et al. also reviewed osteosarcoma.* **The authors highlight** that advances in chemotherapy have increased life expectancy for these patients significantly and that novel chemotherapy agents may continue to improve survival after osteosarcoma.

*Papagelopoulos et al. summarized the management of pathological fractures in primary bone sarcomas.* **They highlight** that pathological fractures are not an absolute indication for amputation and that, frequently, the fracture can be stabilized with casting or external fixation while a definitive plan is developed.

Figure A is a plain radiograph of the femur demonstrating a lesion of the femoral diaphysis with periosteal reaction and characteristic "sun burst" appearance. Figure B is a histological slide demonstrating characteristic features of osteosarcoma, including atypical stromal cells, mitotic figures, and irregular, lace-like osteoid.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Myositis ossificans can present with a soft tissue mass with calcifications. However, the histology would show mature, non-malignant appearing bone with fibroblasts without cellular atypia.
Answer 2: Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma would not show osteoid production on histology, but would rather show atypical, spindle-type cells. It would be unlikely for a pleomorphic sarcoma to have bone formation present.
Answer 3: Chondrosarcoma would present radiographically with popcorn calcifications and would show chondroid matrix on histology.
Answer 4: A femoral stress fracture would not have an associated soft tissue mass, and histology would not show any cellular atypia.
