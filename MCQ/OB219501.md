## Question

Which of the following structures does not form part of the rotator interval tissue and serves to stabilize the structure shown in Figure A at the level of the greater and lesser tuberosities of the humerus?

![[q219501-a-screenshot-2024-02-21-at-5-56-30-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Coracoacromial ligament (CAL)
2. Coracohumeral ligament (CHL)
3. Inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL)
4. Superior glenohumeral ligament (SGHL)
5. Transverse humeral ligament (THL)

## Correct option

**5. Transverse humeral ligament (THL)**

## Answer explanation

The transverse humeral ligament (THL) stabilizes the biceps tendon (indicated by the red arrow) in the bicipital groove at the level of the greater and lesser tuberosities of the humerus (proximal to the biceps pulley tissue) and does not form part of the rotator interval tissue (Answer 5).

The biceps tendon originates off of the supraglenoid tubercle at the level of the superior labrum and then traverses the shoulder joint intra-articularly, at which point it is stabilized by the biceps sling or "pulley" system. This sling is comprised primarily of the coracohumeral (CHL) and superior glenohumeral ligaments (SGHL), which form the rotator interval tissue, as well as contributions from the subscapularis and supraspinatus tendons. At the level of the greater and lesser tuberosities of the humerus, the biceps tendon has stabilized in the bicipital groove, where it is bordered superficially by the transverse humeral ligament (THL). It is important to recognize these structures on both imaging and intra-operatively, as they serve as both surgical landmarks and signals of concomitant pathology when disrupted. For instance, when the biceps sling tissue is disrupted in the setting of an anterosuperior rotator cuff tear, the biceps tendon subluxates medially and signals that a subscapualris tear is present, which at times is missed on initial review of an MRI.

*Frank et al. review the components of the rotator interval of the shoulder and their implications in the treatment of shoulder instability.* **The authors note** that biomechanical studies have shown that repair or plication of rotator interval (RI) ligamentous and capsular structures decreases glenohumeral joint laxity in various directions, with clinical outcomes studies reporting successful outcomes after repair or plication of these structures in patients undergoing shoulder stabilization procedures. **They concluded** that while surgical plication of RI structures may be indicated in a select group of instability procedures, the biomechanical and clinical evidence for routine plication is inconclusive and that additional studies are needed to clarify the role of the RI and its associated structures in the setting of shoulder instability to better define indications and improve surgical techniques.

*Van Deurzen et al. published on the clinical relevance of the anatomy of the bicipital groove.* **The authors state** that the dimensions of the bicipital groove have been reported in the literature with the length ranging from 81.00 mm to 87.33 mm, the width from 7.74 mm to 11.60 mm, and the depth ranging from 3.70 mm to 6.00 mm, with a bottleneck narrowing occurring approximately two-thirds from the most superior aspect. **They note** that the transverse humeral ligament can constrain the bicipital groove and could be involved in anterior shoulder pain, leading them to suggest that when either LHBT tenotomy or tenodesis is performed, routinely releasing the transverse ligament could decrease postoperative anterior shoulder pain, which has frequently been reported in the literature. Finally, **they recommend** that in order to avoid the bottleneck narrowing, a location below the bicipital groove may be preferred for biceps tenodesis over a more proximal tenodesis site.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The CAL is not part of the rotator interval tissue but does not contribute to the stability of the LHBT at the level of the tuberosities.
Answer 2: The CHL is part of the rotator interval tissue.
Answer 3: The IGHL is not part of the rotator interval tissue and is not a primary stabilizer of the LHBT at the level of the bicipital groove.
Answer 4: The SGHL is part of the rotator interval tissue.
