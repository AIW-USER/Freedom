## Question

A 21-year-old male falls off of a unicycle directly onto a semi-flexed elbow. A representative lateral radiograph of his injury obtained in the emergency room is shown in Figure A. The main fracture fragment includes a small portion of the trochlea. Which of the following represents the most appropriate surgical exposure to perform when treating this fracture with headless compression screws?

![[q219186-a-screenshot-2023-10-18-at-9-51-59-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Bryan-Morrey triceps-reflecting approach
2. Hotchkiss approach
3. Lateral column approach
4. Posterior approach with olecranon osteotomy
5. Posterior approach without olecranon osteotomy

## Correct option

**3. Lateral column approach**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained a coronal shear fracture of the capitellum that includes a small portion of the lateral trochlea. A lateral column approach should provide adequate exposure to allow for open reduction and internal fixation with antero-posteriorly directed headless compression screws.

Coronal shear fractures of the capitellum represent 1% of all elbow fractures and 6% of all distal humerus fractures, making them relatively uncommon. Though non-displaced (<2mm) fractures can be treated non-operatively, the vast majority are displaced, demonstrating the "double-arc" sign seen in Figure A, and need to be fixed surgically. The lateral column approach to the elbow is typically used to allow for adequate exposure of the joint anteriorly. It involves making a lateral skin incision (the same as that for the proximal one-half of the Kocher incision), extending from 6 cm proximal to the epicondyle to 3 cm distal to it. The origin of the extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) muscle and distal fibers of the brachioradialis muscle are released from the lateral column of the distal humerus, which provides direct access to the superolateral aspect of the capsule. After making a capsulotomy, the brachialis muscle can be swept from and separated from the anterior capsule with a periosteal elevator, protecting the radial nerve, median nerve, and brachial artery, and providing excellent visualization of the capitellum and lateral trochlea (Illustration A).

*Mighell et al. provide a review of large coronal shear fractures of the capitellum and trochlea treated with headless compression screws.* *The authors retrospectively reviewed 18 patients with a mean follow-up of 26 months and found that all patients, with the exception of 1, had good to excellent functional results by the Broberg-Morrey scale with an average arc of motion of 128 in flexion/extension.* **They concluded** that headless compression screw fixation allows for stable fixation in patients with large coronal shear fractures of the distal humerus without posterior comminution.

*Nauth et al. reviewed distal humerus fractures in adults.* **The authors found** that these injuries are relatively uncommon injuries that require operative intervention in the majority of cases. In terms of displaced coronal shear fractures, **the authors concluded** that given articular surface involvement, operative fixation is required, most typically via a lateral approach.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the elbow demonstrating the "double bubble" or "double arc" sign associated with a coronal shear fracture of the capitellum and the lateral aspect of the trochlea. Illustration A is a representative photograph of the lateral column approach with the inset on the right showing the tip of the coronoid between the two black lines, indicating adequate exposure of the anterior joint over to the lateral trochlea. (H= Humerus, O = Olecranon, L = lateral epicondyle, C= Capitellum, R = Radial head).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The Bryan-Morrey triceps-reflecting approach is performed by releasing the triceps tendon, forearm fascia, and periosteum as one unit from medial to lateral off the olecranon. This requires exposing and protecting the ulnar nerve, which does not need to be done in the setting of an isolated lateral-sided coronal shear fracture of the distal humerus.
Answer 2: The Hotchkiss approach is a medial-based elbow approach that could be used for coronoid fracture fixation but would not provide adequate exposure in this case of a laterally based fracture.
Answers 4-5: Though a posterior approach with olecranon osteotomy does allow for excellent intra-articular visualization, this would be excessively morbid for a patient with an isolated distal humerus fracture involving the capitellum and lateral trochlea that can be approached more easily from the lateral side.
