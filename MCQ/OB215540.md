## Question

An 80-year-old female underwent reverse total shoulder arthroplasty and at her 6 month follow-up, complains of a new increase in pain and decreased range of motion. She denies any recent history of trauma. WBC count, ESR, and CRP are within normal ranges. Radiograph is shown in Figure A. Which of the following is a risk factor for this complication?

![[q215540-a-acromial-stress-fx-rtsa.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Male gender
2. Medialized preoperative center of rotation
3. Hamada cuff tear arthropathy stage 3 or greater
4. Use of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty for proximal humerus fracture
5. Age < 50

## Correct option

**2. Medialized preoperative center of rotation**

## Answer explanation

The patient described is presenting with an acromial fracture 6 months after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA). Preoperatively, a more medial center of rotation is thought to increase risk for acromial stress fracture after RTSA due to the interval increase in force on the acromion due to lengthening of the deltoid lever arm from lateralization of the center of rotation.

RTSA is becoming more popular due to its ability to overcome many of the challenges of traditional shoulder arthroplasty. However, this is not without complication, including acromial stress fractures (~4%). These are thought to occur as the the deltoid lever arm becomes longer and deltoid tension increases after RTSA, causing the force on the deltoid insertion (acromion) to increase. When the preoperative center of rotation is further medial, this places increasing amounts of tension on the acromion after surgery. Other risk factors for acromial stress fracture after RTSA include female sex, osteoporosis, and less change in deltoid length postoperatively. The decrease in deltoid length postoperatively is thought to decrease the mechanical advantage of the deltoid, placing larger amounts of force through the deltoid, and subsequently, the acromion. In the setting of RTSA, when a patient has a sudden, atraumatic increase in pain postoperatively, radiographs should be obtained and CT scan may be necessary to further evaluate/identify the fracture. These should be immobilized in a sling with an abduction pillow to take stress off of the fracture site

*Zmistowski et al reviewed the incidence of acromial stress fracture after RTSA and attempted to identify associated risk factors.* **They noted** a 4.2% incidence of acromial stress fracture throughout their study period, consisting of 958 RTSAs. **They indicated** that predictors of acromial pathology after RTSA included female sex, less change in deltoid length, and increased medialization of center of rotation preoperatively.

*Patterson et al performed a systematic review of 3838 RTSAs with the incidence of acromial stress fractures noted to be 4.1%.* The majority were treated non-operatively, compared with a smaller percentage treated with ORIF. Regardless of ultimate treatment, the group with acromial stress fractures had worse outcomes than those without.

*Neyton et al performed a retrospective review specifically evaluating acromial stress fractures in Grammont style RTSAs.* The majority of these were noted to be atraumatic and were noticed within the first 6 months postoperatively. **They noted** a high frequency of malunion and nonunion with nonoperative management and, again, reiterated poorer results with any acromial stress fracture regardless of treatment.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Females have a higher risk of acromial stress fracture
Answer 3: Hamada stage 3 or greater cuff tear arthropathy demonstrates acetabularization of the acromion but has not been associated with postoperative acromial stress fractures.
Answer 4: Use of RTSA for proximal humerus fracture has not been linked to an increased risk of acromial stress fracture.
Answer 5: Patients with osteoporosis are at higher risk after RTSA. Therefore, patients who are younger are at less risk.
