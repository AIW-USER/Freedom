## Question

A 54-year-old Croatian carpenter has ring finger pain and limited motion at both his MCP and PIP joints. He reports having an injury years ago in the "old country" but has never sought treatment. The carpenter is otherwise healthy and does not use tobacco products. When considering pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty of the MCPJ and PIPJ, which of the following statements are true?

## Options

1. PIPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty will likely improve range of motion
2. PIPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has a high complication rate
3. Implant squeaking has not been reported for neither MCPJ nor PIPJ carbon arthroplasty
4. MCPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty will likely decrease range of motion
5. MCPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has a high complication rate

## Correct option

**2. PIPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has a high complication rate**

## Answer explanation

PIPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has an unacceptably high complication rate and the implant is rarely used for this reason.

PIPJ osteoarthritis (OA) is present in nearly 17% of women and 13% of men and often presents with symptoms including flexion contracture, decreased motion, swelling, and pain. The radial digits are often treated with fusion due to issues with stability, but non-border digits may be better suited for arthroplasty. Pain relief is reported in 98% of patients who undergo silicone arthroplasty and the complication rate is relatively low. The dorsal approach is the historic gold standard and may be technically more straightforward. Surface implants (metal-on-plastic) as well as a silicone implants are available. However, more favorable outcomes have recently been reported with the volar approach and with the use of silicone implants.

*Sweets and Stern reviewed 31 pyrolytic carbon arthroplasties for OA of the PIPJ with an average follow-up of 55 months.* The arc of motion decreased (57 to 31 degrees) Complications included implant fracture (1), dislocation (5), squeaking (11), loosening (15), IPJ contracture (20), and 6 needed re-operation. The results were so poor that **the authors report** that they no longer use this implant for PIPJ osteoarthritis.

*Wall and Stern reviewed 11 pyrolytic carbon arthroplasties for OA of the MCPJ with an average follow-up of 4 years and* **found** only mild increase in the arc of motion (62 to 76 degrees) but near-complete pain relief and 10/11 were fully satisfied. The average quick DASH was 22 and 2/11 experienced some asymptomatic squeaking. All implants eventually developed lucency and the average subsidence was 3mm. The radiographic findings did not correlate with the clinical outcomes.

*Yamamoto et al.* *(2017) performed a systematic review evaluating the various implants and approaches used for PIPJ arthroplasty.* **The authors found** that, as compared to the surface implant with a dorsal approach, the silicone implant placed through a volar approach was associated with significantly less extensor lag, better net gain in arc of motion, better overall post-operative motion, and a lower revision rate (6% vs 18%). **They concluded** that silicone implant via volar approach was associated with the best outcomes for PIPJ arthroplasty.

*Yamamoto et al.* (2018) comprehensively review exposure and implant designs for PIPJ arthroplasty. **They showed** that the volar approach resulted in the best range of motion, with the least extension lag, and lowest compilation rates. The surface implant had the highest rate of surgical revisions. The authors underscored the importance for continued improvement of implant design.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: PIPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has lead to a decrease in post-operative range of motion.
Answer 3: Squeaking has been reported in both PIPJ and MCPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasties.
Answer 4: MCPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has demonstrated a mild increase in range of motion post-operatively.
Answer 5: MCPJ pyrolytic carbon arthroplasty has a low complication rate.
