## Question

The difference in SIADH as compared to Cerebral salt wasting syndrome (CSWS)

## Options

1. Euvolemia
2. Decreased serum osmolality
3. Hyponatremia
4. History of diuretic use

## Correct option

**1. Euvolemia**

## Answer explanation

Ans: A

SIADH has euvolemia or hypervolemia while CSWS has hypovolemia.

SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion)

It is a condition in which pituitary gland releases excess of Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) that’s also called as Vasopressin, in response to drugs (like carbamazepine etc.), malignancies (e.g. small cell lung carcinoma) or some CNS, pulmonary or thyroid disturbances. Excess ADH helps the body to retain water by impairing water excretion resulting in hyponatremia but with euvolemia or hypervolemia. The serum osmolality hence decreases (< 275 mosm/l) but urine osmolality increases (> 100 mosm/l) as compared to normal levels.

Diagnostic criteria for SIADH: -

In the absence of a single laboratory test to confirm the diagnosis, SIADH is best defined by the classic criteria introduced by Bartter and Schwartz in 1967, which remains valid even today. The criteria can be summarized as follows: -

Essential features: -

- Decreased effective serum osmolality (< 275 mosm/kg of water)
- Simultaneous increased urine osmolality>100 mosm/kg of water
- Clinical euvolemia

- no clinical signs of extracellular volume depletion in body viz. orthostatic hypotension, decreased skin turgor, dry mucous membrane etc.
- no clinical signs of excess extra cellular volume in body viz. edema, ascites etc.

- Urinary Na > 40 meq/l despite normal dietary sodium intake
- There is normal thyroid and adrenal function
- There is no recent history of diuretic use

Some Supplemental features: -

- Plasma uric acid decreases (< 4 mg/dl)
- Fluid restriction corrects hyponatremia
- There is abnormal result on Water Load Test: -

 a) < 80% excretion of 20 ml of water/kg body weight over 5 hours, or

 b) inadequate urinary dilution (<100 mosm/kg of water)

- Elevated plasma ADH levels (with hyponatremia and euvolemia)

Clinical presentation: -

The earliest clinical manifestations of acute hyponatremia include nausea and malaise, which may be seen when the serum sodium concentration falls below 125 to 130 mEq/L (normal 135 to 145mEq/L). Vomiting is an ominous sign for patients with acute hyponatremia. With a more severe and acute fall in sodium concentration, headache, lethargy, obtundation, and eventually, seizures can occur.

Chronic hyponatremia allows cerebral adaptation, and the patients remain asymptomatic despite a serum sodium concentration below 120 mEq/L. Nonspecific symptoms like nausea, vomiting, gait disturbances, memory, cognitive problems, fatigue, dizziness, confusion, and muscle cramps can occur with chronic hyponatremia.

SIADH is critical to distinguish from Cerebral Salt Wasting Syndrome. Here an intra cranial disease leads to loss of sodium in urine resulting in hyponatremia but with “hypovolemia”.

Treatment guidelines in brief: -

The situation in SIADH is one of water retention and secondary solute (Na) loss. The more prominent thing is Sodium loss. The goal is maintaining sodium levels > 130 mEq/L by infusing saline.

• In severe sodium depletion <125 mEq/L of < 48 hrs duration or with severe symptoms (coma, seizures): start 3% saline at 1–2 ml/kg body weight/hr + furosemide 20 mg iv QID. Avoid rapid correction or overcorrection to reduce risk of osmotic demyelination. Check serum sodium every 2–4 hours and do not exceed infusing > 1 mEq/L per hour, or 8 mEq/L in 24 hrs.

• In severe sodium depletion <125 mEq/L of duration > 48 hours or unknown duration without severe symptoms: give normal saline infusion @ 100 ml/hr + furosemide 20 mg iv QID.

• Chronic or unknown duration and asymptomatic patient: fluid restriction with dietary salt and protein, and, if necessary, adjuvant drugs (demeclocycline, conivaptan etc).
