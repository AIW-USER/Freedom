## Question

A 67-year-old man, 6 years status post right total hip arthroplasty, falls while walking his dog. He complains of pain and is unable to bear weight on the right leg. He denies any hip or thigh pain before this fall. A radiograph is provided in Figure A. Intraoperatively, the femoral stem is noted to be stable. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

![[q3202-a-vancouver-b1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Traction for 3 weeks followed by 2 months of non-weight bearing mobilization
2. Open reduction and plate fixation with cable augmentation proximally
3. Revision arthroplasty with a cementless long stem bypassing the fracture site by two cortical diameters
4. Revision arthroplasty with cemented femoral stem bypassing the fracture site by two cortical diameters
5. Revision arthroplasty with cementless long stem bypassing the fracture site by two cortical diameters and allograft strut augmentation

## Correct option

**2. Open reduction and plate fixation with cable augmentation proximally**

## Answer explanation

This radiograph demonstrates an oblique diaphyseal fracture around the femoral stem. The stem is stable within the proximal segment, as noted intraoperatively, and is therefore classified as a Vancouver B1 fracture. The proper treatment for this injury is open reduction and lateral plate fixation with cable augmentation proximally (answer choice 2).

Periprosthetic femur fractures are becoming more common as the number of total hip arthroplasty (THA) procedures is increasing each year. The incidence of THA periprosthetic fractures is 0.1-3% for primary cementless THA. These periprosthetic femur fractures can be further classified into early (within 6 months) and late (typically 5 or more years postoperatively). Risk factors include poor bone quality, cementless prostheses, compromised bone stock, and revision procedures. Treatment is based upon the Vancouver classification system, with A fractures typically treated nonoperatively, B1 fractures treated with open reduction and lateral plate fixation, B2 fractures treated with revision of the femoral stem to a long porous-coated cementless stem and plate fixation spanning the fracture by 2 cortical diameters, B3 fractures treated with femoral component revision with proximal femoral allograft or proximal femoral replacement, and C fractures treated with open reduction and plate fixation while leaving the femoral and acetabular components alone.

*Ricci et al reviewed 50 Vancouver B1 patients treated with indirect reduction and fixation utilizing a lateral plate without allograft strut augmentation.* *All patients went on to union.* One was complicated by infection.

In an instructional course lecture, Ricci reviews the evaluation, classification, and management of femoral periprosthetic hip fractures.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of a right hip status post total hip arthroplasty with an oblique diaphyseal fracture around the femoral stem. Illustration A depicts the Vancouver Classification of femoral periprosthetic hip fractures.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer 1: This treatment is not typically utilized in patients with periprosthetic femur fractures. Early/immediate postoperative mobilization is the goal in these procedures.
Answer 3: This would be the treatment pursued if the stem was unstable and therefore, a Vancouver B2 fracture.
Answer 4: Vancouver B2 fractures are typically treated with revision of the femoral component to a long and cementless (not cemented), porous-coated stem. However, given the intraoperative stability of this stem, it is a Vancouver B1 fracture instead.
Answer 5: Revision of the femoral component with allograft strut augmentation is one of the preferred treatment options for Vancouver B3 fractures.
