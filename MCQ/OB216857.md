## Question

A 35-year-old male olympic power-lifter presents to your office after sustaining an elbow injury. He reports he was doing curls when he felt a pop and immediate pain in his elbow. On examination, he has distinct weakness with forearm supination and hook test is positive. Given his desire to continue power-lifting, you recommend surgical repair. During surgery, you perform a volar incision and find the interval between the brachioradialis and pronator teres muscles. You place a deep retractor between these muscles to maintain visualization, but immediately notice a flash of bright red blood in the surgical field. What structure has likely been injured?

## Options

1. Anterior interosseous artery
2. Inferior ulnar collateral artery
3. Recurrent interosseous artery
4. Recurrent radial artery
5. Ulnar artery

## Correct option

**4. Recurrent radial artery**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a distal biceps tendon rupture and during a volar approach, the recurrent radial artery is usually identified superficial to the biceps tuberosity and may need to be ligated.

Distal biceps ruptures are common injuries, particularly in activities involving heavy weight with eccentric muscle contraction. These injuries tend to result in weakness in forearm supination, and to a lesser degree, elbow flexion. Regardless of the fixation technique used (one- or two-incision), a volar approach to the biceps tuberosity is required for distal biceps repair. During this approach, the interval between the brachioradialis and pronator teres is utilized. Immediately deep to this interval the recurrent radial artery crosses the surgical field. The recurrent radial artery can be quite large and may bleed profusely, often necessitating ligation prior to fixation if it can not be safely retracted from the field.

*Eames et al. reviewed the anatomy of the distal biceps and surrounding structures.* **They noted** that often the biceps attachment includes separate attachments from the short and long heads, with the short head inserting distally on the radial tuberosity and the long head more proximally but further from the rotational axis. **They concluded** that this pattern allows the short head to be preferentially responsible for elbow flexion with the long head controlling supination.

*Zeltser et al. reviewed the vascular anatomy relevant to distal biceps repair.* **They noted** that the radial recurrent artery passed an average of 4mm proximal and 15mm superficial to the radial tuberosity. **They concluded** that there is some variability in the location and number of branches of the radial recurrent artery, but typically it is seen proximal and superficial to the radial tuberosity and must be identified during dissection.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The anterior interosseous artery branches from the ulnar artery and courses along the interosseous membrane.
Answer 2: The inferior ulnar collateral artery branches from the brachial artery over the medial elbow and should not be at risk during this dissection.
Answer 3: The recurrent interosseous artery branches from the posterior interosseous artery and is located over the posterior aspect of the forearm.
Answer 5: The ulnar artery runs medial in the forearm between the flexor digitorum superficialis and the flexor digitorum profundus muscle bellies and should not be at risk during this dissection.
