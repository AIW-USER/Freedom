## Question

A 57-year-old female presents to your office complaining of new onset medial knee pain. She says she was going up the stairs in her basement and felt a pop on the inside of her knee. On examination, she has pain primarily over the posteromedial aspect of her joint line. McMurray's sign is positive medially. Radiographs demonstrate no significant arthritic changes. A coronal MRI cut is seen in Figure A. Which of the following is true of the most likely pathology?

![[q218090-a-root-tear.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Healing rates are <75% with surgical re-attachment
2. Causes contact pressures to increase most in extension
3. Commonly associated with meniscal extrusion
4. Best repaired with vertical mattress suture configuration
5. Most commonly occurs in young males

## Correct option

**3. Commonly associated with meniscal extrusion**

## Answer explanation

Medial meniscal root tears are associated with meniscal extrusion, causing the meniscus to be functionally absent.

Meniscal root tears are becoming a more appreciated subset of meniscal tears and are important to recognize as management can be quite different. The importance of meniscal root tears lies in their anatomic and biomechanical function. Without an intact meniscal root, the meniscus is functionally absent and may extrude with any significant forces through the knee. The result of this is increased tibiofemoral contact pressures and a rapid acceleration of cartilage wear and arthritis. Meniscal root tears typically occur in middle-aged to older females and patients often report feeling a pop while in a position of loaded knee flexion. In patients who have relatively preserved joint space, a root repair is preferred in order to restore the knee biomechanics. If significant arthritis is already present, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may be considered.

*Strauss et al. reviewed the evaluation, treatment, and outcomes of meniscal root tears,* **noting** that they compromise the function of the meniscus more so than tears of the body. Untreated, these tears leave the meniscus unable to distribute loading forces and lead to increased tibiofemoral contact pressures. On MRI, signs of a root tear include radial tearing into the root, vertical defect in the root (ghost sign), meniscal extrusion >3mm, and lack of meniscal presence at the root (ghost sign). **They note** that healing rates have been reported to be 96% in some systematic reviews.

*Daney et al. looked at transtibial centralization suture techniques in order to minimize extrusion and restore tibiofemoral contact mechanics in root tears.* **They noted** that repairing the meniscal root +/- a centralization stitch on the posteromedial plateau restored contact mechanics and decreased meniscal extrusion compared to a root tear. They did note that in all cases, the degree of meniscal extrusion increased as the knee was loaded and flexed to 90°.

Figure A shows a coronal MRI sequence that demonstrates a medial meniscal root tear.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Healing rates after meniscal root repair are thought to be >90%.
Answer 2: Meniscal root tears cause contact pressures to increase most in flexion.
Answer 4: While peripheral meniscal body tears should be repaired with vertical mattress sutures, meniscal root tears should be anatomically anchored to re-establish the function and ability to diminish contact pressures.
Answer 5: Meniscal root tears are most common in middle-aged females.
