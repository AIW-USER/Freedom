## Question

In which of the following cases would a vascular exploration with a pediatric supracondylar humerus fracture be indicated?

## Options

1. A pulseless, well-perfused hand on presentation
2. A pulseless, poorly perfused hand on presentation
3. A pulseless, poorly perfused hand that is irreducible with anterior fracture gap intraoperatively
4. Presence of a "brachialis sign" on presentation
5. Presence of a "floating elbow" with compartment syndrome on presentation

## Correct option

**3. A pulseless, poorly perfused hand that is irreducible with anterior fracture gap intraoperatively**

## Answer explanation

Of the answer choices listed above, a vascular exploration would be most indicated in a patient who has a poorly perfused hand that is unable to be reduced with a persistent anterior fracture gap, suggesting vascular entrapment.

Neurovascular structures may become injured in the setting of a pediatric supracondylar humerus fracture. It is imperative that a thorough preoperative and intraoperative neurovascular examination be performed and documented. While the presence of a preoperative cool, white pulseless hand necessitates the timing of surgical intervention; this does not necessitate the need for vascular exploration as this may resolve following intraoperative closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP). Conversely, acute loss of perfusion in a previously well-perfused extremity following CRPP of a displaced supracondylar fracture is likely due to entrapment of the brachial artery in the fracture site, which may be evident intraoperatively by the presence of a fracture gap anteriorly suggesting entrapment of the brachial artery. The first steps in treatment in this clinical situation are to remove the fixation, re-displacement of the fracture, and reassessment of limb perfusion. Lastly, the presence of a pulseless, poorly perfused hand that remains irreducible with anterior fracture gapping intraoperatively is an indication for vascular exploration.

*Badkoobehi et al. reviewed pediatric supracondylar humeral fractures.* **They reported** that patients with a pulseless, poorly perfused hand require emergent operative reduction. **They concluded** that if the limb remains pulseless and poorly perfused after fracture fixation, vascular exploration and possible reconstruction is necessary.

*Weller et al. reviewed the management of patients with a pink hand but no palpable radial pulse in association with a supracondylar humeral fracture.* **They reported** that the lack of a palpable radial pulse after closed reduction and percutaneous pinning was not an absolute indication to proceed with vascular exploration if clinical findings (ie. doppler signal and capillary refill) suggested that the limb was perfused. **They concluded** that careful inpatient monitoring of these patients postoperatively is mandatory to identify late-developing vascular compromise.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The presence of a pulseless, well-perfused hand on presentation may be treated in an urgent but not emergent manner. Pulselessness may resolve following CRPP.
Answer 2: The presence of a pulseless, poorly perfused hand on presentation should be treated in an emergent manner. Pulselessness may resolve following CRPP.
Answer 4: The presence of a "brachialis sign" on presentation suggests that the proximal fragment has buttonholed through brachialis and implies a more serious injury with a higher likelihood of arterial injury, significant swelling, and a more difficult closed reduction. It may resolve with closed reduction.
Answer 5:The presence of a "floating elbow" with compartment syndrome on presentation involves an ipsilateral supracondylar humerus and forearm/wrist fractures. The presence of a compartment syndrome necessitates fasciotomies, but does not itself require vascular exploration.
