## Question

An orthopaedic adult reconstruction surgeon is performing a right primary total hip arthroplasty on an 81-year-old man through a direct anterior approach. Acetabular reaming is conducted sequentially to 51 mm, and he selects a 52 mm hemispherical, highly porous cup for impaction. During cup impaction, a fracture is noted on fluoroscopy to exit the anterior column. The fracture plane is palpated and is nondisplaced. The cup is manipulated and found to be well fixed, and the acetabular columns move together as a single unit. What is the next best step?

## Options

1. Removal of the 52 mm cup, selection of a 54 mm cup, and reimpaction
2. Placement of multiple screws through the currently placed cup, postoperative flat foot weightbearing restriction for two weeks
3. Removal of the cup, anterior intrapelvic approach, anterior column plating, followed by reimpaction of the 52 mm cup
4. Application of a cup-cage with a cemented polyethylene liner
5. Postoperative nonweightbearing restriction with bed rest for 6 weeks

## Correct option

**2. Placement of multiple screws through the currently placed cup, postoperative flat foot weightbearing restriction for two weeks**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained an intraoperative periprosthetic acetabulum fracture. Given the fracture is nondisplaced and the acetabular component is stable, the addition of multiple screws with or without weightbearing restrictions are sufficient in management (Answer 2).

Periprosthetic acetabulum fractures are rare complications of total hip arthroplasty that can occur both intraoperatively and postoperatively. They are severe problems that can compromise implant stability or survival. There are several classification systems available, most of which define implant stability and location. Intraoperative fractures that do not compromise implant stability may be treated conservatively with additional screws and postoperative weightbearing limitations. However, fractures that propagate and lead to acetabular discontinuity and compromise implant stability may require fixation with either column plating or the use of cup-cage constructs to allow for early implant stability and ingrowth.

*Siddiqi et al. review the management of periprosthetic acetabular fractures,* **highlighting** the importance of early detection and the consideration of patient and surgical risk factors for successful management. Acetabular fractures can sometimes be detected postoperatively, particularly when patients report persistent groin pain after surgery. Nondisplaced stable fractures, especially those that are acute traumatic, often maintain a stable pattern and can typically be managed with methodical evaluation and standard practice without the need for immediate surgical intervention.

*Siddiqi et al. perform an additional review on intraoperative acetabular fractures during primary total hip arthroplasty, which are relatively rare but can impact implant stability and longevity.* **They note** that nondisplaced fractures, when stable, are often managed conservatively, with the stability provided by the press-fit acetabular component facilitating healing. The review suggests that surgeons may impose postoperative weight-bearing restrictions and closely monitor the fracture healing through serial imaging. In cases of instability or acetabular discontinuity, use of multihole cups, cup-cage constructs, and other intraoperative techniques may be necessary to facilitate fracture healing and cup ingrowth.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Impaction of a larger cup is likely to propagate the fracture
Answer 3, 4: Given the fracture is nondisplaced and the acetabular component remains well fixed, the addition of increased exposure required to fix the fracture is unnecessary and would add undue morbidity to the procedure
Answer 5: Bed rest in a geriatric patient is likely to lead to significant morbidity, thus, immediate cup stability and early mobilization are critical in hip arthroplasty
