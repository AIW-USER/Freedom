## Question

A 49-year-old woman presents with a 7-month history of painless growth in her left thigh. Cross-sectional imaging is concerning for a primary soft tissue sarcoma, and a biopsy reveals findings shown in Figure A. You suspect a soft tissue sarcoma characterized by a translocation between chromosomes 12 and 16. She undergoes a wide resection and adjuvant radiation. At 12-month follow-up, she is asymptomatic, and CT chest-abdomen-pelvis is negative. What additional imaging studies should be obtained to assess for possible metastasis?

![[q220910-a-myxoid-liposarcoma-05.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Whole-spine MRI
2. MRI of the brain
3. Sentinel lymph node biopsy
4. Bone marrow biopsy
5. FDG-PET/CT

## Correct option

**1. Whole-spine MRI**

## Answer explanation

The histology and cytogenetics provided suggest a diagnosis of myxoid liposarcoma (MLS). These tumors demonstrate a characteristic propensity for extrapulmonary spread—particularly to the spinal axis—so MRI of the entire spine is recommended as an important part of staging and surveillance to assess for spinal metastases (answer choice 1).

Liposarcomas are heterogeneous sarcomas with adipocytic differentiation; myxoid tumors are typically low to intermediate grade and commonly arise in the extremities, especially the lower limb. Definitive diagnosis of MLS is made via biopsy, which characteristically demonstrates proliferating lipoblasts within a myxoid matrix; the most common genetic abnormality in MLS is a t(12;16) translocation (FUS–DDIT3 fusion oncoprotein; note, DDIT3 formerly referred to as CHOP). Because myxoid liposarcoma metastasizes not only to the lungs but also to the abdomen and spine, recommended systemic imaging includes CT of the chest/abdomen/pelvis plus MRI of the entire spine to evaluate for spinal metastases. Wide surgical resection and adjuvant radiotherapy are the mainstay of treatment for most liposarcomas, although chemotherapy may also be employed in cases of high-grade myxoid subtypes.

*Schwab and colleagues reviewed patients with myxoid liposarcoma who developed spinal metastases as confirmed on MRI.* **They found** bone scans and PET identified known spinal lesions poorly (16% and 14% sensitivity, respectively). **They concluded** that screening with whole-spine MRI may lead to earlier detection of spinal metastases, as many of these lesions would have been missed with bone scans or PET alone.

*Schwab et al. additionally reviewed the incidence of osseous metastases in myxoid liposarcoma and its correlation with clinicopathologic and molecular findings.* **They found** that 40 (17%) of 230 myxoid liposarcoma patients developed skeletal metastases, representing 56% of all metastatic events, often occurring early in the disease course, and primarily in high-grade tumors (78%) with a type II TLS-CHOP fusion transcript (84%). **They concluded** that myxoid liposarcoma has a high incidence of osseous metastases, particularly to the spine, and is often associated with the type II TLS-CHOP transcript, thus warranting spinal imaging for high-risk patients.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 2: MRI of the brain is not typically recommended for routine staging/surveillance; the spine is the characteristic extrapulmonary site warranting MRI in myxoid liposarcoma.
Answer choice 3: Although some series have found a propensity for lymphatic involvement by myxoid liposarcomas, sentinel lymph node biopsy is not typically employed as part of routine staging or surveillance.
Answer choice 4: Bone marrow biopsy is not a routine part of staging or surveillance for myxoid liposarcoma.
Answer choice 5: While sometimes used in sarcoma care, PET lacks sensitivity for detecting spinal metastases in myxoid liposarcoma and should not replace whole-spine MRI.
