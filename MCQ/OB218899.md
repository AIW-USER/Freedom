## Question

An orthopaedic surgery resident proposes doing a randomized clinical trial comparing open versus arthroscopic treatment for septic arthritis of the native knee. They perform a pilot study with 10 patients in each group and report their early findings using 95% confidence intervals. They found that arthroscopic treatment does not result in a significantly different hospital length of stay compared to open treatment. However, they proceed to include multiple other community hospitals in the same city and continue the study over the next two years with a final 100 patients in each group. Their final report published in a large journal states arthroscopic treatment results in a significantly shorter hospital length of stay compared to open treatment (Figure A) and formal comparative analysis shows a p-value of 0.04.

What is the most likely cause of the narrowed confidence intervals, and thus different results, in the final publication?

![[q218899-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Selection bias from the difference in patient populations
2. Increased variability in the results the final study
3. The researchers reported a 99% confidence interval in the final study rather than 95%
4. There was a larger sample size in the final study
5. A larger margin of error in the final publication

## Correct option

**4. There was a larger sample size in the final study**

## Answer explanation

Increasing the sample size results in a smaller margin of error, and thus narrower confidence interval (Answer 4).

Confidence interval = sample mean ± margin of error.

Confidence intervals (CI) are being increasingly utilized in lieu of p-values in the orthopaedic surgery literature because the CI conveys the actual magnitude of differences and the precision of measurement (range of 95% CIs). In this example, the 95% confidence intervals overlapped in the pilot study, denoting a possible lack of significant difference between both groups. As the researchers continued to recruit patients thus increasing sample size, the final confidence interval ranges narrowed, resulting in an apparent significant difference in hospital length of stay between both groups. The larger sample size reduces the variability, decreases the margin for error, and ultimately results in a narrow/tighter confidence interval. Very importantly, confidence intervals do NOT denote statistical significance (i.e. if the confidence intervals do not overlap, it does not necessarily mean they are significantly different). A formal comparative analysis test (i.e. T-test, ANOVA, etc) would be required.

*Vavken and colleagues reviewed the use of confidence intervals in reporting orthopaedic research findings.* **They discuss** that statistical significance can only suggest whether there is a difference (p-values), where clinical importance is better described by asking how much of a difference there is (confidence intervals) and if it matters. They assessed the reporting of confidence intervals in orthopaedic literature and found only one in five articles reported confidence intervals.

*Strage and colleagues provide a review of appropriate data reporting on orthopaedic surgery research.* **They emphasize** that given a lack of guidelines for reporting results, there are many inappropriately reported findings leading to misinterpretation from readers. **They conclude** that orthopaedic surgery research needs to focus on presenting data that highlights clinical relevance rather than just focusing on the statistical significance seen in p-values.

*Greenland and colleagues provided a review of basic biostatistical foundational knowledge to prevent interpretive errors.* *The authors provide an explanatory list of 25 common misinterpretations of p-values, confidence intervals, and power.* **They conclude** by emphasizing the importance of understanding what the p-value really means, and how to appropriately report and interpret its value.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: It is important to understand that a difference in patient populations certainly could lead to different results in many circumstances. This is especially important when drawing large conclusions from single-center studies, because their patient population may be different than your own. Multi-centered trials help minimize this form of selection bias. In this example, only community hospitals from a single city were included, which minimizes the chance of differences in the patient population.
Answer 2: One can rationally expect less variability of results with a higher sample size. This is one of the many reasons why studies should be adequately powered a priori (before starting the study). The variability in results will be high when you’re only using 10 patients per group, and thus it’s hard to extrapolate conclusions toward the general population based on that small sample. However, having 100 patients per group decreases the variability in results and makes the outcome more generalizable.
Answer 3: If an author reports a more confident/higher confidence interval (using 99% rather than the original 95%), one expects a WIDER-reported interval range. It’s like getting pulled over for going 65mph in a 60mph speed limit zone. You are 95% confident you were only going 60mph, but 100% confident you were going under 100mph. The increased confidence widens the reported values range.
Answer 5: As discussed, increasing the sample size helps to narrow the margin of error as shown in this example's final publication compared to the pilot study.
