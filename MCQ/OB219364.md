## Question

A 15-year-old female presents to your clinic with a chief complaint of ankle and foot pain. She is an avid ballet dancer and notes that her pain has slowly progressed over the past few months. A select image from her MRI, which was ordered by her primary care provider, is available for review in Figure A. The patient has pain with passive motion of the great toe and also has fullness about the posterior ankle. Which of the following accurately describes the origin of the muscle-tendon unit implicated in this patient's pathology?

![[q219364-a-fhlt.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Anterior surface of the fibula and the adjacent interosseous membrane
2. Cuboid and lateral cuneiform
3. Head of the fibula, upper 1/2 - 2/3 of lateral fibular shaft surface; also anterior and posterior intermuscular septa of leg
4. Inferior 2/3 of lateral fibular surface; also anterior and posterior intermuscular septa of leg
5. Inferior 2/3 of posterior surface of fibula, lower part of interosseous membrane

## Correct option

**5. Inferior 2/3 of posterior surface of fibula, lower part of interosseous membrane**

## Answer explanation

This young gymnast has ankle pain which is reproduced with passive motion of the great toe and her imaging demonstrates extensive fluid in the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon sheath, consistent with the diagnosis of FHL tenosynovitis/tendonitis. The origin of the FHL is the inferior 2/3 of the posterior surface of the fibula and lower part of the interosseous membrane and it inserts on the plantar surface of the base of the distal phalanx of the great toe (Answer 5).

FHL tendonitis/tenosynovitis occurs as a result of impingement of the FHL tendon along the posterior ankle joint. This pathology is more common in patients who perform activities that require excessive ankle plantar flexion, i.e. ballet dancers in the on pointe position, or gymnasts. The impingement typically occurs within the fibro-osseous tunnel of the posteromedial ankle, but it can also occur secondary to an os trigonum.

Patients will typically complain of posteromedial ankle pain and may note locking and/or crepitus of the great toe with range of motion. Patients may also have palpable crepitus along the posteromedial ankle with ankle and/or great toe range of motion. On physical exam, patients will have pain with resisted flexion of the great toe IP joint and with forced plantarflexion of the ankle. The initial treatment for most patients is nonoperative, with a focus on rest, activity modification, and oral NSAIDs. Patients with symptoms recalcitrant to nonoperative measures are typically treated with FHL release, tenosynovectomy, and possible tendon repair.

*Vosseller et al. provide a review of ankle injuries in dancers.* **The authors note** that orthopedic injuries in dancers are common, and typically involve the foot and ankle. **The authors highlight** that FHL tenosynovitis typically occurs secondary to the unique demands of certain styles of dance i.e. ballet and the on-pointe position. The authors do note that high-level dancers may experience symptoms recalcitrant to nonoperative management, and often require surgical intervention.

*Sammarco and Cooper investigated FHL injuries in 26 patients (31 cases) over a 16-year period.* *The authors split their cohort into two groups: those who sustained injury during dance and those who sustained injury secondary to other causes.* *Overall, 27/31 cases (87.1%) required surgery.* **The authors found** that dancers tended to have symptoms for a longer period before seeking treatment, but that this population often responded favorably to surgical intervention if needed.

Figure A is a T2-weighted axial MRI image at the level of the ankle, which demonstrates fluid within the FHL tendon sheath. Illustration A is a labeled version of Figure A.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the extensor hallucis longus (EHL), inflammation of which does not typically cause posterior ankle pain.
Answer 2: This describes flexor hallucis brevis (FHB), which does not cross the ankle joint and causes posterior ankle pain.
Answers 3 and 4: These describe peroneus longus (PL) and peroneus brevis (PB), respectively, injury to which can cause subfibular pain that is exacerbated with eversion and dorsiflexion.
