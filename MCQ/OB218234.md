## Question

An unhealthy 65-year-old patient presents to the ED after being involved in an accident in which they fell 3 feet from a front porch. At this time their vital signs are stable. They do however, endorse loss of consciousness on scene after hitting their head. They report significant pain in the right lower extremity. On exam, there is sluggish cap refill to the right lower extremity. The patient is able to plantarflex and dorsiflex their ankle with moderate pain. You measure an ankle-brachial index (ABI) of 1.1. Which of the following is the best treatment plan for this patient?

## Options

1. Emergent consultation with vascular surgery and a possible arteriogram
2. Emergency room ultrasound consultation to evaluate femoral vasculature
3. Apply skin traction until the patient is optimized for surgery
4. Open reduction and internal fixation of the fracture with vascular exploration
5. Revision of the femoral component with open reduction internal fixation

## Correct option

**5. Revision of the femoral component with open reduction internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

Vancouver B2 fractures are best treated with revision of the femoral component with open reduction internal fixation (ORIF). In this case, the ABI is normal and should be monitored with serial exams.

Periprosthetic femur fractures are most commonly classified using the Vancouver classification, which not only eases communication but also guides treatment. This patient has a fracture around the stem resulting in a loose component with good remaining bone stock consistent with a Vancouver B2 (Illustration A). While the patient may have sluggish cap refill from arterial spasm or other etiology, the ABI is normal (0.9 - 1.3) and can be monitored using serial exams. If the ABI were abnormal or there was other concern for vascular injury further imaging or a vascular surgery consult may be warranted.

*Maurer et al review current methods in the evaluation and management of periprosthetic hip fractures.* **They report** that B2 and B3 fractures should be treated with stem revision as the femoral component is loose. **They conclude** that differentiation between B1 and B2 fractures may be difficult at times and that advanced imaging may help with preoperative planning and determining implant stability.

*Khan et al review the treatment of Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures.* **They report** that patients with B2 fractures treated with ORIF alone were more likely to undergo re-operation compared to those treated with stem revision. **They conclude** that much care should be taken in evaluating stem fixation in the setting of periprosthetic fractures.

Figure A demonstrates a Vancouver B2 fracture with a loose femoral stem and adequate bone stock. Illustration A outlines the Vancouver classification.

Incorrect Answer:
Answer 1: Further workup of sluggish cap refill can be done with serial exams in the setting of a normal ABI. No vascular surgery consult is needed at this time.
Answer 2: Ultrasound is not the preferred method for evaluating arterial injury in the thigh. Further, this patient has a normal ABI, making vascular injury unlikely.
Answer 3: While traction may be helpful for severely shortened fractures, this patient has an intact lateral cortex and is not severely shortened. Secondly, skin traction should not be left in place for an extended period of time.
Answer 4: This patient has a Vancouver B2 fracture with a loose prothesis. ORIF without revision of the femoral component is not recommended in this scenario. Vascular exploration is also not necessary in a patient with a normal ABI.
