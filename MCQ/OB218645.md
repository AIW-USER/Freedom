## Question

A 58-year-old nurse comes to the clinic because she is unable to wear her normal footwear to work and has pain along the medial forefoot with prolonged standing. She has tried numerous activity modifications, shoe insoles, and anti-inflammatories, but is no longer able to stand the deformity of her great toe, which is pictured in Figure A. Her surgeon recommends a biplanar chevron osteotomy and a modified McBride procedure. Which of the following best characterizes the deformity that the treating surgeon is attempting to correct?

![[q218645-a-screenshot-2023-06-28-at-6-34-39-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Hallux Valgus Angle (HVA) 12°, Intermetatarsal angle (IMA) 7°, Distal Metatarsal Articular Angle (DMAA) 5°
2. HVA 20°, IMA 12°, DMAA 6°
3. HVA 20°, IMA 13°, DMAA 12°
4. HVA 35°, IMA 15°, DMAA 9°
5. HVA 50°, IMA 18°, DMAA 12°

## Correct option

**3. HVA 20°, IMA 13°, DMAA 12°**

## Answer explanation

The patient has symptomatic, mild hallux valgus, most commonly characterized by an HVA < 25° and an IMA < 13°, indicating her for a distal metatarsal (chevron) osteotomy with a combined soft tissue procedure (modified McBride). Given that the surgeon is electing to perform a biplanar Chevron osteotomy, her DMAA is likely also elevated > 10°.

Hallux valgus, colloquially referred to as a "bunion," represents a complex, acquired deformity that results in medial great toe pain and difficulty with shoe wear. Affecting up to 23% of the adult US population, the condition has a distinct set of criteria that helps to guide operative management, compared to the baseline normal measurements of HVA, IMA, and DMAA, with normal values being < 15°, 9°, and 10° respectively (Illustration A). For mild deformities, for those with an HVA < 25° and an IMA < 13°, such as in the patient in question, a distal chevron osteotomy provides a lateral, displacing correction of the medially translated distal metatarsal head. If the DMAA is also elevated, indicating medial incongruence of the articular surface, a third cut is added to the typical chevron osteotomy to allow for the removal of a medial wedge of bone, resulting in a closing wedge correction in the horizontal plane, thereby decreasing the DMAA (Illustration B). Further incongruence of the proximal phalanx on the articular surface (i.e., lateral displacement of the proximal phalanx base on the metatarsal head) is addressed with the modified McBride soft tissue procedure, which involves a combination of adductor release from the lateral sesamoid/proximal phalanx, lateral capsulotomy, and medial capsular imbrication. For more severe deformities with an HVA > 25° and IMA > 13°, a proximal metatarsal shaft osteotomy is typically added to the chevron and modified McBride procedures (i.e., scarf osteotomy). In patients with first metatarsophalangeal joint arthritis or hypermobility, a fusion or Lapidus procedure is often indicated (Illustration C).

Shi, et al. provide a comprehensive review of the operative approaches to adult hallux valgus deformity. The authors note that, in general, isolated soft tissue procedures are rarely applicable, operations such as osteotomies that preserve joint function are desirable, and more severe deformities benefit from surgical treatment at a more proximal level. For patients with symptomatic mild deformity without arthritis, they conclude by recommending a distal chevron osteotomy transfixed with a single screw or 2-mm absorbable pin, with the release of only the lateral capsular tissue and not the adductor hallucis.

Kaufmann, et al. reviewed 524 patients who underwent correction of hallux valgus by means of a distal chevron osteotomy to assess the degree of correction loss as a function of the severity of the pre-operative deformity. The authors found that a chevron osteotomy showed significant correction of HVA, IMA, and DMAA, but that the degree of pre-operative deformity, in terms of IMA, HVA, DMAA, and sesamoid position, correlated with the loss of correction, which persisted at 3-month follow-up. To prevent recurrence, they concluded that post-operative IMA should be < 10°, and they do not recommend the application of distal chevron osteotomy for hallux valgus in cases exceeding an IMA of 15.5 degrees preoperatively.

Figure A is a clinical picture demonstrating a bunion. Illustration A demonstrates how to radiographically measure the HVA, IMA, and DMAA on weight-bearing AP radiographs of the foot. The HVA is measured as the angle intersecting the longitudinal axes of the first metatarsal and the proximal phalanx; the IMA is measured as the angle intersecting the longitudinal axes of the first and second metatarsals; and the DMAA is the angle subtended by the longitudinal axis of the first metatarsal and the line perpendicular to a parallel line drawn to the distal articular surface of the first metatarsal. Illustration B depicts the biplanar chevron osteotomy. Illustration C graphically depicts the different treatment options mentioned above: (a) chevron osteotomy (b) scarf osteotomy (c) proximal osteotomy (d) Lapidus procedure.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: These represent normal values for HVA, IMA, and DMAA.
Answer 2: Given the normal DMAA in this patient, a typical (not biplanar) chevron osteotomy with or without a soft tissue procedure would be indicated.
Answers 4 and 5: These patients have moderate to severe deformities and require the addition of a more proximal osteotomy versus a fusion procedure, depending on the presence of arthritis or hypermobility at the first MTPJ.
