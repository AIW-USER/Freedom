## Question

A 55-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has the ulceration seen in Figure A. His BMI is 36, HbA1c is 8.1, albumin is 3.8, and the WBC count is 6,500. His foot is plantigrade without apparent deformity. There is no evidence of infection. Examination reveals ankle dorsiflexion 10 degrees shy of neutral with the knee extended that does not improve with knee flexion. Despite shoe wear modification, orthotics, local wound care, and 3 months of total contact casting, the ulcer has not healed. Which of the following interventions would most improve the likelihood of ulcer healing?

![[q215102-a-screen-shot-2019-01-05-at-3-32-56-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Continued total contact cast for an additional 3 months
2. Debridement of the ulcer to bleeding edges
3. Negative-pressure wound therapy
4. First metatarsal head excision
5. Achilles lengthening

## Correct option

**5. Achilles lengthening**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a chronic forefoot ulcer despite appropriate non-operative management. He would benefit from Achilles lengthening to offload the forefoot and promote healing.

Diabetic foot ulcers are a commonly encountered problem for foot and ankle surgeons. They are due to a combination of a loss of protective sensation from neuropathy and increased pressure over the area of ulceration. In the absence of infection, management consists of footwear modifications to offload the area of ulceration and local wound care. Total contact casting is often indicated for chronic ulcers. There is growing evidence that, when utilized for chronic ulceration, Achilles tendon lengthening or gastrocnemius recession results in an improved rate of healing and lower recurrence rate by offloading the forefoot. The Silfverskiöld test can be used to differentiate between Achilles or isolated gastrocnemius contracture. Improved ankle dorsiflexion with knee flexion is indicated of isolated gastrocnemius tightness whereas equivalent ankle dorsiflexion with knee flexion and extension is indicative of achilles tightness.

*Laborde (2009) studied 11 diabetic patients with midfoot plantar ulcers and treated them with gastrocnemius-soleus recession without any other bony procedure.* **He reports** that 10/11 ulcers healed at an average of 39 months follow up with only one recurrence. **He concludes** that the gastrocnemius-soleus recession as a primary treatment of diabetic midfoot ulcers is a low-risk method of promoting ulcer resolution.

*Laborde (2008) also reviewed 20 patients with forefoot ulcers treated primarily with a gastrocnemius-soleus recession.* *At the 45-month follow-up,* **he reported** healing in 19/20 patients with 3 of the patients having recurrence. In his protocol, patients underwent peroneus longus lengthening or tibialis posterior lengthening if the ulcerations were under the 1st MT head or 5th MT head, respectively.

Figure A shows the plantar aspect of a left foot with a Grade II (Wagner classification) ulceration under the 1st metatarsal head.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient has already failed total contact casting for 3 months and not indicated for any additional casting given the absence of healing.
Answer 2: Debridement of the ulcer without offloading would not lead to a higher chance of ulcer healing.
Answer 3: Using negative pressure wound therapy without offloading would not lead to a higher chance of ulcer healing.
Answer 4: Achilles recession is much less morbid and more effective procedure to offload his forefoot ulcer compared to metatarsal head excision.
