## Question

A 42-year-old female presents to the emergency department with progressively worsening right hip pain. She underwent a total hip replacement (THA) in Mexico 20 years ago and had been doing well except for the past 2 years. She noticed a progressively altered leaning gait which she states is considerably tiring and activity-limiting. She felt a pop in her hip this morning and has not been able to ambulate since. Her presenting images are shown in Figure A. She undergoes revision THA and after debridement of devitalized tissue shown in Figure B. The surgeon plans to revise the femoral stem due to a visibly worn trunnion. She is also found to have posterior capsular insufficiency and a chronic, irreparable gluteus medius tendon tear. Which of the following treatment options would address both of these concerns?

## Options

1. Placement of a constrained liner
2. Conversion to proximal femoral replacement with direct gluteus medius repair to the prosthesis
3. Gluteus maximus split transfer
4. Revise the acetabular cup to a more medialized position
5. Place a high offset femoral stem with bipolar head and dual mobility liner

## Correct option

**3. Gluteus maximus split transfer**

## Answer explanation

A split portion of the gluteus maximus muscle can be transferred to the anterior hip capsule and greater trochanter to treat both capsular deficiency and abductor insufficiency, respectively.

Clinically significant trunnionosis is uncommon and results from mechanically assisted crevice corrosion (MACC; combined fretting/crevice) at the head-neck junction of hip arthroplasty implants. In severe cases, trunnionosis can lead to pseudotumor formation and catastrophic implant failure requiring revision surgery, as shown in this case. The extensive soft tissue damage from the body’s response to metal debris (Figure B) requires aggressive debridement and often results in capsular insufficiency. When combined with a longstanding Trendelenburg gait (from an irreparable gluteus medius tear), a multimodal-purposed muscle transfer is required. The gluteus maximus is a colossal muscle in which multiple limb flaps can be separated and transferred without significant sequala. Single limb flaps can be utilized for capsular deficiency OR abductor insufficiency (Illustration A), however, there have been additionally described techniques with two-limb transfers to treat both conditions (Illustration B). These can be combined with tensor fascia lata transfer for a more robust abductor mechanism reconstruction (Illustration C). These versatile flaps can help address posterior capsular insufficiency and abductor insufficiency (i.e. Trendelenburg gait).

*Whiteside and colleagues (2006, Illustration A) performed a retrospective analysis of a small cohort with greater trochanteric avulsion-escape and Trendeleburg gait treated with single-limb gluteus maximus reconstruction.* **They found** that the flap group patients had less pain, lower incidence of limb and Trendelenburg sign, and less need for supportive devices than patients treated with excision of the greater trochanter alone. **They concluded** that the muscle transfer was effective in closing the posterior aspect of the hip and also restored much of the abduction function.

Whiteside (2012, Illustration B) later described their surgical technique to transfer a two-limb portion of the gluteus maximus muscle for abductor deficiency of the hip. One limb is sutured to the anterior capsule to substitute for the gluteus minimus and capsule, whereas the second limb is transferred to the greater trochanter to substitute for the gluteus medius. They performed this procedure on 11 patients with a high success rate and restoration of the abduction functionality.

Whiteside (2014, Illustration C) also described their combined gluteus maximus and tensor fascia lata muscle transfers for the treatment of hip abductor insufficiency. They first utilized 10 cadaveric models for proof of concept, evaluating the feasibility, and ensuring procedure safety. This was followed by application on five adult patients. In their small series with 1-year follow-up, the surgical procedure was deemed reproducible and effective. They concluded with a call for increased patient inclusion and long-term follow-up.

Figure B displays an intraoperative image with the black discoloration of surrounding soft tissue consistent with an adverse reaction to metal debris. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1. The placement of a constrained liner would be appropriate in the setting of instability. Capsular insufficiency and abductor failure may collectively result in hip instability; however, the constrained liner alone would not address her gluteus medius tear and Trendelenburg gait.
Answer 2. Conversion to proximal femoral replacement is an appropriate option in the revision THA setting when there is inadequate proximal femoral bone stock. This patient’s x-ray is not suggestive of such concern. If the metaphyseal mantle seems inadequate, she could be converted to a diaphyseal engaging stem femoral implant, not a proximal femoral replacement. Further, a chronically torn gluteus medius is likely retracted and scarred with low healing potential and a high likelihood of repair failure.
Answer 4. Revising the acetabular cup is prudent in the setting of instability with a misaligned prior placed acetabular cup. Based on her x-ray and CT scan alone, her prior acetabular cup appears to have an appropriate amount of anteversion and abduction. Revising the cup to a medialized position would decrease the offset and thereby worsen her abductor insufficiency gait pattern.
Answer 5. Increasing the femoral offset and placing a dual mobility implant would serve to improve the biomechanics of the hip and address instability. However, these options would not address the chronic gluteus medius tear and Trendelenburg gait.
