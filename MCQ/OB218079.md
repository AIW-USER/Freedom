## Question

A 54-year-old male presents to your clinic with several months of shoulder pain. He has a history of right shoulder hemiarthroplasty that was done 4 years ago after a 4-part proximal humerus nonunion and had been functioning well until recently. He denies any specific recent trauma. On examination, he is well-appearing and has no erythema about the shoulder. He has 50° of active forward flexion, 30° of abduction, and 30° of external rotation with the arm adducted. Passive range of motion is near normal without crepitus. He does have pain over the lateral deltoid region but his primary complaint is weakness and dysfunction. A radiograph is seen in Figure A. You recommend revision to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. What is the most likely reason for the failure of his hemiarthroplasty?

![[q218079-a-shoulder-hemi-cuff-tear.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Deep infection
2. Advanced glenoid wear
3. Acromial stress fracture
4. Implant loosening
5. Rotator cuff failure

## Correct option

**5. Rotator cuff failure**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a shoulder that is pseudoparalytic after prior hemiarthroplasty (HA), with rotator cuff insufficiency serving as the reason for conversion to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty functions by moving the shoulder's center of rotation medially and inferiorly and relying on the lengthened deltoid lever arm to power the arm. It is useful in many settings and may be a viable option after failed HA or anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty. In cases where patients have pseduoparalysis on exam, meaning they are unable to actively elevate the arm, rTSA can help restore shoulder function effectively. Radiographs are also helpful in these cases to understand the degree of superior escape, any acetabularization of the acromion, and the degree of glenoid wear that may be present prior to utilization of rTSA.

*Sajadi et al. reviewed indications and outcomes of revision shoulder arthroplasty in 35 patients between 1987 and 2005.* **They noted** that outcomes after revision arthroplasty depended heavily on the reason for revision. **They concluded** that those who failed due to glenoid erosion or loosening regained more motion post-operatively compared to those who failed initial surgeries due to infection, soft tissue problems, or undetermined pain.

*Sheth et al. looked at the revision of failed shoulder HA and anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty to rTSA,* **noting** implant survival was 92% at 2 years and 74% at 5 years. **They found** that 70% were "very satisfied" or "satisfied" with their outcome, leading them to call results "modest," in these cases. **They concluded** that complications occurred in 20% of patients and those patients were less satisfied with their outcomes.

Figure A is the AP radiograph of a right shoulder demonstrating a well-fixed, cemented hemiarthroplasty with evidence of superior escape representative of rotator cuff insufficiency.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While deep infection may present with diminished range of motion, passive range of motion would also be affected. In this case, there were no other signs of infection.
Answer 2: While glenoid wear is possible after shoulder HA, this patient's presentation is more representative of rotator cuff tearing with pseudoparalysis, lateral arm pain, and no crepitus with passive range of motion.
Answer 3: Acromial stress fractures more commonly occur in the setting of rTSA as the lever arm of the deltoid is lengthened, placing more stress through the acromion.
Answer 4: Humeral stem loosening in shoulder arthroplasty is most commonly linked to infection and there are no signs of radiographic loosening.
