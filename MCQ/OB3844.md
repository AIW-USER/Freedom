## Question

A 21-year-old patient with polio presents with a flexible equinocavovarus foot deformity. She has exhausted conservative management and wants to undergo surgery. Which of the options below would best address the deformity and weakness around her hindfoot and midfoot (assuming forefoot deformities will also be addressed at the time of surgery)?

## Options

1. Triple arthrodesis
2. Subtalar arthrodesis, medial cuneiform osteotomy
3. Medializing calcaneal osteotomy, flexor digitorum longus tendon transfer
4. Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy, tibialis anterior tendon transfer
5. Achilles tendon lenghtening, tibialis posterior tendon transfer, plantar fascia release

## Correct option

**5. Achilles tendon lenghtening, tibialis posterior tendon transfer, plantar fascia release**

## Answer explanation

Polio most commonly causes an equinocavovarus foot deformity. The only option that would address this deformity would be achilles tendon lengthening, tibialis posterior tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot, and plantar fascia release.

Patients with polio classically present with limited ankle dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior weakness), cavus foot, claw toes, and an equinus deformity. In flexible deformities, bone deformity usually does not exist. Only muscle imbalance and soft tissue contracture result in the deformity. Tibialis posterior tendon transfer to the dorsum of the foot will improve ankle dorsiflexion, plantar fascia release will help address the foot cavus, flexor digitorum longus to extensor digitorum longus transfer of the lesser toes will address the claw toe deformity, and achilles lengthening will address the equinus deformity.

*Younger et al. reviewed management of the adult cavovarus foot.* **They state** that hindfoot equinus is usually measured using the calcaneal pitch angle on radiographs and measuring the tight heel cord clinically. The tight heel cord is then addressed with either a gastrocnemius recession or heel cord lengthening. A weight-bearing lateral radiograph of the ankle may illustrate impinging anterior osteophytes.

*Georgiadis et al. published a review on cavovarus foot in hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies.* **They state** that cavovarus foot deformity comprises a spectrum of anatomical and clinical deformities, and treatment must be individualized to include combinations of soft-tissue releases, osteotomies, tendon transfers, and salvage procedures. **They recommend** that joint-sparing procedures should be performed on skeletally immature patients whenever possible, although arthrodesis has been reported to have good long-term results.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: As a rule of thumb, fixed equinocavovarus foot deformities are best treated with osteotomies and arthrodesis. In a young patient with flexible deformities, one should consider using soft-tissue procedures first.
Answer 3: Medializing calcaneal osteotomy, flexor digitorum longus tendon transfer would be considered for tibialis posterior insufficiency.
Answer 4: Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy, tibialis anterior tendon transfer would not be appropriate. The tibialis anterior is usually affected (weak) and transfer of the tendon would not improve symptoms of foot-drop.
