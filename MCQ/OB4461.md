## Question

A 56-year-old male with a history of prostate cancer presents to your clinic complaining of bilateral foot pain (right greater than left) that he relates to his chronic bunion and claw toe deformities. He denies new trauma, fevers, or weight loss. He says that he is able to ambulate but has had progressively increased pain in the great and lesser toes of both feet, with progressive difficulty in wearing shoes. During his radiographic evaluation a lesion is found in the right tibia which is shown in Figure A. What is the next most appropriate step in management of the tibial lesion?

## Options

1. Core needle biopsy of the right tibia
2. Excisional biopsy of the right tibia
3. MRI of the right tibia
4. Reassurance and repeat radiographs in 3-6 months
5. Staging with CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, and referral to an orthopaedic oncologist

## Correct option

**4. Reassurance and repeat radiographs in 3-6 months**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with an incidental finding of an enchondroma in a patient with a history of prostate cancer and great and lesser toe deformities. Reassurance and repeat radiographs in 3-6 months is the most appropriate treatment.

Enchondromas are benign, chondrogenic tumors composed of hyaline cartilage most often located in the medullary cavity of bones of the hand (60%), distal femur, proximal humerus, and tibia. Most often, lesions are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally on radiographs; treatment includes observation and serial radiographs. Approximately 1% of enchondromas undergo malignant transformation into chondrosarcomas.

*Weiner et al.* **identified** concerning radiographic findings in patients with enchondromas including lucency developing within the calcification, periosteal reaction, cortical thickening, and endosteal erosion and scalloping greater than 50% of the cortex. **They recommend** such findings would warrant referral to an orthopaedic oncologist and likely advanced imaging with biopsy.

*Marco et al. report that enchondromas can resemble intramedullary low-grade chondrosarcomas.* **They note** that, as opposed to enchondromas, low-grade chondrosarcomas are typically painful, larger (>5cm), and associated with cortical endosteal scalloping and lysis.

Rougraff provides a Level V review of incidental bone lesions. Again noted is the importance of recognizing incidental lesions and differentiating those from more concerning lesions based on imaging findings, history and physical examination.

Figure A is an AP and lateral radiographs of the tibia-fibula demonstrating a benign-appearing, well-defined enchondroma in the distal third of the tibial diaphysis; clearly visible is the calcified “popcorn stippled” appearance of the lesion with essentially no endosteal disruption or erosion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3 are not needed for a painless, asymptomatic enchondroma.
Answer 5: Advanced imaging and referral to an orthopaedic oncologist are warranted in cases of symptom development, increased pain, growth of the lesion, or worrisome radiographic findings such as endosteal erosion or cortical scalloping >50% of the cortex.
