Chapter: Orthopedics Oncology MCQs — Giant Cell Tumor

## Question

Regarding the cellular pathogenesis of Giant Cell Tumor (GCT) of bone and the specific mechanism of action of Denosumab, which of the following statements is accurate?

## Options

1. Denosumab directly targets and induces apoptosis in the neoplastic, monoclonal spindle-like stromal cells that harbor the H3.3 G34W mutation.
2. By binding to soluble and transmembrane RANKL, Denosumab inhibits the maturation and survival of osteoclast-like giant cells, leaving the neoplastic stromal components intact.
3. The depletion of osteoclastic giant cells via Denosumab completely halts the proliferation of the H3F3A-mutated stromal cell population.
4. Denosumab acts as a competitive antagonist at the OPG (Osteoprotegerin) receptor site, accelerating host bone mineral density recovery.

## Correct option

**2. By binding to soluble and transmembrane RANKL, Denosumab inhibits the maturation and survival of osteoclast-like giant cells, leaving the neoplastic stromal components intact.**

## Answer explanation

Denosumab functions as a fully human monoclonal antibody against RANKL, effectively eliminating the osteoclast-like giant cells responsible for bone resorption while the underlying neoplastic stromal cell clone persists.

Mechanism of Action of denosumab in GCT

Denosumab is a fully human IgG2 monoclonal antibody that binds with high affinity and specificity to RANKL (Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B Ligand), preventing it from binding to its receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors. By neutralising RANKL, denosumab directly targets the fundamental molecular mechanism driving GCT pathology — the RANKL-mediated recruitment, fusion, and activation of the osteoclast-like giant cells that are responsible for the aggressive bone resorption characteristic of the tumour.

The effects of denosumab on GCT are profound — within weeks of initiation, there is a dramatic reduction in osteoclast-like giant cells (from abundant to virtually absent on histology), a marked decrease in the mononuclear stromal cell population, cessation of bone resorption, and deposition of new woven bone within and around the tumour. Clinically, this translates to rapid pain relief, tumour shrinkage (or at minimum, growth arrest), re-ossification of lytic areas, and reconstitution of cortical integrity (previously destroyed cortex may reform). On imaging, the lytic lesion develops a peripheral rim of new bone and progressive internal ossification, transforming from an aggressive, expansile lytic lesion to a contained, sclerotic, well-defined lesion.

Indications in Appendicular GCT

Neoadjuvant (Preoperative) Denosumab:

This is the most established and impactful role of denosumab in appendicular GCT. Denosumab administered before surgery produces tumour downsizing, re-ossification, and reformation of cortical boundaries, which may:

Convert an unresectable or marginally resectable tumour into a resectable one — large, destructive Campanacci Grade III lesions with extensive soft tissue extension and cortical destruction that would otherwise require en bloc resection (and the attendant morbidity of megaprosthetic reconstruction) may consolidate sufficiently with denosumab to allow downgrading to extended curettage, preserving the native joint and bone stock.

Facilitate safer and more complete curettage — the reconstituted cortical shell and peripheral ossification create a well-defined boundary between tumour and normal bone, making intraoperative identification of the tumour margin easier and reducing the risk of inadvertent cortical breach during curettage. The firmer, ossified tumour is also easier to curette mechanically.

Preserve the subchondral bone and articular surface — when GCT extends to within millimetres of the articular surface, neoadjuvant denosumab promotes new bone formation in the subchondral region, increasing the margin between the tumour and the joint and reducing the risk of articular surface violation during curettage.

However, a significant concern with neoadjuvant denosumab is that it alters the histological interface between tumour and normal bone — instead of a soft, friable tumour that is easily distinguishable from normal bone during curettage, denosumab-treated tumour becomes densely ossified and merges with the surrounding reactive bone, making it difficult to identify the tumour boundary intraoperatively. This may paradoxically increase the risk of incomplete curettage (leaving viable stromal cells behind the ossified front) and has been associated with higher recurrence rates in some studies (20–30% after denosumab-facilitated curettage versus 10–15% without neoadjuvant denosumab in certain series). The stromal cells (the neoplastic population) are not killed by denosumab — they are merely quiescent, and they retain the H3.3 G34W mutation and the capacity to resume RANKL production and tumour growth once denosumab is discontinued.

Unresectable or Recurrent GCT:

For GCT in surgically challenging locations where resection would produce unacceptable morbidity (sacrum, spine, pelvis) or for multiply recurrent disease, denosumab may be used as a definitive long-term treatment to control disease activity. In these cases, denosumab is administered continuously for an extended or indefinite duration, maintaining disease control through sustained RANKL inhibition. However, disease recurrence upon discontinuation is a significant concern — reported in up to 20–60% of cases after stopping denosumab, often within 6–15 months. Rebound osteolysis (aggressive bone resorption exceeding the pre-treatment state) has also been described.

Pulmonary Metastases:

Denosumab has shown efficacy in controlling benign pulmonary metastases from GCT, with reported responses including stability and regression of pulmonary nodules.

Schedule and Dosing

The standard denosumab dosing regimen for GCT follows the protocol established by the pivotal phase II trials (Thomas et al., Lancet Oncology 2010; Chawla et al., Lancet Oncology 2013):

Loading Phase: Denosumab 120 mg subcutaneously administered on Days 1, 8, 15, and 29 (weekly for the first month — three loading doses at weekly intervals, then a fourth dose one week later, providing rapid RANKL saturation and prompt therapeutic effect).

Maintenance Phase: Denosumab 120 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks (monthly) thereafter, continuing until surgery (for neoadjuvant use) or indefinitely (for unresectable disease).

Duration of Neoadjuvant Treatment:
Typically 3–6 months (3–6 monthly doses after the loading phase) before planned surgery. The optimal duration is not firmly established — clinical and radiological response (assessed by pain reduction, CT showing cortical reconstitution and tumour ossification, and MRI showing reduced tumour volume) guides the decision on timing of surgery. Some centres advocate shorter courses (3 months) to minimise the obliteration of the tumour-bone interface, while others use longer courses (6–12 months) for massive lesions requiring maximal downsizing.

Essential Supportive Care

Calcium and Vitamin D Supplementation: All patients receiving denosumab must take daily calcium (at least 500–1000 mg) and vitamin D (at least 400–800 IU) supplementation to prevent hypocalcaemia
— the most important adverse effect of denosumab. By inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, denosumab prevents the release of calcium from bone into the bloodstream, which can produce clinically significant hypocalcaemia (muscle cramps, paraesthesia, tetany, cardiac arrhythmias). Serum calcium must be monitored before each dose and corrected if low before administering denosumab.

Adverse Effects

Hypocalcaemia — as discussed above. Risk is increased in patients with renal impairment, vitamin D deficiency, and malabsorption.

Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ) — reported in approximately 1–5% of patients receiving denosumab for GCT (lower than in oncology doses for metastatic bone disease, but significant). Dental evaluation and necessary dental work should be completed before initiating denosumab. Patients should maintain good oral hygiene and avoid invasive dental procedures during treatment.

Rebound phenomenon on discontinuation — cessation of denosumab produces a surge of osteoclast activity (RANKL, no longer neutralised, activates a large pool of osteoclast precursors simultaneously), resulting in rapid bone resorption, hypercalcaemia, and potential tumour recurrence. Some centres advocate a transition to bisphosphonate therapy (zoledronic acid) after stopping denosumab to mitigate the rebound effect, though evidence for this approach is limited.

Atypical femoral fractures — rare, associated with prolonged use (>2 years), related to the suppression of normal bone remodelling (analogous to bisphosphonate-associated atypical fractures).
