## Question

A healthy 5-year-old boy is referred to your office for leg bowing. Radiographs of his legs are shown in Figure A. Which of the following is the next best step in management?

![[q1071-a-oite-4-yr-old.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Observation and 6 month follow-up
2. Nighttime bracing with knee-ankle-foot orthoses
3. Serial casting
4. Bilateral proximal tibial medial hemiepiphysiodesis
5. Bilateral proximal tibial lateral epiphysiodesis using extraperiosteal plates

## Correct option

**5. Bilateral proximal tibial lateral epiphysiodesis using extraperiosteal plates**

## Answer explanation

The use of lateral tibial epiphysiodesis with extraperiosteal plating allows for compression to be applied to the lateral portion of the physis. This technique is also known as guided growth and is based on the Hueter-Volkmann principle, which states that increasing compression across a growth plate leads to decreasing growth.

Physiologic genu varum progresses from moderate genu varum to neutral (at 12-24 months of age), to moderate genu valgum (at 3 years of age), and finally to physiologic genu valgum (5-10°, at 7 years of age). Pathologic genu varum is known as Blount's disease. Infantile Blount's disease is usually bilateral and is seen in newborns and children up to 3 years old, while adolescent Blount's disease is usually unilateral and is seen in children older than 10 years of age. Treatment of infantile Blount's disease usually depends on three factors: (1) age at presentation; (2) disease severity; and (3) response to previous treatments. Non-operative treatment using knee-ankle-foot orthoses is used for children less than 3 years of age. Operative management is usually reserved for children who are older than 3 years of age, have failed non-operative management, are morbidly obese, or have advanced Blount's disease (Langenskiöld stages IV, V, or VI). Surgical techniques include lateral proximal tibial guided growth (temporary epiphysiodesis) and valgus-producing proximal tibial osteotomies.

*Feldman et al. assessed the accuracy of deformity correction achieved in patients with tibia vara using intraoperative correction compared with gradual postoperative correction.* **They showed** that the accuracy of angulation corrections was significantly greater in the gradual correction group (17/18) than in the acute correction group (7/14).

*Scott published a retrospective review of 12 children with Blount's disease who were treated with lateral guided growth.* **She found** an 89% success rate, and concluded that guided growth is an effective means of treating infantile Blount disease in appropriate patients. **She also found** that, after removal of the plates, the risk of recurrent varus is attributed to the poor growth potential of the proximal medial tibial physis.

Figure A is an AP radiograph demonstrating bilateral tibia vara and pathologic medial proximal tibial deformity (medial metaphyseal beaking and sloping). Illustration A is a diagram depicting the Langenskiold Classification, with type I-IV consisting of increasing medial metaphyseal beaking and sloping, and type V-VI having an epiphyseal-metaphyseal bony bridge (congenital bar across physis). Illustration B is a diagram illustrating the metaphyseal-diaphyseal (Drennan's) angle, which is the angle between a line connecting the metaphyseal beaks and a line perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the tibia; a metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle >16 ° is considered abnormal and has a 95% chance of progression, while an angle <10 ° has a 95% chance of natural resolution of the bowing.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation and 6 month follow-up would not be the best management for pathologic tibia vara.
Answer 2: Nighttime bracing with knee-ankle-foot orthoses is usually not well tolerated and has not shown to be effective.
Answer 3: Serial casting is not well tolerated and has not shown to be effective.
Answer 4: Bilateral proximal tibial medial hemiepiphysiodesis would be inappropriate, as this would accelerate the deformity.
