## Question

The point where pulmonary ventilation disproportionately increases compared to oxygen intake is known as (FNB Pattern Paper 2020)

## Options

1. Anaerobic glycolysis
2. Ventilatory threshold
3. VCo2/ VO2
4. Aerobic threshold

## Correct option

**2. Ventilatory threshold**

## Answer explanation

Pulmonary response to exercise: As the body starts exercising at low intensities (30–50% of maximum aerobic capacity) the response of the respiratory system is to increase total ventilation to match the metabolic needs, or oxygen delivery to the muscle. This is achieved primarily by increasing the depth of breathing via neural stimulation of the inspiratory muscles, predominantly the diaphragm. At low exercise intensities the link between ventilatory volume and oxygen uptake is quite closely coupled and as such there is no excessive hyperventilation.

At moderate to high exercise intensities the volume of ventilation will continue to increase partially to meet the demands of the muscle for oxygen delivery. However, ventilatory volume begins to rise out of line with increases in oxygen uptake and thus we see a relative hyperventilation. This relative hyperventilation is primarily achieved through increases in respiratory rate and volume and thus depends not only upon active inspiration but also active expiration as well.

Exercise-related hyperventilation may be a response to excess carbon dioxide production as a result of lactic acid metabolism in active muscle tissue. Lactic acid in skeletal muscle very quickly disassociates to a lactate molecule and a hydrogen ion. The hydrogen ion can be buffered in the muscle and blood by HCO3 − , which results in the ‘non-aerobic’ release of carbon dioxide. The ensuing change in ventilation, which occurs at about 55–75% of maximal aerobic capacity in most people, is described as the ventilatory threshold or ventilatory breakpoint (also referred to as the anaerobic threshold).

Effect of aging on ventilation: The overall results of aging-related changes will be blunted ventilation at maximal aerobic capacity.

Also know:

The energy continuum for exercises:

These are the 3 Energy Systems

- ATP-PC System or Alactic System – ATP and creatine phosphate (CP) are present in very small amounts in the muscle cells. The system can supply energy very quickly because oxygen is not needed for the process. No lactic acid is produced in the process (Alactic)
- Anaerobic Glycolysis or Lactic Acid System uses carbohydrates (glucose) stored in the muscles as Glycogen. Because no oxygen is required to re-synthesise ATP, energy is produced quickly. Also because no oxygen is used in the process lactic acid is produced as an end product.
- Aerobic System – This system uses carbohydrates (glucose/glycogen) and fats to replenish ATP. Because oxygen is required for the process, energy production takes a little longer but can continue for a much longer duration. Because of the presence of oxygen, no lactic acid is produced.

At the start of an activity the initial source of energy is from the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) stores at the muscle cross bridges. The ATP, when used, is broken down to adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Creatine phosphate (PCr) rapidly replaces the ATP and so, in an indirect sense, becomes the next major source of energy. The anaerobic breakdown of muscle glycogen through glycolysis to form ATP and lactic acid predominates after PCr, whilst aerobic processes predominate from approximately 60 s and beyond. The energy continuum depicts the changes in major energy sources with time when exercise is maximal for each of the phases. For example at 20 s of exercise at maximal intensity, approximately 40% of the energy derives from PCr, 50% from glycolysis, and 10% from aerobic processes, whereas at 40 s the contribution is 5%, 80%, and 15% for PCr, glycolysis and aerobic processes, respectively.

Anaerobic/Ventilatory/lactate Threshold: aerobic to anaerobic transition intensity is one of the most significant physiological variables in endurance sports. Scientists have explained the term in various ways, like, Lactate Threshold, Ventilatory Anaerobic Threshold, Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation, Onset of Plasma Lactate Accumulation, Heart Rate Deflection Point and Maximum Lactate Steady State.

Anaerobic threshold: Point during intense exercise at which metabolism becomes increasingly more anaerobic (aerobic to anaerobic transition) reflects the lactate threshold under most conditions. But the relationship is not always exactly linear.

Ventilatory threshold: alveolar ventilation is coupled to CO2 flow to the lungs, thus leading to maintaining arterial isocapnia during moderate exercise. However, as the intensity of exercise increases hydrogen ions released from lactic acid can no longer be buffered by blood bicarbonate stores and thus metabolic acidosis results. This increase in hydrogen ion further stimulates ventilation by stimulating central as well as peripheral chemoreceptors resulting in further increase in ventilation. Thus the linearity of ventilation with changes of workload and oxygen utilization deviates at certain points which is named as ventilatory anaerobic threshold. In other words, during incremental, non-steady state exercise, a point is reached at which a subject’s ventilation shows a non-linear increase and this is termed as ventilatory threshold.

Lactate threshold: Exercise intensity associated with a substantial increase in blood lactate. It is a sensitive indicator of aerobic training status, predicts endurance performance often with greater accuracy than VO2max,

VO2 max, or maximal oxygen consumption, refers to the maximum amount of oxygen that an individual can utilize during intense or maximal exercise. This measurement is generally considered the best indicator of cardiovascular fitness and aerobic endurance.
