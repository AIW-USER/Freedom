## Question

A 22-year-old female presents to your office with right small finger pain after an injury while playing with her dog 2 days ago. She is otherwise healthy. Radiographs are seen in Figure A. An MRI is also obtained which shows a lobulated metaphyseal fifth metacarpal lesion that is hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2. What is the next best step in management?

![[q215506-a-5th-digit-enchondroma.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Core needle biopsy
2. CT scan
3. Curettage and bone grafting
4. Wide excision
5. Amputation

## Correct option

**3. Curettage and bone grafting**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a pathologic fracture of her right 5th metacarpal in the presence of an enchondroma. This should ideally be treated with curettage and bone grafting.

Enchondromas are benign cartilage tumors and represent the 2nd most common benign cartilage lesion with the most common being an osteochondroma. They occur most commonly in the hands and can lead to pathologic fracture with minimal trauma, although most are asymptomatic. On radiographs, a lucent area is seen, typically with matrix noted in the metaphyseal region. MRI may also be useful and usually demonstrates a lesion that is hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2 sequencing. Cortical expansion may be present but cortical disruption is not typically seen. When enchondromas are noted concomitantly with pathologic fracture, immediate or delayed curettage and bone grafting is the next best step in management to fill the bony void and restore bony stability.

*Romeo et al explored the genetics of benign cartilaginous bony tumors.* **They note** that some of these tumors, such as osteochondromas, occur due to a single gene mutation (EXT), while other bony tumors undergo significant genetic rearrangement with no obvious chromosomal patterns. They further review the genetic findings of these diseases at length.

*Bovee et al discuss cartilage tumors,* **noting** they are the most common primary bone lesions. Interestingly, they relate development of these tumors to deregulation of the hedgehog signaling pathway that affects normal bone development. They continue by describing new molecular and targeted gene therapies that may be useful in the future for targeting these tumors.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the right hand demonstrating a pathologic 5th metacarpal fracture in the setting of an enchondroma. Note the solitary, central, metaphyseal lesion that is slightly expanding the cortices with associated matrix formation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Core needle biopsy is prone to sampling error because of the significant heterogeneity in enchondroma and chondrosarcomas, making it difficult to truly rule out a malignant condition.
Answer 2: CT scan would not help guide management in this particular case, as the radiographic features are quite suggestive of an enchondroma. If advanced imaging is necessary in chases of equivocal enchondroma, MRI may be ordered.
Answer 4: Wide excision is appropriate treatment for chondrosarcoma, not an enchondroma, as is seen in this case
Answer 5: Amputation would not be appropriate in this case as the pathologic fracture is caused by a benign tumor.
