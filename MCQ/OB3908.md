## Options

1. Giant cell tumor
2. Gout
3. Hemophilia
4. Pigmented villonodular synovitis
5. Tumoral calcinosis

## Correct option

**4. Pigmented villonodular synovitis**

## Answer explanation

Based on the imaging characteristics of her MRI, the most likely diagnosis is pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS). The correct treatment for PVNS is synovectomy.

PVNS is an idiopathic monoarticular neoplastic synovial disease. In descending order, PVNS most commonly arises in the knee, hip, ankle, shoulder, and elbow. Affected joints present with mechanical pain and limited motion. Radiographs may have erosions from localized pressure (typically found in localized PVNS) or degenerative changes to the joint (found in diffuse PVNS). MRI findings include mass-like synovial proliferation with lobulated margins that have low signal intensity on both T1 and T2-weighted images. For patients with symptoms that warrant surgery total synovectomy is performed.

*Tyler et al. reviewed PVNS, in which they described the etiology, pathophysiology and natural history, clinical presentation, diagnosis, and treatment of PVNS.* *They explain that PVNS of the ankle may be treated by either arthroscopic or open synovectomy, although localized PVNS of the ankle is rarer and so an open approach may be required.* **They conclude** that the goal of treatment in PVNS is to reduce pain, stiffness and joint destruction, and to increase functional outcomes.

*Lee et al. followed seven patients with PVNS of the ankle who underwent open synovectomy followed by radiotherapy.* At two years' follow up, six patients reported improved function and decreased pain, and no patients had a recurrence. **They concluded** that combined synovectomy and radiotherapy improves function and reduces the risk of recurrence.

*Rhee et al. performed a retrospective review of localized PVNS treated with arthroscopic excision.* *Over 112 months of follow up,* **they determined** that 2 of 11 cases recurred and an additional 2 patients developed secondary osteoarthritis requiring surgical intervention. **They concluded** that arthroscopic excision is a successful treatment for localized PVNS.

Figure A shows a lateral ankle x-ray. Figures B and C are T1- and T2-weighted sagittal MRIs showing synovial proliferation with lobulated margins and low signal intensity. Illustration A is an AP ankle x-ray with an eccentric lytic metaphyseal lesion extending into the distal epiphysis. This is consistent with a giant cell tumor. Illustration B is a lateral hip x-ray with amorphous, cystic, lobular (circular or oval) well-demarcated calcification. This is characteristic of tumoral calcinosis.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The imaging studies in the stem are not consistent with a giant cell tumor. Illustration A shows an x-ray with more typical giant cell tumor characteristics.
Answer 2: Radiographs in gout typically have punched out periarticular erosions with sclerotic overhanging borders and soft-tissue radiodensities (indicative of tophi).
Answer 3: Though hemophilia can present similarly to PVNS with recurrent effusions, the MRI included in the stem is classic for PVNS.
Answer 5: Tumoral calcinosis is characterized by extra-capsular, peri-articular calcinosis. The imaging studies are not indicative of tumoral calcinosis. An example x-ray of tumoral calcinosis can be found in Illustration B.
