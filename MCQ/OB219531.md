## Question

A 20-year-old female college field hockey player presents to clinic with complaints of multiple patellar dislocations during this season and prior seasons. She reports a total of 7 total prior dislocations. A lateral knee radiograph is seen in Figure A with Caton Dechamps index equal to 1.2. She also has an MRI of her knee that demonstrates no major cartilage lesions and a TT-TG distance of 12 mm. On examination, her patella tracks laterally with knee flexion and she has a moderate J-sign. There is no significant crepitus. Lower extremity alignment is grossly normal. What is the most appropriate procedure to perform for this patient?

![[q219531-a-normal-lateral-knee-xray.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. MPFL repair alone
2. MPFL reconstruction alone
3. MPFL reconstruction and tibial tubercle osteotomy with medialization
4. MPFL reconstruction and tibial tubercle osteotomy with distalization
5. Trochleoplasty and MPFL reconstruction

## Correct option

**2. MPFL reconstruction alone**

## Answer explanation

This patient has recurrent patellar instability without trochlear dysplasia, an elevated TT-TG distance, or patella alta and would benefit most from an medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction.

Patellar instability is relatively common, especially in a younger female population. Most commonly, the patella subluxes or dislocates laterally with a twisting motion or direct blow to the medial knee. The MFPL is the primary constraint to lateral patellar translation from 0-30° degrees of flexion and is commonly injured during patellar instability events. As the knee flexes past 30°, the patella typically becomes centered within the trochlea which provides bony stability. If the trochlea is dysplastic, patients may experience feelings of instability with the knee in greater degrees of flexion. Additionally, if patella alta is present it may take a greater degree of knee flexion for the patella to become seated within the trochlea. Both of these bony anatomic factors can increase risk for patellar instability. Additionally, abnormal lower extremity alignment and an increased TT-TG distance can increase the lateral pull of the extensor mechanism and increase the risk for instability. The surgical solution for these patients relates to which structures are injured and which anatomic risk factors are present.

*Placella et al. looked at the size and shape of the MPFL based on 20 cadavers.* **They noted** that the femoral insertion measured approximately 9.5 mm distal/anterior to the adductor tubercle and proximal/posterior to the medial epicondyle. The MPFL inserts on the proximal 1/3 of the patella but its insertion also extends proximally to the vastus medialis and/or distally on the patella in at least 1/3 of cases.

*Hasler et al. looked at trochlear anatomy on MRI to define normal and dysplastic features.* **They noted** the average trochlear depth was 4.0mm (4.2 mm in men, 3.4mm in women). The lateral facet contributed about 63% of the trochlear depth compared to the medial facet.

Figure A is a lateral knee radiograph demonstrating normal bony anatomy without patella alta or significant trochlear dysplasia (no supratrochlear spur or crossing sign).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: MPFL repair would not be the best option in a skeletally mature patient with 7 prior dislocations as the tissue of the MPFL would likely be quite attenuated and lack the stiffness required to achieve a good repair.
Answers 3 & 4: Tibial tubercle osteotomy would be appropriate with medialization in the setting of an elevated TT-TG and distalization in the presence of patella alta, neither of which are indicated in this case.
Answer 5: Trochleoplasty would be useful in the setting of significant trochlear dysplasia, which is not present on the radiographs seen
