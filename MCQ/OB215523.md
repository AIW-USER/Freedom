## Question

Figure A is the radiograph of a 26-year-old male involved in a snow skiing accident. After a failed closed reduction attempt in the operating room, the patient is indicated for open reduction. The surgical approach chosen for this patient’s injury pattern has been shown to decrease blood loss while best reducing the risk of compromising femoral head blood flow. Which of the following correctly describes the muscular and nervous interval(s) for the approach used?

## Options

1. Sartorius (femoral nerve) and tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve)
2. Tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve) and gluteus medius (inferior gluteal nerve)
3. Rectus femoris (femoral nerve) and gluteus medius (inferior gluteal nerve)
4. Gluteus medius (superior gluteal nerve) and vastus lateralis (inferior gluteal nerve)
5. No true internervous plane, splits gluteus maximus

## Correct option

**1. Sartorius (femoral nerve) and tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an irreducible femoral head fracture-dislocation. The anterior (Smith-Peterson) approach has been shown to decrease operative time, blood loss, and improve visualization in this injury pattern (when compared only to the posterior approach to the hip). It also has the benefit of preserving femoral head blood supply, as there is minimal, if any, femoral head blood supply from the anterior hip capsule.

90% of hip dislocations are posterior, and 10-15% of these are associated with a femoral head fracture. In irreducible femoral head fracture-dislocations, the hip is typically dislocated posteriorly, but this can occur anteriorly as well. During the dislocation event, the impact with the wall of the acetabulum can cause part of the femoral head to shear off while the remaining segment exits the joint through a labrum-bone interval. This buttonholing through the labrum can lock the femur in place limiting passive/active range of motion of the affected limb, which is uncharacteristic of a typical hip dislocation where is limb is often mobile. The anterior approach to the hip provides excellent visualization for releasing the constricting tissue preventing relocation of the incarcerated fragment, as well as reduction and fixation of the femoral head fracture. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that there is minimal, if any, contribution of blood supply to the femoral head by the anterior hip capsule. While approaching the hip from a posterior direction may be more familiar to most surgeons, the blood supply to the femoral head is a major concern because the lateral epiphyseal artery lies within 15 mm of the insertions of the external rotator tendons.

*Swinontkowski et al. retrospectively reviewed operative management of 41 patients with femoral head fracture-dislocations treated through either a direct anterior or a posterior approach.* *In their series, femoral head fractures were associated with posterior hip dislocations in all but three patients that were Pipkin type IV injuries.* **They found** that there was a significant decrease in operative time, estimated blood loss, and improved visualization and fixation with the anterior approach.

*Mehta et al. describe their surgical experience of 72 patients with irreducible fracture-dislocations of the femoral head, all of which were posteriorly dislocated.* **They state** that recognition of these injuries based on physical exam and radiographic images is imperative, as closed reduction of these injuries should not be performed.

*Foulk et al. provide a review on hip dislocation evaluation and management.* *They describe the difference in patient outcomes following simple and complex hip dislocations, and management for associated injuries.* **They note** that the appearance of a concentric reduction on plain radiographs and CT does not rule out intra-articular hip pathology and that such an injury may contribute to long-term degenerative changes.

Figures A and B are an AP pelvis and lateral hip radiograph, respectively, demonstrating a right anterior hip dislocation with associated femoral head fracture. Illustration A demonstrates the Pipkin classification of femoral head fractures.

Incorrect Answers:

Answer 2: While this describes the correct muscular interval for the anterolateral (Watson-Jones) approach, which also utilizes the anterior capsule during dissection, the gluteus medius is innervated by the superior gluteal nerve.
Answer 3: While this does describe the correct deep intermuscular interval for the anterior approach, the gluteus medius is innervated by the superior gluteal nerve.
Answer 4: The direct lateral (Hardinge) approach to the hip splits the fibers of the gluteus medius, as are the fibers of the vastus lateralis (which is innervated by the femoral nerve, not the inferior gluteal nerve). Furthermore, with this approach there is no true internervous plane. Of note, the transverse branch of the lateral circumflex artery is often cut as the vastus lateralis is mobilized and must be cauterized during the dissection.
Answer 5: This describes the posterior (Moore or Southern) approach.
