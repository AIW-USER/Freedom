## Question

A 32-year-old man sustains a fall from a ladder after completing autumn maintenance on his gutters. The patient reports that he fell onto his hand with his arm extended, and had an immediate onset of profound elbow pain. Radiographs are obtained and are available for review in Figure A. A CT scan following splint application demonstrates a coronoid tip fracture, in addition to a comminuted radial head fracture. His soft tissues are amenable to surgical intervention, and he is brought back to the operating room. Intraoperatively, through a lateral incision, you perform a radial head replacement and repair of the lateral collateral ligamentous complex. Despite these steps, the elbow continues to sublux posteromedially. Which of the following is true regarding the next best step to restore elbow stability?

## Options

1. The coronoid fracture should be fixed through your current approach, taking care to protect Nerve E in Figure B during exposure
2. The coronoid fracture should be fixed through your current approach, taking care to protect Nerve B in Figure B during exposure
3. The medial collateral ligament should be fixed through a medial approach, taking care to protect Nerve C in Figure B during exposure
4. The medial collateral ligament should be fixed through a medial approach, taking care to protect Nerve B in Figure B during exposure
5. The medial collateral ligament should be fixed through your current approach, taking care to protect Nerve D in Figure B during exposure

## Correct option

**4. The medial collateral ligament should be fixed through a medial approach, taking care to protect Nerve B in Figure B during exposure**

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a terrible-triad injury to his elbow. In the setting of a coronoid tip (Type I) fracture, persistent instability following radial head replacement and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) repair/reconstruction is best managed with repair/reconstruction of the medial collateral ligament (MCL). Given the presence of a lateral incision, the MCL is best addressed through a medial approach, during which care must be taken to identify and protect the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (MABCN) (Answer 4).

A terrible triad injury of the elbow is a traumatic injury pattern of the elbow characterized by a complex elbow dislocation with concomitant radial head/neck and coronoid fractures. These injuries typically result from a fall onto an extended arm that results in a combination of valgus, axial, and posterolateral rotatory forces which produce a posterolateral dislocation. The structures of the elbow fail in a predictable lateral to medial direction, with disruption of the lateral collateral ligament (LCL) occurring first, followed by the anterior capsule and finally the medial collateral ligament (MCL). The concomitant coronoid fractures are classically described according to the Regan and Morrey Classification (Illustration A), with type I representing tip fractures, type II representing fractures of 50% or less of coronoid height, and type III representing fractures of more than 50% of coronoid height.

Nonoperative treatment of these injuries is rare, and surgical treatment necessitates a stepwise approach to fix the damaged structures. The radial head is addressed with ORIF or replacement, depending on the fracture pattern and degree of comminution, and the LCL is typically repaired or reconstructed. The coronoid can be fixed with either suture, suture anchors, screws, or (rarely) plate fixation. It is important to note, however, that studies have demonstrated that fixation of type I and II coronoid fractures confers little additional stability, and that persistent instability following radial head fixation/replacement and LCL repair/reconstruction is best treated through repair/reconstruction of the MCL. While a single posterior approach can grant access to both the medial and lateral elbow, many surgeons will begin with a laterally based approach and utilize a second medial approach only if necessary. During the medial approach to the MCL, the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (MABCN) often courses across the surgical field, and it must be identified and protected.

*Beingessner et al. investigated the effect of suture fixation of type I coronoid fractures on the kinematics and stability of the elbow with and without MCL repair.* Cadaveric testing was performed in stable and MCL-deficient elbows with radial head arthroplasty and with the coronoid intact, with the coronoid fractured, and after suture repair. **The authors found** that coronoid tip fractures caused only small changes in elbow kinematics, changes that were not corrected following suture repair. Overall, **the authors conclude** that terrible triad injuries with coronoid tip fractures and persistent instability following radial head replacement/fixation and LCL repair/reconstruction are best managed with MCL repair/reconstruction as opposed to coronoid fixation.

*Papatheodorou et al. performed a study investigating which types of coronoid fractures, in the setting of a terrible triad injury, required fixation.* *Their study included 14 patients with Type I fractures and 12 patients with Type II fractures.* Following radial head replacement/fixation and LCL repair, all patients demonstrated elbow stability through a 20° to 130° arc of motion. None of the patients at a mean follow-up of 41 months had evidence of postoperative instability, and only one patient demonstrated arthritic changes. **The authors concluded** that small coronoid fractures (Type I and II) do not require fixation, and that fixation confers no additional stability to the elbow. They do highlight that if instability were to persist following radial head replacement/fixation and LCL repair/reconstruction, the MCL, and not the coronoid, should be addressed.

*Cheung et al. review the surgical approaches to the elbow.* With respect to the medial approach, **the authors note** that the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve is often encountered early during surgical exposure, and must be identified and protected throughout the surgical case. The MABCN is a sensory branch of the medial cord of the brachial plexus and receives innervation from C8-T1.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: If instability persists following radial head replacement/fixation and LCL repair/reconstruction, in the setting of a coronoid tip fracture, the next best step is MCL repair/reconstruction.
Answer 3: Nerve C is the medial brachial cutaneous nerve, which is not encountered during the medial approach to the elbow.
Answer 5: Nerve D is the musculocutaneous nerve, which is not encountered during the medial approach to the elbow.
