## Question

Four weeks following the vaginal delivery of her first child, a 23-year-old woman brings her daughter in for evaluation of her left arm. The mother states that she developed gestational diabetes during pregnancy, and that her delivery was quite difficult. Physical examination demonstrates a left upper extremity deformity with limited motor function. The remainder of the physical exam is unremarkable. What is the most common presentation of the upper extremity in this condition?

## Options

1. Pronated forearm and flexed wrist
2. Extended metacarpophalangeal joints (MCP) and flexed interphalangeal (IP) joints
3. Thenar eminence wasting and inability to flex MCP and IP joints of index and long fingers
4. Anterior shoulder dislocation
5. Luxatio erecta

## Correct option

**1. Pronated forearm and flexed wrist**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a brachial plexus birth palsy, which most commonly affects the superior (C5-C6) nerve roots. This injury results in a classic Erb’s palsy, characterized by an internally rotated shoulder, pronated forearm, and flexed wrist (Answer 1).

With a reported incidence of 0.9/1000 live births, brachial plexus birth palsy (BPBP) has several identified risk factors, with shoulder dystocia being the most significant. Other risk factors include induced labor, maternal diabetes mellitus, prolonged labor, forceps-assisted delivery, and infant macrosomia. However, 50% of cases have no risk factors at all. The most common presentation of BPBP is the classic Erb’s (waiter’s tip) palsy, characterized by a C5-C6 superior root injury due to lateral neck bending away from the affected side. Less common is Klumpke’s palsy, which affects the inferior nerve roots due to a traction mechanism on an abducted arm, resulting in an ulnar claw hand. Preganglionic root injuries associated with the sympathetic trunk may also present with a concomitant Horner syndrome, which portends a poor prognosis. Treatment in most cases involves passive range of motion exercises while awaiting nerve function to return; Erb’s palsies carry the best prognosis for spontaneous recovery, and the return of biceps function by 3 months also suggests a good prognosis. Failure of the biceps to return may warrant a Hoffer tendon transfer procedure (latissimus and/or teres major to posterior rotator cuff). However, chronic untreated cases may eventually result in posterior glenoid dysplasia and subluxation of the humeral head, which are contraindications to tendon transfer and are treated with humeral de-rotational osteotomies instead.

*Abzug et al. published a 2019 study on the epidemiology and risk factors surrounding BPBP using the nationwide Kids’ Inpatient Database.* *The authors re-evaluated the previously known risk factors, as well as a specific assessment of hypotonia.* **The authors found** that the incidence of BPBP decreased over a 15-year period, with an incidence of 0.9 per 1,000 live births present in 2012, likely corresponding to the increasing rate of cesarean delivery over the past 2 to 3 decades. Furthermore, cesarean delivery was shown to be protective against a BPBP injury. Additional factors include a decreasing rate of exceptionally large babies, the decreasing use of forceps during delivery, and the increasing rate of multiple births, the latter of which has a protective effect against a BPBP injury. Hypotonia has been proposed as a potential risk factor owing to the possibility of making the plexus more susceptible to stretch during the delivery process, and the current study found that hypotonia is a risk factor for BPBP injury with an OR of 1.93.

*Van der Looven et al. published a 2020 systematic review investigating the risk factors for BPBP.* *The authors included 22 observational studies containing over 29 million patients.* **They identified** shoulder dystocia and macrosomia as having the highest (OR 115.27) and second-highest (OR 9.75) associations with BPBP, with gestational diabetes (OR 5.33), instrument-assisted delivery (OR 3.8), and breech positioning (OR 2.49) having lesser but still significant associations. Despite having well-recognized risk factors, prevention of BPBP remains difficult due to the unpredictability of these factors and their frequent relationship with labor itself. The authors strongly recommend a focus on risk stratification, antenatal counseling, enhanced labor surveillance, and simulation training.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: This describes an ulnar claw hand deformity, most often seen in Klumpke’s palsy and Volkmann contractures.
Answer 3: This describes a benediction hand deformity, most often seen in a median nerve injury.
Answer 4: Birth palsies do not commonly present with anterior shoulder dislocations.
Answer 5. Birth palsies do not commonly present with luxatio erecta, or inferior shoulder dislocations.
