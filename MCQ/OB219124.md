## Question

A patient is involved in a motor vehicle crash and suffers a radial head fracture. As the orthopedist on call, you develop a treatment plan for their orthopaedic injuries. Which of the following correctly pairs injury with treatment?

## Options

1. Radial head open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) for 28 year-old male with 4-part radial head fracture
2. Radial head ORIF for 38 year-old male with Mason Type II with a pronosupination arc of 40 degrees
3. Radial head resection for 48 year-old male with Mason type III radial head fracture
4. Radial head arthroplasty for 54 year-old female with 3-part radial head fracture with minimal comminution
5. Radial head excision for 76 year-old female with a 4-part radial head fracture and ulnar styloid base fracture

## Correct option

**2. Radial head ORIF for 38 year-old male with Mason Type II with a pronosupination arc of 40 degrees**

## Answer explanation

The correct answer is radial head ORIF in a younger patient with a Mason Type II fracture and a block to motion (answer choice 2).

Radial head fractures are common fractures that occur as a result of axial load or valgus force and typically occur along a spectrum of elbow instability. These fractures are most commonly classified by the Mason classification (Illustration A). The treatment options are many, including nonoperative management, ORIF, fragment excision, radial head resection, and radial head replacement. Factors to consider when determining a treatment plan include fracture size, number of fragments, overall elbow stability, blocks to motion, DRUJ stability, and patient age and activity level. For non-displaced fractures without a mechanical block to motion, nonoperative management can be utilized. For fractures with displacement, a mechanical block to motion, or elbow/forearm instability, operative treatment should be utilized.

*Strigini et al. performed a retrospective review of 24 patients with isolated Mason Type III radial head fractures treated with either prosthesis replacement or resection.* *15 patients were treated with prosthesis and 9 were treated with resections.* Patient outcome scores and range of motion were similar between the two groups. **The authors concluded** that both treatment options are viable for isolated Mason Type III fractures; however, arthroplasty may fare better for younger (<50 years old), active patients.

*Ring et al. performed a retrospective review of ORIF for radial head fractures in 56 patients with a 4-year follow-up.* Patient outcome scores and range of motion were worse in patients with greater than 3 fracture fragments after ORIF. In contrast, patients with minimal comminution (3 or fewer fragments) had satisfactory results. **The authors concluded** that ORIF should be performed when 3 or fewer fragments are present and replacement should be utilized for fractures greater than 3 fragments.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Despite this patient's young age, a 4-part radial head fracture does not fare well with ORIF. This patient should be treated with arthroplasty.
Answer 3: A Mason Type III fracture indicates displacement or comminution and should be treated with resection or arthroplasty. As this patient is younger (<50) and a manual laborer, outcomes are better with radial head replacement.
Answer 4: A 3-part fracture with minimal comminution can be successfully treated with ORIF.
Answer 5: This patient has an ulnar styloid base fracture, necessitating a high index of suspicion for a distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) injury. ORIF or replacement is indicated in this scenario to prevent proximal migration of the radius.
