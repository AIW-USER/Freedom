## Answer explanation

Structure E represents a portion of the deltotrapezial fascia, which is most commonly the culprit in the case of an irreducible Grade V acromioclavicular (AC) joint separation (Answer 5).

Acromioclavicular injuries are typically due to direct impact on an adducted shoulder, resulting in varying degrees of injury to the coracoclavicular (CC) and/or AC ligaments. The diagnosis of an AC separation is made with bilateral focused shoulder radiographs to assess for AC and CC interval widening, which is graded across a spectrum from Type I to Type VI according to the Rockwood classification (Illustration A). Types I-III involve an increase in CC distance from 0-100% of normal and are typically reducible, while Types IV-VI can be differentiated by their irreducibility. A Type IV separation involves lateral clavicle displacement posterior through the trapezius on an axillary lateral radiograph, while a Type V separation involves an increase in CC distance > 100% of the contralateral, which can become irreducible if buttonholed through the deltotrapezial fascia, as in the case of the patient in this vignette. A Type VI separation, on the other hand, is quite rare and involves an inferior dislocation of the lateral clavicle, often lying either in the subacromial space or subcoracoid position, tethered by the conjoint tendon. Treatment is often dictated by this classification scheme and the degree of instability, with Types I and II almost always treated non-operatively, Type III often controversially treated non-operatively, and Types IV-VI typically requiring operative intervention.

*Lee et al. reviewed shoulder AC joint reconstruction options and outcomes.* **The authors note** that there is currently no gold standard for the surgical management of these injuries, with multiple reconstructive options existing, including coracoclavicular screws, hook plates, endobutton coracoclavicular fixations, and anatomic ligament reconstructions with tendon grafts. **They conclude** that the anatomic reconstruction of the coracoclavicular and acromioclavicular ligaments with grafts demonstrates encouraging clinical and biomechanical outcomes; however, significant complications remain with these procedures.

*Maier et al. performed a cross-sectional, fundamental study of injury patterns of the acromioclavicular ligament complex in acute acromioclavicular joint dislocations.* **The authors note** that horizontal instability impairs clinical outcomes following acute acromioclavicular joint (ACJ) reconstruction and may be caused by insufficient healing of the superior acromioclavicular ligament complex (ACLC). They prospectively looked at 65 patients with acute-traumatic Rockwood-5 (n = 57) and Rockwood-4 (n = 8) injuries treated operatively utilizing mini-open ACJ reduction and hook plate stabilization and found that ACLC dislocation with deltotrapezial (DT) incarceration caused mechanical impediment to anatomical ACJ reduction in 14/65 (21.5 %) of cases, including all Rockwood-4 dislocations. **They concluded** that mini-open surgery was required in Rockwood-4 and a relevant proportion of Rockwood-5 dislocations to achieve both anatomical ACLC and ACJ reduction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The conjoint tendon attaches to the coracoid tip and is not incarcerated in a Type V AC separation.
Answers 2 and 3: The CC ligaments are completely ruptured in a Type V AC separation, but are not routinely the cause of incarceration.
Answer 4: The CA ligament does not represent a common cause of incarceration and irreducibility in a Type V AC separation.
