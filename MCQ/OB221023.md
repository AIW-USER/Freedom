## Question

A 12-year-old boy presents to your scoliosis clinic after his pediatrician noted a large rib hump on forward-bend testing. XR prior to referral demonstrates a large left thoracic curve. Additionally, the patient reports occipital headaches that worsen with sneezing and coughing. What is the most likely finding on MRI evaluation?

## Options

1. Tethered cord
2. Syrinx and tonsillar herniation of the cerebellum below the foramen magnum
3. Arachnoid cyst formation
4. Longitudinal split of the spinal cord
5. Low-lying conus medullaris

## Correct option

**2. Syrinx and tonsillar herniation of the cerebellum below the foramen magnum**

## Answer explanation

Occipital headaches exacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers in a patient with scoliosis and a left thoracic curve are consistent with a syringomyelia and possible Chiari malformation. A syrinx with tonsillar herniation of the cerebellum into the spinal canal describes a Chiari I malformation (answer choice 2).

In adolescent patients with scoliosis, a right thoracic curve is much more common than a left thoracic curve. Because of the increased incidence of neuraxial abnormalities in patients with left thoracic curves, further evaluation with MRI is indicated. The most commonly identified neural axis abnormality is a syrinx, defined as a fluid-filled cavity within the spinal cord, with or without associated cerebellar abnormalities. The most common cerebellar anomaly is a Chiari I malformation, in which a congenitally small posterior fossa leads to tonsillar herniation of the cerebellum below the foramen magnum. Symptomatic Chiari malformations with or without associated syringomyelia can require upper cervical/foramen magnum decompression before scoliosis correction. The pathophysiology of syrinx-associated scoliosis remains to be elucidated. Hypotheses include asymmetric damage to motor neurons leading to paraspinal muscle denervation, altered CSF dynamics, and cervicomedullary compression due to cerebellar tonsillar herniation.

*Swarup et al. reviewed the incidence of MRI abnormalities in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).* **They noted** that in their consecutive series of 259 patients with AIS who underwent MRI, 44% had abnormalities, including 4.2% with a Chiari malformation, 5% with syringomyelia, and 0.7% requiring operative intervention before deformity correction. **They concluded** that further studies are needed to better define the need for preoperative MRI in patients with AIS.

*Ouellet et al. reviewed the association of an absent Dickson’s sagittal deformity (lordotic deformity at the apex of the coronal deformity) and syringomelia.* **They noted** that in their retrospective series, apical lordosis was absent in 75% of patients with syringomyelia-associated scoliosis, while it was present in 97% of patients with AIS. **They conclude** that the absence of lordosis at the apex of the deformity warrants further investigation with MRI, and a diagnosis of AIS should be made with caution.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While tethered cord can present with scoliosis, it typically causes lower extremity weakness, bladder dysfunction, and cutaneous stigmata rather than occipital headaches that worsen with Valsalva, and is more commonly associated with lumbar or lumbosacral curves rather than isolated thoracic curves.
Answer 3: Spinal arachnoid cysts typically present with back pain, radiculopathy, or progressive lower extremity weakness and are most commonly located in the thoracic region dorsal to the spinal cord, but they do not cause the characteristic occipital headaches that worsen with coughing and sneezing seen in this patient.
Answer 4: Diastematomyelia is a rare form of spinal dysraphism that typically presents with cutaneous stigmata (hairy patch, dimple), lower extremity weakness, and bladder dysfunction, and is more commonly associated with congenital scoliosis or lumbar curves rather than thoracic curves without the occipital headache symptoms characteristic of Chiari malformation.
Answer 5: While a low lying conus medullaris can be associated with scoliosis in tethered cord syndrome, it typically presents with lower back pain, lower extremity weakness, bladder and bowel dysfunction, and cutaneous stigmata rather than occipital headaches that worsen with Valsalva maneuvers.
