## Question

A 67-year-old male with isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis of the knee presents to your clinic. His knee osteoarthritis has been refractory to nonoperative management. He saw a prior surgeon who recommended total knee arthroplasty (TKA), whereas you are also offering him unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA). He wants to know what risks he is uniquely exposed to if he undergoes UKA. Which of the following is a risk after UKA but not after TKA?

## Options

1. Osteoarthritis progression
2. Periprosthetic fracture
3. Periprosthetic joint infection
4. Polyethylene liner dissociation
5. Polyethylene wear

## Correct option

**1. Osteoarthritis progression**

## Answer explanation

Osteoarthritis (OA) progression is the most common reason for revision after unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA). OA progression is not a risk after total knee arthroplasty (TKA).

UKA is an excellent procedure with good long-term clinical outcomes if appropriate indications are used for patient selection. Common causes for revision include progression of arthritis (most common), aseptic loosening, polyethylene wear, and infection. In order to avoid the progression of arthritis, it is important to under-correct the deformity and place the components in the appropriate rotation to avoid impingement.

*Bini et al. compared outcomes of highly performing UKAs and TKAs using the Kaiser Permanente Total Joint Replacement Registry.* *They compared 1,054 UKAs to 74,185 TKAs and found that the adjusted risk of aseptic revision was not significantly higher for UKA than TKA (hazard ratio = 2.02 [0.68, 5.96], P = .203) or all-cause revision (hazard ratio = 1.24 [0.52, 2.98], P = .603).* **They concluded** that UKA likely has a higher rate of revision than TKAs, but the low incidence of revision and the relatively low number of UKAs included in their study led to the difference in rate of revision failing to reach statistical significance.

*Dyrhovden et al. used the Norwegian Arthroplasty Register to compare 60,623 TKAs (2426 revisions) and 7648 UKAs (725 revisions) from 1994 to 2004 (Period 1) and 2005 to 2015 (Period 2).* **They found** that TKA survival improved in Period 2 vs Period 1 (94% vs 91%). With respect to Period 1, revisions resulting from aseptic loosening of the femoral component, polyethylene wear/breakage, patellar dislocation, and unexplained pain decreased, whereas revisions resulting from early infection increased. For UKAs, the 10-year survival free from revision was 80% in Period 1 and 81% in Period 2. Revisions resulting from tibial aseptic loosening, polyethylene wear/breakage, and periprosthetic fractures decreased, but there were more revisions resulting from the progression of osteoarthritis. **They concluded** that implant survival for TKA has improved over time, but that UKAs are still revised at a higher rate than TKAs.

*Murray et al. wrote a commentary on perspectives regarding the utilization of UKA.* In their commentary they describe the hesitance of some surgeons to perform UKA due to the high rate of revision (with comparison to TKA). **They suggest** that if UKA were utilized for 20% of a surgeon's knee arthroplasties, the revision rate would be minimized.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Periprosthetic fracture is a risk of both UKA and TKA.
Answer 3: Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a risk of both UKA and TKA, though certainly a lower risk with UKA.
Answer 4: Polyethylene liner dissociation is a rare complication of both UKA and TKA.
Answer 5: Polyethylene wear takes place in both UKA and TKA.
