## Answer explanation

Treatment of periprosthetic fractures about the hip is determined based on stem stability and the amount of proximal bone stock. Figure C demonstrates a Vancouver B2 (loose stem, good bone stock), which is best treated with ORIF and revision arthroplasty with a diaphysial engaging stem.

Periprosthetic hip fractures are increasing in prevalence as we begin to do more hip replacement and patients live longer. The Vancouver classification is the most common classification for these injuries and eases communication and determines treatment options. The most important factors as it relates to treatment is the stability of the stem and the amount of viable bone around the prosthesis. In patients with a stable stem, treatment can consist of ORIF (Vancouver AG,AL,B1,C). Conversely, patients with a loose stem require revision of the prosthesis, which should also include ORIF to reconstitute the tube of the proximal femur if adequate bone stock remains (Vancouver B2). If a loose stem and poor proximal bone stock is encountered, proximal femoral replacement or allograft reconstruction is necessary (Vancouver B3).

*Sledge et al review an algorithm for the treatment of Vancouver B2 periprosthetic fractures.* **They report** that they typically approach the stem through the fracture fragments, followed by reconstruction of the tube (with 18G cerclage wire), and finally implantation of a new stem. **They conclude** that reconstitution of the tube allows for biomechanical integrity of the proximal part of the femur to promote fracture repair and implant fixation.

*Greidanus et al review the principles and management of treating femur fractures during and after THA in an instructional course lecture.* **They report** that the ultimate treatment is prevention, however, it is important for the treating surgeon to thoroughly understand the principles of managing these fractures. **They conclude** that the Vancouver classification aids in communication as well as determining treatment.

Figure A demonstrates a Vancouver AG. Figure B shows a Vancouver B1 as the stem is stable. Figure C is the radiograph showing a Vancouver B2 with a loose femoral stem as evidence by stem subsidence and loss of contact through the metaphysis. Figure D is a Vancouver B1 with a stable femoral component. Figure E represents a Vancouver B1 with a well fixed stem. Illustration A shows the full Vancouver classification.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Figure A demonstrates a Vancouver AG, and would not require revision of the stem.
Answer 2, 4, and 5: Figures B, D, & E demonstrate Vancouver B1 fractures. which have stable stems and thus can be treated with ORIF alone.
