## Question

A 16-year-old male presents to your clinic after an injury playing flag football where he states his long finger was "hyperextended," as he tried to grab a flag. On examination, he is unable to actively flex the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint and there appears to be a prominent bump on the volar aspect of the distal palmar crease. Which of the following is correct regarding the management of this injury?

## Options

1. Extension block splinting has equivalent long-term outcomes to repair
2. DIP joint pinning is required after repair
3. DIP arthrodesis should be considered in acute injuries with tendon retraction
4. Flexor tendon advancement of 2.5mm is acceptable during repair
5. It is acceptable for the patient to finish his remaining 8 weeks of football season before repair

## Correct option

**4. Flexor tendon advancement of 2.5mm is acceptable during repair**

## Answer explanation

A mild degree of flexor tendon advancement is acceptable, keeping in mind the risk of causing a DIP flexion contracture or quadrigia effect with over-advancement.

A flexor tendon avulsion, better known as a jersey finger, is a traumatic rupture of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) off of the base of the distal phalanx. It is usually caused by forced hyperextension of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint. Diagnosis can be made with the inability of the patient to actively flex the DIP joint. Typically the DIP joint is noted to be resting in slight hyperextension after this injury. The Leddy and Packer classification helps identify the level of tendon retraction after injury and can be useful in guiding surgical management with Type 1 injuries (FDP retraction to palm), which require prompt surgical management within 7-10 days. Acute primary FDP repair or reinsertion with a button are the preferred methods of surgical management. Cadaver studies have suggested that up to 1 cm of advancement is acceptable, but further advancement places the patient at risk for quadrigia, due to the common FDP muscle belly of the long, ring, and little fingers.

*Elzinga and Chung reviewed a variety of finger injuries in football and rugby players,* **noting** that these sports place participants at higher risk of sustaining finger injuries. Depending on the type of injury, some players may return to play quickly with protective taping, splinting, or casting, while others may require more rehabilitation and even surgical intervention. With regards to jersey finger specifically, **they note** that surgery is usually required to restore active ROM at the DIP joint and return to play may be delayed up to 4-6 months for full restoration of range of motion.

*Freilich reviewed the evaluation and treatment of jersey finger and flexor tendon pulley injuries in athletes.* **They note** that these injuries are frequently misdiagnosed as sprains or other minor injuries. In cases of flexor injuries, **they note** that prompt recognition is vital for appropriate early surgery and recovery.

*Shapiro et al. reviewed both the diagnostic criteria and treatment of flexor tendon injuries in athletes as well.* *They discuss, in detail, the anatomy of the flexor tendons and pulleys and the different levels of retraction during flexor tendon injury.* **They also recommend** prompt surgical management to allow for tension-free repair of the FDP tendon at the distal phalanx base.

*Malerich et al. performed a cadaver study looking at the amount of acceptable FDP advancement without causing a quadrigia effect.* **They noted** that the tendon could be safely advanced up to 1cm. **They note** that close attention should be paid to over-advancement, as this may contribute to a loss of hand function in these patients.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Patients with flexor tendon avulsions should be indicated for tendon repair/reattachment rather than extension block splinting.
Answer 2: DIP joint pinning can be used after flexor tendon repair/reattachment to protect the repair but this is not required depending on the integrity of the repair.
Answer 3: DIP arthrodesis is not a good option for patients with acute flexor tendon avulsions. Rather, they should be repaired as quickly as possible.
Answer 5: Ideally, repair of acute flexor tendon avulsions should take place within 7-10 days with retraction of the tendon into the palm or within several weeks if retracted to the PIP joint.
