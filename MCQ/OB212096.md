## Question

A 21-year-old male collegiate basketball player presents with left knee pain and swelling after landing awkwardly from a rebound. The patient previously underwent an ACL reconstruction of the left knee at 16 years of age with an ipsilateral bone-patellar tendon-bone autograft. On physical examination, the patient has a large effusion with a range of motion limited from 10° to 80° of knee flexion. Lachman's and pivot shift are both positive. Radiographs are unremarkable for any fractures but demonstrate 10° of mechanical axis varus, an 87° mechanical lateral distal femoral angle, and 15° of posterior tibial slope. CT scan confirms appropriate tunnel position, with the largest diameter measuring 10mm. He wants to continue playing at the same level. After the patient regains his normal range of motion, which of the following would be the most appropriate treatment option?

## Options

1. Sport-specific bracing and graduated return to play
2. Isolated revision ACL reconstruction with contralateral bone-patellar tendon-bone autograft
3. Single-stage distal femoral osteotomy and revision ACL reconstruction with ipsilateral quadriceps tendon autograft
4. Single-stage lateral closing-wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO) and revision ACL reconstruction with ipsilateral hamstring autograft
5. Staged revision ACL reconstruction with hamstring autograft followed by medial opening-wedge HTO

## Correct option

**4. Single-stage lateral closing-wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO) and revision ACL reconstruction with ipsilateral hamstring autograft**

## Answer explanation

The patient sustained a recurrent ACL rupture in the setting of varus malalignment of the tibia and an increased posterior tibial slope. The ideal treatment for this patient would include simultaneous correction of the tibial varus and neutralization of the tibial slope through a lateral closing wedge osteotomy combined with a revision ACL reconstruction. The ACL reconstruction can be performed in the same surgical setting of the high tibial osteotomy or staged following the high tibial osteotomy.

ACL tears are common athletic injuries and typically result from non-contact pivoting of the knee in slight flexion and valgus. Young athletic patients that desire to return to sporting activities would benefit from ACL reconstruction to prevent continued knee instability, reduce pain, and mitigate the risk of progressive cartilage and meniscus damage. Limb malalignment in the coronal and sagittal plane must be addressed either at the time of revision reconstruction or prior in order to decrease the risk of graft failure. While both medial opening-wedge and lateral closing-wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO) can adequately address proximal tibial varus, a lateral closing-wedge osteotomy is more effective in simultaneously correcting the posterior tibial slope.

*Boden et al. performed a knee MRI analysis of 25 healthy volunteers in either safe landing positions or ACL injury-provocative positions.* **The authors reported** that provocative positions placed the tibiofemoral contact point closer to the femoral sulcus and further away from the posterior tibial plateau and increased tibial plateau angle. **They concluded** provocative positioning of the knee places the ACL at risk for a noncontact injury.

*Ranawat et al. performed a cadaveric study comparing medial opening-wedge osteotomies and lateral closing-wedge osteotomies for tibiofemoral alignment and kinematics.* **They reported** that both techniques were equally effective at restoring appropriate coronal alignment, but lateral closing-wedge osteotomy was more effective at correcting the sagittal alignment of the proximal tibia. **The authors concluded** lateral closing-wedge high tibial osteotomy provides more reproducible posterior tibial slope neutralization and decreased anterior tibial translation in ACL deficient knees.

*Brandon et al. performed a prospective cohort study of 200 patients comparing the posterior inferior tibial slope in ACL deficient patients and healthy controls.* **The authors reported** a higher posterior inferior tibial slope in both male and female patients with ACL-deficient knees compared to healthy controls. In addition, there were higher-grade pivot shift tests in ACL deficient knees with higher posterior tibial slopes. **They concluded** that increased posterior inferior tibial slope was associated with ACL rupture.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The patient is a college athlete that wishes to continue playing at a collegiate level. Revision ACL reconstruction is the most reliable treatment to enable a return to the pre-injury level of play.
Answer 2: Isolated revision ACL reconstruction without addressing the varus malalignment and increased posterior tibial slope will lead to an increased graft failure rate.
Answer 3: A distal femoral osteotomy is not indicated, as the patient's mechanical lateral distal femoral angle is within normal limits, and in fact in slight valgus.
Answer 5: Revision ACL reconstruction should be performed after correcting the varus malalignment and increased posterior tibial slope.
