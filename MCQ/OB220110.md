## Question

A 57-year-old male presents to the emergency room with a three-day history of worsening right index finger pain and swelling. On examination, there is fusiform swelling of the right index finger, which is held in a flexed position at rest. He has tenderness overlying the flexor tendon sheath and severe pain with attempted passive extension of the digit, and is brought to the operating room expeditiously for surgical irrigation and debridement. At the time of surgery, purulence is encountered within the flexor tendon sheath, which does not extend into the overlying subcutaneous tissues. Intraoperative cultures grow methicillin-resistant staph aureus (MRSA) and he is started on an appropriate antibiotic therapy regimen. Which of the following factors places this patient at a greater risk for postoperative stiffness and the potential need for future amputation?

## Options

1. Patient age
2. Monomicrobial infection
3. Absence of subcutaneous purulence
4. Pain with passive extension at the time of presentation
5. Presence of fusiform swelling at the time of presentation

## Correct option

**1. Patient age**

## Answer explanation

Patient age greater than 43 years is an identified risk factor for poorer clinical outcomes associated with pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, including increased amputation risk and decreased total active motion following treatment (Answer 1).

The patient presents with findings consistent with pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis. On examination, four out of the four described Kanavel signs are present (fusiform swelling, tenderness overlying the flexor tendon sheath, flexed posturing of the affected digit, and pain with attempted passive extension). The diagnosis is often made clinically based on a thorough history and detailed physical examination. Treatment most often involves surgical irrigation and debridement followed by culture-directed antibiotics. Risk factors for poorer outcomes after treatment include age greater than 43 years, presence of diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease or renal failure, presence of subcutaneous purulence, digital ischemia, and polymicrobial infection.

*Chapman and Ilyas reviewed the diagnosis and management of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis,* **noting** the lack of standardized treatment algorithms within the existing literature and high associated complication rates regardless of the various treatment protocols described. **They noted** that treatment often combines prompt initiation of antibiotic therapy with surgical decompression and concluded that further research into the role of local antibiotic and corticosteroid administration is needed to potentially minimize the morbidity risk associated with current treatment strategies.

*Pang et al. performed a retrospective investigation to identify potential risk factors for poor outcomes after treatment of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis using amputation risk and total active motion as clinical endpoints.* **They identified** five risk factors associated with poor outcomes, including (1) age greater than 43 years, (2) presence of diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease or renal failure, (3) presence of subcutaneous purulence, (4) digital ischemia, and (5) polymicrobial infection. Based on their results, **the authors proposed** a three-tier classification system to better guide the prognosis and treatment of these infections, based primarily on the presence or absence of subcutaneous purulence and digital ischemic changes.

Incorrect Responses:
Answer 2: Polymicrobial infections are at a greater risk of poor outcomes.
Answer 3: The presence of subcutaneous purulence is a risk factor for poor outcomes.
Answer 4: Pain with passive extension is a common presenting finding and can aid in making the clinical diagnosis of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis.
Answer 5: The presence of fusiform swelling is a common presenting finding and can aid in making the clinical diagnosis of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis.
