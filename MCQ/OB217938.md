## Question

A 19-year-old male left-handed baseball pitcher presents to your office with complaints of vague, posterior shoulder pain. He has taken anti-inflammatories and attempted several months of aggressive physical therapy, but has failed to see any improvement in his symptoms. An MRI is obtained and a sagittal cut is seen in Figure A. Given this assessment, which of the following findings is most likely present upon physical examination of the shoulder?

![[q217938-a-spinoglenoid-notch-cyst.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Weakness with belly press test
2. Weakness with Hornblower's testing
3. Weakness with external rotation while arm adducted
4. Weakness with Jobe's test
5. Weakness with Jobe's test and external rotation with arm adducted

## Correct option

**3. Weakness with external rotation while arm adducted**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a spinoglenoid notch cyst which is likely causing denervation of the infraspinatus via compression of the suprascapular nerve.

The suprascapular nerve is formed by the C5 and C6 nerve roots off of the superior trunk of the brachial plexus and ultimately innervates the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. It may be compressed at the suprascapular notch or at the spinoglenoid notch. If compressed at the suprascapular notch, the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles are both affected, whereas only the infraspinatus muscle is affected if the nerve is compressed at the spinoglenoid notch. Patients typically complain of deep, vague posterolateral shoulder pain and have associated shoulder weakness based on the location of compression. If no structural lesion is seen on MRI, nonoperative management may be appropriate. However, in cases with MRI evidence of a structural lesion, nerve decompression is often indicated. Spinoglenoid notch cysts are commonly associated with posterior labral tears and these should also be addressed at the time of nerve decompression.

*Strauss et al. reviewed the evaluation and management of suprascapular neuropathy,* **noting** that labral or capsular tears can be a common cause of compression along the course of the nerve. They review the diagnosis of suprascapular neuropathy which includes a detailed history, physical exam, imaging and electrodiagnostic studies. **They note** that overhead athletes who undergo nerve compression can return to a high level of play after surgical decompression of the nerve, if necessary.

*Aval et al. review neurovascular injuries of the athlete's shoulder.* *They discuss a variety of pathologies including cervical radiculitis, spinal accessory nerve injury, long thoracic nerve palsy, stingers, and brachial neuritis.* *They discuss specific nerve compression symptoms, including the management of of suprascapular neuropathy.*

Safran specifically discussed suprascapular and axillary nerve injuries in the shoulders of athletes, noting that they are being increasingly appreciated. He mentions that history and examination are vital in coming to a prompt and accurate diagnosis. Further, he discusses the nonoperative and operative management of these injuries, which is primarily based on the the causative pathology.

Figure A demonstrates a sagittal MRI cut showing a spinoglenoid notch cyst abutting the posterior glenoid.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Weakness with belly press testing would indicate pathology involving the subscapularis.
Answer 2: Weakness with Hornblower's testing would indicate pathology involving the teres minor.
Answer 4: Weakness with Jobe's testing would indicate pathology involving the supraspinatus.
Answer 5: Weakness with Jobe's test and external rotation in adduction would indicate pathology involving the supraspinatus and the infraspinatus, likely involving suprascapular nerve compression at the suprascapular notch.
