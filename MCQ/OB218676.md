## Correct option

## Answer explanation

In patients with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, a failure to improve after 48 hours of antibiotic therapy warrants further investigation with MRI to look for the presence of a subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess (Figure A). The organism most likely to cause a subperiosteal abscess in the setting of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis is Staphylococcus aureus with a Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) mutation. Staph aureus can be identified microscopically as a gram-positive cocci that forms clusters (Answer 3).

Osteomyelitis in the pediatric population is most often the result of hematogenous seeding of bacteria to the metaphyseal region of bone. It is relatively common (1 in 5000 children under the age of 13), with an average patient age of 6.6 years. This pathology is 2.5x more common in boys, and most commonly occurs during the first decade of life due to the abundant metaphyseal blood supply and relatively immature immune system. It is important to note that this condition is not uncommon in otherwise healthy children. Over 30% of patients report a history of trauma, which leads to increased susceptibility to bacterial seeding in and around the metaphysis.

The most common causative organism is Staph aureus, a gram-positive cocci that forms in clusters. Strains of Staph aureus with a mutation encoding for the Panton-Valentine leukocidin cytotoxin are more likely to cause a clinical course that is complicated by complex infection, multifocal infections, prolonged fever, abscesses, DVT, and/or sepsis. The initial treatment for patients with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis is antibiotic therapy. Persistently elevated inflammatory markers after 48 hours of antibiotics should alert the physician to the possible presence of either a subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess, and an MRI should be obtained to assist in surgical planning. Prior to the development of current antibiotics, osteomyelitis in pediatric patients had a mortality rate that approached 50%. Promisingly, with current treatment guidelines, this rate has fallen to below 1%.

*Funk et al. provide a comprehensive review of the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis in children.* **They note** that this pathology presents a significant clinical challenge due to constantly evolving epidemiology and an underlying complicated pathogenesis. **The authors highlight** that a multidisciplinary approach has demonstrated efficacy in reducing the length of hospital stay, improving the timing and selection of antibiotic therapy, reducing the delay to initial MRI, and reducing the readmission rate. **The authors recommend** that patients who fail to improve with two days of empiric antibiotics should undergo an MRI to investigate for the presence of a subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess, which would necessitate surgical intervention.

*Stone et al. provide a long-term study of pediatric tibial osteomyelitis.* *Their study included 191 patients treated over a 10-year period at two children's orthopaedic departments.* **The authors note** that the average duration of symptoms before hospitalization was 5.7 days and that less than 40% of patients reported a history of trauma. **The authors highlight** that Staph aureus was the most common causative organism and that 43% of patients eventually required surgical intervention. **The authors conclude** that despite timely diagnosis, patients with acute hematogenous osteomyelitis often experience prolonged treatment courses and that relapse is common, most often secondary to social and antibiotic-associated complications.

Figure A is a T2-weighted coronal MRI of the femur, demonstrating findings consistent with a subperiosteal abscess. Figure B is a microscopic image of a gram-positive cocci in chains consistent with Streptococcus sp. Figure C is a microscopic image of a gram-positive cocci in clusters consistent with Staphylococcus aureus. Figure D is a gram-negative diplococci, consistent with Neisseria sp.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 2: These images represent Streptococcus sp, which may stain positive for pyrrolidonyl arylamidase. While group B strep is the most common cause of osteomyelitis in neonates, this 6-year-old patient is far more likely to have an infection secondary to Staph aureus.
Answer 4: Pyrrolidonyl arylamidase staining is associated with Streptococcus, not Staphylococcus, and it is not associated with the development of abscesses.
Answer 5: Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a common cause of septic arthritis in sexually active teens and young adults. It does not typically cause osteomyelitis.
