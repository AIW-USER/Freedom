## Question

In spastic diplegic cerebral palsy, which muscle most contributes to a stiff-knee gait due to its “overactivity” during the pre-swing and early swing phases of cadence?

## Options

1. Adductor magnus
2. Gluteus medius
3. Rectus femoris
4. Sartoius
5. Tensor fascia lata

## Correct option

**3. Rectus femoris**

## Answer explanation

Quadriceps (rectus femoris) activity is not typically needed when walking at a routine cadence. However, in spastic diplegic cerebral palsy (CP) patients, rectus femoris (RF) overactivity is commonly noted during the pre-swing and swing phases and contributes to a stiff knee gait.

The RF muscle is important in the pre-swing and early swing phases during activities such as running and sprinting. Since this muscle crosses two joints (hip and knee), it helps to generate power to initiate hip flexion while regulating the rate of knee flexion during the early swing phase. In the spastic diplegic CP patient, the RF may become “overactive,” limiting the amount of knee flexion that commonly occurs at the terminal stance and initial swing. With such limitations, the goal of treatment is to retain RF activity for initiation of hip flexion but to lessen its restraint on knee flexion. Distal RF tendon transfer allows for more flexion of the knee during the swing phase than simply releasing the tendon.

*Chambers et al. review 70 patients with CP and stiff-knee gait treated with either a distal RF transfer or release.* The reported-on gait analysis, **demonstrating** the swing-phase peak knee flexion was improved in the transfer group, allowing improved foot clearance and a more efficient gait. **They concluded** that a distal RF transfer is preferred to a distal RF release in CP patients with a stiff-knee gait.

*Ounpuu et al. looked at 136 lower limbs of patients with CP that were treated with either rectus femoris (RF) transfer or release.* **They found** that when preoperative knee ROM was > 80% of normal, there were no significant changes in knee motion in either group. However, in patients with < 80% of normal knee ROM preoperatively, RF transfer was followed by maintained knee flexion in swing; patients who underwent distal RF release showed a decrease in knee flexion postoperatively. **They concluded** that the RF should be transferred and not released when knee ROM is < 80% of normal.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Adductor magnus may contribute to lower extremity adduction contractions, but is not “overactive” during the pre-swing and early swing phases.
Answer 2: Gluteus medius contributes to hip abduction. Typically in spastic diplegic CP, the adductors contribute more to lower extremity contractures than do the abductors.
Answer 4: The sartorius similarly crosses both the hip and knee and contributes to hip and knee flexion. Conversely, the quadriceps contribute to hip flexion and knee extension. Knee extension is most problematic during the pre-swing and early swing phases.
Answer 5: Tensor fascia lata helps to stabilize the hip in extension by assisting the gluteus maximus during hip extension. It does not have significant contributions to the stiff-knee gait seen in spastic diplegic CP.
