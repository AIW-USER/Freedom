## Answer explanation

Several studies have demonstrated increased likelihood of persistent symptoms one-year after treatment for lateral epicondylitis. The origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) leads to tendinosis and inflammation in this condition.

Lateral epicondylitis is an overuse injury involving eccentric overload at the origin of common extensor tendon, leading to tendinosis and inflammation at the origin of the ECRB. Histologic examination demonstrates angiofibroblastic hyperplasia. Labors or athletes who participate in repetitive gripping or lifting tasks are most at risk. On examination, these patients often will have pain with resisted wrist extension, pain with gripping activities, decreased grip strength, and point tenderness at ECRB insertion into lateral epicondyle. Initial treatment often includes activity modification, ice, NSAIDS, physical therapy, and/or ultrasound. Additionally, injection therapies such as botulinum toxin, platelet-rich plasma, autologous blood injection, or hyaluronate injection may be used; corticosteroid is not recommended. With failure of conservative treatment, open or arthroscopic release and debridement of ECRB origin may be indicated.

*Dong et al. performed a systematic review of the literature pertaining to injection therapies for lateral epicondylitis.* **They found** that all of the injection treatments showed a trend towards better effects than placebo, but the peppering technique showed no additional benefits. **They concluded** that injection therapies that can be considered treatment candidates for lateral epicondylitis include botulinum toxin, platelet-rich plasma and autologous blood injection, but corticosteroid is not recommended. Hyaluronate injection and prolotherapy might be more effective, but their superiority has not yet been established.

*Coombes et al. reviewed the effectiveness of corticosteroid injection, multimodal physiotherapy, or both in patients with unilateral lateral epicondylitis.* **They found** that corticosteroid injection resulted in lower improvement at 1 year vs placebo injection. Similarly **they reported** that the physiotherapy and no physiotherapy groups did not differ on 1-year ratings of improvement or recurrence. **They concluded** that in patients with chronic unilateral lateral epicondylitis, the use of corticosteroid injection vs placebo injection resulted in worse clinical outcomes after 1 year, and physiotherapy did not result in any significant differences.
