## Question

A 20 year-old male presents with persistent sensation of his knee giving way after a misstep during a recreational soccer game 2 months ago. His radiograph and MRI are shown in Figures A and B, respectively. What is the ideal tunnel position during reconstruction of the main ligament that is ruptured?

*Figure A*

![[q210256-b-bone-bruise-acl.jpg]]
*Figure B*

## Options

1. 3 mm lateral to the anterior horn of the medial meniscus
2. 2 mm distal to the bifurcate ridge and 5 mm anterior to resident's ridge
3. 6.1 mm posterior to resident's ridge and 1.7 mm proximal to the bifurcate ridge
4. 15 mm anterior to the PCL
5. 16 mm anterior to the median eminence

## Correct option

**3. 6.1 mm posterior to resident's ridge and 1.7 mm proximal to the bifurcate ridge**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a ruptured ACL with instability based on history and physical exam. During reconstruction of the ACL, tunnels should be made nearest to the native ACL origin/insertions. Thus, the tunnel should be placed posterior to resident's ridge and proximal to the bifurcate ridge.

ACL ruptures are common injuries among soccer players, often occurring from a lateral collision to the knee or during a non-contact event while attempting to pivot/cut. The ACL-deficient knee demonstrates anterolateral rotational instability and may lead to other intra-articular damage. The pivot-shift test reproduces the giving way event sustained at the time of injury that leads to the classic bone bruising of the lateral condyle and plateau seen on MRI.

*Ziegler et al. carried out a cadaveric study detailing the native anatomy of the ACL in respect to the bony and soft tissue landmarks.* **They found** the overall femoral attachment of the ACL to be 6.1 mm posterior to resident's ridge and 1.7 mm proximal to the bifurcate ridge, providing a reproducible reference for tunnel placement.

*Ferretti et al. performed cadaveric dissections to detail the articular anatomy of the tibial plateau.* Measurements were obtained of the intra-articular structures in relation to the ACL, **showing** it to be 6 mm anterior to the median eminence and 9 mm posterior to the inter-meniscal ligament. This demonstrates reliable references for tunnel placement during reconstruction.

*Dhawan et al. reviewed newer ACL reconstruction techniques.* The goal of these is to create bone tunnels within the native ACL footprints in order to restore native kinematics and avoid residual instability and early graft failure. Though long-term clinical data is lacking, **they advocate** for femoral tunnel drilling independent of the tibia to avoid creating vertical tunnels.

Figure A is an AP radiograph showing a Segond fracture. Figure B is a fluid-sensitive sagittal MRI showing the common pattern of bony edema in the femoral condyle and tibial plateau.
Illustration A by Zeigler et al shows the femoral ACL origin. Illustration B shows the ACL bundles as the knee goes from extension to flexion. Illustrations C and D by Ferretti et al. show the relationship of the ACL tibial footprint to other intra-articular structures and with respect to the eminence and inter meniscal ligament.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The meniscal roots have variable attachments and on their own aren't reliable references
Answer 2: This position would place the tunnel too distal and anterior
Answer 4: The PCL can be used as a reference, but the center of the tunnel should only be 10mm anterior to this
Answer 5: The center of the tibial tunnel is approximately 6 mm anterior to the median eminence
