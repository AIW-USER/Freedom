## Question

A 28-year-old male presents to the emergency department for evaluation following a stabbing incident to the right medial brachium, in which he was attacked with a serrated knife. He was found to have a brachial artery transection by the trauma team and was immediately taken to the operating theatre by vascular surgery. Intra-operatively, the orthopedic surgery team is consulted after noting a transection of a nerve, which was later identified as the median nerve by your astute senior resident. Following intraoperative debridement of the nerve, a six-centimeter defect is noted. The decision is made to perform cable autografting utilizing the patient's sural nerve. Which of the following functions has the poorest prognosis in terms of recovery?

## Options

1. Wrist flexion
2. Index and middle finger flexion
3. Thumb interphalangeal joint flexion
4. Palmar/radial sided middle finger, index finger, and thumb sensation
5. Thumb opposition

## Correct option

**5. Thumb opposition**

## Answer explanation

This 28-year-old sustained a knife injury resulting in a high-median nerve injury with a resultant six-centimeter defect. The gold standard for treating defects exceeding five centimeters includes autografting with or without cabling. Because of the location of the injury, the likelihood of recovery of the intrinsic muscles innervated by the median nerve is extremely poor (Answer 5).

Peripheral nerve injuries are commonly encountered conditions and range in severity. High-nerve injuries are among the more complex cases, given their remote location concerning their downstream effects. Because of this, they can require multiple surgeries, which commonly lead to modest outcomes. Following a peripheral nerve injury, the distal segment undergoes Wallerian degeneration, with recovery limited by the rate of axonal regrowth, which occurs at a rate of 1 mm/day following a month of quiescence. This is especially problematic in high-nerve injuries, as the time required for nerve recovery often exceeds the motor endplates' ability to recover following axonal injury (typically 12 months). Because of this, intrinsic muscles innervated far from the nerve injury often experience endplate degeneration before nerve recovery, and as such, tendon and/or nerve transfers are most appropriate to restore function.

In this scenario, the most appropriate treatment is autograft due to the size of the defect. Given the size mismatch of the sural nerve to the median nerve, the patient would most likely benefit from cabling. This process includes obtaining multiple sections of donor nerve and joining them at their ends, which are then coapted to the injured nerve. Despite the proximal nerve injury, the patient would have the potential for sensation and extrinsic motor function. However, a tempered expectation concerning the patient's intrinsic function should be had, as the time required for reinnervation to these muscles will most likely exceed the motor endplates' lifespan when denervated. Because of this, nerve transfer (recommended within six months) or tendon transfers (at any time) would be necessary to restore thumb abduction and opposition.

Drs. Isaacs and Ugwu-Oju report on high median nerve injuries, detailing the incidence and anatomical considerations before describing their surgical technique for restoring common deficits seen following high median nerve injuries, including the inability to flex the thumb, index, and middle fingers, or pronate the hand. The authors describe the brachioradialis or extensor carpi radialis brevis to flexor pollicis longus to restore thumb interphalangeal joint flexion, biceps re-routing to restore pronation, and side-to-side transfers of the ring and small fingers to the index and middle finger flexor digitorum profundus tendons to restore flexion.

Geissler and Stevanovic review the management of large peripheral nerve defects necessitating autografting, detailing the indications, timing, and techniques. Autograft remains the gold standard, with acute repair the preferred timeframe, however meticulous evaluation of the nerve must occur to delineate the zone of injury. The most common donor nerves include the sural, medial, and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, and smaller donor nerves must be cabled to match the caliber of the injured nerve. The authors note the heterogeneous populations and injuries complicate the ability to quantify outcomes but find older age, proximal nerve injuries, ulnar nerve injuries, delays in surgery, concomitant bone and soft tissue injury, and graft length all negatively affect prognosis.

*Soldado et al. provide a summary detailing the presentation and treatment of high median nerve injuries, with the latter often including both motor and sensory nerve transfers.* **The authors note** that all patients following a high median nerve injury demonstrate absent interphalangeal flexion in the index finger distal phalanx and thumb, while pinch strength is severely limited. To restore pinch strength, **the authors recommend** nerve to the abductor digiti minimi be transferred to the median nerve motor branch, which should be performed no later than six months.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-4: while high median nerve injuries offer widely variable levels of recovery, the muscles within the forearm tend to have better recovery rates compared to the intrinsics within the hand. Additionally, finger sensation more commonly returns, given the lack of dependence on motor endplate integrity.
