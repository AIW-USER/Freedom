## Question

A 29-year-old male was engaged in a battlefield reenactment when a wooden sword struck his hand. He presented one day later with right index finger tenderness, swelling, and a limited range of motion. On physical exam, there is tenderness to palpation on the radial aspect of the index finger MCPJ. Joint stability testing shows no endpoint on ulnar-directed stress at the right index finger MCPJ. In addition, he complains of difficulty pinching. Regarding index finger radial collateral ligament (RCL) injuries, which of the following is true?

## Options

1. The RCL is not a key component to pinching
2. Stable injuries benefit from early surgical intervention
3. Unstable injuries achieve equovical clinical outcomes with early or late presentation
4. Unstable injuries achieve best outcomes with early surgical reconstruction
5. Unstable injuries achieve best outcomes with early surgical repair

## Correct option

**5. Unstable injuries achieve best outcomes with early surgical repair**

## Answer explanation

An unstable index finger MCPJ with a complete tear of the RCL and without a firm endpoint on exam requires surgical intervention and does better with early repair.

The RCL is a key component to pinching. Injuries to the index RCL are graded on severity and endpoint on stress testing. Grade I injuries have tenderness over the RCL without instability, Grade II injures have laxity on stress testing, but have a definite endpoint, while Grade III injuries have no endpoint on exam and are considered unstable. Grade I-II injuries are best treated with 4-6 weeks of immobilization and excellent results can be anticipated. Grade III injuries that present early (<4 weeks) may be treated with strict immobilization or surgical repair. However, Grade III injuries that present late universally do poorly and often require a ligament reconstruction or even fusion.

*Dy et al. performed a cadaveric analysis of 17 index fingers.* **The authors found** that the radial collateral ligament is 5.8mm in width and 6.4mm in length. **The authors also determined** exact anatomic origin and insertion points of the RCL which may be beneficial in the reconstruction of the index finger RCL.

Gaston and Lourie report on 14 patients with index finger MCPJ with an average follow up of 24 months. Ligament injuries with a firm end-point on stress testing that were treated with immobilization alone (4-6 weeks) had excellent results. When patients with unstable joints were seen early, ligament repair achieved good results, all patients who presented late achieved poor outcomes. The authors underscore the importance of a good physical exam and early, appropriate treatment to optimize outcomes.

*Lourie et al. reviewed collateral ligament injuries of the metacarpophalangeal joints of the fingers.* Although complete rupture is rare, MPCJ collateral ligament injuries are often misdiagnosed or completely missed. Chronic instability often leads to pain and weakness and may precipitate osteoarthritis, particularly in the thumb MCPJ. A careful physical exam with appropriate stress testing and advanced imaging will improve diagnostic accuracy. Late ligament repair is appropriate when no arthritic changes are present, but a salvage procedure may be necessary for the unstable, arthritic MCPJ.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: RCL integrity is critical to pinching. Without the RCL, during pinching, the MCPJ would deviate ulnarly.
Answer 2: Stable injuries are best treated with early immobilization.
Answer 3: Unstable injuries are best treated with early recognition and treatment. Late presentation of unstable injuries have decidedly worse outcomes compared to those with an early presentation.
Answer 4: Unstable injuries presenting early are most amenable to repair, not reconstruction. A ligament reconstruction would be reserved for unstable injuries presenting late when the RCL is patchless and irreparable.
