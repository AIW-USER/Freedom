## Question

A 22-year-old college student sustains a twisting injury to his knee while walking down a wet flight of concrete stairs. He is unable to bear weight and is taken to the local trauma center by emergency medical services. Radiographs obtained in the trauma bay are shown in Figures A and B. He is closed reduced under conscious sedation, and post-reduction radiographs demonstrate a congruent joint. He is sent for an MRI of his knee, and follows up in your clinic the next week. Which of the following is true regarding the definitive treatment of his injuries in an acute (< 3 weeks from time of injury) fashion?

## Options

1. Acute treatment is associated with lower rates of residual knee instability when compared to staged or chronic treatment
2. Acute treatment is associated with the highest rates of good to excellent subjective outcomes
3. Acute treatment is inferior to definitive nonoperative management
4. Aggressive postoperative range of motion is associated with increased joint instability following acute treatment
5. Patients managed acutely are more likely to have postoperative stiffness recalcitrant to intervention

## Correct option

**5. Patients managed acutely are more likely to have postoperative stiffness recalcitrant to intervention**

## Answer explanation

Patients with multiple-ligament knee injuries following knee dislocation (KD III or higher) who are treated in an acute fashion are more likely to have postoperative stiffness that is refractory to additional interventions.

Knee dislocations are high-energy injuries fraught with associated pathology. These injuries have a high rate of neurovascular injury and demand urgent reduction and evaluation of neurovascular status. Multiple ligaments including the ACL, PCL, LCL, MCL, and PLC are at risk with knee dislocation. The Schenck classification classifies knee dislocations by the number of damaged ligaments. Much research has been performed regarding the optimal timing of surgical treatment. There exist three schools of thought with regards to optimal timing: acute (< 3 weeks from time of injury), chronic (> 3 weeks), or staged (a combination of repair and reconstruction in both the acute and chronic periods). While the current evidence continues to evolve, acute treatment is associated with unique risks that may suggest a staged approach is a more effective alternative for management. It is important to note, however, that no one treatment algorithm has demonstrated lasting superiority.

*Mook et al. performed a systematic review of the literature to determine the best treatment algorithm for knee dislocation-associated ligamentous injury.* *They analyzed 24 studies containing 396 knee dislocations, and broke down the time to surgery into three categories: acute (< 3 weeks), chronic (> 3 weeks), and staged (a combination of acute and chronic).* Overall, **they found** that patients who underwent acute management had a higher likelihood of having residual instability and stiffness that is resistant to intervention. **They also found** that patients who underwent staged treatment had a higher likelihood of having good to excellent subjective outcomes.

*Gella et al. presented an instructional course lecture regarding the timing and treatment of knee dislocation-associated ligamentous injuries.* **They report** that while acute repair does produce equivalent functional results when compared to chronic or staged treatment, there is a higher likelihood of arthrofibrosis with acute management.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Acute treatment is associated with higher rates of residual knee instability when compared to staged treatment.
Answer 2: Traditionally, staged treatment was associated with the highest rates of good to excellent subjective outcomes. More recent literature suggests equivalent outcomes with staged versus acute treatment. Regardless, no one treatment algorithm has demonstrated superiority.
Answer 3: Almost all knee dislocation-associated ligamentous injuries require operative treatment. Universally worse outcomes are observed with definitive nonoperative management.
Answer 4: Aggressive postoperative range of motion following acute treatment does not increase the risk of residual joint instability.
