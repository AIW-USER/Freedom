## Question

A 62-year-old female undergoes revision right total hip arthroplasty as part of a 2-stage surgery for prosthetic joint infection. The revision case was challenging secondary to abundant scar tissue around the acetabular rim. However, the case was completed without complication. When she presents to the clinic 6 weeks post-op, she is ambulating without a walker and is satisfied with her recovery. Her plain films taken in the clinic are seen in Figure A. Due to the finding identified by the red arrow, she has what theoretical increased risk when compared to a patient who received a ceramic-on-polyethylene bearing in this setting?

![[q219453-a-mdm-malseat.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Increased risk of dislocation
2. Increased risk of prosthetic joint infection
3. Decreased risk of periprosthetic fracture
4. Increased risk of fretting corrosion
5. Decreased range of motion secondary to impingement.

## Correct option

**4. Increased risk of fretting corrosion**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent the second stage of her revision total hip arthroplasty with modular dual-mobility components as the final implants. The 6-week post-operative cross-table lateral shows liner malseating. Due to this, she has a theoretically increased risk of fretting corrosion.

Instability and dislocation remain a major concern in revision total hip arthroplasty. One method for decreasing the risk of dislocation, particularly in the revision setting, is the use of modular dual mobility implants. As seen in Illustration A, modular dual mobility implants use an acetabular component and metal liner (cobalt-chrome most commonly) with an outer polyethylene bearing and an inner femoral head. The proposed biomechanical benefit of this design is an increased arc of motion prior to impingement. Although dual mobility implants decrease dislocation rates in revision settings, they have unique challenges. One of these challenges is malseating of the acetabular liner within the cup. One theory for this process is that hard bone can cause plastic deformation of the cup when press fitting. Cobalt-chrome is inelastic and subsequently may not sit inside the cup appropriately. This can be troublesome, as in vitro studies have shown an increased fretting corrosion rate with malseated liners. This fretting corrosion can lead to metal ion release and adverse local tissue reactions. Long-term clinical data is needed to evaluate the potential risk for adverse local tissue reactions. Regardless, fretting corrosion on malseated acetabular liners in modular dual mobility implants remains a legitimate theoretical risk.

*Chalmers et al report on a single surgeon’s experience with a modular dual mobility implants in 305 patients.* *Of the 305 patients in the study, 4 had radiographic evidence on a cross-table lateral of incomplete liner seating.* The prominence in all 4 was at the inferior lip, which is the most difficult portion to visualize intraoperatively. None of these patients had pain, complications, change in liner position, or underwent revision surgery during the 1- year follow-up. The author attributed this lower rate (compared to the prior literature-reported rate of 5%) to meticulous surgical technique. The paper describes the “4 quadrant test” to evaluate for any incomplete linear seating. The acetabular rim is cleared of any soft tissue or osteophytes and the entire rim is visualized. A cup rim impactor is then impacted at the 6 o’clock, 12 o’clock, 3 o’clock, and 9 o'clock positions.

*Romero et al report on a combined clinical and in vitro study.* The clinical arm of the study reviewed 551 modular dual mobility implants and found an overall malseating rate of 5.8%. **They noted** the incidence of malseating was significantly higher in low-volume dual-mobility surgeons (p<0.001). The in vitro arm included 6 dual-mobility cups and cobalt-chrome liners that underwent cyclic loading at increasing magnitudes. 3 of these were well-seated and 3 were malseated with 6 degrees of canting. Well-seated liners had significantly lower fretting current values at all peak loads over 800N (p<0.044). Malseated liners also had lower fretting onset loads. **The authors conclude** that these results support the hypothesis that malseatead liners are at an increased risk of fretting corrosion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Dual mobility implants decrease the rate of postoperative dislocation. There has been no reported increased rate of instability with malseated liners.
Answer 2: There has been no reported increased risk of infection with malseated liners in modular dual mobility implants.
Answer 3: There has been no reported increased risk of periprosthetic fracture with malseated liners in modular dual mobility implants.
Answer 5: Dual mobility implants have an increased range of motion before impingement. There has been no reported decrease in range of motion with malseated liners.
