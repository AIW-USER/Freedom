## Question

A 47-year-old male with a history of poorly controlled diabetes who is three months status post right long finger trigger finger release presents to the emergency room with three days of right index finger pain, swelling, and erythema. Available laboratory data demonstrates a normal white blood cell (WBC) count, and mildly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. The decision is made to proceed with operative intervention. During the subsequent surgical dissection, gross purulence is encountered within the index finger flexor tendon sheath. Which of the following findings was most likely present on initial physical examination?

## Options

1. Inability to actively flex at the index finger distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint
2. Index finger held resting in a hyperextended position
3. Pain with passive extension of the index finger
4. Tenderness localized to the level of the index finger A1 pulley
5. Discrete area of swelling at the level of the index finger proximal phalanx

## Correct option

**3. Pain with passive extension of the index finger**

## Answer explanation

The diagnosis of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis is made with the identification of Kanavel's signs, which include flexed posturing of the affected digit, tenderness along the length of the flexor tendon sheath, pain with passive extension of the affected digit, and fusiform swelling.

The patient presents with pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis. In rare cases with mild symptoms and acute presentation, a trial of intravenous antibiotic administration alone can be utilized for initial treatment. However, urgent operative irrigation and debridement with culture-directed antibiotic therapy are the mainstays of treatment in most cases.

*Chapman and Ilyas reviewed the diagnosis and management of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis,* **noting** the lack of standardized treatment algorithms within the existing literature and high associated complication rates regardless of the various treatment protocols described. Treatment often combines prompt initiation of antibiotic therapy with surgical decompression. **The authors concluded** that further research into the role of local antibiotics and corticosteroid administration is needed to minimize the morbidity risk associated with current treatment strategies.

*Koshy and Bell reviewed an array of hand infections including pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis.* **They noted** the low negative predictive diagnostic value of laboratory data. The authors cite evidence that among the established Kanavel signs, fusiform swelling is most commonly present on initial examination. Pain with passive extension of the digit is most reliable in reaching a clinical diagnosis. They also described various treatment strategies ranging from antibiotic therapy alone in mild cases presenting within 24 hours of symptom onset to open extensile debridement in severe cases. Overall, the authors underscored the importance of prompt diagnosis, appropriate management, and close clinical monitoring to help mitigate the risk of morbid complications including loss of motion and amputation.

Incorrect Responses:
Answer 1: The inability to actively flex at the DIP joint is not indicative of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis and may be more consistent with an injury to the flexor digitorum profundus tendon.
Answer 2: In pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, the affected digit is commonly held in a flexed position.
Answer 4: In pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, there is commonly tenderness to palpation along the length of the flexor tendon sheath. Localized tenderness to the area of the A1 pulley (often with a palpable nodule) is more consistent with a trigger finger diagnosis.
Answer 5: Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis presents with fusiform swelling of the affected digit.
