## Question

He is a hurdler and his pain is worse at the extremes of flexion during these events to the point where is currently not able to participate. On examination, flexion and internal rotation reproduce his pain. He denies pain with a resisted straight leg raise or with a resisted sit-up. He is able to bear weight regularly without significant pain while walking. ## Answer explanation

Based on the clinical description, this athlete has cam-type femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), which is depicted in Figure C.

FAI represents an increasingly recognized pathology at the hip which can be due to abnormalities of the proximal femur, acetabulum, or both. During high degrees of flexion, combined with adduction and internal rotation, the proximal femur will abut the acetabulum, leading to soft tissue damage including labral tears and chondral injury. Two types of FAI are commonly referenced, including Cam and pincer type. Cam type, as described above, is seen classically in young athletic males and occurs when there is a decreased femoral head/neck ratio with asphericity of the head. This is denoted with an increased alpha angle (>42-50 degrees) on a Dunn view radiograph (Illustration A). It is now thought that FAI represents a risk factor for developing osteoarthritis of the hip as patients age. Treatment includes activity modification vs. surgical intervention with arthroscopic vs. open methods.

*Philippon et al. reviewed FAI in professional athletes and how arthroscopic decompression affected their ability to return to sport.* **They found** that 93% of athletes returned to the same level of play at an average of 1.6 years. **They concluded** that in high-level athletes without concomitant arthritis, hip arthroscopy performed to address FAI is efficacious in allowing return to sport.

*Nepple et al. compared preoperative findings of dysplasia and FAI on radiographs to intraoperative findings during hip arthroscopy.* **They noted** that cam-type FAI with alpha angle >50 degrees was associated with labral disease, while dysplasia and pincer-type FAI weren't associated with intra-articular damage. **They concluded** that cam-type FAI, along with male sex and older age were associated with more severe intra-articular hip disease.

*Schoenecker et al. described surgical management of hip issues in adolescents and young adults.* *They discuss multiple pathologies that cause femoral and acetabular sided abnormalities and noted that surgical hip dislocation provides complete access to the hip joint to correct any deformity.* **They conclude** that regardless of surgery performed, correction of hip morphology improves function and pain in this population.

Figure A represents an AP pelvis radiograph demonstrating bilateral adult hip dysplasia with shallow acetabuli. Figure B demonstrates a left hip AP radiograph consistent with stress fracture of the inferior femoral neck. Figure C represents an AP pelvis radiograph showing bilateral cam deformities of the proximal femora with what also appears to be a crossover sign present on the left hip. Figure D shows an AP radiograph of the left hip consistent with avascular necrosis of the proximal femur. Figure E represents a T2 coronal MRI sequence showing a right adductor strain.

Illustration A demonstrates a Dunn view radiograph of the left hip with the alpha angle measured.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Figure A represents a patient with adult hip dysplasia, which would not have the same physical examination findings, despite being at risk for early progression to arthritis.
Answer 2: Figure B represents a femoral neck stress fracture which would likely cause proximal thigh pain with axial loading.
Answer 4: Figure D shows avascular necrosis of the proximal femur which would present insidiously with pain with impact and inclines, and would not usually cause pain at the end-range of motion or with flexion/adduction. Additionally, this patient does not demonstrate any of the common risk factors for avascular necrosis.
Answer 5: Figure E shows an adductor muscle strain, which is not fully consistent with the patient's examination, given the lack of pain with resisted hip flexion/sit-up.
