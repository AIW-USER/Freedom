## Question

A 42-year-old female recreational long-distance runner presents with left buttock pain that has been present over the past 6 months. She endorses a dull ache when sitting for a prolonged period. Her pain worsens with knee extension and hip flexion. She is found to have a chronic proximal hamstring avulsion and undergoes operative repair. During the procedure, the surgeon exposes the ischial tuberosity and places large Hohmann retractors around the exposed bone. One retractor is placed aggressively superior and medial to the ischial tuberosity. Which nerve is at risk with this retractor placement?

## Options

1. Sciatic nerve
2. Pudendal nerve
3. Posterior femoral cutaneous nerve
4. Inferior gluteal nerve
5. Superior gluteal nerve

## Correct option

**2. Pudendal nerve**

## Answer explanation

The pudendal nerve crosses 2-3cm superior and medial to the ischial tuberosity proximal hamstring origin. It can be injured with aggressive retractor placement in this region during the surgical approach for proximal hamstring repair (Answer 2).

The proximal hamstrings have two major origins along the ischial tuberosity: (1) the inferior-medial footprint of the conjoined tendons (semitendinosus and biceps femoral) and (2) the superior-lateral footprint of the semimembranosus tendon (Illustration A). Proximal hamstring ruptures vary in severity (i.e., complete or partial tears) and chronicity. Although both may be indicated for surgery, patients with acute complete tears appear to have superior improvements with operative management compared to those with chronic partial tears. When performing the operative approach, the ischial tuberosity must be identified for proximal hamstring tendon repair to its native footprint. While most surgeons’ attention is drawn towards performing neurolysis of the sciatic nerve just lateral to the ischial tuberosity, aggressive retractor replacement superior-medially can result in iatrogenic injury to the pudendal nerve (Illustration B). In such cases, patients often report sexual dysfunction, dyspareunia, impotence, and other symptoms exacerbated by sitting or defecation. To avoid this complication, some surgeons recommend using less aggressive/penetrating glenoid retractors over large Hohmann retractors in the superomedial region of the ischial tuberosity to avoid potential pudendal nerve injury.

*Arner and colleagues provided a comprehensive review article regarding managing hamstring injuries.* **The authors highlight** that acute repair of proximal avulsions, both partial and complete, results in successful outcomes, whereas chronic tears are more difficult to treat with less reliable outcomes. **They conclude** by emphasizing that hamstring reinjury is common, making the continuation of preventive therapies after return to sport essential.

*Cvetanovich and colleagues performed a cadaveric study utilizing six separate specimens to define the anatomy of the pudendal nerve in relationship to the proximal hamstring and other nearby neurological structures using proximal hamstring repair.* **They report** that while the sciatic nerve was an average of 1.1cm lateral to the ischial tuberosity (with the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve lying between both structures), the pudendal nerve was only 2-3cm superior-medial from the ischial tuberosity (Figure B). **The authors conclude** that awareness of these nearby neurological structures may prevent iatrogenic damage during surgical dissection and retraction when performing proximal hamstring repair or deep gluteal space endoscopy.

*Miller and colleagues performed a similar cadaveric study utilizing 14 specimens and reported similar findings.* **The authors also highlighted** that the inferior gluteal nerve lies just deep to the gluteus maximus (i.e., not visualized as it is retracted superiorly with the gluteus maximus during the approach), and temporary traction neuropraxia may be experienced with aggressive retraction. **The authors conclude** that the proximal hamstrings have intimate relationships with nearby peripheral nerves, which are at risk during both surgical dissection and retraction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The sciatic nerve is located lateral to the ischial tuberosity, not medial. To identify and protect the sciatic nerve throughout the case, neurolysis should be performed just lateral to the hamstring tendon sheath, where the sciatic nerve can consistently be found. Transient nerve palsy is common due to the necessary lateral retraction of the nerve to expose the ischial tuberosity and place anchors.
Answer 3: The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve exits the greater sciatic foramen and travels with the inferior gluteal artery just deep to the gluteus maximus. During the proximal hamstring repair surgical approach, it can be found immediately adjacent to the sciatic nerve, in which both nerves course lateral to the ischial tuberosity. Transient posterior thigh numbness is common due to the necessary lateral retraction of the nerve with the sciatic nerve.
Answer 4: The inferior gluteal nerve exits the greater sciatic foramen inferior to the piriformis and travels along the undersurface of the gluteus maximus. During the proximal hamstring repair surgical approach, the nerve is retracted indirectly with the gluteus maximus.
Answer 5: The superior gluteal nerve exits the greater sciatic foramen inferior to the piriformis but immediately travels superiorly to supply the gluteus medius musculature. During the proximal hamstring repair surgical approach, it courses far superior and lateral to the ischial tuberosity and does not lie within the operative field.
