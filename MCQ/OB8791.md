## Question

A 14-year-old athletic male with a medium build presents to your office with 3 weeks of atraumatic left hip and thigh pain. For that last 12 hours, he has been unable to bear weight, even with crutches. He denies right hip pain. He has a normal neurovascular exam, but has obligatory external rotation of the left hip upon flexion. Radiographs are seen in figures A, B, and C. What is the best next step?

## Options

1. MRI of left hip
2. Strict bedrest, urgent transfer to hospital and surgery on the left hip, with close follow-up
3. Strict bedrest, urgent transfer to hospital and surgery on both hips, with close follow-up
4. Crutches, schedule surgery within the next 2 weeks
5. Strict bedrest for 1 week, NSAIDs, surgery if no improvement in 1 week

## Correct option

**2. Strict bedrest, urgent transfer to hospital and surgery on the left hip, with close follow-up**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a displaced, unstable slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) at the left hip. This warrants bedrest to prevent further displacement, and urgent surgery to stabilize the slip.

SCFE is most common in obese children around the age of 12-13. Patients most often present with hip pain, but pain can be referred to the knee in 15-23% of patients by the posterior branch of the obturator nerve. In this patient, it is important to recognize he has an 'unstable' slip, which is defined as the inability to walk, even with crutches due to such severe pain. This prognosticates a 50% chance of femoral head osteonecrosis, compared to almost 0% in a stable SCFE. Treatment of an unstable SCFE is somewhat controversial. Most surgeons recommend treatment with 8 hours, incidental reduction, single-screw fixation, and non-weightbearing for 6-8 weeks following surgery. Joint aspiration of hematoma to decompress the intra-capsular pressure and theoretically reduce the risk of osteonecrosis is recommended by some surgeons.

*Hosseinzadeh et al. conducted a retrospective review of 149 patients with 196 stable SCFEs and* **found** that non-orthopaedists took on average 94 days to diagnose a SCFE compared to 2.9 days for orthopaedists. Delay in diagnosis was significantly longer if the patient presented initially with knee pain, indicating the need for high index of suspicion for this condition.

*Aronsson et al. review the current management of SCFE and discuss the prevailing thoughts on when to treat the contralateral hip.* Contralateral SCFE is 2,335 times more likely to develop in the contralateral hip after unilateral SCFE. However, **they report** that it is important to consider the patient's bone age and a Modified Oxford Bone Age Score can be utilized in calculating the patient's risk (see illustration C). Contralateral SCFE did not develop in patients with a score > 21.

Figure A is an AP of the pelvis with disruption of Klein's line (see illustration A), signifying a possible left SCFE. Figure B is an AP of the left hip with a left SCFE. Figure C is a frog-leg lateral of the left hip with evidence of a moderate SCFE (40 degree Southwick angle: 55 left hip - 15 right hip) (see illustration B).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: MRI can be useful to diagnose a radio-occult pre-slip condition. This patient's slip is obvious on radiographs.
Answer 3: Prophylactic pinning of the contralateral hip is indicated in patients believed to be at greater risk for a contralateral hip. These are patients that are obese, less than 10, or with endocrine disorders.
Answer 4: Discharge with elective surgery is inappropriate for a patient with an unstable slip.
Answer 5: There is no role for non-operative management of this condition.
