## Question

A 19-year-old female soldier sustained a blast injury to her right leg while deployed. Her injury consisted of a comminuted right tibial shaft fracture with bone loss and a soft-tissue defect of 4 cm of the anterior medial tibia. The patient underwent two irrigations and debridements and recently had an intramedullary nail placed. Negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has been used to cover the defect since the injury. The risk of an infection developing in the tibia is increased in which scenario below?

## Options

1. Definitive soft tissue coverage between 3 and 7 days post-injury
2. Use of negative-pressure wound therapy
3. Timing of initial debridement between 6 and 24 hours post-injury
4. Blast mechanism of injury
5. Bone grafting procedure performed greater than 3 months post-injury

## Correct option

**4. Blast mechanism of injury**

## Answer explanation

Blast injury has been shown to be an independent risk factor for the development of infection in open tibial fractures.

Open fractures most commonly occur in the tibia and phalanges. Infection occurring in the setting of open fractures can be a catastrophic complication. Treatment is with intravenous antibiotics and urgent irrigation and debridement. Injury pattern dictates which antibiotic(s) are indicated, but at a minimum, a first generation cephalosporin (e.g. cefazolin) is used. Tetanus prophylaxis and provisional fracture stabilization are also part of the emergency department treatment of these injuries. Risk factors for infection include blast-type mechanism of injury, greater than 7 days between injury and definitive soft tissue coverage, higher grade of Gustilo-Anderson classification, and contamination of the wound with foreign bodies.

*Webb et al. analyzed 156 patients from the Lower Extremity Assessment Project (LEAP) study group with limb-threatening open tibial diaphyseal fractures for outcomes and complications.* **They found** patients treated definitively in external fixation did worse than those treated definitively with intramedullary nailing regardless of wound coverage technique. They looked at timing to wound debridement (less than 6 hours versus between 6 and 24 hours), the timing of soft tissue coverage (less than 3 days versus greater than 3 days), and timing to a bone grafting procedure (less than 3 months versus greater than 3 months) and did not find any difference in infection rates, union rates, or functional outcomes.

*Tribble et al. retrospectively reviewed 130 tibia osteomyelitis cases and 85 control patients with combat-sustained open tibia fractures between 2003 and 2009, assessing risk factors for the development of osteomyelitis.* *The patients who developed osteomyelitis most commonly had gram-negative infections with Acinetobacter calcoaceticus.* Independent risk factors identified included blast injury (odds ratio [OR] 2.8), classification of Gustilo-Anderson 3B or higher (OR 8.99), and presence of a foreign body fragment at the tibia fracture site (OR 3.9)). **They also noted** a significantly longer average time to radiographic union in the osteomyelitis group (210 days versus 165 days).

*Bhattacharyya et al. reviewed the trauma registry at Massachusetts General Hospital and* **identified** 38 patients with grade 3B open tibia fractures between 2002 and 2005. As is standard of care at their hospital, patients were treated with NPWT after initial debridement. **They found** an overall infection rate of 36%. Timing to definitive coverage greater than 7 days was found to be associated with infection (57% versus 12.5% in those with less than 7 days to coverage). Fifty-five percent of infections were due to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. The most common reason for the delay in coverage was due to operating room availability. **They conclude** that NPWT does not decrease infection rates for those patients who undergo delayed definitive soft tissue coverage greater than 7 days.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The LEAP study demonstrated no difference in infection rates between soft tissue coverage before 3 or after 3 days.
Answer 2: Negative pressure wound therapy has not been shown to increase the risk of infections in the setting of open fractures.
Answer 3: Another finding from the LEAP study group is no difference in complications between debridement in less than 6 hours versus 6-24 hours.
Answer 5: There was no difference in infection rates comparing bone grafting less than 3 months post-injury compared to greater than 3 months in the LEAP study.
