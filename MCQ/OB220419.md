## Question

A 47-year-old recreational tennis player presents for evaluation of subacute ulnar-sided left wrist pain. A posteroanterior radiograph obtained in the clinic is demonstrated in Figure A. A wrist MRI is subsequently obtained, which reveals an absence of intra-articular pathology; however, thickening and high intrasubstance signal within the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) tendon on T2-weighted images is noted. Based on these findings, which of the following maneuvers on physical exam would most likely be positive?

![[q220419-a-633px-x-ray-of-normal-wrist-by-dorsoplantar-projec.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Patient actively abducts wrist in ulnar deviation, with the examiner passively flexing the thumb into the palm
2. Patient grasps their thumb within their hand and clenches it with the other fingers, followed by passive ulnar abduction of the wrist by the examiner
3. Patient’s elbow is flexed to 90º with the forearm fully supinated, the fingers are fully extended, and the patient is asked to maintain thumb and middle finger abduction against resistance
4. Patient’s wrist is moved from ulnar to radial deviation, with direct pressure over the volar scaphoid
5. Patient holds full elbow extension and forearm pronation, with attempted wrist extension against resistance

## Correct option

**3. Patient’s elbow is flexed to 90º with the forearm fully supinated, the fingers are fully extended, and the patient is asked to maintain thumb and middle finger abduction against resistance**

## Answer explanation

The patient’s imaging findings suggest ECU tendonitis. The ECU synergy test (answer 3), is often positive and has been shown to be a helpful part of clinical evaluation for this condition.

ECU tendonitis exists as part of a spectrum of diseases involving the ECU tendon, often occurring concurrently with subluxation or dislocation of the tendon from its groove on the ulna, a condition referred to as snapping ECU. These conditions typically present with ulnar-sided wrist pain, often stemming from overuse injuries. Subluxation is caused by attenuation or rupture of the ECU subsheath, causing the tendon to subluxate out of its groove during supination. As ulnar-sided wrist pain may originate from a multitude of etiologies, various physical exam maneuvers have been described to aim to differentiate between common causes. One such maneuver is the ECU synergy test, which involves having the patient rest their elbow on an exam table in 90º of flexion and full forearm supination, then with the wrist maintained in a neutral position and the fingers extended, asking the patient to perform resisted thumb and digital abduction. Recreation of ulnar-sided wrist pain with this maneuver is considered indicative of ECU tendonitis. Initial treatment typically involves rest and splinting, but for refractory cases, surgical reconstruction of the ECU subsheath, possibly with wrist arthroscopy to evaluate and treat concurrent pathologies (e.g., triangular fibrocartilage complex tears), may be necessary.

*Ruland and Hogan first described the ECU synergy test, providing context to its utility in a retrospective cohort study of 55 adult patients presenting with greater than 4 months of dorsal ulnar-sided wrist pain.* *They sought to highlight its value in differentiating ECU tendonitis from other, particularly intra-articular, etiologies of pain, by utilizing a series of exam maneuvers aimed at evaluating various sources of ulnar-sided wrist pain (e.g., TFCC compression, DRUJ manipulation).* **They found** that the test was fairly reliable in this task, as 11 patients with positive synergy tests with no pain with the remainder of the exam all had greater than 90% pain relief after ECU tendon sheath injection, with 5 remaining pain-free and 5 of the remaining 6 with temporary improvement followed by MRI evaluation that confirmed the diagnosis of isolated ECU tendonitis. **The authors concluded** that the test may be a useful part of clinical evaluation and serve in diminishing the reliance on MRI and diagnostic arthroscopy to differentiate between intra-articular and extra-articular pathology.

*Sato et al. evaluated the diagnostic performance of the ECU synergy test utilizing ultrasonography, in a prospectively collected cohort of 39 patients.* **They reported** a sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of 73.7%, 85.7%, 82.4%, and 78.3%, respectively. **They concluded** that the ECU synergy test could serve as a useful provocative maneuver for diagnosing ECU abnormalities in individuals with chronic dorsal ulnar-sided wrist pain.

*DaSilva et al. provided a more generalized review of the evaluation of ulnar-sided wrist pain,* **noting** it presents diagnostic challenges due to the complex anatomy, overlap of symptoms, and potential for both intra- and extra-articular pathology. **They found** that systematic physical examination with provocative maneuvers, such as the ulnocarpal stress test and ECU synergy test, can refine the differential diagnosis, although imaging modalities like radiographs and MRI, with or without arthrography, are also important. **They concluded** that careful clinical assessment, appropriate use of imaging, and wrist arthroscopy can aid in accurate diagnosis and management of ulnar-sided intra-articular wrist pathology.

Figure A demonstrates a normal PA radiograph of a wrist joint. In the context of evaluating for ulnar-sided wrist pain, particular attention is paid to the ulnar variance, as abnormalities may indicate a potential source of the presenting complaints; in the provided radiograph, the ulnar variance is not significantly abnormal. Illustration A demonstrates the ECU synergy test, provided from the Ruland study. The white arrow highlights the ECU tendon contracting under the skin, indicating the active contraction of the muscle with the maneuver.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choices 1 and 2: These answers describe the Finkelstein and Eickhoff maneuvers, respectively. When radial-sided wrist pain is created with these maneuvers, they are considered positive for de Quervain’s tenosynovitis.
Answer choice 4: This describes a portion of the Watson test, utilized in the evaluation of scapholunate ligament injuries. A palpable and painful clunk with the release of this pressure is considered a positive test.
Answer choice 5: This describes one test to evaluate for lateral epicondylitis or Tennis elbow.
