## Options

1. Apex dorsal angulation of 30 degrees
2. Disruption of the deep transverse metacarpal ligament
3. Fracture shortening of 5mm
4. Presence of a short oblique fracture
5. Rotational malalignment

## Correct option

**1. Apex dorsal angulation of 30 degrees**

## Answer explanation

Dorsal angulation of index and middle finger metacarpal fractures is less well tolerated than that of the ring and small fingers, which results in clinical "pseudoclawing" of the affected digit due to metacarpal phalangeal joint (MCPJ) hyperextension.

Metacarpal fractures represent the most common hand injury and can be subdivided into those affecting either the head, the neck, or the shaft. As a rule, angulation is less well-tolerated in metacarpal shaft fractures than in metacarpal neck fractures; thus, when angulation > 10 degrees exists, a reduction attempt is recommended. This is particularly important in index and middle finger metacarpal fractures because of the decreased mobility of their associated carpometacarpal (CMC) joints compared with the fourth and fifth CMC joints, allowing for less accommodation of excessive angulation. When these fractures are allowed to heal with apex dorsal angulation, the MCP joints will hyperextend to overcome the flexion posture of the metacarpal, ultimately leading to the possibility of clinical pseudoclawing of the affected digit.

*Diaz-Garcia et al. reviewed the current management of metacarpal fractures.* **The authors note** that as with any hand fracture, the primary goals are to achieve anatomic and stable reduction, bony union, and early mobilization to minimize disability. In the setting of metacarpal shaft fractures, angulation is better tolerated among the ulnar digits than in the index or middle finger. **They conclude** that the presence of pseudoclawing with a prominent dorsal deformity should prompt consideration of operative intervention.

*Friedrich et al. provide an evidence-based approach to the management of metacarpal fractures.* **The authors note** that when analyzing evidence for treatment protocols, the highest quality evidence is found in studies of nonsurgical treatment of this injury. When operative treatment is indicated, because of the long, slender nature of the metacarpal, combined with the close juxtaposition of the extensor tendons, intramedullary fixation continues to be an attractive option, while plate fixation is also favored by many practitioners due to the ease of application and ability to quickly mobilize the plated digit. **They conclude** that when practicing evidence-based medicine, the surgeon should consider the strength of the available evidence and integrate the evidence with his or her clinical expertise and the patient’s values and preferences to develop an appropriate treatment plan.

*Kollitz et al. review the treatment options and complications associated with metacarpal fractures.* **The authors note** that metacarpal fractures comprise between 18–44 % of all hand fractures, with the fifth finger most
commonly involved. While many metacarpal fractures do well without surgery, there is a paucity of literature and persistent controversy to guide the treating physician on the best treatment algorithm. The authors conclude that the index and middle finger can tolerate only minimal apex dorsal angulation, and reduction should be attempted with greater than 10° of angulation. Careful attention must be paid on exam to the ability to extend the PIP joint in both MCP flexion and extension to assess for pseudoclawing.

Figure A demonstrates a chronic, healed index metacarpal shaft fracture with an apex dorsal deformity. Figure B is a clinical figure showing index finger MCPJ hyperextension and resultant pseudoclawing.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The deep transverse metacarpal ligament is a narrow fibrous band, that runs across the volar surfaces of the heads of the second, third, fourth, and fifth metacarpal bones, connecting them together, and remains intact in the setting of a metacarpal shaft fracture. This, along with reduced mobility at the CMC joints, contributes to pseudoclawing, not disruption of the ligament.
Answer 3: Fracture shortening of up to 5mm is reasonably well tolerated in index and middle finger fractures.
Answer 4: Excessive angulation (not oblique fracture type) is the primary cause of pseudoclawing in the index and middle fingers due to the decreased baseline mobility of the second and third CMC joints which causes compensatory hyperextension through the MCP joints to overcome the flexion posture of a dorsally angulated metacarpal fracture.
Answer 5: Rotational malalignment leads to inappropriate finger overlap, not pseudoclawing.
