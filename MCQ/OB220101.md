## Question

A 70-year-old male undergoes right successful THA. Two weeks after his surgery, he suffers a ground-level fall and presents to the emergency department with the radiograph shown in Figure A. Which of the following correctly pairs his Vancouver classification and appropriate surgical intervention?

![[q220101-a-b2-fx.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Vancouver A: ORIF of the fracture using cables and locking plate
2. Vancouver B1: ORIF of the fracture using cables and locking plate
3. Vancouver B1: Revision of the femoral component to a long cementless stem with fixation of the fracture
4. Vancouver B2: ORIF of the fracture using cables and locking plate
5. Vancouver B2: Revision of the femoral component to a long cementless stem with fixation of the fracture

## Correct option

**5. Vancouver B2: Revision of the femoral component to a long cementless stem with fixation of the fracture**

## Answer explanation

Figure A shows a Vancouver B fracture around the femoral prosthesis. Given that the original surgery was two weeks before presentation, the prosthesis would not have adequate ingrowth and appears to have subsided thus it would be classified as a Vancouver B2 fracture. The most appropriate operation would be to revise the femoral component to a long, porous-coated, cementless stem and fixate the fracture with a plate and cerclage wires (Answer 5).

Periprosthetic femur fractures, which occur around a hip prosthesis, present challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. Diagnosis involves classifying the fracture based on its location, assessing the stability of the femoral component, and finally, the integrity of the remaining bone. Distinguishing between stable and loose prostheses preoperatively can be challenging but involves evaluating patient history, symptoms, and radiographic findings, potentially requiring intraoperative assessment. Treatment goals focus on achieving fracture union, maintaining or obtaining a functional prosthesis, and facilitating early weight-bearing. For fractures around loose femoral components, femoral revision with a long-stem or modular tapered stem is often the primary treatment. Treatment strategies vary depending on fracture morphology, bone quality, and prosthetic stability, with options including nonoperative management for stable fractures in certain locations, open reduction and internal fixation, and various revision arthroplasty techniques like using cortical strut allografts or proximal femoral replacement in cases with significant bone loss.

*Shah et al. comprehensively address periprosthetic fractures occurring around loose femoral components following total hip arthroplasty (THA).* *They examine the epidemiology and risk factors contributing to these fractures,* **emphasizing** the significance of component loosening and increased patient activity. The authors outline the Vancouver classification system used for diagnosis, which guides clinical management. Ultimately, the reviewers underscore the necessity for surgeons to accurately diagnose implant stability and choose appropriate revision strategies, such as femoral revision with long-stem prostheses, to achieve favorable patient outcomes despite the associated mortality and complication risks.

Kelly also reviews Periprosthetic Femoral Fractures following THA. The author divides the discussion into three key areas: classification, examination of their etiology and preventative measures, and a detailed overview of various treatment options. The article provides a structured understanding of this challenging orthopedic problem, guiding clinicians in diagnosis and management to achieve fracture healing and maintain a functional hip prosthesis.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: According to the Vancouver classification, fractures around the stem, such as this one, are categorized as B-type.
Answers 2-3: In this example, the patient presents two weeks after surgery with stem subsidence. According to the Vancouver classification a loose stem with good proximal bone stock is a B2.
Answer 4: Loose femoral stems with good bone stock are categorized as B2 and should be treated with revision of the femoral component in addition to the ORIF of the fracture.
