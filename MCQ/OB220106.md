## Correct option

## Answer explanation

The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABCN), the terminal sensory branch of the musculocutaneous nerve which is a branch of the lateral cord of the brachial plexus (Answer 2), is at risk for injury during a distal biceps tendon repair.

Distal biceps tendon avulsions are injuries to the biceps tendon at the radial tuberosity insertion that generally occur due to a sudden excessive eccentric contraction of the biceps brachii. The diagnosis can be made clinically in the setting of complete tears with a palpable biceps tendon on hook testing, in which the patient actively flexes the elbow to 90° and attempts to fully supinate the forearm while the examiner uses an index finger to attempt to hook the lateral edge of the biceps brachii tendon. The test is negative if the examiner is unable to hook the torn tendon; however, a partial tear or an intact lacertus fibrosis may result in a false positive test, thus, MRI studies can be used to discern between complete and partial tears when this is equivocal. Treatment most commonly involves anterior single-incision repair performed between the interval formed by the brachioradialis and pronator teres, which has been shown to reduce the incidence of heterotopic ossification and synostosis seen with the double-incision technique. Though injury to the LABCN is the most common overall complication seen with this technique, injury to the superficial radial nerve (SRN) and PIN can also occur, with injury to the SRN being the second most common cutaneous nerve injured.

*Grewal et al. randomized 47 patients to have single-incision distal biceps tendon repair with two suture anchors and 44 patients to have two-incision transosseous suture repairs.* **The authors reported** that the single-incision technique had a higher rate of transient neuropraxia to the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, affecting 19 of 47 in the suture anchor group and three of 43 in the transosseous group. **They concluded** no significant differences in outcomes between the single and double-incision distal biceps repair techniques other than a 10% advantage in final flexion strength with the latter, with most complications occurring with a significantly greater prevalence in the single-incision group.

*Cain et al. retrospectively reviewed 198 consecutive surgical repairs of the distal biceps tendon and* **noted** a 36% overall complication rate, including 26% paresthesia of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, 6% paresthesia of the sensory branch of the radial nerve, 2% superficial infection, 4% injury to the posterior interosseous nerve, 3% symptomatic heterotopic ossification, and 2% re-rupture. **They concluded** that although minor complications were common after distal biceps tendon repair, most were sensory nerve injuries that resolved with time.

*Banerjee et al. reviewed the high complication rate seen following distal biceps re-fixation with cortical buttons.* *The authors evaluated 27 male patients after an average of 36.1 months following distal biceps tendon repair with cortical button technique and found that nine patients had 14 different complications including four transient lesions of the posterior interosseous nerve, two persistent lesions of the superficial branch of the radial nerve, one symptomatic massive heterotopic ossification and one disengaged cortical button.* **They concluded** that there is a high complication rate of distal biceps tendon repair with the cortical button technique, which resulted in inferior functional results and satisfaction.
