## Question

A 12-year-old girl presents with worsening left buttock pain, lower back pain, and a limp for approximately 1 week. She denies any inciting events. On physical examination, the left hip has full range of motion without significant pain. However, severe posterior pain is elicited with FABER testing. She is febrile with a temperature of 102° F (38.8 C), blood pressure of 125/83 mm Hg, and heart rate of 74 beats per minute. Labs are remarkable for WBC count of 14,000 cells/mm^3 (reference <11,000 cells/mm^3) with a significant left-shift. Blood cultures demonstrate gram-positive cocci on gram stain. Final cultures are pending. Current imaging is shown in figures A-C. What is the best initial treatment option for this patient?

## Options

1. Immediate ultrasound-guided aspiration of the left hip
2. Immediate irrigation and debridement of the left hip
3. Initiate IV broad spectrum IV antibiotics and monitor for clinical improvement
4. Initiate ketorolac and monitor for improvement of likely transient tenosynovitis
5. Immediate irrigation and debridement of the left sacroiliac joint

## Correct option

**3. Initiate IV broad spectrum IV antibiotics and monitor for clinical improvement**

## Answer explanation

The patient has septic sacroiliitis of the left SI joint as manifested by positive blood gram-stain, provocative maneuvers (FABER testing), and pelvis MRI. The ideal treatment option for this would be broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and monitoring for clinical improvement.

Septic sacroiliitis is a rare cause of infectious lower back pain and usually has a nonspecific clinical presentation. Patients usually complain of nonspecific lower back pain and hip pain with possible radicular symptoms, depending on the presence of inflammation compressing the L5 nerve root. Historically, nuclear medicine studies would aid in the diagnosis, but magnetic resonance imaging has become the study of choice. Typically, T2 sequence hyperintensity surrounding the SI joint, with or without the presence of fluid, suggests the diagnosis in the setting of elevated inflammatory markers and leukocytosis. First-line treatment involves empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics that are scaled-down once speciation and sensitivities are obtained from cultures. Patients that fail IV antibiotics and those that have large abscesses are indicated for surgical debridement.

*Aprin and Turen reviewed the diagnosis and treatment of pyogenic sacroiliitis in pediatric patients.* **They recommend** performing a thorough physical exam including the FABER test in addition to blood cultures, direct aspiration, and technetium scans to aid in the diagnosis. The authors described initial treatment consisting of IV antibiotics and surgical debridement reserved for refractory patients and those with advanced sacroiliitis.

*Osman and Govender retrospectively studied 31 patients treated for septic sacroiliitis in South Africa.* **They reported** that the presenting complaints were vague and most consisted of buttock pain, low back pain, and difficulty walking with 14/31 patients diagnosed with tuberculosis. Of the patients treated, only two required open incision and drainage for a large buttock abscess due to tuberculosis sacroiliitis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient's hip ultrasound and physical exam do not suggest hip pathology. As such, a hip aspiration will likely not yield any diagnostic findings.
Answer 2: The patient does not have physical exam findings suggestive of septic arthritis of the left hip, which does not necessitate an immediate I&D.
Answer 4: The patient is unlikely to have transient tenosynovitis based on laboratory findings (significant leukocytosis) and MRI findings (fluid in the left SI joint). Administration of NSAIDs is unlikely to change the course of this patient's infection.
Answer 5: First-line treatment for septic sacroiliitis involves IV antibiotics with surgical debridement reserved for patients who have failed medical treatment or in the presence of a large abscess.
