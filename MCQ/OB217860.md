## Answer explanation

Figure A demonstrates AP radiographs of an allograft-prosthesis construct (APC), which is indicated in Paprosky type IV femoral defects (Figure E).

The Paprosky classification is used as an objective assessment of bone loss in order to provide reconstruction options during revision total hip arthroplasty (THA). The classification is based on the location of femoral bone loss (metaphyseal or diaphyseal), degree of remaining support of the proximal femur (i.e., degree of cancellous bone loss), and the amount of isthmus remaining for diaphyseal fixation. Patients with Type IV defects have complete loss of the femoral isthmus, and biologic fixation is unlikely. Options include the use of an allograft prosthetic composite (APC), a long cemented stem, impaction grafting with a long cemented femoral component, or proximal femoral replacement (PFR).

*Viste et al. performed a retrospective review of 44 patients with bone loss classified as Paprosky IIIB or IV who underwent revision THA using a PFR.* **They reported** improvement in Harris Hip Score (42.8 to 68.5) and survivorship free of any revision or removal of an implant of 86% at five years and 66% years at ten years. **They also reported** that 12 patients (27%) had a complication including six with a dislocation. **They concluded** that PFRs provide a useful salvage option for patients, particularly the elderly with massive proximal femoral bone loss who require revision THA.

*Klein et al. performed a retrospective study on the outcomes of revision THA with the use of a PFR in a cohort of 21 patients who had a Vancouver type-B3 periprosthetic fracture.* **They reported** that at the time of the latest follow-up (mean, 3.2 years), all but one of the patients were able to walk and had minimal to no pain. Complications included persistent wound drainage that was treated with incision and drainage (two hips), dislocation (two hips), refracture of the femur distal to the stem (one hip), and acetabular cage failure (one hip). **They concluded** that despite a relatively high complication rate, PFR is a viable option for the treatment of periprosthetic fractures in older patients with severe bone deficiency.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Figure B demonstrates a Paprosky type II femoral defect. This could be treated with an extensively porous-coated femoral stem.
Answer 2: Figure C demonstrates a Paprosky type I femoral defect. Primary THA components would be appropriate, as would an extensively porous-coated femoral stem.
Answer 3: Figure D demonstrates a Paprosky type IIIB femoral defect (note that there is an intact isthmus, which is denoted by the two lines, but <4cm). While an APC could be used, other options are better suited including extensively porous-coated cylindrical stems, modular tapered stems with splines (e.g., circumferential fluted projections around the tapered stem that confer rotational stability of the implant), and cemented stems with impaction bone grafting.
Answer 5: Figure F demonstrates a Paprosky type IIIA femoral defect with >4 cm of isthmus remaining for diaphyseal fixation (denoted by white lines). This could be managed with an extensively porous-coated cylindrical stem, modular tapered stem with or without splines, or a cemented stem with impaction bone grafting.
