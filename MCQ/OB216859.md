## Question

A 64-year-old male presents to your clinic with a loss of range of motion of his right shoulder. He notes that he underwent an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) on this shoulder approximately 10 years prior. His postoperative course was originally uncomplicated, but notes that he has had progressive loss of range of motion over the past 2 years. Infection work-up is negative, and a CT-scan demonstrates complete tearing with retraction and grade 3 fatty atrophy of both his supraspinatus and infraspinatus. What is the most common cause of early revision following the appropriate surgical treatment for this patient?

## Options

1. Acromion Fracture
2. Axillary Nerve Neuropraxia
3. Infection
4. Instability
5. Scapular Notching

## Correct option

**4. Instability**

## Answer explanation

The most common cause of early revision reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is component instability (Answer 4).

rTSA is the preferred revision surgical procedure for a patient with a prior anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) with an irreparable rotator cuff tear. While patients with a repairable rotator cuff tear in the setting of an aTSA may be treated with rotator cuff repair, the restoration and fatty degeneration encountered in this patient makes repair unlikely to be successful. A rTSA obviates the need for an intact rotator cuff, as it relies on the deltoid to generate movement. While this is a reliable procedure with regards to restoration of range of motion following failed primary aTSA, there is an increased complication rate in the revision setting. Overall, the most common cause of early revision is component instability.

*Patel et al. reported on the outcomes of rTSA for failed aTSA.* *Their study population consisted of 28 patients with a mean follow-up of 40.7 months.* **They note** that patients treated with rTSA demonstrated a statistically significant increase in ASES, UCLA, and SST scores, and a decrease in pain at final follow-up. Patients demonstrated a 64° increase in forward flexion at final follow-up. Overall, 67.9% of patients reported good or excellent outcomes.

*Walker et al. also reported on the outcomes of rTSA for failed aTSA.* *Their study included 24 consecutive patients who were treated with a revision rTSA for failed aTSA with minimum of two-years follow-up.* Average patient age at time of revision surgery was 68 years. 19/24 (79.2%) of patients were revised for glenohumeral instability. Their patients, on average, demonstrated an increase in shoulder function and decrease in pain, with a marked improvement in range of motion. The authors did note, however, that the complication rate (22.7%) was higher than that seen in primary rTSA.

*Chalmers et al. provide a review on revision rTSA, including an overview of reasons for failure and subsequent revision.* **They note** that overall glenohumeral instability is the most common cause of early failure following rTSA, and that other etiologies including infection, fracture and nerve injury are far less common. **They also note** that the complication rate is increased in the revision setting when compared to primary rTSA.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3: these are much less common causes of early failure than glenohumeral instability.
Answer 5: while the progression of scapular notching may portend deterioration of range of motion and an increased risk of long-term failure, it is an uncommon cause of early failure.
