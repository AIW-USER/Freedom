## Question

A 32-year-old male returns to the clinic two years after undergoing fixation of a humeral shaft fracture. At the time of injury, the patient was noted to have a radial nerve palsy and was subsequently lost to follow-up, and he continues to report the inability to extend his wrist or fingers. He is subsequently sent for nerve conduction studies, which demonstrate absent nerve conduction past the zone of injury. Which of the following treatments would be most appropriate?

## Options

1. Radial nerve neurolysis with primary coaptation
2. Radial nerve neurolysis with sural nerve autografting
3. Isolated anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) transfer to extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB)
4. AIN transfer to ECRB with nerve to flexor carpi radialis (FCR) transfer to posterior interosseous nerve (PIN)
5. Pronator teres transfer to ECRB, flexor carpi ulnar transfer to extensor digitorum communis, palmaris longus transfer to extensor pollicis longus

## Correct option

**5. Pronator teres transfer to ECRB, flexor carpi ulnar transfer to extensor digitorum communis, palmaris longus transfer to extensor pollicis longus**

## Answer explanation

This 32-year-old male sustained a humeral shaft fracture, and was subsequently lost to follow-up, only found to have a radial nerve disruption many months following the injury. Because his nerve injury occurred greater than a year ago, tendon transfers, including pronator teres transfer to ECRB, flexor carpi ulnar transfer to extensor digitorum communis, palmaris longus transfer to extensor pollicis longus, would be the most appropriate treatment (Answer 5).

Radial nerve palsies are among the most commonly seen peripheral nerve injuries, having an overall incidence between 8 and 15%. The general consensus for the early management of radial nerve palsies is conservative, as the vast majority of these injuries are neurapraxic and 85-90% experience a return of function within two to three months. For those with absent return of function, EMG/NCS is obtained around three months to further characterize the nature of the nerve injury. For those exhibiting neurotmetic lesions/conduction blocks, exploration is required, with direct coaptation ideal but often not applicable given the typical course of treatment. As such, other options, including autografting or nerve transfers, pose as the next best options. For lesions greater than 12 months, tendon transfers remain the best option because of their reliability in restoring function. Furthermore, the viability and function of the muscles within the nerve's myotome are irreversibly compromised.

*Patterson et al. performed a retrospective review examining the functional outcomes of 46 patients (16 with nerve transfers, 30 with tendon transfers) who sustained radial nerve palsies at a mean follow-up of 1.28 years.* While both groups experienced significant improvements in Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) and quality-of-life (QOL) scores, as well as grip and pinch strength, only grip strength in the nerve transfer group was found to be significantly better. **The authors conclude** both tendon and nerve transfers are viable options for patients with radial nerve palsies.

*Bertelli retrospectively reviewed nerve versus tendon transfers in 27 patients with radial nerve palsies, with the endpoints being wrist, thumb, and finger extension, as well as grip strength.* Better recovery was observed in those undergoing nerve transfer, as more patients experienced the ability to extend their fingers and thumb fully. Regardless of group, recovery of EPL was equally poor, with an average 30-degree lag. **The authors conclude** nerve transfer appears to outperform tendon transfers, though recovery of the thumb is limited, regardless of intervention.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: these options would be indicated in acute nerve transections, with the size of the defect determining the need for grafting.
Answers 3 & 4: nerve transfers are viable options, but not ideal in scenarios where nerve palsies have been present for over 12 months.
