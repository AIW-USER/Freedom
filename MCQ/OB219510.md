## Question

A 63-year-old male presents with left knee osteoarthritis with symptoms recalcitrant to conservative measures. He undergoes a cemented total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and does well initially. However, three years after surgery he has worsening knee pain with updated imaging shown in Figure A. Appropriate workup effectively rules out periprosthetic infection and he undergoes a single-stage revision surgery. Which of the following potentially increases the risk for this type of TKA failure?

![[q219510-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Performing range of motion and gentle ligamentous stability testing after component implantation but before the cement fully cures
2. Donning fresh gloves before handling final implants to minimize lipid contamination on the implant undersurface
3. Suctioning and drying the bone surface before inserting and impacting the final tibial tray into place
4. Applying cement to both the implant undersurface and also directly on the bone before component implantation
5. Using a tibial tray with a roughened undersurface rather than a smooth, polished undersurface

## Correct option

**1. Performing range of motion and gentle ligamentous stability testing after component implantation but before the cement fully cures**

## Answer explanation

This patient’s TKA underwent aseptic loosening of the tibial tray with varus collapse. Performing range of motion and ligamentous stability testing before allowing the cement to fully cure has been associated with lower tibial tray pull-out strength and potentially higher rates of tibial tray aseptic loosening (Answer 1).

Aseptic loosening following primary TKA is recognized as the most common modality for long-term (>2 years from initial surgery) failure. While contemporary highly cross-linked polyethylene designs have greatly diminished osteolysis and wear-related failures, aseptic loosening remains prevalent. Increased attention is being given to other contributing factors for aseptic loosening, including polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement manufacturing and intraoperative handling. PMMA cement has been shown to have diminished mechanical properties when there is lipid contamination on the interface between the cement and the TKA implant and/or the patient’s native bone. While there is no standardized cementing technique, limiting lipid contamination along the implant-cement and bone-cement interfaces and allowing adequate time for the cement to fully cure collectively maximize the PMMA cement’s material properties.

*Martin and colleagues performed a retrospective review of 149 patients who underwent revision TKA for aseptic tibial loosening to determine the location and pattern of fixation failure.* **They report** that the cause for aseptic failure was found at the implant-cement interface (94%) rather than failure at the cement-bone interface (6%). Once loosening occurred, 64% of patients developed a varus collapse pattern compared to 35% loosening without angulation. **The authors concluded** that improvements in cementing techniques and implant undersurface design may mitigate the risk of aseptic loosening, especially in high-risk risk overly-active patients or those with high BMI.

*Martin and colleagues performed a cadaveric study comparing tibial tray pull-out strength when (1) the tibial component was held without motion following impaction and (2) when the implant was taken through gentle range of motion and stability assessment only 7 minutes after cement mixing.* **The investigators reported** the average pull-out strength for the no-motion cohort (5462 N) significantly exceeded the motion cohort (4473N; p=0.001). Further, **they found** that the amount of lipid contamination on the undersurface of the tibial tray inversely correlated with pull-out strength. **The authors concluded** that knee motion during cement polymerization is associated with significant decreases in tibial implant fixational strength.

Figure A demonstrates this patient’s early and long-term (>2 years) post-operative radiographic imaging with tibial tray aseptic loosening (specifically at the implant-bone interface) with varus collapse.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Donning fresh gloves (i.e. removing contaminated gloves with blood/fat from the surgical approach) before handling final implants is considered one method to minimize lipid contamination along the implant-cement interface.
Answer 3: Suctioning and drying the bone surface before inserting the final tibial tray into place helps prevent lipid contamination along the bone-cement interface.
Answer 4: Applying cement to the bone surface and a separate cement layer to the implant surfaces has been shown as a unique method with the potential to limit lipid contamination (PMID 30648489).
Answer 5: Using a tibial tray with a roughened undersurface rather than a smooth, polished undersurface helps improve the overall surface area of the implant-cement interface and has demonstrated lower rates of aseptic loosening.
