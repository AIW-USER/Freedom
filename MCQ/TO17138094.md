Chapter: Nerve Injury MCQs — Radial Nerve Palsy

## Question

A patient landed up with Fracture shaft of humerus. Post fracture he landed up with complete RNP. Appropriate management plan for this patient would be (FNB 2022)

## Options

1. Radial nerve exploration with plating
2. Functional brace application and nerve exploration if no recovery at 3 weeks
3. Hanging cast application and nerve exploration if no recovery at 3 months
4. Nailing for the fracture and observation for the nerve recovery

## Correct option

**3. Hanging cast application and nerve exploration if no recovery at 3 months**

## Answer explanation

Answer is (c)

In a patient with Fracture shaft humerus with intact Radial nerve: -

Initial immobilization begins with a hanging arm cast or coaptation splint. A transition is made to a functional fracture brace based on patient comfort (mostly 2-4 weeks). Patient compliance, acceptable reduction, adequate healing potential, and healthy soft tissues are required for nonoperative management to be successful. Contraindications for functional bracing include massive bone or soft- tissue loss, failure of closed reduction, and inability to maintain acceptable fracture alignment (like in segmental fractures etc).

Fracture shaft humerus with RNP: -

In general, a radial nerve palsy may be partial or complete. Complete palsies occur in approximately 50% to 68% of cases.

Primary nerve palsies identified during the initial evaluation of the fracture occur at the time of the injury. Ten percent to 20% of nerve palsies develop during the course of treatment. These are referred to as secondary radial nerve palsies. They are thought to occur from nerve entrapment at fracture site, that’s how, even unreamed nailing of the fracture could infrequently cause permanent nerve damage.

Although most authors recommend conservative management based on the experience that RNP associated with humerus fractures often resolves spontaneously; for instance, a primary RNP following a closed humeral shaft fracture has been reported to recover in > 70%, it still remains debated as to what the best treatment strategy for RNP associated with humeral shaft fractures should be.

To simplify, the management of a humeral shaft fracture with a radial nerve injury can precisely be divided into 3 categories: no exploration, early exploration, and late exploration.

No Exploration

Many researchers have emphasized that, in most cases, the radial nerve palsy caused by a shaft fracture is a contusion and, therefore, exploration of the nerve is unnecessary. Pollock et al reported that only 12% of injured nerves were transacted in routine exploration. In general, spontaneous recovery is likely to occur in most cases if the fracture is closed and the palsy develops at the time of the injury.

Early Exploration

Indications for early exploration cited in the literature include an open fracture, vascular injury, radial nerve deficit after attempted closed reduction (secondary nerve palsy), high-velocity gunshot wounds, sharp or penetrating injury, high suspicion of nerve laceration with spiral oblique fractures, and severe soft- tissue injury.

Late Exploration

If a patient does not show signs of improvement by 7 months, then there is no advantage to waiting any longer for spontaneous recovery. Proceeding with exploration of the radial nerve after 3 to 6 months of expectant management is an appropriate strategy. It is especially prudent to proceed with exploration before 6 months, especially in patients with electrophysiologic evidence showing failure of reinnervation patterns 3 months after injury and with no clinical signs of recovery.
