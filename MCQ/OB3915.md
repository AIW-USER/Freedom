## Question

A 19-year-old male injured his ankle while playing basketball 2 months ago. He describes that a defender fell onto his planted foot and as he was turning the opposite way. He has nagging pain during ambulation that worsens with more dynamic maneuvers, but does improve when he wears a tight circumferential wrap just above the ankle joint. Standard weightbearing ankle radiographs are unremarkable. Which structure is most likely injured?

## Options

1. Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)
2. Anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL)
3. Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL)
4. Inferior peroneal retinaculum (IPR)
5. Posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL)

## Correct option

**2. Anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL)**

## Answer explanation

This mechanism of injury in addition to his history is consistent with an anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL) injury destabilizing the syndesmosis.

Syndesmotic injuries take many forms, ranging from purely ligamentous (high-ankle sprain) to fracture-dislocations. This articulation is composed of many ligamentous structures that all contribute to rotational, translational, and axial stability. The AITFL spans the tibiofibular articulation, originating from Chaput's tubercle on the anterolateral tibia, and inserting on Wagstaffe's tubercle of the fibula. Injury to one or all of the syndesmotic ligaments will cause the widening of the tibiofibular relationship and alter the ankle mortise function. A common mechanism of injury for isolated ligamentous syndesmotic injuries is forced external rotation of the foot, or if the foot is fixed while the rest of the lower extremity internally rotates (as in the question stem). Brief immobilization followed by progressive weightbearing is often successful in treating these injuries but when this fails and persistent instability is seen and confirmed with physical exam and imaging, surgical stabilization is indicated. These surgical techniques vary but all provide stable fixation of the fibula into the incisura of the tibia (the same temporary effect that circumferential taping provides).

*Zalavras and Thordarson described the management of syndesmotic injuries and their contribution to ankle stability.* **They discussed** that sequential sectioning of the syndesmosis ligaments led to increasing instability, yet this instability increased immensely when the deltoid ligament was also transected. This highlights the critical importance of the deltoid in ankle mortise stability.

*Nelson described his technique for restoration of the syndesmosis.* *He prefers to repair the AITFL when possible, or reconstruct this articulation with either 4th EDL tendon autograft or with a suture repair.* His case series notes anatomic restoration of the syndesmosis with no hardware requiring removal.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1, 3, and 5- These are involved in the more common low-ankle sprains, usually due to an inversion injury. Variable degrees of ecchymosis and swelling occur following these events.
Answer 4- This is implicated with peroneal tendon injuries, again usually seen following an inversion event, with tendon subluxation reproducible on an exam.
