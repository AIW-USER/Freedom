Chapter: Trauma MCQs — Coronoid Fractures

## Question

A 32 years male fell on the ground while playing hockey and dislocated his elbow. After close reduction his AP and lateral views of elbow joint shows subtle coronoid fracture with intact radial head. CT of elbow joint further shows anteromedial coronoid fracture. On examination the arm has crepitus and pain with attempted motion with the arm abducted from the side. Which of the following is likely to be associated with this injury pattern?

![[TO17137251-1.png]]

## Options

1. Varus posteromedial rotatory instability of elbow joint
2. Valgus posterolateral rotatory instability of elbow joint
3. PIN palsy
4. Stable elbow

## Correct option

**1. Varus posteromedial rotatory instability of elbow joint**

## Answer explanation

Varus posteromedial rotatory instability of elbow joint:

Mechanisms of Injury for Posteromedial Rotatory Instability of the Elbow:

 The anteromedial coronoid fracture has been postulated to occur by pronation, varus, and axially directed forces. It is commonly accompanied by avulsion injuries to the LCL and posterior bundle of the MCL, resulting in PMRI. Injury to the anterior bundle of the MCL can also occur with AMC fractures.

Unlike terrible triad injuries, the radial head is usually not fractured in patients with anteromedial coronoid fractures and this is a key to its recognition. An anteromedial coronoid fracture should be suspected in any patient who appears to have a coronoid fracture when a radial head fracture is not present. While some patients present with a frank dislocation, the majority of patients do not: In such individuals, a history of dislocation, “clunking,” or instability with spontaneous reduction can often be elicited.

Classification of Anteromedial Coronoid Fractures: O'Driscoll et al. described three anteromedial coronoid fracture subtypes. Subtype I involve the anteromedial rim only, subtype II involves the rim and tip, and subtype III involves the rim and sublime tubercle, with or without involvement of the tip.

Examination: The elbow should be carefully palpated for signs of tenderness, particularly over the LCL. A patient may complain of crepitus with elbow motion with the arm abducted from the side (which produces a varus stress). This symptom is secondary to malt racking due to varus PMRI.

Involvement of the sublime tubercle (subtype III) results in concomitant valgus instability due to disruption of the ulnar insertion of the MCL.

Treatment: Most patients with PMRI require surgical management to achieve a stable elbow allowing early motion. Residual subluxation or crepitus with motion suggests dynamic incongruity and are indications for surgery. Restoration of varus posteromedial rotatory stability is achieved by internal fixation of the coronoid and repair of the LCL. The sublime tubercle is fixed or the MCL is repaired if injured.

Answer: 1
