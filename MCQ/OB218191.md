## Answer explanation

The patient has isolated atrophy of the infraspinatus (Figure A), most commonly caused by a spinoglenoid notch cyst (Figure F).

Suprascapular neuropathy represents a compressive neuropathy of the suprascapular nerve that occurs either at the suprascapular notch or the spinoglenoid notch, most typically in the form of a cyst. The origin of the cyst frequently involves the extrusion of synovial fluid from a posterior labral tear, though this is not always evident on imaging. Clinically, patients present with either combined supraspinatus and infraspinatus weakness (with or without clinically evident atrophy) or isolated infraspinatus weakness depending on whether the nerve is compressed at the suprascapular notch or the spinoglenoid notch, respectively. When a structural lesion is seen on MRI (Figure F) in the setting of clinical weakness and trophy, surgical treatment can be performed in the form of arthroscopic cyst decompression often with a concomitant labral repair.

*Piasecki et al. provide a review of suprascapular neuropathy.* **The authors note** that it is a relatively uncommon but significant cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction. Because of the tortuous course of the suprascapular nerve from the neck to the posterior shoulder, there are several potential causes of nerve entrapment/site of extrinsic compression to consider; however, **they note** that traction neuropathy may occur following excessive nerve excursion during overhead sports or as a result of massive, retracted rotator cuff tears in older patients, at times making the diagnosis difficult. **They conclude** that discrete nerve compression or failure of nonsurgical measures warrants early surgical intervention, with most patients improving in terms of function and pain relief, though the return of strength and muscle bulk is less predictable.

*Ringel et al. reviewed the clinical features, as well as the preoperative and postoperative electrodiagnostic studies in two professional pitchers with suprascapular neuropathy, and dissected sagittal sections of a cadaver with the arm fixed in the acceleration phase of the pitching motion to demonstrate five possible sites of trauma to the suprascapular nerve.* **They note** that the suprascapular and spinoglenoid notches may not be the only sites of potential trauma to the suprascapular nerve during the pitching motion and propose three additional sites where the suprascapular nerve may be subjected to connective tissue entrapment: (1) at the level of the superior trunk of the brachial plexus, (2) within the fascial network enclosing the subclavius and omohyoid muscles, and (3) within the supraspinous fossa between the base of the coracoid process and the supraspinatus muscle belly.

Figure A represents clinical atrophy of the infraspinatus. Figure B depicts a SLAP tear. Figure C depicts a full-thickness rotator cuff tear. Figure D depicts a subscapularis tear. Figure E depicts a Bankart lesion of the anteroinferior labrum. Figure F represents a large cyst at the level of the spinoglenoid notch.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Though SLAP tears can lead to the formation of compressive cysts, there is no evidence of a cyst on this MRI.
Answer 2: The patient's symptoms and clinical findings are not consistent with a full-thickness rotator cuff tear, which would be highly unusual in this age group in the absence of trauma.
Answer 3: The patient's exam findings are not consistent with a subscapularis tear.
Answer 4: Isolated infraspinatus atrophy is not associated with anteroinferior labral tears (Bankart lesion) or anterior instability.
