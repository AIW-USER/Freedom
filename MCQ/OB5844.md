## Question

A 21-year-old long-distance runner presents in your office after an ankle sprain. In her medication history, she relates that she has been on an albuterol inhaler for wheeze and dyspnoea during training. You refer her to a sports physiologist. Test procedure results are shown in Figure A. Continuous laryngoscopy during exercise (CLE) results are shown in Figure B. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB, or exercise-induced asthma)
2. Supraglottic level exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction (EILO)
3. Glottic level exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction (EILO)
4. Both exercise-induced bronchoconstriction and EILO
5. Cardiac condition

## Correct option

**3. Glottic level exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction (EILO)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has negative bronchoprovocation tests (BPT) and bronchodilator reversibility tests (BDR), and positive CLE. She has EILO. Figure B shows closure of the vocal cords, corresponding with glottic level EILO.

There is a poor correlation between asthma-like symptoms and evidence of EIB. Many athletes diagnosed with EIB have little evidence to support this diagnosis because many conditions that mimic asthma also produce a wheeze and dyspnoea from temporary obstruction at the level of the larynx. The term EILO describes this, and includes exercise-induced laryngomalacia and vocal cord dysfunction. It is a spectrum of disease ranging from mild cross-sectional area decrease at the laryngeal inlet, to complete obstruction, from supraglottic (arytenoids, supporting structures) to glottic (vocal cords). The gold standard for diagnosis is CLE.

*Nielsen et al. retrospectively reviewed 88 athletes worked up for asthma with CLE.* **They found** that 35% of patients had EILO. Specifically, 23% had EILO, 26% had asthma, 12.5% had both asthma and EILO, and 39% had neither. In each group, approximately 60% were on asthma medication. **They concluded** that EILO is prevalent in athletes and an important differential diagnosis. It can mimic asthma, and is not discernible by clinical appraisal alone.

*Eichenberger et al. performed a systematic review to evaluate the effect of exercise on airway hyperreactivity in asthma.* **They found** 17 studies involving 599 patients. **They found** that exercise improved quality of life, bronchial hyerresponsiveness, EIB and FEV1 compared with control conditions. **They recommend** exercise as supplementary therapy to medication.

Figure A shows the results of BDR (beta2-agonist reversibility test), BPT (methacholine challenge, mannitol challenge and eucapnic voluntary hyperventilation). With positive BPT, there will be significant reduction in FEV1. I asthmatics, FeNO results are high. Figure B shows glottic level EILO on CLE with glottic closure during exercise (right panel) compared with pre-exercise (left panel). Illustration A shows the difference between supraglottic and glottic EILO and the different grades of each.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: EILO can mimic asthma. Asthmatics (at rest, or induced by exercise), will manifest positive BDR and BPT.
Answer 2: Supraglottic level EILO will show laryngeal closure ABOVE the level of the glottis (Illustration A).
Answer 4: There is no test evidence of EIB. This patient has EILO alone.
Answer 5: Cardiac conditions are a valid differential. However there is sufficient CLE evidence to make a diagnosis of EILO.
