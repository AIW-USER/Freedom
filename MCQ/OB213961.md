## Question

A 40-year-old male presents to your clinic for evaluation of 8 months of left shoulder pain and weakness after a fall while skiing. MRI studies are shown in Figures A and B. Which of the following physical exam findings would be highly probable in this patient?

![[q213961-b-axial-mri-t2-2nd-cut.jpg]]

## Options

1. Increased passive flexion of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder
2. Increased active internal rotation of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder
3. Increased passive extension of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder
4. Increased passive external rotation of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder
5. Increased passive abduction of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder

## Correct option

**4. Increased passive external rotation of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder**

## Answer explanation

The patient's MRI is consistent with medial subluxation of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) which is indicative of a subscapularis tear. Physical exam would likely reveal increased passive external rotation of the left shoulder compared to the right shoulder.

Acute subscapularis tears are most commonly noted in the younger patient population generally due to trauma or a fall. Mechanism of injury is typically a forceful hyperabduction/external rotation force to the shoulder. Symptoms generally mimic subcoracoid impingement with anterior shoulder pain with various degrees of abduction, adduction, and rotation. Patients will also present with weakness in internal rotation, increased passive external rotation of the shoulder, a positive lift-off test, and a positive belly press test. MRI studies will typically show medial subluxation of the LHBT due to the lack of constraint of the tendon within the bicipital groove.

*Tokish et al. performed an electromyography (EMG) study to determine the validity of the belly-press test as a clinical test for the subscapularis muscle.* *The authors obtained EMG data of 7 muscles (upper subscapularis, lower subscapularis, infraspinatus, latissimus dorsi, teres major, pectoralis major, and supraspinatus) in 16 healthy volunteers.* **They noted** the belly-press and lift-off tests activated the upper and lower portions of the subscapularis muscle greater than the other muscles. Furthermore, the belly-press test was found to activate the upper subscapularis muscle significantly more than the lift-off test. **The authors conclude** that these findings may improve the clinical testing and assessment of the subscapularis muscle.

*Arai et al. performed a cadaveric study to clarify the functional anatomy regarding the stabilization of the LHBT.* *The authors evaluated 20 embalmed shoulders anatomically and noted that the most superior part of the subscapularis tendon was attached to the upper margin of the lesser tuberosity and extended as a thin tendinous slip to the fovea capitis of the humerus.* **The authors conclude** that to keep the LHBT in place and stabilized, the tension in the superior glenohumeral ligament and the buttress support of the most superior insertion point of the subscapularis from behind the ligament are necessary.

Figures A and B reveal T2-weighted axial MRI that reveals medial LHBT subluxation in the setting of a subscapularis tear.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There would be no expected increase in passive flexion of the left shoulder with a subscapularis tear.
Answer 2: There would be an expected DECREASE in active internal rotation of the left shoulder with a subscapularis tear.
Answer 3: There would be no expected increase in passive extension of the left shoulder with a subscapularis tear.
Answer 5: There would be no expected increase in passive abduction of the left shoulder with a subscapularis tear.
