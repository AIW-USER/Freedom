## Question

A 24-year-old construction worker presents to your clinic with a 2-month history of worsening pain, erythema, and swelling of his wrist. He previously underwent application of a wrist-spanning external fixator for a distal radius fracture, which was removed 6 months ago. ## Answer explanation

This patient has signs and symptoms concerning for an underlying infection, in this case, a Brodie’s abscess (Figure E, Answer 5).

Chronic osteomyelitis may manifest as a Brodie’s abscess, which is more common in children but can be seen in adults. This patient's relatively recent history of a wrist-spanning external fixator may be a potential source of the infection. Chronic osteomyelitis may lead to the formation of a sequestrum of necrotic bone just deep to the elevated periosteum. A new layer of bone (involucrum) formed by the periosteum surrounds the sequestrum, walling off the necrotic bone and the infection. Chronic abscesses, on the other hand, may persist and become surrounded by sclerotic bone and fibrous tissue, manifesting as a Brodie’s abscess. Treatment consists of a trial of antibiotic therapy; however, this may not be effective in chronic, walled-off abscesses which may ultimately require open debridement.

*Johnson and Bindra published a 2008 case report of a Brodie’s abscess following a percutaneous pinning of a distal radius 4 years prior.* The patient underwent open intralesional curettage of the surrounding granulation tissue, and the defect was injected with vancomycin-containing calcium sulfate bone cement. The cement was gradually resorbed and replaced with bone, and at 13 months the cavity was completely replaced with bone except for a residual 5-mm defect in the center. At a final follow-up of 2 years, CRP had returned to normal and the patient remained symptom-free.

van der Naald published a 2019 systematic review of the Brodie’s abscess literature, aiming to describe diagnostic methods, therapeutic consequences, and outcomes. The authors found that most cases were afebrile, had an insidious onset, and occurred in mostly young males, with a median duration of symptoms before diagnosis of 12 weeks. A vast majority of cases underwent surgical intervention with postoperative IV antibiotics, with favorable outcomes. Staphylococcus aureus was the pathogen most often found in the culture.

Figure A is an MRI of an aneurysmal bone cyst. Figure B is an MRI of Kienbock’s disease. Figure C is an MRI depicting an attritional rupture of the FPL due to a Mannerfelt lesion. Figure D is an MRI of a ganglion cyst. Figure E is an X-ray of a Brodie’s abscess.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1: In the setting of a recent external fixator, elevated CRP, and worsening erythema and swelling, an aneurysmal bone cyst is not the most likely diagnosis.
Answer 2: Given the patient’s history and presentation, Kienbock’s disease is not the most likely diagnosis.
Answer 3: The patient has full active range of motion of all digits, therefore an attritional rupture of the FPL is not the most likely diagnosis.
Answer 4: Ganglion cysts are usually non-painful and do not present with erythema and swelling. Additionally, in the setting of a recent external fixator and elevated CRP, a ganglion cyst is not the most likely diagnosis.
