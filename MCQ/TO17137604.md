## Question

34-year-old man suffers a posterior hip dislocation associated with a femoral head fracture below the fovea. Patient undergoes closed reduction with adequate anesthesia, the hip remains stable. Radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans are obtained and reveal an 8mm displacement of fracture fragment. The next step in management is:

## Options

1. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) via anterior approach
2. Skeletal traction
3. Excision of fragment
4. Open reduction and internal fixation via posterior approach

## Correct option

**1. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) via anterior approach**

## Answer explanation

Anterior approach will be better here
In Pipkin type 1/2 fractures Anterior approach is preferred. In Pipkin type 4, generally you go from posterior.

Hip dislocation

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint that is inherently stable because of its bony geometry and strong ligaments, allowing it to resist significant increases in mechanical stress. Anatomic components contributing to the hip's stability include the depth of the acetabulum, the labrum, joint capsule, muscular support, and surrounding ligaments.

Causes

Dislocations of the hip can be classified as congenital or acquired. Congenital dislocations result from the physiologic position of the fetus in utero pressed against the abdominal wall of the mother, with the additional component of the posterior force acting against a dysplastic hip joint in flexion. Acquired hip dislocations are either native dislocations or dislocations after total hip replacement.

Epidemiology

Most common cause of hip dislocation includes high velocity injuries like RTA mainly dashboard injury. Associated injuries include knee ligament injury, femoral shaft fractures and ankle and foot fractures.

Anterior hip dislocation

Anterior dislocations occur in approximately 10% to 15% of all traumatic hip dislocations with the femoral head being displaced to come to lie anterior to the coronal plane of the acetabulum. This results in the leg assuming an extended and externally rotated position.

Epstein described a classification based on whether the femoral head was displaced superiorly (Type I) or inferiorly (Type II), and additionally subdivided the classification depending on the presence of associated fractures either of the femoral head (Type B) or the acetabulum (Type C).

Posterior hip dislocation

Posterior dislocation is by far the most common pattern and usually is the result of a direct longitudinal force applied to the femur and hip (a dashboard type injury). Consequently, the femoral head comes to lie posterior to the acetabulum in the coronal plane with the hip assuming a semi flexed internally rotated position.

Reduction techniques – Posterior dislocation

Allis technique

The patient is in supine position with the physician standing above the patient. The physician applies inline traction on the ipsilateral leg, flexing the ipsilateral knee to 90° while an assistant stabilizes the pelvis against the stretcher for countertraction. Gentle extension of the ipsilateral leg with external rotation as the hip reduces allows the femoral head to enter the acetabulum. An audible sound, or “clunk,” is heard with successful reduction.

Bigelow technique

With the patient in supine position, the physician grasps the ipsilateral limb at the ankle with one hand and places the free hand behind the knee. An assistant applies a downward force on the anterior superior iliac spine for countertraction. The physician applies inline longitudinal traction, flexing the patient's knee to 90°. As the limb reduces, the physician applies gentle extension, abduction, and external rotation for the femoral head to move into the acetabulum.

East Baltimore lift technique

The patient is supine. The physician stands on the affected side, and an assistant stands on the opposite side. The ipsilateral limb is flexed so the hip and knee are at 90°. With the physician and assistant facing the head of the bed, both place one arm underneath the knee of the ipsilateral hip, hooking their arms under the popliteal fossa and resting their hands on each other's shoulders. With the physician stabilizing the pelvis with a free hand, a second assistant applies a downward force while the physician and first assistant apply an inline upward force with extension of their knees. As the limb reduces, the physician can also apply adduction, abduction, and internal and external rotation using the ipsilateral ankle. If only 2 people are available, this technique can still be completed. The physician uses the arm closest to the patient's ipsilateral hip as the pivot and the other arm to grab the ipsilateral leg. The assistant stabilizes the pelvis while helping the physician apply inline traction to the ipsilateral limb by extending the legs until the hip is reduced

Reduction techniques –Anterior hip dislocations typically can be reduced with longitudinal traction, laterally directed force on the thigh, and often internal rotation to complete the reduction. If closed reduction fails, the Smith-Petersen approach is used for open reduction.

It is an easier technique, since anterior wall of acetabulum is shallow, reduction is easier and atraumatic. Technique involves abduction and flexion keeping towel at the knee joint followed by external rotation to reduce the joint.

Allis Leg Extension Method: The patient is supine, and the physician may either climb on the stretcher or stand on the affected side. With an assistant stabilizing the patient's pelvis, the physician grasps the ipsilateral knee and applies inline traction until the hip is reduced. For pubic-type dislocations, hyperextension of the hip is required for reduction.

Reverse Bigelow Method: The patient is supine, and the physician grasps the ipsilateral limb at the ankle with one hand and places the free hand behind the knee. Traction is applied in line with the deformity, and the hip is adducted, internally rotated, and extended.

After reduction, assessment of limb length, ROM and stability is done. If closed reduction fails, open reduction is indicated. Indications for open reduction include hips that have been dislocated for long periods of time, inability to achieve adequate sedation safely in the emergency department, dislocations that are irreducible, fractures of the femoral head or shaft, and persistent instability or redislocation following treatment.

Irreducible posterior hip dislocations can be treated with the Kocher-Langenbeck approach in which the surgeon accesses the posterior structures of the acetabulum by demarcating the posterior superior iliac spine, greater trochanter, and femoral shaft. Anterior hip dislocations can be treated with the Smith-Petersen or Watson-Jones approach in which the surgeon accesses the anterior structures of the acetabulum by demarcating the anterior superior iliac spine, greater trochanter, and femoral shaft.

For femoral head fractures following hip dislocations, emergency closed reduction of hip joint under sedation is performed. Post reduction, stability of the joint is tested. Post reduction repeat X-Ray and CT scan is performed.

Definitive management –

Following closed reduction of the hip, non-operative treatment can be considered for Pipkin type 1 and type 2 fractures if the following criteria are met

- Anatomic or near anatomic reduction <2 mm displacement
- A stable hip
- No interposed fragment preventing a congruent hip reduction

Complications

Minimizing the time to reduction is critical to prevent AVN of the femoral head. If the hip is reduced within 6 hours, the incidence of AVN drops significantly. Avascular necrosis is a risk of traumatic hip dislocation; the risk is between 2 % to 10% if reduced within 6 hours. A 2016 metanalysis of AVN following traumatic hip dislocation found that if the limb is reduced after 12 hours, the odds of developing AVN is 5.6 times greater vs reduction prior to 12 hours. Signs of full blown AVN are seen from 18 months to 2 years hence patient is asked to follow up until 18 months and sports related activities are avoided.

Sciatic nerve injuries are seen in 10-15% of pure posterior dislocations.
