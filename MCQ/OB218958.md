## Options

1. Trial of conservative management with physical therapy and NSAIDs for symptomatic relief
2. Neoadjuvant radiation (50-60 Gy) then repeat MRI after to determine best surgical option
3. Extended intralesional curettage followed by bone-void grafting and prophylactic fixation
4. Proximal humeral resection with megaprosthesis reconstruction
5. Forequarter amputation

## Correct option

**3. Extended intralesional curettage followed by bone-void grafting and prophylactic fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a symptomatic atypical cartilaginous tumor of the proximal humerus, which is best treated with extended intralesional curettage and bone void grafting (+/- prophylactic fixation, Answer 3).

Grade 1 chondrosarcomas of the extremities are now classified as atypical cartilage tumors (ACTs) by the World Health Organization to better reflect their overall very low-malignancy potential. They appear similar to large enchondromas in that ACTs can be sizable (>5cm) and display endosteal scalloping on MRI. However, they are NOT accompanied by aggressive features such as a large extraosseous soft tissue mass, which would be considered diagnostic for high-grade chondrosarcoma. They can be initially treated with observation and serial follow-up in asymptomatic cases. However, any concerning changes in tumor size/appearance or symptomatic cases warrant tumor removal. Due to their recognized low-malignant potential, ACTs are treated with extended intralesional curettage and bone void filling (+/- prophylactic fixation, if needed). This treatment has shown equivalent local recurrence rates to wide resection with much more favorable functional outcomes. Importantly, axial-based (i.e., spine and pelvis) cartilage tumors are considered a separate category and are treated more aggressively due to the poor concordance with biopsy results and higher malignant potential than their appendicular (i.e., arms and legs) counterparts.

*Chen and colleagues performed a systematic review and meta-analysis regarding treating grade 1 chondrosarcomas (ACTs).* **They found** that intralesional resection had lower complications with better functional outcomes with no significant increase in the risk of recurrence and metastasis compared to wide resection procedures. **They concluded** that intralesional resection is safe, function-preserving, and the best surgical option for these patients.

*Wells and colleagues provided an overview of conventional cartilaginous tumors and their respective diagnostic workup and treatment.* *They review the spectrum of cartilage tumors (enchondromas, ACTs, chondrosarcomas) and their respective treatment options.* *They stress that symptomatic enchondromas and ACTs can be treated with curettage and bone void filling, allowing far superior functional outcomes to wide resection and megaprosthesis reconstruction with equivalent oncologic outcomes.* **They conclude** that differentiating between chondroid tumors may be difficult, and any ambiguity warrants evaluation by a musculoskeletal oncology multidisciplinary team.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Asymptomatic large enchondromas and atypical cartilaginous tumors that lack aggressive features (i.e. soft tissue extension or pending pathologic fracture) could be initially managed with a trial of observation or conservative management. This allows for monitoring of the tumor with regular follow-up to ensure no changes occur. However, this patient is symptomatic with activity-limiting pain, which justifies proceeding with operative management.
Answer 2: Many soft tissue sarcomas are treated with neoadjuvant radiation or chemotherapy followed by a repeated MRI to evaluate the best surgical option. These modalities target peripheral cancer cells and allow for higher rates of limb-sparing surgeries. Unfortunately, most cartilaginous tumors are generally not responsive to radiation or chemotherapy.
Answer 4: Resection of the proximal humerus is indicated for sarcomas confined to the bone without extraosseous extension. This resection used to be considered the standard of care for these ACTs confined to the proximal humerus. However, the very low malignant potential of ACTs has shifted surgical treatment towards less aggressive procedures.
Answer 5: Forequarter amputation entails en block removal of the entire upper extremity with the scapula and lateral aspect of the clavicle. This is reserved for tumors that could be potentially cured but could not be treated with limb-sparing surgery. Thankfully, with advances in tendon transfers and megaprosthesis devices, forequarter amputation is rarely indicated.
