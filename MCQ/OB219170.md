## Answer explanation

Figure C depicts bridge plating which is a form of relative stability that leads to secondary bone healing.

The biomechanics of plate utilization are important to understand. When used to provide absolute stability (<2% strain), the plate serves as a construct that heals with primary (intramembranous/Haversian) bone healing. This may be achieved with the use of a lag screw or a compression plate to eliminate micromotion which reduced the strain at the fracture site with high fixation stiffness. Relative stability serves as a construct that heals with secondary (endochondral) healing. The strain rates must be <10%, or fibrous union will predominate. Plate functions are compression, buttress, tension band, antiglide, or bridging. Bridge plating provides relative stability, length, and alignment while preserving the blood supply to the fracture fragments as the fracture site is undisturbed during the operative procedure. This improves secondary bone healing by allowing some motion at the fracture site leading to callus formation, key to secondary bone healing.

*Wenda et al. reviewed bridge-plating advantages in terms of vascularity and bone healing in the treatment of comminuted femoral fractures.* **They reported** that bridge-plating protects the fracture site vascularity during the operative procedure. **They concluded** that indirect reduction of femoral fragments is much easier since the integrity of the surrounding muscles and soft tissue is preserved, the fragments often being reduced simply by traction.

*Beltran et al. reviewed stress modulation as it relates to manipulating bridge plate variables to provide a flexible fixation construct that allows callus formation through uneventful secondary bone healing.* **They reported** that obtaining absolute stability through the anatomic reduction of all fracture fragments comes at the expense of fracture biology. **They concluded** that overly stiff plating constructs are associated with asymmetric callus formation, early implant failure, and fracture nonunion.

Figure A is a distal fibula fracture that was treated with a lag screw and a neutralization plate. Figure B is a plate being used in a neutralization fashion at the metatarsophalangeal joint. Figure C is a femur fracture that is being stabilized with a bridge plate. Figure D is a tibial plateau fracture that is being stabilized with a buttress plate. Figure E is a transverse femur fracture that is being compressed and stabilized with a compression plate.
