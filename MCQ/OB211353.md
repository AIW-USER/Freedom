## Question

A 32-year-old male presents with right hip pain after a motor vehicle accident. Injury radiographs are shown in Figure A, and the patient subsequently undergoes a closed reduction. Current imaging is demonstrated in Figure B. Which of the following is the most reliable method for determining instability of the hip?

![[q211353-a-edit-ap-pelvis.jpg]]
*Figure A*

![[q211353-b-posterior-wall-fracture-edit.jpg]]
*Figure B*

## Options

1. Size of the posterior wall fragment
2. Hip dislocation at the time of the injury
3. Presence of a posterior wall comminution
4. Exam under anesthesia
5. Roof arc angle

## Correct option

**4. Exam under anesthesia**

## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained a right posterior wall acetabular fracture. An examination under anesthesia is the best method by which to determine the stability of the hip.

Posterior wall fractures may occur in isolation or in the presence of a hip dislocation. Many posterior wall fractures can be treated nonoperatively. Open reduction internal fixation is recommended when there is hip instability in the presence of a posterior wall fracture. The most effective method for determining instability is an examination under anesthesia. Radiographic or injury characteristics may suggest the presence of instability but are less sensitive and less reliable predictors.

*Firoozabadi et al. performed a retrospective review of 138 posterior wall acetabular fractures to determine those radiographic parameters most predictive of hip instability.* **The authors reported** the average distance of the cranial exit point of the fracture relative to the dome to be 5.0 mm in the unstable group and 9.5 mm in the stable group, although 23% of unstable fractures were associated with posterior wall fragments less than 20%. **They concluded** that fractures with cranial exit points near the dome were more unstable and that this was a more reliable predictor of instability than fragment size.

*Davis and Moed performed a retrospective study of 15 posterior wall fractures to determine if high-quality AP radiographs and CT scans would reliably predict hip instability.* **The authors reported** a 53% and 52% correct identification rate for unstable hips at two separate time points with an examination under anesthesia as the gold standard. **They concluded** that radiographs and CT alone were insufficient for determining hip stability following posterior wall fractures and recommended that open reduction internal fixation should be performed if there is any doubt.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of the pelvis demonstrating a right posterior hip dislocation in the setting of an associated posterior wall fracture. Figure B is an axial CT scan of the pelvis with a posterior wall fracture and well-reduced femoroacetabular joint.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: While the size of the posterior wall fragment has been shown to suggest instability, examination under anesthesia is a more reliable predictor.
Answer 2: The presence of a hip dislocation at the time of the injury is not correlated with post-reduction hip instability.
Answer 3: Comminution of the posterior wall is an unstable fracture pattern, but does not necessarily connote hip instability.
Answer 5: The roof arc angle is a radiographic measurement which has been correlated with hip stability, though examination under anesthesia remains the gold standard.
