## Question

While covering a collegiate wrestling competition, you are called to the sideline after a wrestler is accidentally kneed in the face by the opposing wrestler. On examination, he has a clear deformity to his nose and bleeding from his nares. Using your pen light, you inspect the septum and realize the patient requires urgent incision and drainage. What complication are you preventing with this intervention?

## Options

1. Deviated Septum
2. Recurrent epistaxis
3. Chondral necrosis
4. Olfactory nerve damage
5. Turbinate swelling

## Correct option

**3. Chondral necrosis**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained nasal trauma with a septal hematoma. Decompression via incision and drainage (I&D) of the hematoma prevents the development of septal cartilage necrosis.

Facial trauma is common in all sports and can affect the ears, nose, eyes, and mouth. While these areas are not typically associated with Orthopedics, it is important to know basic side-line first aid if you are functioning as a sports team physician. Nasal fractures are the most common type of facial fracture, accounting for up to 50% of sports-related fractures. Evaluation will typically demonstrate swelling, deformity, crepitus, and epistaxis. Proper lighting is important to evaluate for cribiform plate involvement and CSF leaks, and septal hematomas. The most common presentation of a septal hematoma is nasal congestion and proper identification is important as it can result in serious issues including septal necrosis and secondary collapse, and septal abscess formation. Necrosis of the septal cartilage results in a saddle nose deformity. Treatment is urgent decompression under local anesthesia.

*Leinhart, Toldi, and Tennison reviewed fascial trauma in sports.* *They provide a concise review of relevant anatomy, followed by a discussion of the most common injuries including lacerations and fractures.* Septal hematomas will often present with nasal obstruction with inspiration and should be treated with I&D and anterior packing. CT is the imaging study of choice for facial fractures. Furthermore, **they report** the importance of full face shields in sports and return to play (RTP). While there is limited evidence-based guidance, **they recommend** RTP at 3 weeks after facial trauma.

*Cannon et al. published a retrospective review of nasal injuries in sports over a 10-year period.* **They found** that basketball (26.4%) and baseball (22.0%) were the most common sports. Nasal bone fractures occurred most often at 64.8% of injuries, followed by contusions (29.7%) and septal injuries (5.5%). The mechanism of trauma was sport dependent: elbow-to-nose trauma most often in basketball, while ball-to-nose contact in baseball/softball, and opponents head-to-nose contact in soccer.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Deviated septum can occur as a sequela of septal injury, however urgent I&D does not prevent this.
Answer 2: Recurrent epistaxis would not be prevented with I&D.
Answer 4: The olfactory nerve branches reside in the superior aspect of the nasal passage in the cribiform plate. Septal hematomas are not associated with olfactory nerve damage, although can create olfactory dysfunction by decreasing air conduction to the olfactory nerves.
Answer 5: Turbinate swelling can occur with facial and nasal trauma, but I&D of the septum would not affect this.
