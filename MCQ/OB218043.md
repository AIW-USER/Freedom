## Question

An 82-year-old female presents to your office with complaints of worsening left shoulder pain and a decline in function over the last 6 months. She previously had an anatomic total shoulder replacement performed 15 years prior and did well until she had a ground-level fall approximately 6 months ago. Which of the following is the most likely cause of failure in this patient?

![[q218043-a-1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Aseptic glenoid loosening
2. Acromial fracture
3. Prosthetic joint infection
4. Humeral component loosening
5. Rotator cuff failure

## Correct option

**5. Rotator cuff failure**

## Answer explanation

This patient likely sustained a rotator cuff tear as suggested by her "high-riding" humerus on radiographs. Conversion to a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) would be the most appropriate treatment for this patient if she elected for surgical intervention.

Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is often used for end-stage glenohumeral arthritis with an intact rotator cuff. Several complications may occur following TSA including aseptic glenoid loosening (the most common cause of TSA failure) and rotator cuff failure. This patient was doing well until a mechanical ground-level fall. Radiographs now reveal a high-riding humerus which is suggestive of a rotator cuff tear. Conversion of the TSA to RSA would be the most predictable treatment option for this patient.

*Sperling et al. reviewed 42 painful shoulder arthroplasties; 21 of which required revision surgery.* **They reported** that in those patients that required revision surgery, there were full-thickness rotator cuff tears in 11 of 21 shoulders; MRI correctly predicted these in 10 of 11 shoulders. **They noted** that full-thickness subscapularis tears were the most common finding (8/11 shoulders). **They concluded** that MRI may be a useful technique with which to determine the integrity of the rotator cuff and residual cartilage and, thus, is potentially a tool in the management of painful shoulder arthroplasty.

*Shields et al. compared outcomes after conversion of TSA to RSA and a matched cohort.* **They reported** that revision of TSA to RSA for component loosening or rotator cuff failure results in function comparable to primary RSA. **They concluded**, however, that more complications, worse subjective shoulder value scores, and lower patient satisfaction should be expected.

Figure A demonstrates a well placed TSA that has a "high-riding" humerus suggestive of a rotator cuff tear.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Aseptic glenoid loosening is the most common cause of TSA failure (30% of primary OA revisions). Risk factors include insufficient glenoid bone stock and rotator cuff deficiency.
Answer 2: There is no evidence of an acromial fracture on imaging. Acromial fractures are more common following RSA.
Answer 3: There is nothing in the patient's history that would suggest infection as the cause of her problem.
Answer 4: Humeral loosening is more common in RA and osteonecrosis. When present, it is important to rule out infection.
