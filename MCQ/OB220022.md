## Question

A 36-year-old recreational pickleball player presents with a finger deformity of his dominant hand that is preventing him from holding his paddle correctly. He notes that he had a metacarpal fracture in the same hand while playing Division II football in college that he treated non-operatively, and he has always had a "weird" finger since that time. On exam, when the metacarpophalangeal joint (MCPJ) of the index finger is flexed, the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) is unable to be passively flexed; however, when you passively extend the index finger MCPJ, flexion of the PIPJ improves. The distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ) is supple and there is no PIPJ hyperextension. Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's finger deformity and how should it be treated?

## Options

1. Boutonniere deformity treated with dynamic splinting or serial casting
2. Central slip injury treated with an extensor turndown or lateral band mobilization
3. Extrinsic tightness treated with tenolysis or fractional extensor lengthening
4. Intrinsic tightness treated with second dorsal interosseous tenotomy
5. Swan neck deformity treated with fowler central slip tenotomy

## Correct option

**3. Extrinsic tightness treated with tenolysis or fractional extensor lengthening**

## Answer explanation

The patient had a remote metacarpal fracture with residual deformity causing extensor tendon adhesions over the index finger resulting in extrinsic tightness. Treatments include tenolysis or extensor tendon fractional lengthening with or without corrective metacarpal osteotomy depending on the degree of residual bony deformity.

Causes of extensor tendon injuries include penetrating mechanisms, blunt trauma, or overuse. In general, disruption of the tendon at different zones results in distinct deformities (Illustration A). For example, disruption of the terminal extensor tendon may result in a "mallet finger" and disruption of the central slip may result in a Boutonniere deformity. Depending on how these injuries are treated, in addition to concomitant surrounding injuries, residual "tightness" may be experienced that limits normal function. This abnormal tightness can be subdivided into "intrinsic" or "extrinsic" tightness. Intrinsic tightness is most commonly due to crush injury to the intrinsic musculature of the hand, resulting in contracture and fibrosis that results in loss of PIPJ flexion in a position of MCPJ extension that improves with MCPJ flexion due to relaxation of the involved interosseous and lumbrical muscles (Bunnell Test). Extrinsic tightness, conversely, is often due to extensor tendon adhesions to surrounding bone, and results in the exact opposite of the exam seen with intrinsic tightness, namely an increase in passive flexion of the PIPJ with extension of the involved PIPJ.

*Suwannakhan et al. performed a cadaveric study assessing extensor tendons and variations of the medial four digits of the hand.* *The authors dissected 100 cadaveric limbs to study the anatomy of the forearm and hand extensor musculature.* **They found** four types of contributions to the index finger for the extensor indicis proprius (EIP), including one new type where the double tendons of the EIP insert volar and radial to the extensor digitorum communis of the index finger (EDC-I). **They also identified** four variant muscles including the extensor medii proprius (EMP), extensor digitorum brevis manus (EDBM), extensor indicis et medii communis (EIMC), and extensor pollicis et indicis (EPI). **The authors concluded** that awareness of the variations on the dorsum of the hand is essential for diagnosis, surgical planning, and treatment of diseased hands.

*Georgiev et al. examined 176 cadaveric upper limbs and* **found** normal anatomy in 84%, with variations of the EIP in the others, which included the presence of an extensor indicis brevis (EIB). In some of these patients, the EIB replaced the EIP. **They concluded** that knowledge of anatomical variations of forearm muscles is important because they can cause clinical syndromes or have implications for reconstructive surgery.

*Arnaout et al. performed an anatomic study examining fractional lengthening of the forearm flexor muscles.* *The authors performed dissections on 20 cadaveric specimens and found that fractional lengthening is dependent on the anatomy of the musculotendinous junction of each individual muscle.* **They concluded** that when considering fractional lengthening of the forearm muscles, differences between the tendons should be considered, and surgeons should be prepared for alternative approaches.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A Boutonniere deformity is caused by central slip disruption and subsequent lateral band volar subluxation resulting in DIPJ hyperextension, not extrinsic tightness as described.
Answer 2: Central slip disruption, as above, results in a Boutonniere deformity, not extrinsic tightness.
Answer 4: Intrinsic tightness results from contracture and fibrosis of the lumbrical muscles and/or interossei, causing reduced PIPJ flexion with MCPJ extension, not the opposite phenomenon described in the vignette.
Answer 5: A swan neck deformity is characterized by dorsal subluxation of the lateral bands and PIP joint hyperextension which can be treated with terminal extensor tenotomy, among other methods.
