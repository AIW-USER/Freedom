## Answer explanation

This patient has an incidental finding of a non ossifying fibroma (NOF) which, in the absence of fracture, is treated with observation. Histologically, NOFs demonstrate fibroblastic spindle cells in a whirled/storiform pattern on a background of fibroblastic connective tissue (Figure D).

NOFs are benign fibrogenic lesions most commonly found in the metaphysis of long bones. The typical patient is usually aged 5 to 15, with a NOF discovered incidentally on radiographs. The diagnosis is made radiographically, with NOFs presenting as a metaphyseal eccentric "bubbly" lytic lesion surrounded by a sclerotic rim. 80% of NOFs are found in the lower extremity, and the distal femur is the most likely location for these lesions. Given the typical age, location, and radiographic appearance, obtaining a complete and accurate history and physical examination is important. Both giant cell tumors and osteosarcoma may mimic a NOF. GCTs are rare in skeletally immature patients, often cross the physis, lack mineralization, and are typically painful. Osteosarcomas are also painful, present with an associated periosteal reaction and mineralized soft-tissue mass, and often have an irregular zone of bony destruction with an ill-defined zone of transition. The first line of treatment for a NOF is observation, as most of these lesions resolve spontaneously as the patient enters his or her 2nd and 3rd decades of life.

*Collier et al. performed a longitudinal study investigating the natural history of benign bone tumors of the extremities in asymptomatic children.* *Their study included 262 patients who were screened with 25,555 radiographs.* **The investigators identified** 35 tumors in 33 patients, including 16 NOFs, eight enostoses, seven osteochondromas, and two enchondromas. Overall, 7/19 (37%) of NOFs resolved during the study period, and **the authors felt** that the remainder were progressing toward resolution at the time of the final radiograph. **The authors concluded** that the prevalence of benign childhood bone tumors in an asymptomatic population was 18.9%, and the most common tumor identified was a NOF.

*Herget et al. investigated the radiographic morphology and life span of NOFs to determine the absence/presence of a critical stage concerning pathologic fracture.* *Their study population consisted of 87 patients with 103 NOFs and a mean follow-up time of 27 months.* Lesions with polycyclic borders on radiographs were at the highest risk of fracture, and 66% of fractures happened through NOFs in the tibia. **The authors concluded** that NOFs with polycyclic borders are at the highest fracture risk. Patients with these types of NOFs should be followed with routine 6-12 month surveillance radiographs until the lesions begin to calcify and resolve.

Figure A demonstrates an eccentric "bubbly" or "lobulated" lytic lesion in the distal femur meta diaphysis. The lesion is polycyclic in appearance and wholly consistent with a NOF. Figure B demonstrates cells with marked nuclear atypia on a background of lacey osteoid consistent with intramedullary osteosarcoma. Figure C demonstrates multiple multinuclear giant cells on a background of fibrous stroma consistent with giant cell tumor. Figure E demonstrates a cystic area with a thin fibrous lining, fibrous tissue, scant giant cells, and occasional hemosiderin pigment consistent with a unicameral bone cyst. Figure F demonstrates chondroblasts arranged in a chickenwire pattern consistent with chondroblastoma.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The treatment of osteosarcoma includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. Observation would not be appropriate for osteosarcoma.
Answer 2: The correct treatment of giant cell tumors ranges from medical management to curettage and reconstruction. Furthermore, the radiographic appearance of a giant cell tumor is a uniform lytic lesion, not a "bubbly" or "lobulated" polycyclic lesion.
Answer 4: Unicameral bone cysts commonly occur in the proximal humerus and will cross the physis on radiographs.
Answer 5: Symptomatic chondroblastomas are treated with curettage and bone grafting, not observation. They, like unicameral bone cysts, cross the physis on radiographs.
