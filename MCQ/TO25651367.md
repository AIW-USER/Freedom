Chapter: Metabolic Bone Disorder MCQs — Osteoporosis and Fragility Fractures

## Question

What is the primary risk associated with the abrupt discontinuation of Denosumab without transitioning to another therapy?

## Options

1. Acute symptomatic hypercalcemia.
2. Multiple vertebral fractures.
3. Osteonecrosis of the jaw.
4. Atypical femoral fractures.

## Correct option

**2. Multiple vertebral fractures.**

## Answer explanation

Upon cessation of Denosumab, there is a rapid rebound in bone turnover markers above baseline, leading to rapid BMD loss and a significantly increased risk of multiple vertebral fractures.

Denosumab (Prolia)

Mechanism: A fully human monoclonal antibody (IgG2) that binds RANKL with high affinity, preventing it from activating its receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors. By neutralising RANKL, denosumab potently inhibits osteoclast differentiation, activation, and survival — producing profound, sustained suppression of bone resorption. Unlike bisphosphonates (which bind to the bone mineral and persist in the skeleton for years), denosumab acts in the circulation and is cleared within 6 months — its effect is completely reversible upon discontinuation.

Dosing: 60 mg subcutaneous injection every 6 months — administered in the clinic by a healthcare professional, ensuring 100% compliance (unlike oral bisphosphonates).

Efficacy:
The FREEDOM trial (Fracture Reduction Evaluation of Denosumab in Osteoporosis Every 6 Months — Cummings et al., NEJM 2009) demonstrated a 68% reduction in vertebral fractures, 40% reduction in hip fractures, and 20% reduction in non-vertebral fractures over 3 years. The 10-year extension data demonstrated continued BMD gains (lumbar spine BMD increased by 21.7% over 10 years — compared to a plateau with bisphosphonates at 3–5 years) with sustained fracture reduction and no increased adverse events. Denosumab is the only osteoporosis drug that produces continuous, progressive BMD gains without a plateau effect.

Indications:
First-line alternative to bisphosphonates (particularly when bisphosphonates are contraindicated — renal impairment with eGFR <30–35 mL/min, oesophageal disorders, intolerance — or when patient preference favours the 6-monthly injection over oral medication), and as second-line therapy when bisphosphonates have been inadequate.

Critical Concern — Rebound Effect on Discontinuation: The most important clinical issue with denosumab. Because its effect is completely reversible (unlike bisphosphonates, which remain bound to bone for years after discontinuation), stopping denosumab produces a rapid rebound of bone resorption — bone turnover markers surge above pre-treatment levels within 3–6 months, BMD decreases rapidly (potentially returning to or below pre-treatment levels within 12–18 months), and there is a significantly increased risk of multiple vertebral fractures during the rebound period. This rebound vertebral fracture phenomenon has been reported in multiple case series and is a genuine clinical hazard. Therefore, denosumab should never be discontinued without transitioning to an alternative antiresorptive agent — the recommended approach is to administer a bisphosphonate (typically zoledronic acid 5 mg IV, or oral alendronate for 1–2 years) starting 6 months after the last denosumab injection, to consolidate the BMD gains and prevent rebound resorption.

Other Adverse Effects: Hypocalcaemia (rare in the osteoporosis-dose setting if calcium and vitamin D are supplemented — more common in patients with renal impairment or vitamin D deficiency), skin infections (cellulitis, erysipelas — a small but statistically significant increase in the FREEDOM trial), and the same rare risks as bisphosphonates (ONJ — incidence ~0.04% at osteoporosis doses; atypical femoral fractures — extremely rare, reported in case reports).

Answer: 2
