## Options

1. Apply postoperative splint and discharge home from recovery room
2. Apply postoperative splint and admit for observation
3. Remove the pins and perform repeat closed reduction
4. Remove the pins and perform immediate open exploration
5. Maintain the pins and perform immediate open exploration

## Correct option

**2. Apply postoperative splint and admit for observation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a pink hand and normal doppler signals indicative of intact distal perfusion; therefore, immediate vascular exploration is not indicated. She should be admitted for careful observation for the development of vascular compromise or compartment syndrome.

In displaced supracondylar humerus (SCH) fractures, vascular compromise (pulseless extremity) can occur in as many as 20% of patients. Most often, the brachial artery is stretched over the displaced fracture fragments and tethered by fascial bands or branches. The brachial artery can also be compressed by soft tissues, become entrapped in the fracture site or sustain direct injury resulting in a contusion, laceration or intimal injury with delayed formation of an aneurysm or occlusive thrombus. Pulseless limbs with evidence of poor distal perfusion (cold, pale hand with sluggish capillary refill and distal tip turgor) following closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) should undergo immediate vascular exploration, as brachial artery injury rates have been reported to be as high as 82% in this scenario. However, lack of a palpable pulse after CRPP is not an absolute indication to proceed with vascular exploration if clinical findings suggest distal perfusion is intact, such as a pink hand, brisk capillary refill or intact Doppler signals.

*Weller et al. performed a retrospective cohort study of the management of pulseless SCH fractures.* *Twenty (37%) of the 54 patients who presented initially pulseless had a dopplerable pulse and a pink hand after CRPP, but the radial pulse remained nonpalpable.* These patients were observed and 19 (95%) regained a palpable pulse either prior to discharge or by the time of the first postoperative visit. One of the 20 patients (5%) required vascular repair after developing a pale hand 9 hours after CRPP.

*Scannell et al. retrospectively reviewed the vascular status of children who had sustained a pulseless perfused SCH fracture and were managed with CRPP and observation.* *All patients demonstrated palpable radial pulses, normal arm growth and good to excellent functional outcomes at intermediate follow up (average of 20 months).* *Five of the 20 patients had an occluded brachial artery but none of these patients had functional problems or a wrist-brachial index that was sufficiently abnormal to warrant vascular reconstruction.* **The authors found** no correlation between the presence or absence of a doppler signal and brachial artery occlusion. Concomitant complete median nerve palsy was associated with brachial artery occlusion.

The AAOS guidelines for treatment of pediatric SCH fractures recommends nonoperative management of type I (nondisplaced) fractures with cast immobilization and CRPP of displaced type II and III fractures with 2-3 laterally introduced pins (moderate strength of recommendation). Emergent closed reduction of displaced SCH fractures should be performed in patients with decreased perfusion and open exploration should be performed in patients who remain underperfused following CRPP (consensus). In patients who remain pulseless but with intact distal perfusion, evidence guiding management is inconclusive.

Incorrect Responses:
Answer 1: The patient should be admitted for observation to ensure late-developing vascular compromise does not occur.
Answer 3: There is no indication to perform repeat closed reduction in the setting of a well-perfused limb.
Answers 4 and 5: There is no indication to perform immediate vascular exploration in the setting of a well-perfused limb.
