## Question

A 16-year-old female presents with a several-month history of multiple, painless, soft-tissue masses that have gradually increased in size in her posterior thigh and calf. She reports activity-related size fluctuations. Physical examination reveals several soft, palpable, somewhat ill-defined lumps deep within the muscle. MRI is ordered, demonstrating multiple non-encapsulated infiltrative-appearing masses within the muscle compartments. These masses show a heterogeneous lesion with numerous small blood vessels and fatty infiltration, appearing like a "bag of worms". Based on the patient's presentation of multiple, infiltrative lesions with mixed tissue and fat components, which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Desmoid tumor (Aggressive Fibromatosis)
2. Intramuscular Lipoma
3. Low-flow Venous Malformation (VM)
4. Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumor (MPNST)
5. Schwannoma

## Correct option

**3. Low-flow Venous Malformation (VM)**

## Answer explanation

Multifocal, infiltrative soft-tissue lesions with an interspersed fat signal on MRI are characteristic of low-flow venous or lymphovenous malformations (answer choice 3).

Low-flow venous malformations (VMs) are congenital errors of vascular development that often present in adolescence due to hormonal influences. They are typically multifocal, deep, and infiltrative, with a soft, "bag of worms" consistency on palpation and imaging. On MRI, their key feature is the heterogeneous signal representing slow-flowing blood, interspersed with fibrofatty tissue (i.e., the fat signal suppressing on STIR). While benign, their infiltrative nature can lead to significant symptoms. Initial management is nonoperative, but options like sclerotherapy or surgical resection are used for symptomatic lesions.

McCuaig provided an update on the classification and diagnosis of vascular malformations, emphasizing the differences between various types, including low-flow lesions. She noted that venous malformations are typically characterized by slow flow, compressibility, and the presence of phleboliths on imaging. She concluded that accurate classification of these lesions is vital for guiding the specific, targeted treatment approach.

*Fayad et al. reviewed the MR imaging features of vascular malformations in the extremities,* **emphasizing** their role in guiding treatment. **They noted** that low-flow venous malformations typically show high T2 signal intensity due to the stagnant blood, with an infiltrative pattern, and often contain interspersed fat. **They concluded** that MRI is the imaging modality of choice for characterizing venous malformations, which appear as poorly defined, multifocal soft-tissue lesions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Desmoid tumors are dense, firm fibrous tumors that would appear as solid, dark masses on T1 and T2 MRI and would not contain fat components.
Answer 2: A lipoma is composed of pure mature fat, and while it would suppress on fat-suppressive sequencing, it would appear diffusely bright and homogeneous on T1 imaging, without the mixed vessel/stroma signal.
Answer 4: MPNST is a malignant, solid tumor and would not typically have a significant interspersed fat signal as a primary component.
Answer 5: A schwannoma is a benign nerve sheath tumor that is usually solitary and solid, lacking the multifocal, infiltrative appearance and interspersed fat component.
