## Question

A 15-year-old female gymnast presenting with complaints of activity-related low back pain for the past 5 weeks. On examination, she has pain with single-limb lumbar extension and noticeable hamstring tightness. Initial radiographs are unremarkable. Figure A is the MRI image that was obtained recently. What is the best initial course of treatment?

![[q2285-a-spondylolysis-mri.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Observation without activity limitations
2. Oral steroids with repeat imaging in 3 weeks
3. Par interarticular repair
4. Activity modification with consideration of bracing and physical therapy
5. Posterolateral fusion with bone grafting

## Correct option

**4. Activity modification with consideration of bracing and physical therapy**

## Answer explanation

This patient has clinical and MRI findings suggestive of spondylolysis. In patients with symptomatic isthmic spondylolysis, the initial treatment is activity modification/rest, possible use of an anti-lordotic brace, and physical therapy.

The initial diagnostic work-up for spondylosis should include plain radiographs of the lumbosacral spine. However, these radiographs may be negative because it can take weeks or months for the characteristic changes to become apparent. MRI may be useful adjunct in the diagnosis of spondylolysis when plain radiographs are negative and has largely replaced single photon emission computer tomography (SPECT) as it has demonstrated similar sensitivity and specificity without radiation exposure. When diagnosed, the initial management in the symptomatic isthmic spondylolysis is physical therapy targeted at hamstring stretching, pelvic tilts, and abdominal strengthening with avoidance of hyperextension activities.

*In an older study, Collier et al. compared planar bone scintigraphy (PBS) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) in 19 adults with radiographic evidence of spondylolysis and/or spondylolisthesis.* **They report** that SPECT was more sensitive and allowed for more accurate localization of "painful" defects in the pars interarticularis. **The authors conclude** that pars defects in spondylolysis or spondylolisthesis are best detected and localized by SPECT.

*Ciullo et al. review vertebral injury in the young gymnast.* **They report** that hyperextension and repetitive microtrauma lead to posterior element injuries of the vertebrae in these young athletes. **They conclude** that early recognition may lead to a more favorable clinical outcome.

*Jackson et al. review 100 lumbar spines of young female gymnasts engaged in high-level competition.* **They report** an incidence of pars interarticular defects was 11%, which is 4x higher than their non-athletic female peers. **They note** that the female athlete has the same incidence of pars interarticularis defects as the male performing similar activities.

Figure A is the sagittal MRI demonstrating a lumbar pars interarticularis defect consisent with spondylolysis

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Observation without activity limitations is indicated in the asymptomatic patient with low-grade spondylolisthesis or spondylolysis
Answer 2: Oral steroids are typically avoided in pediatric patients. Physical therapy is the first line of treatment
Answer 3: Par interarticular repair may be indicated in L1-L4 isthmic defects that have failed conservative management
Answer 5: Posterolateral fusion with bone grafting is reserved for the L5 spondylolysis that has failed conservative management
