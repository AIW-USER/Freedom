## Question

A 25-year-old male was playing ultimate frisbee and sustained an ankle injury. He initially went to an urgent care clinic and was diagnosed with an ankle sprain and placed into a lace-up ankle brace. Two weeks later, he continues to have pain with any weight-bearing. His urgent care radiographs are presented in Figures A and B. What would be the next most appropriate step(s)?

## Options

1. Switch patient to high air cast boot and weight bearing as tolerated
2. Switch patient to high air cast boot and make non-weight bearing for 4-6 weeks
3. MRI of the ankle
4. Ankle stress radiographs and ipsilateral tibia-fibula radiographs
5. Ipsilateral tibia-fibula radiographs

## Correct option

**4. Ankle stress radiographs and ipsilateral tibia-fibula radiographs**

## Answer explanation

This patient likely has an unstable high ankle sprain (syndesmosis) injury, which would be best worked up with an ipsilateral tibia-fibula radiograph and bilateral ankle stress radiographs to compare syndesmosis parameters for instability.

High ankle sprains are ankle syndesmotic injuries secondary to an external rotation force. Treatment depends on whether the syndesmosis is stable or unstable. Stable syndesmotic injuries are sprains without diastasis of the syndesmosis or ankle instability. Unstable injuries demonstrate instability or diastasis on stress radiographs and can be associated with a proximal fibula fracture (Maisonneuve Fracture). Treatment for stable injuries involves delayed weight-bearing and placement into a CMA boot or cast for 2-3 weeks. Unstable injuries or those refractory to non-operative treatment require syndesmotic fixation with either a screw or suture button.

*Rammelt et al. reviewed acute and chronic injuries.* *They discussed the relevant anatomy and biomechanics of the syndesmosis with the mechanism of injury, diagnosis, and treatment of syndesmotic injuries.* In summary, instability requires a rupture of two or more of the ligament. Treatment involves reduction and fixation of the syndesmosis and outcomes are based on adequate reduction not the type of fixation.

*Hunt et al. reviewed high ankle sprains and syndesmotic injuries in athletes.* *They discussed the relevant anatomy, diagnosis, classification and management of syndesmotic injuries.* In summary, many syndesmotic injuries are treated non-operatively, but it is important to determine the stable versus unstable injuries.

Figure A is a mortise ankle radiograph depicting possible medial clear space widening and decreased tib-fib overlap. Figure B is a lateral ankle radiograph with no evidence of osseous injury. Illustrations A and B are AP and latera tibia-fibula radiograph respectively demonstrating a proximal fibula fracture (Maisonneuve Fracture).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Since he continues to have pain with weight-bearing, further radiographs should be taken.
Answer 2: Keeping the patient non-weight bearing is appropriate, but further radiographs should be taken to determine if the injury is stable or unstable.
Answer 3: MRI of the ankle might be appropriate if bilateral stress radiographs and tibia-fibula radiographs are inconclusive, but those should be done prior.
Answer 5: It is correct to get ipsilateral tibia-fibula radiographs, but stress radiographs should be obtained as well to determine if it is a stable or unstable syndesmosis injury.
