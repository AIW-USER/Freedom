## Question

A 37-year-old manual laborer is being seen in your clinic with a chief complaint of dorsal wrist pain and decreased grip strength. Select images from the patient's radiographic record are available in Figure A for review. You discuss the patient's pathology with him and, after this discussion, elect to perform a vascularized bone graft using the 4+5 extensor compartment pedicle. Which of the following is the primary advantage of this pedicle choice compared to other commonly used pedicles during the harvest of a vascularized distal radius bone graft?

![[q218065-a-stage-2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Antegrade blood supply
2. Large arc of rotation
3. No need for post-operative immobilization and/or unloading of the lunate
4. Predominantly radial location of the vessels
5. Use of a volar approach during harvest

## Correct option

**2. Large arc of rotation**

## Answer explanation

A variety of arterial pedicles have been described when harvesting a vascularized distal radius bone graft in the treatment of pre-fragmentation Kienbock's disease. Many surgeons prefer the use of the 4+5 extensor compartment pedicle due to its large arc of rotation, which allows for a more predictable transfer while simultaneously placing the pedicle at a lower risk of iatrogenic injury (Answer 2).

Kienbock's disease is avascular necrosis of the lunate, a pathology that often leads to progressive wrist pain and abnormal carpal motion. It most commonly affects males between 20-40 years old, and individuals with a history of wrist trauma are at a higher risk for the development of Kienbock's disease. A variety of risk factors have been identified including, but not limited to, ulnar negative variance, decreased radial inclination, and repetitive carpal trauma. Furthermore, aberrations in both the anatomy and vascular supply of the lunate can increase the likelihood of this clinical phenomenon. Three major variations in the blood supply to the lunate have been identified (Y-pattern, X-pattern, and I-pattern), with 31% of patients with Kienbock's disease exhibiting the I-pattern (Illustration A).

*Kienbock's disease is classified based on the work of Lichtman et al.* (Illustration B). The treatment of early-stage (II-IIIA) disease often requires joint-leveling or revascularization procedures, while salvage procedures are utilized for late-stage (IIIB-IV) disease. Vascularized bone grafts, utilizing a variety of arterial pedicles, have demonstrated promising early results for Stage I, II, IIIA, and (in some cases) IIIB disease, but do face a paucity of long-term supportive data. With regards to pedicle choice, the 4+5 extensor compartment pedicle, which routes the retrograde blood supply of the fifth extensor compartment artery (ECA) in an orthogonal direction into the fourth ECA, has gained popularity due to a large arc of rotation when compared to other pedicle choices. Furthermore, the relatively ulnar location of the pedicle allows for a dorsal arthrotomy to access the lunate with little risk of iatrogenic injury to the pedicle itself.

*Moran et al. provide a case series of patients with pre-fragmentation Kienbock’s disease treated with a 4+5 ECA distal radius vascularized bone graft.* *Their cohort consisted of 26 patients with a mean age of 32 years.* *The average follow-up was 31 months.* In the short term, a majority of patients reported improvements in wrist range of motion, grip strength, and pain levels, and 85% of patients had satisfactory results according to the Lichtman outcome score. Furthermore, 71% of patients showed evidence of revascularization on MRI. **The authors conclude** that the arc of motion, large arterial diameter, and relatively ulnar location of the graft are all advantages of this pedicle compared to other commonly used pedicles.

Elhassan and Shin provide a review of vascularized bone grafting for the treatment of Kienbock’s disease, with a special focus on the 4+5 ECA graft. **The authors note**, similar to Moran et al., that the large arc of motion, large arterial diameter, and predominantly ulnar location of this graft are all advantages when compared to other pedicle choices. The authors do note that the wrist is vulnerable to overuse following vascularized bone grafting, and that post-operative immobilization (either with K-wires or external fixation) is required.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The retrograde flow of the 5th ECA is routed in an orthogonal direction into the 4th ECA, following ligation of the posterior branch of the anterior interosseous artery. This results in an orthogonally oriented antegrade blood supply. Many other pedicles available for use also have an antegrade blood supply, and this is not a unique strength of this pedicle choice.
Answer 3: Between two and three months of post-operative immobilization are required following vascularized bone grafting with the 4+5 ECA pedicle.
Answer 4: The 4+5 ECA pedicle is relatively ulnar, not radial, on the distal radius, which allows for arthrotomy and access of the lunate without placing the pedicle at direct risk of iatrogenic injury.
Answer 5: The 4+5 ECA is harvested through a dorsal, not volar, approach.
