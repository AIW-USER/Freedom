## Options

1. Continued immobilization in a semi-rigid cervical orthosis for 6 to 8 weeks
2. Reduction and posterior instrumented C1-C2 fusion
3. Open reduction and internal fixation of the odontoid process with an anterior odontoid screw
4. Resection of the odontoid process through a transoral approach
5. Reduction with Gardner-Wells tong traction and 6 weeks of skeletal traction

## Correct option

**2. Reduction and posterior instrumented C1-C2 fusion**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a type II odontoid fracture with several risk factors for nonunion: age >50, current smoker, and diabetes. The optimal treatment would be a reduction and posterior C1-2 arthrodesis.

Odontoid fractures are can be classified into three types with type II fractures occurring at the base of the odontoid as it attaches to the body of C2. This region is in a vascular watershed area between the internal carotid and vertebral arteries, making it prone to nonunion. Type II fractures can be managed nonsurgically or surgically, with nonunion being frequently asymptomatic. However, nonsurgical management in geriatric patients is associated with high mortality rates. Surgical treatment can be performed with anterior odontoid screw fixation and posterior C1-2 arthrodesis with instrumentation. Anterior fixation is relatively contraindicated in patients with significant fracture displacement, barrel-shaped chests that inhibit appropriate screw trajectory, high risk of dysphagia in elderly patients, and significant osteoporosis, which increases the risk of screw cut out.

*Shilpakar et al. reviewed operative techniques for the treatment of type II odontoid fractures.* **They recommended** the use of anterior odontoid screw fixation in acute type II fractures as well as rostral type III fractures. In cases of chronic type II fractures or with transverse ligament disruption, **they recommended** posterior transarticular facet screw fixation supplemented with bone graft and interspinous C1-2 wiring.

*Subach et al. retrospectively studied patients treated with anterior odontoid screw fixation that presented with acute type II and rostral type III odontoid fractures.* **They found** at 30-months follow-up there was a 96% fusion rate with no complications related to the surgical technique. Of the included patients, **they reported** one patient requiring external immobilization due to poor screw placement and one requiring posterior stabilization due to insufficient fixation of the anterior screw. **The authors concluded** that single screw anterior odontoid screw fixation is an effective surgical treatment option with high fusion rates and low complication rates in type II and rostral type III odontoid fractures.

*Fountas et al. retrospectively studied the long-term fusion rates of patients undergoing anterior odontoid screw fixation for patients with type II and shallow type III odontoid fractures.* **They reported** a fusion rate of 87.1% with the remaining 12.9% of patients developing a pseudoarthrosis. Of the 12.9% that developed pseudoarthrosis 9.6% were stable and 3.2% with instability. **The authors concluded** that anterior odontoid screw fixation is a safe and effective therapeutic option for type II and shallow type III odontoid fractures.

Figures A and B are the odontoid view and lateral radiographs of the cervical spine with a displaced type II odontoid fracture. Figures C and D are the coronal and sagittal CT scans of the cervical spine demonstrating a displaced type II odontoid fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Given the patient's risk factors for nonunion, continued immobilization is not recommended.
Answer 3: Anterior odontoid screw fixation in this patient would not be ideal due to the elderly age and co-morbidities which may increase the chance of screw pullout and nonunion. Patients with COPD are often barrel-chested and can also inhibit appropriate screw trajectory. Lastly, this elderly patient is at a higher risk of dysphagia with a high anterior cervical dissection.
Answer 4: Resection of the odontoid process would not be preferred treatment option unless it is combined with a posterior spinal fusion.
Answer 5: Non-operative treatment would not be preferred given the many risk factors of nonunion; elderly patients treated non-operatively are also at higher mortality risk.
