## Question

An 85-year-old male who recently moved from Puerto Rico presents to the emergency department with the imaging shown in Figure A. He is closed reduced under conscious sedation (Figure B) and follows up in the clinic. He states this is his tenth dislocation since his hip replacement five years ago, of which one of the dislocations required open reduction. He has multiple comorbidities including chronic kidney disease on dialysis, diabetes, and active smoking. CT scan imaging shows approximately 25 degrees of cup anteversion (Figure C), 40 degrees of cup inclination, and radiolucency around the femoral stem. His operative report displays a unique implant company that, despite multiple attempts, is unable to be contacted. His ESR is 12mm/hr (normal: 0-20 mm/hr) and CRP is 0.6mg/dL (normal: 0.8-1.0mg/dL). A precautionary hip aspiration shows a synovial WBC count of 1,100 cells/mL with 76% segmented neutrophils and no growth on cultures. Intraoperatively, the stem is loose, and the acetabular component is well-fixed. Which of the following would be considered the most appropriate next step in management while minimizing potential complications?

## Options

1. Revise the femoral stem and add additional retroversion during stem placement for stability
2. Revise the femoral stem and cement a constrained acetabular liner into current acetabular component
3. Removal of all current implants and plan for one stage revision arthroplasty with constrained liner
4. Removal of all current implants and plan for two stage revision arthroplasty with constrained liner
5. Removal of all current implants for conversion to Girdlestone resection

## Correct option

**2. Revise the femoral stem and cement a constrained acetabular liner into current acetabular component**

## Answer explanation

The most definitive surgery with the lowest potential complications for this high-risk patient includes revising the loosened femoral stem and cementing a constrained acetabular liner into his current, well-fixed acetabular component (Answer 2). This approach would also negate the need to special order a matching polyethylene liner from this patient’s unknown implant company.

In the setting of revision THA surgery, it is imperative to know the patient’s original implants. Companies offer different acetabular component shell shapes (hemispherical vs elliptical), various locking mechanisms between the acetabular shell and the polyethylene liner, and numerous femoral head/stem options. This information facilitates modular component exchange during revision surgery, such as swapping out a standard polyethylene liner for a constrained liner in the setting of instability. In this case, the femoral stem has undergone aseptic loosening and must be revised. The acetabular cup appears to be in an appropriate position and is determined to be well-fixed intraoperatively. In general, appropriately positioned acetabular shells with solid fixation should be left in place. This is because the removal of a well-fixed acetabular shell can incur significant bone loss, result in intraoperative pelvic fractures, and make the revision surgery much more technically difficult. When the acetabular shell locking mechanism is disrupted/malfunctioning (or unknown in this case), surgeons can elect to cement the liner into the underlying acetabular shell. This technique negates the need to remove the acetabular shell, can allow for some adjustments/corrections to version/inclination, and serves as an appropriate bail-out option. While this is an unconventional technique in routine arthroplasty, multiple revision arthroplasty case series have reported high long-term survival rates (>90%) without liner loosening or catastrophic failure even when using a constrained liner (i.e. higher constraint and greater force on poly-cement interface).

Van der Merwe provided a comprehensive review article regarding current constraining devices in total hip arthroplasty. They discuss various implant options including bipolar femoral heads, various dual mobility systems, and constrained acetabular liners. They addressed the potential need to cement a liner into a shell, which can be indicated if:

(1) There is a confirmed, cementless, well-fixed acetabular shell
(2) The current acetabular shell has appropriate positioning (inclination and version)
(3) The capturing mechanism for the acetabular liner is broken or inadequate
(4) There is room for a 2-4mm cement mantle between the shell and the new liner

The author stresses the importance of utilizing the least constraining device needed to achieve stability in order to maximize functional results and minimize the added stress at the various host-implant interfaces (i.e. higher rates of loosening). They conclude that constraining devices should predominantly be utilized in salvage procedures in the elderly or very low-demand patients with instability.

*Callaghan and colleagues performed a retrospective case-series review of 31 hips in which a worn acetabular liner was revised by cementing a new liner into an existing shell that was stable and well-positioned.* *They scored the acetabular liner in a “spider web” configuration (i.e. combination of longitudinal and circumferential scoring patterns) prior to cementation in the native shell.* The longitudinal scoring adds torsional stability to the liner-cement interface while the circumferential scoring adds pull-out strength (described in the authors’ prior biomechanical study). With a minimum follow-up of 2 years (mean 5.3 years, range 2-10 years), **they reported** that no hips required re-revision. They did note 2 hips dislocated but were able to be managed with conservative treatment alone. **They concluded** that cementing a liner into a well-fixed cementless shell after appropriate liner scoring provided secure fixation without any failures at mid-term follow-up.

Figure A displays this patient’s AP radiographic imaging demonstrating their right-sided THA posterior dislocation. Note their prior lumbar spinal fusion, which is another potential contributing factor towards instability. Figure B displays the same patient’s post-reduction AP radiograph. Figure C is a single cut of their axial CT scan at the level of the THA femoral head center of rotation demonstrating appropriate anteversion (~25 degrees). Illustration A demonstrates this patient’s revision THA with a constrained liner cemented into the native acetabular shell. The operative surgeon elected to utilize a cemented stem with antibiotics mixed into the cement and placement of additional absorbable antibiotic beads. This was performed in an attempt to minimize potential infection in this high-risk patient.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1. Retroverting the femoral stem would worsen the patient's posterior instability. Revising the femoral stem with increased anteversion may add provide some additional stability. However, as an isolated procedure, stem revision alone would still result in continued dislocation events without adding some level of increased constraint (i.e. constrained liner) in a multi-dislocating patient with likely short external rotator deficiency.
Answer 3&4: Acetabular shell component removal is indicated in the settings of chronic periprosthetic joint infection, gross mal-positioning, or if the cup was cemented into the acetabulum (i.e. high risk of long-term loosening). Given this patient’s cup is in the appropriate positioning and confirmed to be well-fixed intraoperatively, the least morbid option would be to cement in a constrained liner. This would preclude the need for shell removal in this high-risk patient.
Answer 5. Removal of all current implants for conversion to Girdlestone resection would be considered appropriate only after all other options have been exhausted. Girdlestone resection should be reserved for non-ambulatory patients who lack bone or soft tissue coverage for reimplantation (most commonly in the setting of recurrent periprosthetic joint infections).
