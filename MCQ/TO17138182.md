## Answer explanation

Answer: 3

Definition

Obstetric Brachial Plexus Palsy (OBPP) is a plexus injury occurring during childbirth, causing weakness or paralysis in an infant's arm, often due to shoulder dystocia, macrosomia or a difficult vaginal delivery.

Risk Factors for Obstetric Brachial Plexus Palsy

 - prolonged labour

 - shoulder dystocia

 - breech presentation

 - macrosomia

 - multiparous pregnancy

 - forceps delivery

 - obstructed labour

Patterns

The level and nature of root involvement varies from a neuropraxia to varying levels of axonotomesis to neurotomesis. In the worst injuries, even a root avulsion is possible. Three typical syndromes of brachial plexus injuries are seen. The majority of obstetrically injured infants will have sustained damage to the upper (C5 and C6) roots, with a resulting Erb palsy (50-60% cases). The second most frequent type of injury is the pan-plexus lesion, in which there is some or complete damage to all the roots. The lower plexus palsy (Klumpke paralysis) involves the C8 and T1 nerve roots, but in practice, isolated lower plexus injuries are actually rare (2–3%). The remaining cases are combined total lesions.

Management

Although difficult to identify exact pattern at birth, the predominant level of the lesion should be well established by 2–6 weeks of age. In practice, at the 4-week visit, the injury has usually declared itself as an unchanged complete paralysis or an upper root paralysis. Electrophysiology is recommended at 4 weeks initially to confirm the diagnosis and get a baseline reading of the involvement of various nerves and muscles as well as sensory parameters.

The 3-month visit is critical. For those patients exhibiting spontaneous recovery (up to 80-90 % cases), especially in biceps and deltoid, further watchful waiting (up to 1 year) is recommended. If no substantial recovery has occurred by the 3-month visit, an electromyogram is performed to confirm the type and degree of brachial plexus injury and further define the particular nerve roots affected. Nerve root avulsions, recovery is not anticipated and surgery is opted. For post ganglionic lesions, Gilbert's criteria for surgery says that if a child with upper plexus lesion does not have a biceps function exceeding MRC grade 3 (antigravity movement) at 3 months of age, then surgery is recommended for nerve repair. Hence, an Erb palsy case, with no recovery in biceps and deltoid at 3 months also identifies a likely surgical candidate. Surgery is typically undertaken at 4–6 months of age in these patients.

Type of Surgeries in these Infants

 - Neurotization or nerve transfer: Performed for preganglionic lesions or root avulsions.

 - Micro surgical nerve grafting: Performed for postganglionic injury with intact nerve roots with segmental injury to nerve.

Now, in the given question winged scapulae (long thoracic nerve), and rhomboid atrophy (dorsal scapular nerve), elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic nerve), and pale and flail upper limb all indicate preganglionic lesion; hence choice C has been chosen as answer.

Details of Surgery for Root Avulsion / Preganglionic Lesion

The surgical exploration warrants exposure of the entire supraclavicular and infraclavicular plexus. After complete and thorough exposure of the plexus, including intraforaminal dissection and external neurolysis of the nerve-in-continuity, intraoperative electrodiagnostic studies follow. First, motor-evoked stimulation is used to determine which roots are conducting by observing the distal muscles contraction, occasionally augmented by needle electromyography. Conduction of nerve action potentials across the neuroma or from clinically nonfunctioning roots are next recorded, with large, fast-conducting (preganglionic) nerve action potentials seen in the situation in which the nerve root is avulsed. Somatosensory evoked potentials from the opposite cortex help determine the integrity of sensory roots but appear to not be as useful in infants as in adults and, moreover, do not assay the motor (ventral) roots.

Which Nerve is to be Transferred

A meta-analysis conducted on the nerve transfer literature noted that for restoration of shoulder abduction, it is best to use an accessory nerve transfer to the suprascapular nerve, whereas for elbow flexion, intercostals without graft should be performed. This set of transfers is certainly appropriate for the situation in which the patient has a complete flail arm with all five spinal nerve roots avulsed. In the grim scenario of complete avulsions, the addition of a contralateral C7 transfer with an interposed vascularized ulnar nerve graft directed to the entire or perhaps the lateral root of median nerve in the axilla could be considered.

If C5 and C6 are avulsed but C7 is clearly intact, intraplexal graft repairs from the C7 may be considered to reinnervate the shoulder abductors and elbow flexors.

Alternatively, directed discrete intraplexal transfers should be performed, and based on the most recent literature, this seems to be a favored approach. A combination of distal accessory to suprascapular, ulnar nerve fascicle to biceps nerve (perhaps augmented by a portion of medial pectoral nerve via graft to brachialis nerve), and long head of triceps nerve (or thoracodorsal nerve) to the anterior portion of the axillary nerve should be performed.
