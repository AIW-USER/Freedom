## Question

A junior resident is reviewing a recently published scientific article that detected no difference in two groups of five patients undergoing an arthroscopic intervention for adhesive capsulitis, in which the P-value for the compared outcomes is P = 0.079. Which of the following statements should the junior resident make regarding these findings?

## Options

1. A Bonferroni correction should be made post-hoc to correct for the study's effect size
2. An underpowered study increases the likelihood of a Type I error
3. The investigators may have made a Type II error
4. The null hypothesis should be rejected
5. The study's P-value indicates a large effect size

## Correct option

**3. The investigators may have made a Type II error**

## Answer explanation

The results of this study are not statistically significant, given the P-value (P = 0.079) is > 0.05, which means that the null hypothesis is accepted. However, given the exceedingly small sample size, it is possible that the investigators may have made a Type II error, in which the null hypothesis is accepted but is actually false due to the study being underpowered to detect a true difference.

The power of a study can be calculated as follows: Power = 1 - p(Type II error), in which the probability of a Type II error can be defined as Beta, or the probability of a "false negative." A false negative occurs when a study fails to find a statistically significant difference (as above) even though one actually exists. This most often occurs when the study is underpowered to detect an effect and is directly related to the sample size available for statistical analysis. It is important to note that the P value is determined by the difference between the two groups, the variance in this difference, and the sample size. Therefore, it is difficult to identify any statistical significance in smaller sample sizes.

*Vavken et al. reviewed the use of confidence intervals in reporting orthopaedic research findings.* **The authors note** that statistical significance can only suggest whether there is any difference when comparing outcomes, but that one way to combine statistical significance and effect sizes is to report confidence intervals. **They conclude** that the probability of statistically significant results to predict at least a 10% between-group difference was only 69% (95% confidence interval, 55%–83%), suggesting that a high proportion of statistically significant results do not reflect large treatment effects and that confidence intervals could help avoid such erroneous interpretation by showing the effect size explicitly.

*Porcher et al. reviewed the use of confidence intervals and p-values in the reporting of results in orthopaedic research.* **The authors note** that one possible reason for investigators favoring statistical testing (i.e., p-values) over confidence intervals may be the lack of understanding of the usefulness of confidence intervals, and the habit of seeing p-values in almost all surgical papers. **They conclude** that confidence intervals and p-values also only account for the effects of sampling variation on the precision of the estimated parameter but cannot control for biases in sampling or study conduct.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Bonferroni correction refers to a post-hoc statistical correction made to P-values when several dependent or independent statistical tests are being performed simultaneously on a single data set.
Answers 2 and 4: An underpowered study increases the likelihood of a Type II error, or a "false negative," in which the null hypothesis is accepted but a true difference does exist. In this instance, the sample size is far too small to confidently state that the null hypothesis should be rejected.
Answer 5: The P-value does not tell anything about the size of an effect.
