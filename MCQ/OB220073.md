## Question

A 74-year-old female with a history of rheumatoid arthritis presents to the clinic for a second opinion regarding her painful total hip arthroplasty. She had a metal-on-metal hip placed 15 years ago by an outside surgeon. She now presents with pain and loss of motion. Radiographs obtained confirm a metal-on-metal total hip prosthesis without any radiographic evidence of loosening or fracture. An MRI demonstrates a large fluid collection consistent with adverse local tissue reaction (ALTR). Her laboratory results are as follows:

Serum ESR: 34 mm/hr
Serum CRP: 12 mg/L
Serum cobalt: 14 ppb
Serum chromium: 11 ppb
Synovial WBC count: 4,100 WBC/µL
Synovial alpha-defensin: +

You would like to rule out prosthetic joint infection (PJI) before revision surgery. Which of the following is true regarding the diagnostic workup of this patient?

## Options

1. A positive alpha-defensin assay indicates the presence of a PJI.
2. Elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) confirm the presence of PJI in this patient.
3. The alpha-defensin test cannot be used in this patient secondary to her history of rheumatoid arthritis.
4. PJI can be effectively ruled out in this patient due to the presence of ALTR on imaging.
5. A more thorough work-up for infection (cultures, manual WBC count, percent polymorphonuclear cells) is required for diagnosis.

## Correct option

**5. A more thorough work-up for infection (cultures, manual WBC count, percent polymorphonuclear cells) is required for diagnosis.**

## Answer explanation

Diagnosing PJI in the setting of ALTR presents a difficult challenge due to the similar picture and triggered elevation in inflammatory markers. For this reason, a more rigorous work-up, including synovial fluid cultures, manual WBC count, and differential is often required for diagnosis (Anwser 5).

Metal-on-metal (MoM) hip arthroplasties and resurfacings present unique challenges in diagnosing periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) due to the inflammatory response caused by adverse local tissue reactions (ALTR). ALTR is a pathologic response of the tissues from the release of wear debris (cobalt or chromium) or corrosion products. The particles trigger an exaggerated immune response, leading to chronic inflammation and potentially tissue degradation. Patients with ALTR present similarly to patients with a PJI, including hip pain, decreased range of motion, and swelling. To make matters worse, ALTR can elevate inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and increase synovial white blood cell (WBC) counts, mimicking infection from a laboratory standpoint. Furthermore, the alpha-defensin test, normally a highly sensitive and specific test for PJI, can yield false-positive results in MoM hips due to the underlying inflammatory environment in ALTR. For this reason, most clinicians recommend a more aggressive and thorough approach to diagnosing PJI in the setting of ALTR. This includes a manual count of the WBCs and a differential analysis, with 80% neutrophils being the cutoff. Additionally, cultures are recommended in the pre-revision setting to ensure the presence or absence of infection. Careful integration of these findings is critical to distinguishing PJI from ALTR and ensuring appropriate management.

*Okroj et al. performed a retrospective review on 26 patients with confirmed ALTR and an alpha-defensin test to evaluate the accuracy of the alpha-defensin test in this cohort.* *Of the 26 patients included in this study, one patient was confirmed to have a PJI, based on the Musculoskeletal Infection Society criteria.* Despite this, the alpha-defensin test yielded nine positive results, of which eight were false positives. As a result, **the authors concluded** that the presence of ALTR produced elevated false positive results in the alpha-defensin test. Therefore, **the authors recommend** a more aggressive preoperative evaluation for PJI in patients with ALTR, including manual cell counts, differentials, and cultures to increase the accuracy of a PJI diagnosis.

*Yi et al. published a retrospective review of 150 revision hip cases with adverse local tissue reactions.* The researchers evaluated the effectiveness of various serological (ESR, CBC) and synovial (WBC count, differential) tests in diagnosing PJI in this patient cohort. Using Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria, 19 total patients were diagnosed with PJI. The serological and synovial values were compared between those diagnosed with PJI and those who were not. This study found that the synovial WBC count was the most reliable test for diagnosing PJI, with an area under the curve of 985 and an optimal cutoff value of 4,350 WBC/μL. **The authors recommend** using a manual WBC count and a more rigorous pre-revision evaluation of patients with MoM bearings due to the increased difficulty of PJI diagnosis in these patients.

*Miyamae et al. published a study to evaluate the accuracy of the alpha-defensin test for PJI in patients with inflammatory disease.* *This retrospective review included 41 patients with confirmed PJI (based on MSIS criteria) with preoperative alpha-defensin results.* The alpha-defensin test demonstrated a sensitivity of 93%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100% and negative predictive value of 96%. Of the 41 patients in this study, only one patient (with poliomyositis) had a false negative result. **The authors concluded** that the alpha-defensin test retains its highly accurate diagnostic abilities in the setting of inflammatory diseases.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer choice 1: In the setting of ALTR, the alpha-defensin test can often be falsely positive. Unlike a native hip, a positive test in this scenario does not indicate PJI.
Answer choice 2: The elevated ESR and CRP should caution clinicians that infection may be present, although PJI cannot be confirmed by these markers alone. Furthermore, inflammatory markers are often falsely elevated in the setting of ALTR.
Answer choice 3: The alpha-defensin test remains an accurate test for PJI in the presence of inflammatory disease, as demonstrated by the Miyamae study.
Answer choice 4: The presence of ALTR does not exclude the diagnosis of PJI. Patients presenting with the symptoms in the vignette should be worked up for PJI, despite concurrent ALTR or not.
