## Question

A patient presents to the emergency room following a twisting injury of the right lower extremity. They are found to have a rotational ankle fracture for which they undergo a closed reduction. The following week, they present to your clinic, where radiographs confirm maintenance of adequate reduction. Which of the following patients would be the most appropriate candidate for primary retrograde tibiotalocalcaneal intramedullary nailing rather than open reduction and internal fixation?

## Options

1. 34-year-old male smoker with a BMI of 31 and a bimalleolar fracture with syndesmotic widening
2. 58-year-old female with type 2 diabetes mellitus, a hemoglobin A1c of 6.1%, without no peripheral neuropathy or peripheral vascular disease
3. 71-year-old female with type 2 diabetes mellitus, peripheral neuropathy, and a hemoglobin A1c of 9.4%
4. 45-year-old male with peripheral vascular disease and a trimalleolar fracture dislocation
5. 67-year-old active female with osteopenia, a history of prior ipsilateral ankle ORIF, and a displaced lateral malleolus refracture through retained hardware

## Correct option

**3. 71-year-old female with type 2 diabetes mellitus, peripheral neuropathy, and a hemoglobin A1c of 9.4%**

## Answer explanation

Patients with complicated diabetes—defined by the presence of neuropathy, nephropathy, or peripheral vascular disease—carry substantially elevated risks of wound breakdown, infection, hardware failure, and Charcot arthropathy following open ankle fracture fixation. In this patient population, primary retrograde tibiotalocalcaneal nailing offers some advantages over open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), as it provides a minimally invasive, robust construct that limits soft tissue dissection and permits earlier weight bearing (answer choice 3).

Ankle fractures in patients with complicated diabetes mellitus are associated with significantly increased rates of complications regardless of whether operative or nonoperative treatment is employed, including delayed fracture healing, wound complications, infection, amputation, and Charcot arthropathy. Patients with sequelae of poorly controlled diabetes have complication rates more than twice those of patients who lack these comorbidities, and current management favors operative intervention over nonoperative treatment, given the high rates of fracture reduction loss and Charcot arthropathy development seen with casting. Enhanced fixation techniques—including multiple quadricortical syndesmotic screws, tibiotalar Steinmann pins, hindfoot nailing, and ankle-spanning external fixation—have been described as adjuncts or alternatives to standard fixation, with recent evidence supporting acute or subacute tibiotalocalcaneal fusion in this population. Regardless of fixation method, meticulous soft-tissue management, stable rigid fixation, and prolonged protected immobilization are paramount in minimizing complications and yielding good functional results.

*Ebaugh et al. conducted a retrospective study of 27 patients with complicated diabetes who underwent primary retrograde tibiotalocalcaneal nailing of their ankle fracture without formal joint preparation, hypothesizing that this minimally invasive, robust construct would result in high limb salvage rates, acceptable complication rates, and less loss of function.* **They found** an overall limb salvage rate of 92.6%, a fracture union rate of 88%, a surgical complication rate of 18.5%, and a mean time to weight bearing of 6.7 weeks, with no instances of malunion or Charcot arthropathy. **They concluded** that primary tibiotalocalcaneal nailing can be considered an appropriate approach to increase the overall survivability of threatened limbs in this population, offering a minimally invasive single operation that allows for earlier weight bearing compared with formal open reduction and internal fixation.

*Chaudhary et al. reviewed the pathophysiology, evaluation, and treatment of ankle fractures in patients with diabetes mellitus,* **highlighting** that this population carries significantly higher rates of in-hospital mortality, postoperative complications, and nonroutine discharges compared with nondiabetic patients. **They found** that diabetic patients are at significant risk for soft tissue complications, that fracture healing is significantly delayed, and that Charcot ankle arthropathy occurs more commonly in patients who experience a delay in immobilization or who are treated nonsurgically for displaced ankle fractures. **They concluded** that adherence to meticulous soft tissue management, stable rigid fixation, and prolonged protected immobilization are paramount in minimizing complications and yielding good functional outcomes.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Smoking is a recognized risk factor for adverse outcomes following ankle fracture fixation, but this patient lacks the defining features of complicated diabetes that drive the preference for tibiotalocalcaneal nailing over standard open fixation.
Answer choice 2: Well-controlled diabetes without peripheral neuropathy or peripheral vascular disease does not meet the definition of complicated diabetes; patients without these comorbidities do not carry the markedly elevated wound complication risk that makes tibiotalocalcaneal nailing preferable to ORIF.
Answer choice 4: Although peripheral vascular disease does present an independent risk factor for surgical complications, there is no literature support for tibiotalocalcaneal nailing for this indication alone.
Answer choice 5: Osteopenia and prior hardware in the setting of a refracture may complicate standard plate fixation, but these features alone do not obviate the possibility of revision ankle ORIF or represent a strong indication for tibiotalocalcaneal nailing, which, although provides stable fixation, does sacrifice ankle and subtalar motion.
