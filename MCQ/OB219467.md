## Question

A 64-year-old female presents to the clinic for continued management of right posterior heel pain unresponsive to eight months of nonoperative treatment which has included shoe wear modifications, physical therapy, and anti-inflammatories. On examination, a prominence is noted on the posterior heel with tenderness to palpation, and the foot can be dorsiflexed to neutral when the knee is extended but 15 degrees when flexed. Lateral radiographs obtained previously in the clinic are shown in Figure A, and the patient is subsequently scheduled for surgery. Intraoperatively, 40% of the tendon is debrided secondary to calcifications. What other treatments are indicated for this patient?

## Options

1. Calcaneoplasty/Haglund excision
2. Flexor hallucis longus transfer to Achilles
3. Gastrocnemius recession
4. I and III
5. I, II, and III

## Correct option

**4. I and III**

## Answer explanation

This 64-year-old female demonstrates insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) with a Haglund deformity and gastrocnemius contracture. The most appropriate surgical treatment includes Achilles tendon detachment with Haglund deformity excision/calcaneoplasty and gastrocnemius recession.

Insertional Achilles tendinopathy is a common condition falling under a spectrum of diseases affecting the Achilles tendon (in addition to retrocalcaneal bursitis and Achilles tendinitis). IAT often occurs in middle-aged patients with tight gastrocnemius muscles and possible bony prominences of the calcaneus. Clinically, patients exhibit rather exquisite midline posterior heel pain with a possible bony prominence and an aversion to closed-heel shoes secondary to the significant tenderness. The diagnosis is typically made clinically, however, plain films are necessary to assess for the presence of Haglund formation and intratendinous calcifications/enesthesophytes. Initial management includes shoewear modifications, physical therapy (emphasizing eccentric exercises and stretching), and NSAIDs, though high rates of failure have been reported in the literature. As such, operative intervention is commonly indicated in the form of tendon detachment with calcaneoplasty/Haglund excision and tendon reattachment. Gastrocnemius recession can be performed in the presence of a gastrocnemius contracture (as evidenced by improved dorsiflexion with knee flexion during the Silfverskiold test). Flexor hallucis longus (FHL) transfer to the Achilles is indicated when tendon debridement comprised greater than 50%.

*Chen et al. performed a review examining the management of insertional Achilles tendinopathy.* **The authors highlight** nonoperative treatment for a minimum of three months, but note high failure rates (45.6%). When indicated, surgical management typically involves tendon detachment with calcaneoplasty followed by tendon repair with possible FHL augmentation for tendon debridements greater than 50% and gastrocnemius recession in those with gastrocnemius contracture. **The authors ultimately recommend** performing the procedure prone via an open technique involving tendon detachment, Haglund removal, and tendon reattachment.

*DeOrio et al. similarly review surgical techniques for insertional Achilles tendinopathy,* **noting** a number of valid approaches to treat the disease, which include endoscopic and several open methods. However, **the authors highlight** the importance of augmenting the Achilles tendon repair in instances of debridements surpassing 50% of the entire tendon. **The authors conclude** by describing their preferred open technique, which includes a direct posterior midline approach.

Figure 1 is a lateral ankle radiograph revealing a Haglund deformity of the calcaneus with enesthesophyte formation within the Achilles tendon.

Answers 1,2,3, and 5: in addition to tendon debridement, only calcaneoplasty and gastrocnemius recession are needed secondary to the patient’s Haglund deformity and gastrocnemius contracture. FHL transfer is not required in this patient as less than 50% of the tendon was debrided.
