## Question

A healthy pediatric patient presents to your office because of a leg length discrepancy. On physical examination, the Galeazzi test is positive. They proceed with an open hip reduction, iliac osteotomy, femoral shortening with a derotational osteotomy, and spica cast application. Which of the following clinical scenarios best fits this surgical treatment strategy?

## Options

1. 9-month old (not yet ambulatory) patient with reducible hip on exam who has already previously failed a Pavlik harness
2. 22-month old ambulatory patient with an irreducible hip, normal external and internal rotation, and a mildly increased acetabular index
3. 5-month old patient with positive Barlow and Ortolani maneuvers on exam
4. 26-month old ambulatory patient with severe acetabular dysplasia and an irreducible hip, increased hip internal rotation, and decreased hip external rotation on exam
5. 12-month old (not yet ambulatory) patient with an irreducible hip, normal internal/external rotation on exam, and mild dysplasia

## Correct option

**4. 26-month old ambulatory patient with severe acetabular dysplasia and an irreducible hip, increased hip internal rotation, and decreased hip external rotation on exam**

## Answer explanation

The patient who best fits the surgical treatment strategy mentioned in this question stem is a 26-month-old ambulatory patient with severe acetabular dysplasia, an irreducible hip, increased hip internal rotation, and decreased hip external rotation on exam (answer choice 4). This patient is >2 years of age, ambulatory, and demonstrates physical examination findings consistent with a leg length discrepancy and increased femoral anteversion. Therefore, he will benefit most from an open hip reduction, iliac osteotomy to correct the acetabular dysplasia, femoral shortening and derotational osteotomy to correct femoral anteversion and facilitate proper stability, in addition to spica cast application.

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is the result of abnormal development leading to acetabular dysplasia, femoral head subluxation, and possible dislocation of the hip secondary to capsular laxity and mechanical instability. DDH is the most common orthopedic disorder in newborns, with dysplasia (1:100) being more common than dislocation (1:1,000). This condition is much more common in female (6:1) Native Americans. Risk factors include firstborn children, females, breech presentation, and family history, among others. Primary care providers perform newborn screening, and if concerning findings are noted, orthopedic providers are involved, and a hip ultrasound is performed in those 0-4 months of age. Treatment of this condition is based on many factors. A Pavlik harness can be used in those <6 months of age, open versus closed reduction and spica casting can be utilized in those 6-18 months of age, femoral derotational +/- shortening osteotomies are typically performed in those >2 years of age with residual dysplasia, and pelvic osteotomies can be performed >2 years of age with severe dysplasia.

*Scott, Dolan, and Weinstein compared long-term outcomes between closed reduction alone (45 patients) versus open reduction with Salter Innominate osteotomy (58 patients) in children between the ages of 18 and 60 months.* *A minimum follow-up of 40 years (up to 48 years) was achieved in this study.* **They found** that at 48 years, 50% of the hips survived without a total hip arthroplasty (THA), whereas 69% of hips in the open reduction group survived without a THA. At 45 years, the open reduction group with a Salter innominate osteotomy had a higher hip survival probability than the closed reduction group (0.63 vs 0.55). **They conclude** that both treatments provide significant long-term benefits and that patient age and laterality should be considered when deciding on the type of treatment.

Wenger provided a comprehensive overview of operative management of DDH. He states the goal of surgical treatment is to achieve and maintain a concentric hip reduction to optimize conditions for normal hip development and minimize long-term degenerative changes. Surgical treatment strategies are guided by patient age and ossification status: 6-18 months of age can be treated with an open reduction via a traditional anterior or medial approach, and after walking age (>18-24 months), open reduction is typically combined with femoral and/or pelvic osteotomies to improve stability and acetabular containment. He also notes that femoral shortening in older, walking-age children has been shown to reduce the risk of redislocation and avascular necrosis. Wenger concludes that successful treatment of DDH involves both bony and soft-tissue management to achieve stable reduction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: In a 9-month-old patient with a reducible hip on exam and previous failure of a Pavlik harness, she would benefit most from closed reduction and spica cast application.
Answer 2: A 22-month-old patient with an irreducible hip, mild dysplasia, and normal hip range of motion would benefit from an open reduction and spica cast application. If severe dysplasia was present and the patient was >24 months of age, pelvic +/- femoral osteotomies would be recommended.
Answer 3: A 5-month-old patient with positive Barlow and Ortolani testing on exam demonstrates a reducible hip that would benefit from a Pavlik harness.
Answer 5: A 12-month-old patient with an irreducible hip on exam, mild dysplasia, and normal hip range of motion would benefit from an open hip reduction and spica cast application.
