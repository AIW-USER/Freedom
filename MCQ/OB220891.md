## Question

A 20-year-old male presents to your office with a history of chronic left ankle pain that was found to be caused by a lateral talar dome osteochondral defect (OCD) 2 years ago. He successfully underwent retrograde drilling at that time. Of the following OCD characteristics listed below, which are associated with the best outcomes following retrograde drilling +/- bone grafting?

## Options

1. 1.5 cm defect with fragment displacement and subchondral edema on MRI
2. 0.8 cm defect without fragment displacement or subchondral edema on MRI
3. 1.2 cm defect with intact cartilage cap and no fragment displacement
4. 0.5 cm defect with fragment displacement and subchondral edema on MRI
5. Acute injury with osteochondral loose body and 1.2 cm defect

## Correct option

**3. 1.2 cm defect with intact cartilage cap and no fragment displacement**

## Answer explanation

The patient developed a lateral talar dome osteochondral defect (OCD) that was successfully treated with retrograde drilling. Retrograde drilling with or without bone grafting is associated with the best outcomes in those lesions >1 cm with an intact cartilage cap and no fragment displacement (answer 3).

Osteochondral lesions involving the talus are focal injuries to the talar dome with variable involvement of the subchondral bone and cartilage. These are either caused by acute trauma or repetitive microtrauma over time. These occur in approximately 69% of ankle fractures and 70% of ankle sprains. 10% are bilateral. The location of these lesions typically reveals the underlying mechanism, with medial talar dome lesions more commonly presenting without trauma and lateral talar lesions presenting following trauma. Diagnosis of these lesions can be made initially with a thorough physical exam. Plain radiographs can help diagnose OCD lesions, and an MRI can help determine the size and stability of lesions while evaluating for additional injuries or intra-articular loose bodies. Treatment depends on the size and stability of the lesion and other associated injuries. In acute, nondisplaced fragments with incomplete fractures, these are treated with immobilization (non-weight-bearing) and nonoperative treatment. Operative intervention is reserved for the unstable lesions and those with intra-articular loose bodies. These include arthroscopy with debridement/marrow stimulation, retrograde drilling +/- bone grafting, and osteochondral grafting.

*McGahan et al. wrote a review on OCD lesions in the talus.* *They describe the etiology as arising from either an acute ankle injury or repetitive loading.* **They report** that OCD lesions involving the medial aspect of the talar dome are more common than the lateral lesions, with an incidence of 50-56% compared to 34-44%. **They note** that lateral OCD lesions in the talus are associated with trauma in approximately 90% of cases versus 60% in medial lesions. **They report** that advanced imaging is critical for proper evaluation of the stability, size, and location of the lesion. **They conclude** that marrow stimulation with debridement of the lesion has been shown to be effective in a majority of symptomatic talar OCD lesions.

*Ahmad et al. performed a prospective study evaluating the intermediate-term operative outcomes in talar OCD lesions (1.5 cm2 or smaller) treated with arthroscopic excision, microfracture, and allograft cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM).* *Between 2012 and 2015, 30 patients received allograft cartilage ECM at their microfractured OCD lesions after failing nonoperative treatment.* **They reported** a mean follow-up of 20.2 months with significantly improved pain and functional outcome scores postoperatively (p<0.05). **They conclude** that the use of allograft cartilage ECM in their study resulted in a high rate of improvement in ankle function and pain in patients with talar OCD lesions 1.5 cm2 or smaller.

Incorrect Answer Choices:
Answer 1: Fragment displacement is best treated with either fixation or debridement and antegrade drilling/microfracture, not retrograde drilling.
Answer 2: This lesion is less than 1 cm, and there is an absence of subchondral edema, signifying this is a stable and small lesion that would be better treated with immobilization initially.
Answer 4: The fragment is displaced and small (0.5 cm); therefore, debridement and antegrade drilling/microfracture will provide better outcomes, not retrograde drilling.
Answer 5: You can assume the intra-articular loose body is from a displaced osteochondral fragment following an acute injury. Therefore, arthroscopic removal of the loose body, combined with debridement and antegrade drilling, will provide better outcomes.
