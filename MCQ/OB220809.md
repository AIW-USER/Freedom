## Question

A 45-year-old immigrant man, recently arrived from a region with a high prevalence of endemic infectious diseases, presents with a 6-month history of chronic, worsening ankle pain, swelling, and stiffness. He denies any history of trauma. Physical examination shows a diffusely swollen, warm ankle with a restricted range of motion. Laboratory workup reveals mildly elevated inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP), but is negative for rheumatoid factor (RF) and antinuclear antibodies (ANA). MRI shows extensive bony destruction and joint erosion on both sides of the tibiotalar joint, highly suspicious for an aggressive infective or inflammatory arthropathy, but no discrete mass. Given the patient's history, presentation, and atypical nature of the joint destruction, what is the most appropriate next diagnostic step?

## Options

1. Initiate empirical treatment with broad-spectrum oral antibiotics
2. Order an extensive panel of inflammatory markers, including HLA-B27 and anti-CCP
3. Perform an open biopsy of the joint and bone for tissue histology and specialized cultures
4. Schedule the patient for an immediate ankle arthrodesis for definitive pain control
5. Start a therapeutic trial of high-dose corticosteroids to rule out an inflammatory arthritis

## Correct option

**3. Perform an open biopsy of the joint and bone for tissue histology and specialized cultures**

## Answer explanation

An open biopsy provides definitive tissue diagnosis and allows for specialized cultures necessary to identify atypical pathogens, such as tuberculosis, which can cause chronic destructive monoarthritis.

The patient presents with chronic, destructive monoarthritis in the ankle, which, combined with his travel history and non-diagnostic lab work for common inflammatory conditions, raises suspicion for an atypical infectious arthropathy (e.g., tuberculosis, fungal infection). Unlike pyogenic septic arthritis, which is acute, these atypical infections are indolent and cause gradual bony erosion. Synovial fluid aspiration often yields low bacterial counts. Therefore, the definitive step is an open biopsy to obtain a large tissue specimen for histology (looking for granulomas) and to perform specialized cultures (e.g., for acid-fast bacilli and fungi) that are critical for identifying these slow-growing, less common pathogens. Identifying the etiology is paramount before any definitive surgical reconstruction, like ankle fusion.

*Guerra and Resnick evaluated the correlation between radiographic and pathological findings in various arthritides affecting the foot.* **They noted** that certain chronic infections, like tuberculosis, can present with insidious onset and slow, progressive joint destruction that mimics other forms of arthritis. **They concluded** that histological analysis combined with radiographic findings is crucial for distinguishing between infectious, inflammatory, and degenerative arthropathies.

*Biviji et al. reviewed the various musculoskeletal manifestations of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection.* **They noted** that immunocompromised patients are at a higher risk for unusual or endemic atypical infections and inflammatory conditions that can affect the joints. **They concluded** that when confronted with atypical arthropathy, considering uncommon infectious agents is vital, particularly in patients with predisposing factors.

*Feiler et al. presented a case of ankle tuberculosis and reviewed the relevant literature.* **They noted** that tuberculosis of the ankle is rare and often presents chronically, leading to a delay in diagnosis due to its non-specific symptoms and radiographic findings. **They concluded** that an aggressive biopsy, including tissue for histology and specialized cultures, is required to definitively diagnose chronic granulomatous infections like tuberculosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Empirical antibiotics are unlikely to cover atypical organisms and may sterilize the site, hindering a future diagnosis without addressing the underlying pathology.
Answer 2: These markers are for specific seronegative or rheumatoid arthritides, which are less likely than an infection in this specific clinical context.
Answer 4: Fusion should not be performed until the infection is identified and definitively treated, as it risks fusing an active infection.
Answer 5: Corticosteroids would suppress the immune response and potentially worsen an underlying active infectious process, such as tuberculosis.
