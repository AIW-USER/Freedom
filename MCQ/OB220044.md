## Question

## Options

1. Counsel on risks of over-training, recommend decreased intensity, and refer to physical therapy for stress injury
2. Dietician consultation given her BMI, normal radiographs, and increased nutritional requirements at her level of training
3. Immediate cessation of training and referral for MRI
4. Order a bone scan and refer to an orthopaedic oncologist given incidental radiographic finding
5. Physical therapy consultation for bone stimulator and therapeutic ultrasound treatment of tibial stress injury

## Correct option

**3. Immediate cessation of training and referral for MRI**

## Answer explanation

The patient has a femoral stress injury as seen on her radiographs (Figures C-D). Given her progressive pain, including at rest, she should be withheld from further training until advanced imaging is obtained to ensure that she does not have a femoral stress fracture (Answer 3).

Relative energy deficiency in sport (REDS) represents a triad of low energy availability (inadequate caloric intake) with or without disordered eating, amenorrhea (as defined by a lack of menstrual periods for 3 months or more), and low bone mineral density. In an athlete who satisfies these conditions with a primary symptom of pain with ambulation, it is imperative to rule out a stress fracture. First-line treatment involves cessation of sport and obtaining imaging to diagnose a bone stress injury. Given that radiographs may be normal or equivocal, MRI is the preferred imaging method and should be obtained prior to return to sport to differentiate a stress reaction from an impending or complete stress fracture. Bone scan, on the contrary, has largely fallen out of favor given the requirement for use of a radioisotope and relatively high levels of ionizing radiation.

*Beck et al. provide a narrative review of the diagnosis and management of bone stress injuries in adolescent athletes.* **The authors note** that some athletes who train intensely are at risk of bone stress injury (BSI), with the incidence in adolescent athlete populations approaching 19% and recurrence rates as high as 21%. **They conclude** that the gold standard approach to managing BSIs in adolescent athletes is prevention and recommend a multidisciplinary approach that should include: enhancing bone development with targeted bone loading throughout childhood (maximizing variety of loading and using resistance and impact training), managing training load with graduated increments in intensity and rest periods, optimizing energy balance, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, rectifying biomechanical and kinematic anomalies, encouraging diversity of sports participation (minimizing sports specialization), and recognizing and managing medications of relevance to bone health.

*Gallant et al. reviewed bone stress injuries in female athletes.* **The authors note** that females are at a higher risk than males for bone stress injuries due to a number of unique intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors. In particular, increased energy expenditure compounded by inadequate nutrition during adolescence may lead to menstrual and hormonal irregularities resulting in a hypo-estrogenic state that impairs bone health and bone density, ultimately leading to lower peak bone mass that is irreversible and may put them at risk for bone stress injuries later in life. **They conclude** that, though most low-risk stress fractures can be managed with a period of rest and activity modification, surgical management may be indicated in certain high-risk or recurrent stress fractures, making an understanding of the pathophysiology, risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment of stress fractures essential for all clinicians treating female athletes.

Figures A and B are normal AP and frog leg lateral radiographs of a right hip. Figures C and D are AP and lateral radiographs of a femur showing smooth cortical thickening in the distal medial femoral diaphysis consistent with a femoral stress injury. Figures E and F are normal AP and lateral radiographs of the knee showing no abnormalities in the proximal tibia. Illustrations A and B are axial and coronal STIR MRI images demonstrating edema in the distal, medial femoral diaphysis periosteum, cortex, and bone marrow, with smooth bony callus overlying the area of edema but without a definite fracture line seen.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Cessation of training should be recommended to ensure that she does not go on to a catastrophic complete femoral stress fracture.
Answer 4: Though she does have a femoral lesion, this is most consistent with a stress injury, and does not merit an immediate referral to an orthopaedic oncologist.
Answer 5: The patient has a femoral, not tibial, stress injury, for which bone stimulator and ultrasound therapeutic evidence is limited.
