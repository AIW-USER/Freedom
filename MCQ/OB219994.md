## Question

A 65-year-old male presents to your clinic for the initial evaluation of chronic right knee pain from osteoarthritis. The patient has been utilizing ibuprofen daily to mitigate his painful symptoms for the past year. The patient is concerned that his chronic utilization of ibuprofen may predispose him to side effects such as gastrointestinal upset and gastric ulcer formation. What is the mechanism by which ibuprofen causes the aforementioned adverse reactions?

## Options

1. Selective inhibition of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) leads to decreased thromboxane production and disruption of the intestinal phospholipid bilayer
2. Selective inhibition of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) leads to decreased prostaglandin production and uncoupling of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation
3. Nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to increased prostaglandin production and disruption of the intestinal phospholipid bilayer
4. Nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to decreased thromboxane production and increased prostaglandin production with uncoupling of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation
5. Nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to decreased thromboxane and prostaglandin production with disruption of the intestinal phospholipid bilayer

## Correct option

**5. Nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to decreased thromboxane and prostaglandin production with disruption of the intestinal phospholipid bilayer**

## Answer explanation

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including Ibuprofen, decrease the production of both thromboxane and prostaglandins through the nonspecific inhibition of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) which leads to low-grade intestinal inflammation and secondary disruption of the intestinal phospholipid bilayer (Answer 5).

The nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 via NSAIDs such as Ibuprofen is responsible for a multitude of adverse drug reactions, including but not limited to gastrointestinal (GI) upset, ulcer formation, and bleeding. There are two isoforms of COX: COX-1, which has widespread functions including systemic hemostasis and cytoprotection for gastric mucosa, and COX-2, which has a crucial role in prostanoid production for the inflammatory response. The mechanism by which nonspecific NSAIDs lead to the aforementioned adverse side effects includes uncoupling mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, initiating biochemical changes that increase intestinal permeability, low-grade inflammation, and disruption of the phospholipid bilayer within the intestinal mucosa.

*Trasolini et al. reviewed the safety and efficacy of postoperative NSAIDs in sports medicine patients.* **They found** that utilization of nonspecific NSAIDs decreases the production of thromboxanes, prostaglandins, and prostacyclins predisposing to GI upset and ulcer formation when used chronically but can be effective for short-term usage in the form of a multimodal pain control regimen to alleviate pain and decrease opiate usage. **The authors conclude** that NSAIDs vary in COX-1 and COX-2 selectivity, and selective COX-2 inhibitors may effectively reduce inflammation with fewer gastrointestinal side effects.

*Siow et al. reviewed the utility of a multimodal pain regimen in decreasing opioid consumption after orthopedic trauma surgeries.* *All patients within their study group received a continuous peripheral nerve block with 0.2% ropivacaine placed preoperatively and removed at home on postoperative day 3 in conjunction with acetaminophen, ketorolac, gabapentin, and oxycodone.* **They found** that utilization of this protocol led to a 59% reduction in opioids consumed in the first 4 days after surgery. **The authors conclude** that implementation of a multimodal pain control protocol can decrease opioid consumption by greater than 50% in the first 4 days postoperatively.

*Hagedorn et al. reviewed the basic science of opioid pain medications, factors influencing efficacy, and the role of non-opiate pain medications within a multimodal pain control regimen.* Opioids, including alternatives like tramadol, provide pain relief by acting on the mu-opioid receptor (MOR), but factors like enzyme metabolism can influence their effectiveness. Non-addictive options such as NSAIDs and acetaminophen can complement pain management. Still, opioid therapy must be carefully monitored using tools like oral morphine equivalent (OME) tables, and enzyme interactions must be considered to optimize safety and efficacy. **The authors conclude** that opioid pain medications can help control pain; however, surgeons should be competent in the proper utilization and understanding of OME tables for the de-escalation of opiate use, CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 interactions that may decrease opiate effectiveness, and the thoughtful utilization of non-opiate pain medication in the treatment of postoperative pain.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Ibuprofen is a nonselective NSAID. An example of a COX-1 selective NSAID would be Ketorolac
Answer 2: Ibuprofen is a nonselective NSAID. An example of a COX-2 selective NSAID would be Celecoxib
Answer 3: The nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to decreased prostaglandin production, not increased
Answer 4: The nonspecific inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2 leads to decreased prostaglandin and thromboxane production
