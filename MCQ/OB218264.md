## Question

A 63-year-old female presents to your office with complaints of persistent knee pain which has been worsening for the last 7-8 months. She is 5'1" and weighs 200 lbs (BMI=38 m/kg^2). She has previously taken glucosamine/chondroitin supplements in combination with a self-directed low impact exercise program but these modalities have not improved her pain significantly. Which of the following treatment strategies is strongly recommended as the next best step based on the 2021 AAOS clinical practice guidelines for knee arthritis?

## Options

1. Physical therapy
2. Weight loss consultation
3. Intra-articular injection (corticosteroids or hyaluronic acid)
4. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDS)
5. Tramadol

## Correct option

**4. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDS)**

## Answer explanation

The AAOS clinical practice guidelines (CPG) for treatment of knee osteoarthritis (OA) were recently updated in 2021, with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDS) receiving a strong recommendation for use.

Knee OA is one of the most common orthopedic complaints, with an incidence of >240/100,000 people. The goal of the AAOS CPG recommendations are to provide advice to practitioners treating these patients as to which modalities are evidence-based. In their most recent update for knee OA, they strongly recommend the following: supervised/self-managed exercise, patient education, oral/topical NSAIDS, and oral acetaminophen. They also strongly recommend against the use of oral narcotics and lateral wedge insoles. In a patient who has failed a home exercise program, medications like acetaminophen and NSAIDS (topical and/or oral) can be helpful in relieving pain and improving function.

*Brophy et al. published a systematic review outlining the recent 2021 AAOS clinic practice guidelines for the management of knee OA.* *They review all 29 recommendations, with 8 of them being strong recommendations.* Finally, **they note** there is more investigation needed into the use of intra-articular corticosteroid injections, hyaluronic acid injections and platelet-rich plasma injections.

*Schnitzer et al. reviewed the efficacy and safety of NSAIDS compared to a placebo when used over 53 weeks for knee OA.* **They noted** that changes in pain scores were improved compared to placebo at 13 and 26 weeks. **They also concluded** that oral NSAIDS were safe, with no considerable changes in blood pressure or other complications.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While self-directed and supervised therapy both have strong recommendations by the AAOS, this patient has already failed therapy and should be offered a different treatment strategy that is also strongly recommended.
Answer 2: Under current AAOS guidelines, weight loss is recommended but only represents a moderate strength recommendation due to the inconsistent ability of studies to show weight loss correlates with significant outcome improvement.
Answer 3: Current AAOS guidelines have a moderate strength recommendation against the use of hyaluronic acid injections for knee OA, as it is felt that patients do not demonstrate a clinically relevant difference when compared to controls. These guidelines also established only a moderate strength recommendation in favor of corticosteroid injections.
Answer 5: Current AAOS guidelines recommend strongly against tramadol and other narcotic pain medication given the fact they increase adverse events without being effective at improving pain or function for patients with knee OA
