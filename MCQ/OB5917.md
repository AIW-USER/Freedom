## Question

A 50-year-old-man with a history of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus has a plantar foot ulcer that has failed 2 months of contact casting. Physical exam is significant for a persistent deep, full-thickness ulcer but with no gross purulence. The foot is erythematous, and this persists despite adequate elevation. What is the next best step?

## Options

1. Continuation of contact casting
2. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics for 6 weeks
3. In-office wound culture with directed antibiotic therapy
4. Intra-operative debridement and irrigation with culture
5. Skin grafting

## Correct option

**4. Intra-operative debridement and irrigation with culture**

## Answer explanation

The next best step for an intractable deep, full-thickness diabetic foot ulcer is formal debridement and irrigation and antibiotic therapy prior to continuation of contact casting. Osteomyelitis is present in most cases of chronic full-thickness ulcers with exposed bone.

Diabetic foot osteomyelitis often results from contiguous infection via foot ulcerations that begins due to a combination of predisposing factors to include but not exclusive to diminished protective sensation, autonomic dysfunction with angiopathy, impaired immune response, and decreased healing capacity. Deep ulcerations, especially those that probe to bone, have been found to be associated with osteomyelitis in nearly two-thirds of cases. Radiographs may show demineralization of the local osseous structures, though MRI is the most sensitive modality for diagnosis of associated osteomyelitis. Management dictates formal debridement and irrigation with intra-operative bone cultures for chronic osteomyelitis in this setting. In-office wound cultures are nonspecific. Severity of infection, associated ischemia, and location of the ulcer and compromised bone will dictate the type of surgical procedure. Antibiotic therapy is guided by bone biopsy results, with the most common pathogens being polymicrobial or staphylococcus aureus.

*Fujii et al. evaluated the efficacy of MRI in diagnosis of osteomyelitis in the presence of diabetic foot ulcers.* *The authors compared the results of the pre-operative MRI findings with the histopathologic exams of resected bone specimens.* **They found** that although preoperative MRI was very accurate in identifying osteomyelitis in the setting of neuropathic diabetic foot ulcers, it was less effective for ischemic ulcers, given that findings were more often negative or equivocal.

*Ertugrul et al. compared the presentation and diagnosis of osteomyelitis and charcot neuro-osteoarthropathy (CN) in the diabetic foot.* *The authors discussed the differences in physical exam, appearance on diagnostic imaging, and laboratory results for each of these conditions.* Differentiating between the two conditions is important as treatments for the two differ. While both may appear with clinical signs of infection, elevation of a foot with CN typically results in abatement of the erythema after time. CN is furthermore not typically associated with elevated inflammatory markers. **They note** that while MRI is excellent for diagnosis of osteomyelitis, leukocyte scintigraphy may better differentiate between infection and CN. **The authors state** that the work up is usually sufficient to provide a diagnosis, however, a bone biopsy will provide definitive diagnosis if there is any doubt.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Continued contact would be inappropriate due to the persistence and now high-grade of the ulcer. It is contraindicated in the setting of active infection.
Answer 2: Intravenous antibiotics are part of the treatment, however this is preceded by a bone biopsy in order to direct therapy.
Answer 3: Cultures should be performed in a formal procedure, as superficial wound cultures have been shown to be nonspecific.
Answer 5: Skin grafting, whether full- or split-thickness is not appropriate in the setting of a full-thickness ulcer with underlying osteomyelitis.
