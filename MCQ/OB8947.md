## Question

A 21-year-old male football player sustains a traumatic anterior shoulder dislocation that is successfully reduced. He is diagnosed with an anterior-inferior labral tear and taken to the OR two weeks later. Intraoperatively, the glenoid measures 9mm from the bare area to the anterior edge (Figure A, line A) and 12mm from the bare area to the posterior edge (Figure A, line B). The glenoid defect is 5mm in length (Figure B, line X). There is a Hill Sachs lesion that does not engage the anterior glenoid at 90° abduction and 90° external rotation. What is the next best step?

## Options

1. Bankart repair
2. Bankart repair with remplissage procedure
3. Capsular shift
4. Reconstruction of the glenoid with coracoid transfer (Latarjet procedure)
5. Reconstruction of the glenoid with distal tibia allograft

## Correct option

**1. Bankart repair**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a Bankart lesion with only 12.5% glenoid bone loss (GBL) and a non-engaging Hill Sachs lesion, and therefore would benefit from a Bankart repair alone.

Failure to identify and properly manage significant GBL is a common cause of recurrent shoulder instability. There are numerous methods for calculating GBL on CT scan and arthroscopically. Though the definition of “significant” GBL varies, current literature supports the idea that less than 15% GBL can be managed successfully with soft tissue stabilization alone. GBL greater than 25-30% often necessitates osseous augmentation techniques such as the Latarjet procedure, autograft iliac crest or allograft options including distal tibia and femoral head.

*Burkhart et al. retrospectively reviewed 194 arthroscopic Bankart repairs to identify factors related to recurrence of instability.* *Patients with significant GBL (defined as “inverted pear” glenoid) had an 89% recurrence rate, compared to 4% in patients without significant GBL.* In the absence of GBL, arthroscopic and open Bankart repairs yielded equal results in both contact and non-contact athletes. **The authors conclude** that significant GBL is a contraindication for arthroscopic Bankart repair and that a Latarjet procedure should be considered.

*Bushnell et al. reviewed the diagnosis and management of bony instability.* The Instability Severity Index Score takes into account age at surgery (less then 21 years), degree of sport participation (competitive), type of sport (contact), baseline shoulder hyperlaxity (anterior or inferior laxity), Hill Sachs lesion (visible on AP radiograph in external rotation) and GBL (loss of contour on AP radiograph). A score greater than 6 had at least a 70% risk of recurrence after arthroscopic Bankart repair.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The remplissage procedure involves suturing the infraspinatus and posterior capsule into the Hill Sachs lesion and is indicated when an engaging Hill-Sachs lesion is present.
Answer 3: The Bankart lesion must be repaired. Capsular shift alone would not provided sufficient stability.
Answers 4 + 5: Osseous reconstruction techniques using coracoid autograft (Latarjet procedure) or distal tibia allograft are more appropriate in cases of significant GBL (>25-30%).
