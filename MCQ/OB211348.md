## Question

Figure A is the radiograph of a 22-year-old collegiate athlete who presents for the evaluation of bilateral hip pain. He reports longstanding hip problems that began when he was an infant. When asked, he denies any specific injuries or traumatic events. He endorses bilateral activity-related groin pain that is also worsened by prolonged sitting. What physical exam finding is most consistent with this patient’s pathology?

![[q211348-a-d701fc0760b5bc170ce09092e9b33e-big-gallery.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Positive Stinchfield test
2. Pain with flexion abduction and external rotation (FABER) test
3. Positive anterior impingement test
4. Trendelburg gait
5. Pain with half sit-up and palpation of the pubic rami

## Correct option

**3. Positive anterior impingement test**

## Answer explanation

Figure A demonstrates bilateral cam lesions of the proximal femur. Patients with cam-type femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) classically have anterior impingement signs including pain with the combination of hip flexion, adduction, and internal rotation.

FAI is separated into cam-type, pincer-type, and combined CAM and pincer type lesions which occur due to abnormal contact between the femur and acetabulum. This leads to labral damage and varying degrees of chondral injuries. Cam-type lesions are femoral sided and are characterized by a decreased head-to-neck ratio, aspherical femoral head, decreased femoral offset or femoral neck retroversion. Radiographs classically demonstrate a pistol grip deformity due to the asphericity and contour of the femoral head/neck. Patients typically report activity-related hip pain that is exacerbated by the combination of hip flexion, internal rotation, and adduction (anterior impingement test). Treatment begins with nonoperative modalities including activity modification and PT followed by arthroscopic or open osteoplasty for those who do not improve.

*Philippon et al. review the associated pathologies and return to sport following arthroscopic decompression in 45 professional athletes with FAI.* **They report** that 93% of patients were able to return to sport, with 78% returning to professional competition following arthroscopic treatment. **They conclude** that arthroscopic treatment of FAI is a prudent option that allows even the most competitive athletes to return to sport.

*Nepple et al. review the clinical and radiographic predictors of intra-articular hip disease identified during arthroscopy.* **They report** that labral tears and acetabular cartilage lesions were present in 90.1 and 67.3% of patients, respectively. Furthermore, following logistic regression analysis, male sex, older age, Tonnis grade, and alpha angle were independent risk factors for more severe intraarticular disease. **They conclude** that the recognition of these associations is an important part of patient selection and surgical planning.

*Naal et al. review surgical hip dislocations for the treatment of FAI in high-level athletes.* **They report** that 96% (n=21) of patients returned to professional sports following surgery and that 82% were satisfied with their hip surgery. **They conclude** that surgical hip dislocations for the treatment of FAI allow for both good results and return to sports in this very demanding cohort.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The Stinchfield resisted hip flexion test is positive if the patient experiences groin pain, which may be indicative of a femoral neck fracture, iliopsoas tendonitis or abscess.
Answer 2: The FABER test is a provocative test for lumbar spine and iliopsoas pathologies, and is not routinely used for the diagnosis of FAI.
Answer 4: A Trendelenburg gait is caused by unilateral weakness of the hip abductors and is not likely to be caused by FAI.
Answer 5: Pain with half sit-up and palpation of the pubic rami is consistent with athletic pubalgia, which can be a cause of groin pain in athletes. However, this patient has radiographs consistent with FAI, which is more likely to be causing his symptoms.
