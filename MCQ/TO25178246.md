## Question

A 7-year-old child with spastic diplegic cerebral palsy is being evaluated for a long-term mobility plan. The child is able to walk indoors and outdoors on level surfaces and can climb stairs holding onto a railing. However, when navigating uneven terrain, inclines, or long distances in the community, he requires a handheld mobility device (like a walker or crutches). He is unable to run or jump.
According to the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS), what is this child's level?

## Options

1. Level I
2. Level II
3. Level III
4. Level IV

## Correct option

**3. Level III**

## Answer explanation

The transition between Level II and Level III often centers on the need for assistive devices and the environment of ambulation.

 - Level II children walk in most settings but may have difficulty with long distances or uneven terrain. Crucially, they do not typically require a handheld mobility device for most indoor walking after age 4.

 - Level III children use a handheld mobility device (walker, crutches, canes) for most indoor walking. They may climb stairs holding onto a railing with supervision or assistance. For community mobility and long distances, they usually use wheeled mobility.

 - Level I children walk at home, school, and outdoors; they can climb stairs without a railing and perform gross motor skills like running/jumping (though with decreased speed/coordination).

Why the case study points to Level III:

Hint is in this sentence-

"...when navigating uneven terrain, inclines, or long distances in the community, he requires a handheld mobility device (like a walker or crutches)."

In the GMFCS-E&R criteria:

 - Level II children typically walk in the community and at school. They might use a railing for stairs, but they are "community ambulators" who generally don't use walkers/crutches once they've reached age 4–6. These children may have difficulty with uneven terrain or long distances, but they typically do not use handheld devices. They might use a railing for stairs or wheeled mobility only for very long community distances to save energy.

 - Level III children are "assisted ambulators." The moment a child requires a handheld device for the "community" or "uneven surfaces" while being able to manage a railing indoors, they fall into Level III. They can often "shuffle" or walk short distances indoors on level ground without a device (often holding onto furniture), but they require a handheld device (walker/crutches) for any functional ambulation outdoors or on surfaces that aren't perfectly flat.

The critical conceptual principle underlying the GMFCS is that it classifies typical performance (what the child actually does in daily life) rather than best capability (what the child can do under ideal conditions). Distinctions between levels are clinically meaningful — they are based on functional limitations and the need for assistive technology, rather than on the quality of movement.

Level I — Walks Without Limitations

The child walks indoors and outdoors and climbs stairs without limitations. Gross motor skills include running and jumping, though speed, balance, and coordination may be mildly impaired compared to typically developing peers. The child does not require any assistive mobility device. These children participate in sports and recreational physical activities, though performance may be below age-appropriate norms. Prior to age 2, these infants move in and out of sitting and floor-sit independently with both hands free for manipulation.

Level II — Walks With Limitations

The child walks indoors and outdoors and climbs stairs holding a railing but has limitations walking on uneven surfaces, inclines, and in crowded or confined spaces. The child has at best only minimal ability to run or jump. A handheld mobility device may be needed for long distances or unfamiliar terrain in the community. These children do not require wheeled mobility for community function. The key distinction from Level I is the presence of environmental limitations — Level II children walk but with reduced quality and endurance in challenging environments.

Level III — Walks Using a Handheld Mobility Device

The child walks indoors and outdoors on level surfaces with an assistive mobility device (commonly a posterior walker, forearm crutches, or quadripod canes). The child may climb stairs holding a railing with supervision. For longer distances or uneven terrain, the child may be transported or use wheeled mobility (manual or powered wheelchair). Sitting ability is functional, though pelvic or trunk support may be needed for optimal hand function. The defining distinction from Level II is the mandatory requirement for an assistive device for all indoor walking; and from Level IV, the ability to walk with a device at all.

Level IV — Self-Mobility With Limitations; May Use Powered Mobility

The child uses methods of mobility that require physical assistance or powered mobility in most settings. The child may walk short distances at home with physical assistance or a body support walker but relies primarily on wheeled mobility (pushed manual wheelchair or powered wheelchair) for community mobility. Sitting requires adaptive seating for trunk and pelvic control. Transfers require physical assistance from a caregiver. Some children achieve self-mobility using a powered wheelchair with appropriate adaptations. The distinction from Level III is that Level IV children cannot functionally walk with a handheld device even indoors, and the distinction from Level V is that Level IV children have some means of self-generated mobility.

Level V — Transported in a Manual Wheelchair

This represents the most severe functional limitation. The child has no means of independent mobility and is transported by caregivers in a manual wheelchair in all settings. Trunk and head control are severely impaired, and the child requires extensive adaptive equipment for seating, positioning, and all activities of daily living. Self-mobility is only possible if the child can learn to operate a powered wheelchair with extensive adaptations (head control, scanning switches), but even this is achieved by very few. All transfers are dependent, requiring full caregiver assistance with lifting equipment.

Clinical Significance of GMFCS

The GMFCS is a powerful prognostic tool. Motor development curves (Rosenbaum et al., 2002) have demonstrated that gross motor function for each GMFCS level follows a predictable trajectory — Level I and II children reach near-maximum motor potential by age 5, Level III by age 8, while Levels IV and V plateau by age 7 and may actually decline in adolescence. This informs realistic goal-setting with families.

The GMFCS also guides orthopaedic surveillance — for instance, hip displacement surveillance is stratified by GMFCS level. The incidence of hip displacement (migration percentage >30%) increases dramatically from approximately 1% in Level I to nearly 90% in Level V. Screening hip radiographs are recommended annually for GMFCS levels III–V and at clinical concern for levels I–II.

Surgical decision-making is also influenced by GMFCS level. Single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS) for gait optimisation is typically indicated in GMFCS levels I–III (ambulatory children), while surgery in levels IV–V is directed towards comfort, seating, hygiene, and pain management rather than ambulatory goals.
