## Question

A 74-year-old female underwent a left total knee arthroplasty (TKA) 10 weeks ago. Postoperatively, she was discharged to a skilled nursing facility (SNF). You last saw her 8 weeks ago at which time her staples were removed and her incision appeared benign. She was participating in daily therapy at her facility. At follow up today, her incision is healthy appearing and her range of motion testing reveals 10-70° of flexion. Which of the following is true regarding manipulation under anesthesia (MUA)?

## Options

1. Manipulation should be performed in patients not achieving 110° degrees of motion by 10 weeks postoperatively despite adequate therapy
2. Best results are obtained when performed within 3 months of surgery
3. Increased risk of the need for manipulation when the skin is closed with staples
4. Increased risk of the need for manipulation when discharged to a SNF postoperatively
5. Use of a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine postoperatively decreases the need for MUA

## Correct option

**2. Best results are obtained when performed within 3 months of surgery**

## Answer explanation

Early manipulation (<3 months) has been shown to provide superior results compared to late manipulation (>3 months) for the treatment of stiffness following TKA.

Stiffness following TKA has been reported to be as high as 6% and has numerous etiologies including preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors. Patients with poor preoperative motion or who had previous surgery on the knee are at an increased risk for needing MUA. Intraoperatively, patients with poorly balanced gaps, oversized implants, and those with anterior tibial slope or inadequate resections have all been implicated. Postoperative risk factors include poor patient effort and infection. Thus, infection should always be ruled out prior to performing an MUA. Patients who fail to achieve 90° of motion by 12 weeks postoperatively should undergo MUA to achieve the best results.

*Tibbo et al. review idiopathic stiffness following TKA.* *They report on approximately 49k patients and found an overall prevalence of 4% with obese females being at an increased risk for developing stiffness.* **They recommend** the use of a common definition of failure as the inability to achieve 90° of flexion by 12-weeks postoperatively when discussing stiffness following TKA.

*Zachwieja et al. review manipulation under anesthesia and stiffness after TKA.* **They report** the etiology of postoperative stiffness to be multifactorial, but highlight that infection should always be ruled out prior to offering an MUA. **They conclude** that once infection has been ruled out, manipulations should be performed within 3 months of surgery to achieve the best results.

*Bong et al. review stiffness after TKA.* *They report on the numerous preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors thought to be related to decreased motion following surgery.* **They conclude** that manipulation is most successful when performed within 3 months of surgery and that lysis of adhesions should be considered for treatment in patients who develop stiffness >3 months postoperatively.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Manipulation should be considered in patients failing to achieve 90° of motion within the first 12 weeks postoperatively despite therapy.
Answer 3: Skin closure technique has not been shown to be related to postoperative stiffness.
Answer 4: Patients discharged to a SNF have been shown to have higher re-admission rates and increased complication rates, however, no link has been established between discharge to SNF and increased need for MUA.
Answer 5: Use of a CPM has not been shown to increase postoperative range of motion or decrease the need for MUA.
