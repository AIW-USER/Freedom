## Question

A 33-year-old Crossfit athlete presents with knee pain, swelling, and mechanical symptoms after performing a set of heavy squat cleans at the gym. An MRI is obtained that shows a meniscal tear. Which of the following tear types, if left unrepaired, would be most likely to show a significant difference in the tibiofemoral contact pressures compared with an uninjured meniscus when subjected to axial loading?

## Options

1. Horizontal cleavage tear with 1.5 mm of extrusion and a concomitant 2x2 cm cyst
2. Oblique tear extending to 50% of the thickness of the meniscus
3. Parrot beak tear extending to 60% of the thickness of the meniscus
4. Radial tear extending to the meniscocapsular junction
5. Vertical tear at the white-white junction with 8 mm of rim width

## Correct option

**4. Radial tear extending to the meniscocapsular junction**

## Answer explanation

A complete radial tear that extends to the meniscocapsular junction is biomechanically equivalent to a posterior root tear, which disrupts the normal hoop stresses and significantly increases the contact pressures above the intact state (Answer 4).

Meniscal tears are common sports-related injuries in young athletes, but can also present as degenerative tears in older populations. While lateral meniscal tears occur more commonly in the setting of an acute ACL tear, medial meniscal tears are the most common overall, with degenerative tears in older patients usually occurring in the area of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus. In general, tears considered amenable to repair are those in the peripheral (red-red, or vascularized) zone with a rim width of < 5mm and vertical or longitudinal tear types 1-4 cm in length. Posterior root tears, in addition, which are functionally equivalent to total meniscectomies, should also be repaired to restore the biomechanical function and stability of the meniscus.

*Ode et al. reviewed the effects of serial sectioning and repair of radial tears in the lateral meniscus.* *The authors tested five matched pairs of fresh-frozen human cadaveric knees in axial compression (800 N) at 2 knee flexion angles (0° and 60°) under six meniscal conditions: (1) intact lateral meniscus and radial width tears of (2) 50%, (3) 75%, and (4) 100%; (5) meniscal repair; and (6) total meniscectomy.* **They found** that only complete radial tears of the lateral meniscus produced significant increases in mean contact pressure and decreased contact area compared with the intact state, while lesser degrees of radial tears were not significantly different from the intact state. **They concluded** that complete radial meniscal tears of the lateral meniscus have a detrimental effect on load transmission.

*Bedi et al. reviewed the dynamic contact mechanics of radial tears (RTs) of the lateral meniscus and the implications for treatment.* *The authors studied six human cadaveric knees under the following conditions: intact meniscus, 30% RT (at the popliteal hiatus), 60% RT, 90% RT, repair, and partial meniscectomy.* **They found** that RTs of up to and including 60% had no effect on pressure magnitude or location, while RTs of 90% resulted in significantly increased peak pressure in the postero-peripheral aspect of the tibial plateau and reduced contact area versus the intact knee. **They concluded** that large RTs in the region of the popliteal hiatus show unfavorable dynamic contact mechanics.

*Lin et al. performed a critical analysis review on updates and advances in the management of lateral meniscal radial tears.* **The authors note** that because of their increased mobility, lack of resistance to hoop stresses, and decreased blood supply, radial tears of the lateral meniscus are more troublesome to heal than vertical longitudinal tears. **They concluded** that although a variety of suture techniques, including the double horizontal mattress and horizontal butterfly patterns, have demonstrated improvements in patient outcomes, evidence is still limited with regard to the ideal suture pattern for radial tears.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Horizontal cleavage tears tend to be degenerative tears in older populations associated with meniscal cysts, though they do not disrupt the biomechanical stability of the meniscus as significantly as a complete radial tear. Meniscal extrusion less than 3mm is not considered "significant."
Answers 2 and 3: Tears involving < 70% of the thickness of the meniscus, as shown in the cadaveric studies above, have not been associated with significant changes in tibiofemoral contact pressures when compared to a full-thickness radial tear.
Answer 5: A vertical tear at the white-white junction with a rim width > 8mm is most amenable to partial meniscectomy and is unlikely to significantly increase tibiofemoral contact pressures.
