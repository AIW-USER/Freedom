## Question

A 72-year-old male presents as a level I trauma patient after being involved in a head-on motor vehicle collision. He is resuscitated with 2 units of blood and platelets per mass transfusion protocol. The radiographs of his chest and cervical spine reveal no gross abnormalities. Figure A shows his pelvis radiograph after being placed in an appropriately applied pelvic binder. He is found to have a positive FAST examination and proceeds emergently for an exploratory laparotomy, resulting in the trauma team performing a splenectomy. No other intra-abdominal injuries are found. While the patient is less unstable over the next 72 hours, he continues to require multiple units of blood with gradually decreasing hemoglobin levels, unresolved hypotension, and persistent tachycardia. Furthermore, his lactate remains >5.0 mmol/L and pH remains near 7.1. An updated pelvic CT scan shows hemoperitoneum, minimal fluid tracking through the bilateral paracolic gutters, and an enlarging hematoma near the base of the corpus cavernosum (Figure B). There is no appreciable fluid extravasation near the splenectomy site. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Discontinue pelvic binder for a 24-hour period while continuing blood transfusions
2. Perform a CT scan of the entire spine to identify the cause of spinal shock
3. Consult interventional radiology for pelvic arterial embolization
4. Proceed with single-stage anterior pubic symphyseal plating and posterior pelvic fixation
5. Proceed with percutaneous posterior pelvic fixation only with symphyseal plating at a later date

## Correct option

**3. Consult interventional radiology for pelvic arterial embolization**

## Answer explanation

Despite having an emergent splenectomy performed as the expected cause for bleeding, the patient displays persistent hemodynamic instability secondary to intra-pelvic bleeding from the right pudendal artery as confirmed on the most recent CT scan. The most appropriate management would include consulting interventional radiology for arterial embolization to address the patient’s hemodynamic instability prior to definitive fixation.

High-energy pelvic fractures commonly present with potentially life-threatening concomitant injuries. Most of the associated intra-pelvic bleeding is reported to be venous or directly from the fracture sites, both of which may be stabilized with the use of a pelvic binder in volume-expanding pelvic ring injuries. Patients who have persistent hemodynamic instability despite fluid resuscitation, initiation of packed red blood cell transfusion, and pelvic binder application should undergo further investigation for intra-abdominal or intra-pelvic sources of bleeding. Positive FAST examinations generally result in emergent exploratory laparotomies by the general trauma team. During the same procedure, the pelvis may be stabilized with an external fixator and pelvic packing, as this has been shown to decrease mortality rates. Persistent hemodynamic instability warrants further investigation for potentially missed causes of bleeding. In this case, a repeated CT scan revealed a right pudendal artery injury which, in the setting of continued hemodynamic instability, warrants interventional radiology consultation for angioembolization (Illustration A) prior to proceeding with definitive fixation.

Angioembolization may also be performed emergently after the initial patient presentation when early CT scans reveal active bleeding. In such cases, angioembolization has been shown to improve mortality rates similar to that of pelvic packing. The choice between each treatment is highly institutionally dependent.

*Vaidya and colleagues present an updated review article regarding angiography and embolization in the management of bleeding pelvic fractures.* **They report** that, in descending order, the most common vessels reported to undergo angioembolization are the internal iliac artery and its branches, the superior gluteal artery, the obturator artery, and the internal pudendal artery. **They also found** that Young and Burgess lateral compression injuries are the most often reported fracture subtypes requiring angioembolization, followed by anterior-posterior compression fractures and Tile C fractures. **The authors conclude** that angioembolization can be effective in the management of pelvic fractures with bleeding arterial injuries, however, the risks of complications must be considered (i.e. gluteal necrosis after internal iliac embolization).

*Langford and colleagues present an updated review article regarding the evaluation, classification, and resuscitation of pelvic ring injuries.* **They discuss** that, in indicated fracture types, an initial reduction of pelvic blood loss can be obtained via pelvic volume-reducing treatment via pelvic binders, sheets, or some form of external fixation. **They recommend** that selective arterial embolization, pelvic packing, or a combination of the two interventions should be pursued in patients with persistent hemodynamic instability. The authors reviewed the different classifications (primarily Young and Burgess; Illustration B) and concluded that trauma team coordination facilitates efficient resuscitative efforts and may affect definitive management by optimizing incision, ostomy, or catheter placement.

*Langford and colleagues present an updated review article regarding the indications and techniques for the definitive surgical management of pelvic ring injuries.* **They discuss** that contemporary management employs primarily percutaneous reduction and fixation in the supine position. The authors further discuss various operative techniques and review relevant intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging. **They conclude** that even with anatomic restoration of the pelvis, long-term outcomes are below population norms primarily due to chronic pain and sexual dysfunction.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Some authors advocate for temporary discontinuation of pelvic binders that have been in place for >24 hours to provide soft tissue rest over the bony prominence of the greater trochanters. However, the pelvic binder holiday should be brief (1-2 hours) followed by reapplication, particularly in the setting of a volume-expanding pelvic ring injury.
Answer 2: Spinal shock classically presents as hypotension without reflexive tachycardia. This patient displays hypotension with tachycardia and steadily decreasing hemoglobin, all indicative of hemorrhagic shock.
Answers 4&5: Given the patient’s persistent hemodynamic instability and inadequate resuscitation, definitive fixation should be delayed. Initial acidosis should be improved to at least one of the following (based on Early Appropriate Care criteria): lactate ≤4.0 mmol/L, pH ≥7.25, or base excess between -5.5 mmol/L and +5.5 mmol/L.
