## Question

A 72-year-old male presents with complaints of right shoulder pain which has been present for the last 10 years. He complains of a loss of motion and crepitus with motion. Radiographs demonstrate significant glenohumeral osteoarthritis with humeral osteophytes and 9º of glenoid retroversion. He has attempted conservative care with physical therapy, NSAIDS, and steroid injections, most recently 4 months ago. You recommend stemmed anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty for the patient. In your preoperative discussion of risks, what is the most likely mode of failure you should warn this patient about?

## Options

1. Rotator cuff tearing
2. Glenoid loosening
3. Humeral stem loosening
4. Periprosthetic fracture
5. Infection

## Correct option

**2. Glenoid loosening**

## Answer explanation

Glenoid loosening is the most common cause of failure after anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA), being responsible for ~30% of revision total shoulder arthroplasty.

aTSA has been shown to provide predictable pain relief and improved range of motion for patients with glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis (OA). Survival rates are upwards of 90%. However, complications that lead to failure exist and must be well understood as the number of primary aTSA continues to increase. Possible modes of failure include: glenoid loosening, humeral stem loosening, rotator cuff failure (subscapularis/supraspinatus), dislocation, infection, or periprosthetic fracture. Glenoid loosening is the most common of these modes of failure and risk for failure is increased in cases where there is questionable rotator cuff deficiency or where glenoid bone stock is poor/glenoid deformity is present.

*Sowa et al. looked at medium and long term outcomes of total shoulder arthroplasty for primary glenohumeral arthritis in 21 patients with mean age of 55.* **They noted** that outcome scores consistently improved, but 2 patients needed a revision and 2 others were recommended revision. **They also pointed out** that radiolucent lines behind the glenoid component became more prominent between medium and long term follow-up but they did not correlate this to patient outcomes.

*Neyton et al. also looked at outcomes of aTSA in patients <60 years old, but in a larger cohort of 202 patients.* Revision was necessary in 16.3% of shoulders with the mode of failure being glenoid loosening in 88% of these. They also compared the results of aTSA with hemiarthroplasty, **noting** significantly better functional and subjective outcomes in the aTSA group.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Tearing of the supraspinatus or subscapularis is a reason for failure of aTSA, but is not as common as glenoid loosening.
Answer 3: Humeral component loosening is fairly uncommon in cases of primary aTSA, especially with the use of a stemmed implant.
Answer 4: Periprosthetic fracture is increasing in prevalence as the number of shoulder replacements rises, however, it is not as common as glenoid loosening.
Answer 5: Infection is less common in shoulder arthroplasty than hip/knee arthroplasty and is frequently caused by C. acnes. However, it is less common than glenoid loosening as a cause for revision.
