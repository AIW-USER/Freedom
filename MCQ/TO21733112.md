## Answer explanation

High Tibial Osteotomies Around Knee Joint

 Medial Opening Wedge Osteotomy

 Medial opening wedge osteotomy is a relatively straightforward procedure that requires only a single osteotomy and limited soft tissue dissection. Unlike other techniques, it does not involve a fibular osteotomy, which reduces the risk of neurovascular complications, nor does it require the resection of bone from the lateral tibia. The procedure preserves the normal shape of the tibia, which is beneficial for future knee replacement surgeries. Additionally, the degree of correction can be accurately measured and adjusted during the surgery in both the coronal and sagittal planes, ensuring proper alignment. The technique also helps prevent or correct limb shortening, a potential complication in other osteotomy approaches.

 However, medial opening wedge osteotomy is associated with certain drawbacks. These include a higher risk of nonunion, a prolonged period of restricted weight-bearing post-surgery, and the possibility of leg lengthening. Patients may also experience morbidity at the bone graft harvest site, loss of correction due to unstable fixation, and an increase in posterior tibial slope due to the anterior placement of the metal plate used for stabilization.

 This technique may be particularly advantageous for patients with conditions such as a leg length discrepancy of ≥2 mm, a combined posterior cruciate ligament injury, patella alta, or medial collateral ligament laxity. In these cases, medial opening wedge osteotomy may offer better outcomes compared to the closing wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO).

 The surgery begins with a 5-cm vertical incision along the medial aspect of the tibia, positioned between the tibial tuberosity and the posteromedial tibia, just below the joint line. The pes anserinus is detached to expose the superficial medial collateral ligament. The distal part of the ligament is then separated from the bone, and a retractor is inserted to protect the nearby neurovascular structures. Subperiosteal dissection is carried out from the tibial tuberosity to the posteromedial tibia. Two guide wires are placed at a point 3.5–4 cm below the medial joint line, extending obliquely towards the tip of the fibular head, just below the lateral tibial articular margin.

 Once the positioning is confirmed with fluoroscopy, the tibial osteotomy is performed below the guide wires using an oscillating saw or osteotome. The osteotomy line extends from the tibial tuberosity to just 1 cm medial to the lateral tibial cortex, in alignment with the posterior tibial slope in the sagittal plane. The mobility of the osteotomy site is assessed, and the osteotomy is opened by applying valgus force. If the opening is insufficient, stacked osteotomes are used to reduce the risk of intraarticular fractures. A calibrated wedge is then inserted to achieve the desired opening, and fluoroscopy is used to verify alignment by ensuring that an alignment rod or wire cable lies over the hip and ankle joints at 62.5% of the tibial plateau width. Once the correct alignment is reached, a metal plate is fixed internally.

 Various metal plates can be used for fixation, such as the Puddu plate, Tomofix, Aesculap (dual) plate, or spacer plates, with the latter being the most commonly used. The size of the metal block should match the calibrated wedge, and proximal fixation screws are inserted under fluoroscopic guidance. If a bone defect of ≥10 mm is present, it should be grafted with iliac crest autograft, allograft, or a bone substitute. Smaller defects may not require grafting, but for larger defects, corticocancellous autografts or allografts are recommended.

 Lateral Closing Wedge Osteotomy

 Lateral closing wedge osteotomy is particularly effective for correcting deformities near the maximal point of misalignment. The procedure promotes rapid bone healing due to the large contact area of cancellous bone at the osteotomy site. This allows for early weight-bearing and rehabilitation, as the quadriceps femoris muscle is utilized effectively. Another benefit is the ability to directly visualize the articular surface through the incision, ensuring precise correction. Additionally, the risk of losing correction post-surgery is minimal.

 However, the procedure has several downsides. It requires either a fibular osteotomy or the release of the proximal tibiofibular joint, which can increase the risk of neurovascular complications. The lateral bone resection may also lead to shortening of the lower limb. Furthermore, future total knee replacement surgery could be more challenging, as stem impingement or metal augmentation may be necessary due to the proximal tibial deformity and the loss of lateral condyle bone.

 This procedure is particularly suitable for patients who are at risk for nonunion, such as those who are heavy smokers, have diabetes, or have a history of prolonged steroid use. It can also be performed when patellar baja is present, when the patient prefers to avoid bone grafting, or when anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is required in conjunction with the osteotomy.

 A 10-cm vertical incision is made, and the tibialis anterior muscle is elevated to expose the proximal tibiofibular joint. Depending on the case, the joint can be managed through joint disruption, fibular osteotomy at a point 15 cm below the fibular head, or excision of the fibular head. Retractors are used to protect the lateral margin of the patellar tendon and the neurovascular structures along the posterolateral tibia.

 The osteotomy begins 2 cm below the lateral articular surface of the tibia. The proximal osteotomy cut is made parallel to the articular surface, while the distal cut is oblique to avoid damaging the patellar tendon. The lateral bone wedge is removed using an angular cutting guide. The medial tibial cortex is left intact but perforated with multiple holes to act as a hinge when the osteotomy is closed. The hinge is closed gradually to allow plastic deformation, and care is taken to prevent overcorrection, which can occur if the proximal and distal cortices overlap. Fluoroscopy is used to confirm complete wedge removal.

 Once the osteotomy is closed by applying valgus force, the alignment is checked. Ideal correction is achieved by overcorrecting the mechanical axis by 3–5 degrees. This is verified when an alignment rod lies at 62.5% of the tibial plateau’s width. Fixation is typically achieved using one or two staples placed from the lateral to medial direction, anterior to the fibula. For more robust fixation, a contoured T-plate or locking plate may be used.

 Hemicallostosis Openwedge Osteotomy

 Progressive callus distraction is carried out through an opening wedge osteotomy with external fixation using either an axial or ring fixator. This technique is typically applied for significant corrections and involves performing the osteotomy below the tibial tubercle to minimize alterations in patellar height. The external fixator provides the ability to correct multiple axes, enables early rehabilitation and weight-bearing, and allows for precise microalignment of the lower limbs. However, it can also lead to discomfort, pin site infections, and the risk of losing the correction once the fixator is removed.

 Dome Osteotomy

 Dome osteotomy is recommended when significant correction is required, such as an 18–20 mm opening or closing, or an angular correction of 20 degrees or more, typically for conditions like traumatic varus deformity or Blount disease. This procedure involves making an inverse U-shaped cut in the proximal tibia and stabilizing it using a metal plate or an external fixator for gradual correction. One key advantage of dome osteotomy is that it allows for the necessary correction without altering the height of the patella.

 Chevron Osteotomy

 This procedure involves making an inverse V-shaped bone cut, inserting a lateral wedge medially, and securing the area with rigid metal plate fixation. While it avoids bone loss and eliminates the need for bone grafting, it is not commonly used because of its technical complexity and the invasive nature of the surgery.
