## Question

A 30-year-old recreational soccer player collides with an opponent during a slide tackle. A heated argument ensues and he initially returns to play with a yellow card, but within minutes he is side-lined with increasing tachypnea and shortness of breath. The team physician diagnoses the patient with a tension pneumothorax. What is the current ATLS recommendation for the most appropriate next step management?

## Options

1. Decompression using a standard (5cm) 14-gauge needle placed in the 2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line
2. Decompression using a standard (5cm) 14-gauge needle placed in the 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line
3. Decompression using a long (8.25cm) 14-gauge needle placed in the 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line
4. Chest tube placement in the 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line
5. Digital thoracostomy

## Correct option

**2. Decompression using a standard (5cm) 14-gauge needle placed in the 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line**

## Answer explanation

While the 2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line is still the recommended site for needle decompression in pediatric patients, ATLS 10th edition has updated the recommendation for ADULTS to the 4th or 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line, using a standard 14-gauge 4.5cm angiocatheter (5cm needle).

Patients with tension pneumothorax often present with tachypnea, desaturation, and potentially late tracheal deviation. Emergent decompression is indicated in the setting of rapid decompensation. Decompression relies on appropriate placement of the needle or chest tube based on anatomic landmarks, positioning within the pleural space, and patency of the tubing. The 5th intercostal space is now currently recommended as the most reliable site for needle thoracostomy. The reasons for the change are that: (1) use of a conventional 4.5cm angiocatheter (5cm needle) in the 2nd intercostal space has been found to be ineffective in as many as 22-50% of trauma patients due to chest wall thickness; (2) incorrect placement at this level can lead to internal thoracic, subclavian or cardiac injury; and (3) the original recommendation was developed in the absence of supporting evidence. The advantages at the level of the 5th intercostal space is less risk to vital structures and improved success due to a thinner chest wall on average, especially in patients where body habitus, pectoral muscle bulk or body armor may limit access to the pleural space. Options for failed decompression in the setting of the correct diagnosis are to ensure patency of the catheter, use a longer needle (8.25cm), or perform a finger thoracostomy.

*Inaba et al. performed a cadaveric study that served as the basis for the ATLS 10th edition update for emergent needle decompression in the setting of thoracic trauma.* *The authors compared the efficacy of standard 14-gauge 5cm needle placement in the 2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line, and 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line for both the right and left chest walls.* **They found** that placement in the 5th intercostal space was accurate 100% of the time versus just over 50% of the time in the 2nd intercostal space, leading them to conclude that utilization of the 5th intercostal space would improve success in needle decompression.

Perron reviews the diagnosis and management of chest pain in athletes. He discusses the spectrum of etiologies from minor musculoskeletal strain to life-threatening pathologies including tension pneumothorax. The author concludes that despite the preponderance of benign pathology, the physician is charged with timely and accurate diagnosis and intervention.

Amaral discusses thoracoabdominal injuries in the athlete. He reviews the etiology, work-up, and management of thoracic, abdominal, and groin injuries. The author notes that while uncommon, undiagnosed injuries can result in severe and catastrophic sequelae.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The 2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line is still recommended in pediatric patients but is no longer the recommendation for adults.
Answer 3: A standard 14-gauge 5cm needle is most often of sufficient length at the 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line. This should be attempted first given the increased risk of visceral injury with a longer needle. However, so long as the diagnosis is accurate and the catheter is patent, the use of longer needle (8.25cm) may be attempted. An 8.25cm needle has furthermore been shown to be more effective at the 2nd intercostal space than a standard 5cm needle.
Answer 4: A chest tube will likely be placed in a hospital setting but needle thoracostomy should be attempted first to temporize the patient. Chest tube supplies are furthermore often not on-hand.
Answer 5: A digital thoracostomy may be attempted in the setting of failed needle decompression, but should not be the initial management for a tension pneumothorax.
