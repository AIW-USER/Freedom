## Question

A 17-year-old male presents with a history of recurrent lateral ankle sprains while playing on his high school football team. He has become frustrated with the laxity and pain he has on the lateral aspect of his ankle. On exam, he has a grossly positive anterior draw and nearly 20 degrees of ankle inversion on talar tilt testing. In terms of gross alignment, he exhibits an elevated longitudinal arch and hindfoot varus with a prominent callus along the lateral forefoot and weak peroneus longus and posterior tibialis musculature on strength testing when compared to the contralateral side. If he decides to undergo isolated surgical reconstruction of his torn lateral ligamentous complex, which of the following represents the most likely cause of surgical failure and recurrent ankle instability in this patient?

## Options

1. Baseline ligamentous laxity
2. Excessively pronated subtalar joint
3. Fixed dorsiflexion of the first ray
4. Native hindfoot alignment
5. Planovalgus foot deformity

## Correct option

**4. Native hindfoot alignment**

## Answer explanation

A patient with a native hindfoot varus will stress an isolated lateral ligamentous complex reconstruction and lead to an increased risk for surgical failure if the patient's hindfoot varus is not concomitantly addressed.

Hindfoot malalignment is the most common reason for recurrent instability and failure after lateral ankle ligamentous repair/reconstruction. Normal hindfoot alignment is approximately 5-7 degrees of hindfoot valgus with the mechanical axis passing through the medial aspect of the calcaneus, which would result in medial collapse if not for the strong, dynamic and static restraints within the foot that maintain our arch and the three points of contact during gait. When a patient has varus malalignment of their hindfoot, biomechanical forces are directed laterally and stress the lateral stabilizers including our lateral ankle ligaments and the peroneal tendons. Unfortunately, these structures are not as robust as the medially based structures of the foot and will more frequently fail with continuous stress. Therefore if a patient has native hindfoot varus, they will be at high risk for recurrent instability if this is not corrected after lateral ankle ligamentous reconstruction.

*Yasui et al. review surgical procedures for managing chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI).* **The authors note** that options include anatomic direct repair, anatomic reconstruction with an autograft or allograft, and arthroscopic repair. **They also note** that a correlation between cavovarus foot deformity and CLAI has been well-documented, citing that in a cohort of ankles requiring revision of lateral ligament reconstruction, the most commonly associated condition was hindfoot varus alignment (28%), with good clinical outcomes occurring in those who underwent lateral ankle ligament reconstruction and realignment osteotomy for their cavovarus foot deformity. **They conclude** that in patients with CLAI and foot deformities, simultaneous correction of the deformity may be necessary for the success of an ankle stabilization procedure.

*Lintz et al. reviewed the relationship between chronic lateral ankle instability and hindfoot varus using weight-bearing cone beam computed tomography.* **They note** that hindfoot varus contributes to reduced shock absorption power and also increases the risk of damage to the lateral structures of the hindfoot through an increased supinating moment, resulting in weakened lateral support that becomes a predisposing factor for recurrent inversion injuries and failure of surgical reconstruction. The authors included 43 feet with CLAI and concluded that a positive relationship exists between varus hindfoot alignment and the risk of having CLAI based on weight-bearing CT findings.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Baseline ligamentous laxity is assessed by the Beighton scoring system, which is not noted in this patient's clinical history. The laxity exhibited in the question stem is due to his pre-operative injury.
Answer 2: In a varus hindfoot, the subtalar joint supinates to compensate for excessive hindfoot varus in order to allow the lateral border of the forefoot to touch the ground during the phases of gait.
Answer 3: Fixed plantarflexion, not dorsiflexion, of the first ray is often associated with compensatory hindfoot varus that would lead to excessive stress on an isolated lateral ligamentous complex reconstruction.
Answer 5: Planovalgus deformity does not stress the lateral ligamentous complex in the same fashion as a fixed cavovarus foot deformity.
