## Answer explanation

Surgical fixation of humeral nonunions is highly associated with radial nerve palsy (Answer 2).

Humeral shaft nonunion is characterized by the failure of a humeral shaft fracture to heal, representing a notable complication following both nonoperative and operative management. This occurs in approximately 2 to 33% of nonoperative cases and 5 to 10% of surgical cases, and is more common with certain fracture patterns, open fractures, and systemic factors. Diagnosis relies primarily on serial radiographs and clinical signs, while CT scans are useful for assessing the extent of the nonunion and planning revision surgery. Complications include persistent pain, malunion, and nerve injury, with radial nerve palsy being a common associated finding and potential complication of treatment, with some studies suggesting iatrogenic palsy rates of 7-19%. Treatment generally involves operative intervention, most often open reduction and internal fixation with compression plating, frequently supplemented with bone grafting to promote healing.

*Naclerio et al. reviewed the evaluation and surgical techniques for treating humeral shaft nonunion.* **They found** that nonunion is associated with reduced quality of life and variable success rates across different surgical techniques, emphasizing the need for thorough preoperative evaluation to identify contributing factors. **They concluded** that surgical intervention is often necessary for humeral shaft nonunion to improve function and quality of life, utilizing techniques like open reduction and plate fixation with bone grafting. Furthermore, **they report** that iatrogenic radial nerve palsy is a potential complication of humeral shaft nonunion repair, with reported rates ranging from 6.9% to 18.5%.

*Chaudhry et al. reviewed updates and controversies regarding the management of radial nerve injuries, discussing anatomy, injury mechanisms, evaluation, and treatment options, including observation, nerve repair, and tendon transfers.* **They found** that spontaneous recovery rates for radial nerve palsy after humeral shaft fractures are high (70% at 7 weeks) and that longer observation periods may be warranted in adults, but early exploration is indicated in penetrating injuries or high-energy open fractures. **They concluded** that management should be individualized based on injury type and patient factors, with surgical techniques for nerve repair evolving towards tension-free coaptation and tendon transfers remaining a viable option for restoring function.
