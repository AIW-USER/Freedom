## Question

A football player collides with another football player with his foot firm on ground. He is hit on lateral aspect of DISTAL leg. Which injury will you see [FNB 2023]

## Options

1. Forced valgus and MCL tear
2. Forced varus and LCL tear
3. Forced flexion, valgus and ACL tear
4. Forced valgus and ACL tear

## Correct option

**3. Forced flexion, valgus and ACL tear**

## Answer explanation

Ans: C

EXPLANATION: -

Mechanisms of MCL Injury
ACL injury can be caused by direct contact injury (~30%) but majority injuries are non-contact injuries (~70%) due to faulty movement mechanisms.

![[TO19356240-1.jpg]]

1. Forced flexion, valgus of knee with foot planted on the ground, femoral adduction, tibial external rotation and ankle eversion. This can occur during sudden pivoting or cutting in sports like football and basketball.

![[TO19356240-2.png]]

2. Landing from a Jump with a Stiff or Improper Position
• If an athlete lands with the knee extended (stiff landing) or with excessive valgus collapse (knee caving inward), the ACL can tear.
• Seen in volleyball, basketball, gymnastics.
3. Sudden Deceleration or Stopping
• Stopping abruptly after sprinting or running can put excessive force on the ACL, leading to injury.
• In soccer, skiing, and football.
4. Hyperextension injury can injure the ACL as tibia reciprocally moves forward (see pic)

![[TO19356240-3.png]]

![[TO19356240-4.png]]

The risk of ACL injury is increased due to

1. Poor core stability – more lateral and posterior displacement of trunk during single leg landings is seen to increase the risk of ACL injury
2. Stiff landings – landing with reduced knee flexion coupled (<200), increases the loading in the knee and decreases shock absorption and this has been seen as a risk factor for ACL injury.
3. Weak hip abduction strength – leads to more adducted positions during single leg landings
4. Increased knee valgus
5. Heel strike landing – decreased activation of gastrocnemius-soleus complex and increased ground reaction forces.

Mechanisms of MCL Injury

1. Valgus Stress (Direct Blow to the Outer Knee) – Most Common
• A forceful impact on the lateral (outer) side of the knee pushes the knee inward (valgus movement), stretching or tearing the MCL.
2. Non-Contact Valgus Stress with Twisting (Pivoting or Cutting Movements)
• Occurs when the foot is planted, and the athlete suddenly twists or changes direction, forcing the knee inward.
• Seen in sports like basketball, skiing, and tennis.
• Often associated with ACL injuries when the knee also rotates excessively.
3. Hyperextension with Valgus Force
• A combination of forceful knee straightening (hyperextension) and valgus stress can lead to MCL injury.
• This mechanism is less common but can occur in gymnastics, skiing, and football when landing awkwardly.
4. Fall with the Knee Bent Outward
• Falling with the knee forced into valgus position (e.g., catching an edge while skiing or falling sideways in soccer) can strain or tear the MCL.

*ACL injuries are more common when hit over lateral aspect of distal leg while knee is in slight flexion while MCL tears when the lateral force is a little higher up over the thigh or knee more so in knee extension.

Mechanisms of PCL Injury
1. Direct Trauma ("Dashboard Injury")
• A posteriorly directed force on the proximal tibia while the knee is flexed (e.g., hitting the dashboard in a car accident).
• This force pushes the tibia backward, stressing and potentially rupturing the PCL.
2. Hyperflexion Injury
• Sudden forceful knee flexion beyond its normal range, especially with plantarflexed foot.
• Common in falling on a bent knee (e.g., falling forward in football or basketball).
• The tibia gets pushed backward, leading to PCL sprain or tear.
3. Hyperextension Injury
• Occurs when the knee is forcefully extended beyond its normal range, often with a rotational component.
• Seen in sports like soccer, gymnastics, and skiing where sudden stopping or awkward landings occur.
• This often involves both ACL and PCL tears.
4. Rotational Injury
• PCL injuries can occur with knee twisting movements, especially if combined with valgus (inward) or varus (outward) stress.
• Common in contact sports (rugby, football, wrestling) where players experience twisting tackles.
5. Fall on the Knee with Foot in Plantarflexion
• When an athlete falls directly onto the knee while the foot is pointing downward (toes down), the tibia is forced backward.
• This mechanism is common in wrestling, ice hockey, and American football.
