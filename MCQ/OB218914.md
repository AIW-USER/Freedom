## Question

A 16-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with a chief complaint of bilateral lower extremity weakness followed by upper extremity weakness within the past 72 hours. The patient denies neck pain, recent falls, and denies recent trauma. She reported fever and diarrhea one week ago. What diagnostic test will best support the diagnosis for this patient?

## Options

1. Magnetic resonance imaging of the cervical spine
2. Computed tomography of the cervical spine
3. Urinalysis for M spike protein
4. Lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis
5. Stool analysis

## Correct option

**4. Lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis**

## Answer explanation

The patient in this vignette presents with Guillan Barre Syndrome (GBS). The best diagnostic test is a lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid analysis (Answer 4).

Guillian-Barre Syndrome is a progressive neurological disorder and the most common cause of acute, flaccid, neuromuscular paralysis in the USA. It is a cross-reactive, immune-mediated pathology that is typically preceded by an infection by 1-6 weeks, often a gastro-intestinal illness. Patients will present with proximal and distal flaccid weakness, sensory disruption, and areflexia or hyporeflexia. Autonomic nerves can be involved leading to life-threatening cardiac arrythmias. Additionally, up to 30% of patients can have diaphragmatic involvement. Treatment is symptomatic support, intravenous immunoglobulin, and plasma exchange. At 6 months, 80% of patients achieve independent ambulation, but 20% have continued morbidity and there is up to a 5% mortality rate during the acute phase of the illness.

*Jasti et al. published a review article suggesting that lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis is a valuable diagnostic tool in GBS.* **The authors emphasize** that CSF examination typically reveals elevated protein levels without pleocytosis, supporting the diagnosis of GBS. This finding is known as albuminocytologic dissociation, which aids in differentiating GBS from other causes of acute inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy.

*An article by Willison et al. reports on the use of CSF analysis as a diagnostic modality in GBS.* *The authors write that CSF protein levels are significantly higher in patients with GBS than those without the condition, further validating the utility of CSF analysis in confirming the diagnosis.*

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) of the cervical spine are not indicated in the initial evaluation of GBS, as the diagnosis is primarily clinical. These imaging modalities may be utilized in some instances to rule out other conditions that mimic GBS, such as spinal cord compression or structural lesions.
Answer 3: Urinalysis for M spike protein is irrelevant to diagnosing GBS. M spike protein is associated with multiple myeloma, a malignancy involving plasma cells, and is not directly associated with GBS.
Answer 5: Stool analysis may help assess the infectious etiology of the patient's recent febrile illness and diarrhea, but it is not specific to supporting the diagnosis of GBS.
