## Question

An 18-year-old woman reports lifelong flat feet with a three-year history of medial hindfoot pain and dorsolateral foot pain located anterior to the lateral malleolus. Orthotics and physical therapy have failed to improve symptoms, and weight-bearing radiographs demonstrate rigid hindfoot valgus with forefoot abduction. CT shows a talocalcaneal coalition involving approximately 70% of the posterior facet, with otherwise well-maintained joint spaces throughout the hindfoot and normal subchondral bony architecture. Which of the following is the most appropriate surgical management?

## Options

1. Isolated coalition resection
2. Coalition resection with flatfoot reconstruction
3. Subtalar arthrodesis
4. Triple arthrodesis
5. Bony flatfoot reconstruction without coalition resection

## Correct option

**5. Bony flatfoot reconstruction without coalition resection**

## Answer explanation

Flatfoot reconstruction without coalition resection or arthrodesis is most appropriate when a talocalcaneal coalition occupies >50% of the posterior facet, without evidence of arthritis throughout the Chopart joints. In this case, the deformity is believed to be the primary driver of symptoms; realignment aims to create a plantigrade, pain-free, and mobile foot while avoiding the poorer outcomes associated with resecting large bars (answer choice 5).

Tarsal coalition is a congenital failure of segmentation between tarsal bones that is often asymptomatic. If symptomatic, patients typically present in adolescence or early adulthood with pain, rigid flatfoot and hindfoot valgus that fails to correct on toe stand, and limited subtalar motion. Diagnosis relies on radiographs, with CT and MRI utilized to further specify coalition characteristics (e.g., size, location) and evaluate for fibrous coalitions or associated local inflammatory changes, respectively. Nonoperative care commonly fails in symptomatic cases; surgical options include resection with interposition for talocalcaneal coalitions <50% of the posterior calcaneal facet, deformity correction with or without coalition resection, or arthrodesis for degenerative joints or coalitions >50%. Poor outcomes are associated with resection of larger coalitions, uncorrected valgus, or the presence of degenerative changes. When the coalition is >50%, but joints are not arthritic, literature suggests that surgeons should prioritize deformity correction to achieve durable alignment and symptom relief, with arthrodesis reserved for cases when >50% bars coexist with degenerative changes or failed reconstruction.

*Masquijo et al. evaluated 13 patients (14 feet) with severe hindfoot valgus (>16°) undergoing surgical reconstruction either with simultaneous coalition resection (8 feet; mean coalition size 45%) or with isolated reconstruction (6 feet; mean coalition size 66.7%).* **They found** significant improvements in radiographic parameters in both groups and large AOFAS gains, from 45 to 98 with resection and reconstruction, and from 60 to 92.3 with reconstruction alone, with all patients asymptomatic and satisfied at follow-up. **They concluded** that in selected severe valgus cases, comprehensive realignment can succeed even without resection, particularly when bars are large and joints are not degenerated.

Kothari and Masquijo provided a review article regarding the surgical treatment of tarsal coalitions in children and adolescents. They highlighted that outcomes worsen when large (>50%) talocalcaneal bars are resected or when valgus is left uncorrected, and recommended surgical realignment to obtain a plantigrade foot while preserving motion when feasible. The authors also illustrated successful correction without resection in severe deformity with large coalitions, and emphasized avoiding triple arthrodesis in young patients without diffuse degeneration. They concluded that selecting between resection, realignment, and fusion should hinge on coalition size, hindfoot alignment, and the health of adjacent joints.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Isolated coalition resection is associated with poorer results when the coalition occupies >50% of the posterior facet and valgus remains uncorrected.
Answer choice 2: Resection with reconstruction is favored for resectable coalitions <50% of the facet; with a 70% bar and healthy joints, joint-preserving realignment without resection is preferred.
Answer choice 3: Subtalar arthrodesis sacrifices motion and is generally reserved for large bars with subtalar degeneration.
Answer choice 4: Triple arthrodesis is indicated for multijoint degeneration or failed reconstruction; it is unnecessary when Chopart joints are healthy, particularly in a young patient.
