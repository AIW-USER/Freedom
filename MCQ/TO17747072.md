## Answer explanation

The muscular dystrophies are a group of hereditary disorders of skeletal muscle that produce progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle and associated weakness.

Inheritance of muscular dystrophies:

X-linked dystrophies
Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Becker muscular dystrophy, and Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy

Autosomal recessive muscular dystrophies
Limb-girdle muscular dystrophy and congenital muscular dystrophy

Autosomal dominant muscular dystrophies
Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy

Duchenne muscular dystrophy:

Diagnosis: A dramatically elevated level of creatine kinase (50 to 100 times normal) and genetic testing of blood samples confirm the diagnosis. Muscle biopsy shows variations in fiber size, internal nuclei, split fibers, degenerating or regenerating fibers, and fibrofatty tissue deposition. Dystrophin testing of the muscle biopsy specimen will help confirm the type of muscular dystrophy but is not 100% confirmatory

Clinical features: onset is between 2 to 6 years. Proximal musculature weakens before the distal muscles. Common clinical features include the tendency to toe-walk; a widely based, lordotic stance; a waddling Trendelenburg gait; and a positive Gower test indicative of proximal muscle weakness. Calf pseudohypertrophy is caused by infiltration of the muscle by fat and fibrosis. The posterior tibial muscle retains its strength for the longest time, this causes equinovarus deformity of the foot . Eventually all voluntary muscles are affected including heart and lungs. Survival is uncommon beyond early thirties.

Gower sign- decrease in the ability to rise from the floor without assistance of the upper extremities.

Meryon sign: it is elicited by lifting the child with one arm encircling the child’s chest. Most children contract the muscles around the shoulder to increase shoulder stability and facilitate lifting. In children with muscular dystrophy, however, the arms abduct because of the lack of adductor muscle tone and severe shoulder girdle muscle weakness until the child eventually slides through the examiner’s arms unless the chest is tightly encircled.

Treatment: The use of prednisone and deflazacort has been shown to preserve or improve strength, prolong ambulation, and slow the progression of scoliosis. Orthopaedic treatment requires tendon lengthening, contracture release and spinal fusion to improve ambulation and posture.

Becker muscular dystrophy: it is similar to Duchenne, but less severe. Onset of symptoms usually occurs after age 7 years, and patients may live to their mid-forties or later. Progression is slow and variable, but can affect all voluntary muscles. The dystrophin in Becker muscular dystrophy, although present, is altered in size or decreased in amount or both. The severity of the disease depends on the amount of functional dystrophin in the muscles. . In patients with large amounts of functional dystrophin, orthopaedic procedures frequently are not needed until after childhood, and in patients with more severe forms of the disease, treatment consideration is the same as for Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Emery-Dreifuss- Muscle biopsy of patients with Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy shows normal levels of dystrophin but an absence of emerin.

Clinical features: During the first few years of life, patients have muscle weakness, an awkward gait, and a tendency for toe-walking. The full syndrome, usually occurring in the teens, is characterized by fixed equinus deformities of the ankles, flexion contractures of the elbows, extension contracture of the neck, and tightness of the lumbar paravertebral muscles.

Cardiac abnormalities consisting of bradycardia and atrial ventricular conduction defects can lead to complete heart block. It is important to recognize Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy because of the cardiac abnormalities, which initially are almost always asymptomatic but lead to a high incidence of sudden cardiac death, which may be averted by a cardiac pacemaker. Most patients are able to ambulate until the fifth or sixth decade of life. Orthopedic treatment involves release of the heel cord contractures and other muscles around the foot.

Limb girdle muscular dystrophy: The disease usually occurs in the first to fourth decades of life. The initial muscle weakness involves the pelvic or shoulder girdle. The clinical characteristics are sometimes indistinguishable from those of Becker muscular dystrophy, but normal is noted on laboratory examination. Lower extremity weakness usually involves the gluteus maximus, the iliopsoas, and the quadriceps. Upper extremity weakness may involve the trapezius, the serratus anterior, the rhomboids, the latissimus dorsi, and the pectoralis major. Surgery is rarely required.

Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy: It is an autosomal dominant condition with characteristic weakness of the facial and shoulder girdle muscles. Onset of the disease may be in dystrophin early childhood, in which case the disease runs a rapid, progressive course, confining most children to a wheelchair by age 8 to 9 years; alternatively, onset may occur in patients 15 to 35 years old, in which case the disease progresses more slowly.

Clinical features: The most striking clinical manifestation is facial weakness with an inability to whistle, purse the lips, wrinkle the brow, or blow out the cheeks. The greatest functional impairments are the inability to abduct and flex the arms at the glenohumeral joints and winging of the scapula, both caused by progressive weakness of the muscles that fix the scapula to the thoracic wall, whereas the muscles that abduct the glenohumeral joint remain strong. As the disease progresses, weakness of the lower extremities, especially in the peroneal and the anterior tibial muscles, results in a foot drop that requires the use of an ankle-foot orthosis.

The inability to flex and abduct the shoulder functionally usually is treated by stabilization of the scapula, with scapulothoracic arthrodesis.

Myotonic dystrophy: Myotonic dystrophy is characterized by an inability of the muscles to relax after contraction. It is progressive and usually is present at birth, although it may develop in childhood. In addition to the inability of the muscles to relax, muscle weakness causes the most functional impairment. Other defects include hyperostosis of the skull, frontal and temporal baldness, gonadal atrophy, dysphasia, dysarthria, electrocardiographic abnormalities, and mental retardation. Orthopaedic intervention is usually required for club foot deformities, hip dysplasia and scoliosis.
