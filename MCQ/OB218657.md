## Question

A 33-year-old male presents after a fall from the back of a truck trailer with the isolated injury shown in Figure A. He is successfully reduced in the emergency department followed by external fixation placement in the operating room. He undergoes multi-ligamentous reconstruction two weeks later with external fixator removal, and the surgeon did not perform neurolysis at that time. He is following up for his three months post-operative appointment and continues to endorse foot drop requiring an ankle-foot orthosis. He states the foot drop has been present since the injury and has not appreciably changed. On physical examination, he has some firing of the tibialis anterior musculature, albeit weak, when prompted. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

![[q218657-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Continue physical therapy alone without any further intervention
2. Obtain nerve conduction and electromyography studies
3. Perform neurolysis of the common peroneal nerve
4. Perform posterior tibialis tendon transfer to the lateral cuneiform
5. Perform posterior tibialis tendon transfer/tenodesis to the tibialis anterior

## Correct option

**2. Obtain nerve conduction and electromyography studies**

## Answer explanation

This patient experienced a peroneal nerve injury after a knee dislocation with some appreciable firing of the tibialis anterior muscle 3 months after the injury. Obtaining a nerve conduction study at this time will allow for (1) determining the severity of injury, (2) determining the anatomic location of the injury, and (3) establishing a baseline for future nerve studies to monitor changes.

Knee dislocations can result in devastating neurovascular injuries resulting in the potential need for amputation. The popliteal artery and common peroneal nerve both lie in close proximity to the knee joint and serve as two commonly injured structures. While an arterial injury can easily be appreciated after reduction utilizing ankle-brachial-index or CT-angiography, peroneal injuries have more ambiguous presentations with variable recovery. In general, partial peroneal nerve injuries have a greater chance for full recovery (~85%) compared to that of complete peroneal nerve injuries (~35%) without the need for operative intervention. Persistent peroneal nerve palsy warrants close monitoring with serial physical examinations and, if indicated, nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) evaluation. Obtaining these studies in the acute setting results in unreliable findings, and therefore should not be attempted until 4-6 weeks after injury. In patients with partial nerve recovery, NCS/EMG studies can serve to monitor functional recovery in corroboration with physical examination findings. In general, most studies suggest waiting 6 months – 1 year before performing operative intervention on partial peroneal nerve injuries as the nerve may continue to recover with conservative management alone. Meanwhile, patients should be placed in an ankle-foot-orthosis (AFO) to prevent an equinus contracture. In patients with complete palsy, early (4-6 weeks post-injury) NCS/EMG studies displaying complete neurotmesis warrant earlier nerve repair/reconstruction and/or tendon transfer (i.e. posterior tibial tendon transfer).

*Poage and colleagues provided a comprehensive review of peroneal nerve palsy evaluation and management.* **They discuss** that common peroneal nerve palsy is the most common compressive neuropathy of the lower extremity and often resolves over time with nonsurgical measures. **They emphasize** reserving operative intervention for refractory cases, in patients with compelling NCS/EMG findings, or when the prognosis for nerve recovery is poor. **They conclude** by highlighting their techniques for performing common peroneal nerve neurolysis and posterior tibial tendon transfer.

*Woodmass and colleagues performed a systematic review of peroneal nerve palsy and recovery following traumatic knee dislocation.* **They report** functional recovery (MRC ≥ 3/5) following complete common peroneal nerve palsy was only 38.4% whereas partial nerve palsy had an 87.3% chance of full recovery (MRC 5/5). **The authors reported** younger age as a positive predictor of recovery. **They concluded** that there is a tremendous difference in nerve recovery prognosis between presenting partial versus complete peroneal nerve palsies after knee dislocation. **They further emphasize** that the most predictable means of reestablishing antigravity dorsiflexion in patients with persistent foot drop is a posterior tibial tendon transfer.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1. Continuing physical therapy alone without further intervention would be warranted when the patient’s nerve is recovering unequivocally. Given his stagnant recovery, NCS/EMG studies would be prudent.
Answer 3. Performing neurolysis of the common peroneal nerve could be considered if the patient fails to improve and if the NCS/EMG studies show a reliable compression area. Surgeons may elect to perform common peroneal nerve neurolysis at the time of ligamentous reconstruction.
Answer 4. Performing a posterior tibialis tendon transfer to the lateral cuneiform is the gold standard treatment to restore ankle dorsiflexion in patients with peroneal palsy foot drop that fails to improve over time. Given partial nerve function appreciated on the patient's exam with potential for continued recovery, posterior tibialis tendon transfer should only be considered at a later point if foot drop persists (≥6 months after injury).
Answer 5. Performing posterior tibialis tendon transfer/tenodesis to the tibialis anterior should only be considered if the surgeon is unable to obtain enough tendon excursion during a planned transfer to the lateral cuneiform. While tenodesis is a potential treatment, it is not as reliable as transferring the tendon to the lateral cuneiform. Further, tenodesis to the tibialis anterior has been shown to have inferior functional outcomes with decreased dorsiflexion strength as compared to transfer to the lateral cuneiform.
