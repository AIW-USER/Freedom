## Question

During the posterior exposure of the atlas arch in the cervical spine, excessive retraction of >2 cm past midline would cause injury to an anatomical structure. Which of the following statements is true about this structure?

## Options

1. Injury to this structure causes postoperative hoarseness
2. The structure is a branch of the vagus nerve
3. Injury to this structure causes weakness in forearm supination
4. The structure typically originates from the subclavian artery and travels cephalad through the transverse foramen
5. The structure is a terminal branch of the common carotid artery

## Correct option

**4. The structure typically originates from the subclavian artery and travels cephalad through the transverse foramen**

## Answer explanation

The vertebral artery is at significant risk of injury during posterior atlas arch exposure >2 cm beyond the midline. The vertebral artery classically originates from the subclavian artery and travels through the transverse foramen in the majority of patients.

The typically described anatomy of the vertebral artery is that it originates from the subclavian or innominate artery, enters the transverse foramen of the 6th cervical vertebrae, and travels superior until it reaches the base of the axis before turning posteriorly to enter the transverse foramen in the posterolateral aspect of the ring of atlas and perforates the posterior C1-C2 membrane to enter the foramen magnum. Vertebral artery injury risk increases when the vascular anatomy is atypical, dissection strays away from the midline, and during excessive bone, and/or disc removal.

*Eskander et al. characterized the anatomy of vertebral arteries using MRI scans of 250 patients.* *They classified the observed vertebral artery anomalies into 3 main groups (intraforaminal anomalies-midline migration, extraforaminal anomalies, and arterial anomalies), with midline migration most common.* **They emphasized** the importance of preoperative understanding of a patient's anatomy for the safe and successful execution of cervical spine procedures.

*Currier et al. performed an anatomic study of the internal carotid artery (ICA) location with respect to C1 based on CT imaging.* **They reported** that the mean distance from the ICA to C1 was 2.88 mm on the left and 2.89 mm on the right. **They recommended** preoperative CT imaging with contrast in all cases involving C1 instrumentation.

*Peng et al. reviewed vertebral artery injury during common cervical spine procedures with an evaluation of the surgical anatomy, management, and prevention of this injury.* **They reported** that ligation-associated morbidities such as cerebellar infarction, cranial nerve palsies, or hemiplegia can occur in up to 12% of cases. **They recommended** that direct repair should be considered as a first-line treatment when local hemostasis can be achieved.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (more common in anterior cervical procedures), which is a branch of the vagus nerve, would result in postoperative hoarseness.
Answer 3: Injury to the C5 nerve root would result in a C5 palsy. This is not the most likely injured structure with excessive lateral exposure of the atlas posterior arch.
Answer 5: The ICA is the terminal branch of the common carotid artery. In the posterior cervical approach, the ICA is a deeper structure and would be at high risk of injury with bicortical fixation of C1 lateral mass or C1-2 transarticular instrumentation.
