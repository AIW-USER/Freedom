## Question

A 6-year-old child sustains a comminuted ulnar shaft fracture with anterior radiocapitellar dislocation after a fall. Closed reduction fails to maintain reduction of the radiocapitellar joint. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Repeat closed reduction of the ulna with elbow flexed 120 degrees
2. Closed reduction with pinning of the radiocapitellar joint
3. Closed reduction of the radiocapitellar joint with intramedullary flexible nail placement in the ulna
4. Open reduction of the radiocapitellar joint with intramedullary flexible nail placement in the ulna
5. Closed reduction of the radiocapitellar joint with open reduction and plate fixation of the ulna

## Correct option

**5. Closed reduction of the radiocapitellar joint with open reduction and plate fixation of the ulna**

## Answer explanation

This patient has failed closed reduction of her Monteggia fracture and the next step in management would be an open reduction and stabilization of the comminuted ulna. An alternate strategy is immediate intramedullary (IM) pin fixation for transverse/short oblique fractures and open reduction and internal fixation for long oblique/comminuted fractures even if initial radial head reduction is successful.

Reduction of the radiocapitellar joint in Monteggia fractures depends on restoring proper length and bow of the ulna. Significant ulnar displacement, complete ulnar fractures, and comminuted ulnar fractures place the radiocapitellar joint at risk for loss of alignment. In the setting of a comminuted ulnar fracture, a plate and screw construct will maintain ulnar length and bow and allow for radiocapitellar joint reduction.

*Wilkins et. al. in their review of Monteggia fractures discuss the various mechanisms that can cause the different types of Monteggia fracture patterns.* **They report** that the Bado 3 pattern, with lateral radial head dislocation, is the most common type to have soft tissue interposition of the annular ligament requiring open reduction.

*Kay et al. discuss the potential pitfalls of pediatric Monteggia fractures.* Radial heads that are dislocated for greater than 3 years, or past the age of 12, have a higher chance of failing operative treatment due to remodeling of the radiocapitellar joint.

*Ring et. al. provide a thorough review of both pediatric and adult Monteggia fractures.* In contrast to pediatric fractures, adult Monteggia fractures generally require open reduction and internal fixation of the ulna.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Repeat closed reduction and splinting is not indicated after a failed initial attempt.
Answer 2: Pinning of the radiocapitellar joint will maintain joint reduction but this does not address the comminuted ulnar shaft fracture. In addition, pins across the RC joint may be at risk for breaking or failing to maintain reduction.
Answers 3 and 4: Closed or open reduction with intramedullary flexible nail placement is not ideal for a comminuted fracture. While nails are appropriate for transverse and short oblique fractures, they will not prevent shortening in the setting of a comminuted fracture.
