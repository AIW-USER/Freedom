## Question

A 24-year-old active duty service member presents with the feeling of catching and locking of his knee since his last Advanced Combat Fitness Test two weeks prior. He describes a feeling of “something stuck in my knee that releases when I bend it back and forth.” The soldier undergoes diagnostic arthroscopy and is found to have the isolated pathology seen in Figure A from the lateral portal. Which of the following would be a possible finding on his pre-operation magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)?

![[q217882-a-medial-meniscal-tear.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Bone bruising in the middle 1/3 of the lateral femoral condyle and posterior 1/3 of the lateral tibial plateau
2. Bone bruising along the medial patellar facet
3. Double posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) sign
4. Subchondral fluid surrounding a lesion found on the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle
5. T2 signal intensity at the posterior tibial sulcus below the articular surface

## Correct option

**3. Double posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) sign**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a longitudinal medial meniscus tear. These tears are considered unstable and can displace centrally (denoting bucket handle nomenclature) resulting in the characteristic “double PCL” sign on MRI imaging.

Acute meniscal tears are commonly associated with a sports-related injury in younger patients as a result of a twisting movement on a loaded flexed knee. On the other hand, degenerative tears predominate in older individuals (>40 years of age) secondary to the loss of elasticity within the menisci. Younger patients with meniscal tears more often undergo meniscal repair due to the superior outcomes seen for the prevention of osteoarthritis and resumption of sports activities when compared to partial meniscectomy. These benefits are not as apparent in older patients and thus older individuals more often undergo nonoperative management or partial meniscectomy. It is imperative that orthopedic surgeons have an understanding of meniscal tear morphology and pre-operative imaging that may suggest the need for meniscal repair instrumentation. The double PCL sign indicates a displaced longitudinal (aka bucket handle) meniscal tear that may be amenable to repair.

*Venkatanarasimha and colleagues reviewed the double PCL sign and its potential causes.* **They identified** the meniscofemoral ligaments (ligament of Humphrey and ligament of Wrisberg), double-barreled PCL variations, and even fat globules as potential alternative explanations for the double PCL sign on MRI imaging. **They conclude** that while there is a high specificity for medial meniscus tears, the physician should be aware of these other potential non-pathologic causes of the double PCL sign and diligently scrutinize the sequential multiplanar images.

*Ververidis and colleagues retrospectively correlated arthroscopic findings of bucket handle meniscal tears to the preoperative MRI in 36 patients.* **They found** that the most common imaging findings included “fragment in the notch sign” and “absent bow tie sign” (both 88%) followed by the double PCL sign (42%). **The authors concluded** that arthroscopy remains the diagnostic gold standard in symptomatic bucket handle tears, however, characteristic pre-operative MRI findings help suggest tear morphology for appropriate surgical planning.

*Wells and colleagues reviewed the mechanism and classification of meniscal injuries.* **They report** that meniscal tears may be identified on MRI imaging as an increased intrasubstance signal where the menisci contact the articular surface or as an alteration in the size or shape of the meniscus. **They conclude** that MRI findings should be utilized in conjunction with patient characteristics and intra-operative assessment for the determination of performing meniscal repair versus partial meniscectomy.

Figure A contains arthroscopic imaging from a lateral viewing portal displaying a displaced longitudinal tear of the medial meniscus (aka bucket handle tear).

Answer 1: Bone bruising in the middle 1/3 of the lateral femoral condyle and posterior 1/3 of the lateral tibial plateau is characteristic in the setting of an anterior cruciate ligament rupture.
Answer 2: Bone bruising along the medial patellar facet would be seen in the setting of patellar instability. With patella subluxation/dislocation, the medial patellar facet contacts the lateral femoral condyle and can result in cartilaginous injury.
Answer 4: Osteochondritis dissecans most commonly involves the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle. MRI findings with subchondral fluid surrounding a lesion would be concerning for the overall stability of the lesion and would warrant early surgical fixation.
Answer 5: T2 signal intensity at the posterior tibial sulcus below the articular surface would be seen in posterior cruciate ligament injuries, as this is the location of its ligamentous origin.
