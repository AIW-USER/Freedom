## Question

A 38-year-old male presents to your clinic complaining of shoulder pain. He reports that three days prior, he fell out of his truck, landing directly on his right shoulder. He initially presented to an urgent care clinic, where radiographs were obtained and a referral to your clinic was placed (Figure A). Which of the following is true regarding the patient's injury?

![[q218943-a-acromioclavicular-joint-injury-type-3-1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Type II injuries feature torn acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments
2. Anterior and inferior acromioclavicular ligaments are most important for overall stability
3. The trapezoid ligament inserts 4.5 cm medial from lateral edge of clavicle
4. This injury is best visualized radiographically with the Serendipity view
5. The conoid ligament is the most important for vertical stability.

## Correct option

**5. The conoid ligament is the most important for vertical stability.**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an acromioclavicular joint injury with complete tears of his acromioclavicular (AC) ligaments and coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments. Of the ligamentous structures, the conoid ligament is the most important ligament for the vertical stability of the AC joint.

Acromioclavicular joint injuries (i.e. "shoulder separations") are common, traumatic injuries that often result from direct blows to the shoulder resulting in disruption of AC and/or CC ligaments. Patients will typically present with nonspecific pain to the affected shoulder and occasionally a visible step-off at the acromioclavicular joint. On exam, patients will often be tender to the AC joint and pain will be elicited with the crossbody adduction test or the O'Brien's test. Standard shoulder radiographs are used to assess the injury with emphasis on bilateral images of the AC joints to correctly identify type III vs type V injuries and an axillary view to correctly identify a type IV injury. AC joint injuries are most commonly classified radiographically by the Rockwood classification. Generally, types I & II are treated with immobilization in a sling, and types IV, V, and VI are treated with CC ligament reconstruction vs ORIF. Type III AC injuries are typically treated conservatively although this is controversial.

*Saccomanno et al. reviewed AC joint instability and ligamentous support.* **They report** four AC ligaments make up the thin but substantial joint capsule: superior, inferior, anterior, and posterior. **They report** that the CC ligament complex comprises the conoid ligament that inserts more posteromedial and the trapezoid ligament that inserts more anterolateral on the undersurface of the distal clavicle. **The authors concluded** that the AC ligaments provided the primary horizontal stability while the CC ligaments provide the primary vertical stability. **They concluded** that the Zanca view is the most accurate radiographic view for the evaluation of AC pathology.

*Minkus et al. reviewed AC joint dislocations in a prospective cohort in an attempt to correlate clinical and radiographic findings with MRI findings.* Using the Rockwood classification, **the authors found** that approximately half of the cohort was classified incorrectly based on radiographs when confirmed with MRI. **The authors also found** that CC ligamentous integrity had the highest bearing on clinical outcomes.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Type II injuries feature torn acromioclavicular ligaments and sprained coracoclavicular ligaments. Both AC and CC ligaments are torn in Types III-VI.
Answer 2: The posterior and superior AC ligaments are most important for overall stability.
Answer 3: The trapezoid ligament inserts 3 cm medial from the lateral edge of the clavicle while the conoid ligament inserts 4.5 cm medial from the lateral edge of the clavicle.
Answer 4: The serendipity view is used to evaluate the sternoclavicular joint. It involves a 40° cephalic tilt view of sternum and clavicle. The Zanca view is used for evaluating the acromioclavicular joint with a 10-15° cephalad tilt view of the glenohumeral joint.
