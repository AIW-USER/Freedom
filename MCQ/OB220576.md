## Question

A 25-year-old male presents to the emergency department with a Type II open tibial shaft fracture. In the trauma bay, you perform a bedside debridement and stabilize his open fracture with a splint. He has a significant soft tissue injury associated with his fracture, requiring flap reconstruction. In general, when considering flap reconstruction options for the tibia, which flap corresponds with the correct arterial blood supply?

## Options

1. Hemisoleus flap supplied by perforating branches from the anterior tibial artery
2. Medial gastrocnemius flap supplied by the posterior tibial artery
3. Lateral gastrocnemius flap supplied by the lateral sural artery
4. Anterolateral thigh free flap supplied by the popliteal artery
5. Medial gastrocnemius flap supplied by the peroneal artery

## Correct option

**3. Lateral gastrocnemius flap supplied by the lateral sural artery**

## Answer explanation

This patient sustained an open tibial shaft fracture. The lateral gastrocnemius flap can be used for coverage in open proximal tibial fractures, and it is supplied by the lateral sural artery (answer choice 3).

Open tibial shaft fractures typically occur following trauma from a high-energy mechanism. Soft tissue injuries are commonly implicated and, depending upon the severity, may require flap reconstruction to provide proper coverage of the underlying tissue and bone. Pedicled or free flap reconstruction is more commonly used in open tibial shaft fractures due to the scarcity of soft tissue in the leg. Flap coverage is typically divided into regions, including the proximal 1/3, middle 1/3, and distal 1/3. The proximal 1/3 can be covered by the medial or lateral heads of the gastrocnemius, which are supplied by the medial and lateral sural arteries, respectively. The middle 1/3 of the tibia is most commonly covered by a medial or lateral hemisoleus flap, which is supplied by perforators from the posterior tibial artery on the medial aspect and the peroneal artery on the distal and lateral aspects. The distal 1/3 of the tibia can be covered by either a free flap or a reverse sural artery flap.

*Blair et al. reviewed post-traumatic soft tissue coverage of the lower extremity.* **They reported** that proximal third lower leg wounds can be properly covered with medial gastrocnemius flaps, middle third wounds covered with rotational soleus flaps, and distal third wounds can be covered with a reverse sural fasciocutaneous flap. **They recommend** maintaining a pedicle width of 4 cm, minimizing handling of the flap while being meticulous in dissection without peeling the underlying fascia away. **They conclude** that orthopedic surgeons are capable of performing these flap reconstructive techniques to treat open wounds in the lower leg.

*Song et al. reviewed the soleus muscle flap for lower extremity reconstruction.* **They report** that patients who sustain middle to distal third open tibia fractures are at a higher risk of limb loss without adequate soft tissue coverage. **They also show** that the medial hemisoleus muscle flap is a reliable local option to reconstruct less extensive wounds in these regions. In contrast, free tissue flaps can be used for more extensive wounds and those in which the soleus muscle is damaged. **They conclude** that local tissue reconstructive techniques should be mastered by the surgeon to augment their skills when treating these extensive injuries while minimizing donor-site morbidity.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer 1: A hemisoleus flap is supplied by the posterior tibial artery medially and the peroneal artery laterally and distally.
Answers 2 and 5: The medial gastrocnemius flap is supplied by the medial sural artery, which is a branch of the popliteal artery.
Answer 4: The anterolateral thigh free flap is primarily supplied by the lateral femoral circumflex artery.
