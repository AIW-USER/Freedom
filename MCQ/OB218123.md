## Question

Over the last 20 years, humerus hemiarthroplasty has become infrequently utilized due to narrowing indications and improvements in both anatomic- and reverse-total shoulder arthroplasty implants/techniques. For which of the following patients is a hemiarthroplasty most indicated?

## Options

1. 44M after sustaining an anterior shoulder dislocation and displaced greater tuberosity fracture
2. 36M with a three-part proximal humerus fracture with an intact articular surface and medial calcar >8mm
3. 46M with a four-part proximal humerus fracture with a comminuted head split
4. 53M with glenoid flattening and a large inferior osteophyte
5. 68M with moderate arthritis and acromiohumal interval <6mm

## Correct option

**3. 46M with a four-part proximal humerus fracture with a comminuted head split**

## Answer explanation

Four-part proximal humerus fractures in the young population that involve a displaced fracture along the humeral head articular surface should be treated with hemiarthroplasty (Answer 3).

Displaced three- and four-part proximal humerus fractures in the younger population should be treated with open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) unless there is a comminuted fracture involving the humeral head articular surface (i.e. “head split”). This finding imparts poor outcomes with high rates of failure when treated with ORIF and is best managed with hemiarthroplasty. Post-operative outcomes are predicated on the healing of the greater and lesser tuberosities in appropriate positions without displacement/migration over time. Patients should be counseled that hemiarthroplasty reliably treats pain and negates the risk for osteonecrosis/nonunion/malunion, however, does not completely restore shoulder function. Specifically, their range of motion is expected to be limited compared to their pre-injury status.

*Kancherla and colleagues provided a review article on the management of acute proximal humerus fractures.* *The authors reviewed various fracture classifications and treatment options, citing that ultimately the fracture pattern and patients’ functional demands dictate management.* **The authors conclude** that despite which treatment is utilized, patients should be counseled that their range of motion is often chronically limited compared to their pre-injury status.

*Greiwe and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institutional outcomes treating three- and four-part proximal humerus fractures.* **They report** that head split fracture patterns may be appreciated on plain radiographs if the pelican sign (greater tuberosity in continuity with a portion of the articular surface) is present. **They found** that hemiarthroplasty resulted in a better range of motion in this cohort compared to other three- and four-part proximal humerus fractures without a head split. **The authors conclude** that hemiarthroplasty for head-split fractures is an appropriate treatment option.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Surgical indications at the time of injury are dictated by the amount of residual fracture displacement after shoulder reduction and the overall functional demands of the patient. In general, greater tuberosity fractures displaced more than 5mm or patients with high physical demands are reported to have better functional outcomes when treated with ORIF compared to nonoperative management.
Answer 2: The radiographic and CT scan images should be scrutinized in young patients who sustain a three-part proximal humerus fracture. Without a strong indication to perform hemiarthroplasty (i.e. comminuted head split fracture), younger patients have better outcomes with ORIF over hemiarthroplasty.
Answer 4: Younger patients with glenohumeral arthritis (i.e. goatbeard deformity) who fail conservative management can be considered for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty. Hemiarthroplasty would not address the arthritis along the glenoid articular surface.
Answer 5. Arthritis in an older individual with decreased acromiohumeral interval <8mm is suggestive of rotator cuff arthropathy. These patients are best managed with a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty.
