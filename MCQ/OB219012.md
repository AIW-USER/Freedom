## Question

When assessing pre-operative sitting and standing spinopelvic radiographs prior to performing a total hip arthroplasty, which of the following measured parameters listed below would indicate that the patient likely has a hyper-mobile hip joint and is at increased risk for dislocation post-operatively?

## Options

1. Sitting anteinclination (AI) of 50°
2. Change in pelvic-femoral angle (PFA) of 60°
3. Change in sacral slope (SS) of <10°
4. Standing Pelvic-femoral angle (PFA) of 190°
5. Sitting combined sagittal index (CSI) of 175°

## Correct option

**3. Change in sacral slope (SS) of <10°**

## Answer explanation

A change in sacral slope (SS) of <10° indicates a stiff spinopelvic junction. In response to a stiff spine that does not accommodate changes from standing to sitting, the hip joints exhibit hypermobility that places the hip arthroplasty at increased risk for dislocation operatively.

The nuances of spinopelvic motion and the impact of a stiff spine on hip dislocation rates have been elucidated in recent years. When a patient with a normal, flexible spine goes from standing to sitting, the pelvis tilts posteriorly 15-20° and the acetabulum opens anteriorly to between 40 and 60° of anteinclination to accommodate the femur, which itself flexes 55-70° (Illustration A). When these normal motions occur, lateral spinopelvic radiographs demonstrate average changes in sacral slope of between 11 and 29° and changes in pelvic-femoral angle of 51-69° (Illustration B). In a pathologically stiff spinopelvic junction, however, this normal motion does not occur, which leads to < 10° of change in sacral slope when going from standing to sitting. This results in a pelvis that can be considered either "stuck standing," in which it does not tilt posteriorly to accommodate a flexed femur, or "stuck sitting," in which the pelvis remains fixed in a posteriorly tilted position when going from sitting to standing. In the case of the former, a hyper-mobile femur will impinge anteriorly, and place the patient at risk for posterior dislocation; while in the case of the latter, a hyper-mobile femur will impinge posteriorly, placing the patient at risk for anterior dislocation.

Heckmann. et al. reviewed twenty consecutive patients who sustained a late dislocation after total hip arthroplasty, and found spinopelvic stiffness due to degenerative changes of the spine to be a unique cause of 9 anterior and 11 posterior late dislocations. The authors report that for every 1° decrease in spinopelvic motion, there was an associated 0.9° increase in femoral motion that was correlated with risk of impingement and subsequent dislocation.

Stefl, et al. provide a radiographic study of 160 hips that prospectively had lateral spinopelvic hip radiographs for skeletal and implant measurements and pre-operatively identified 42/160 (26.2%) hips with spinopelvic imbalance that required precise acetabular component implantation to mitigate risk of impingement and dislocation. Thirty-three of these cups were placed in a fashion that demonstrated normal post-operative anteinclination, while seven remained stiff with high risk for impingement. The authors note that in this latter subset of patients, increased constraint with use of components such as the dual mobility articulation may be indicated.

Illustration A represents the normal values for SS, AI, and PFA in an appropriately mobile spine on lateral standing and sitting spinopelvic radiographs. Illustration B is a table listing the normal ranges for SS, AI, PFA, and CSI in both the standing and sitting positions, as well as the normal delta values for SS and PFA that are used to determine pelvic stiffness and femoral hypermobility, respectively.

Incorrect Answers:
1. A sitting anteinclination of 50° falls within the normal range of 41-63°.
2. A change in pelvic-femoral angle of 60° falls within the expected change of 51-69° when going from standing to sitting. Numbers outside of this range indicate hip hypermobility in the setting of a stiff spine.
4. A standing pelvic-femoral angle of 190 degrees falls within the normal range of 165-195°.
5. A sitting combined sagittal index (which represents the AI + PFA) of 175° falls well-within the normal range of 162-198°.
