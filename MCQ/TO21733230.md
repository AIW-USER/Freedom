Chapter: General Orthopedics MCQs — Surgical Approaches

## Question

False statement regarding distal radius volar (Henry) approach

## Options

1. FPL is retracted ulnarwards with a finger protecting cutaneous branch of median nerve
2. Pronator quadratus is erased from ulnar side of distal radius
3. Modified Henry approach is medial to radial artery
4. Thompson is more preferred approach for proximal radius surgeries as it gives a more cosmetic scar

## Correct option

**2. Pronator quadratus is erased from ulnar side of distal radius**

## Answer explanation

Ans: B
EXPLANATION: -
Pronator Quadratus (PQ) is erased from its radial border. It is stripped off the distal radius together with the periosteum. A finger is used to sweep FPL muscle belly towards ulna.
The classical Henry approach goes between brachioradialis and the radial artery, i.e., radial to the radial artery. The modified approach is ulnar to the radial artery (between FCR and Radial artery).

HENRY APPROACH TO VOLAR RADIUS
Henry approach can provide access to whole length of volar radius, although it is particularly preferred in middle and distal thirds region. The posterolateral (Thompson) approach is rather more often opted for middle < distal third of the radial shaft as Henry’s incision in that area could result in an unsightly scar.

The landmarks for the skin incision are:

![[TO21733230-1.png]]

Proximally: the biceps tendon which crosses the front of the elbow joint, medial to the brachioradialis muscle.
Distally landmark is the radial styloid process.

Superficial plane: Develop the interval between the brachioradialis (mobile wad) and flexor carpi radialis muscles. The radial artery lies deep to the brachioradialis in the middle part of the forearm and between the tendons of brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis distally. It can be identified by its two venae comitantes, which run alongside it. The superficial radial nerve runs under the brachioradialis muscle on the lateral aspect of the radial artery and should be retracted laterally.

![[TO21733230-2.png]]

From here Deep dissection differs depending on area to be exposed.

FOR PROXIMAL THIRD
The supinator muscle covers the lateral aspect of the proximal radius. The posterior interosseous nerve (also known as deep branch of the radial nerve) lies within the substance of the supinator muscle. The forearm should be fully supinated to displace the posterior interosseous nerve away from the surgical field.
The supinator muscle is then incised along its most medial edge, and gently elevated from the bone surface only to the extent that it is necessary for the required exposure of the radial shaft.

FOR MIDDLE THIRD

![[TO21733230-3.png]]

The forearm should be fully pronated to expose the lateral border of the pronator teres muscle and its insertion. For exposure of radial shaft, partially detach the pronator teres from the radius, preserving at least some of its insertion.

FOR DISTAL THIRD

![[TO21733230-4.png]]

Develop the interval between flexor carpi radialis tendon medially (retract medially) and the radial artery laterally (retract laterally). Dissecting thru the FCR sheath ensures no damage is caused to radial artery. Now, deepen further the incision between the flexor pollicis longus and the radial artery. A finger is used to sweep the flexor pollicis longus muscle belly towards the ulna, carefully protecting the palmar cutaneous branch of median nerve. This exposes the Pronator Quadratus muscle.

![[TO21733230-5.png]]

![[TO21733230-6.jpg]]

The pronator quadratus muscle is incised along its radial border, exposing the distal radius. It is stripped off the distal radius together with the periosteum.
