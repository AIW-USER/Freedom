## Question

A 43-year-old former professional beach volleyball player returns to you with persistent right shoulder pain. She has been to 2 physical therapists over 5 months and has not received any pain relief. She also feels the shoulder is weak with reaching for objects overhead. She undergoes shoulder arthroscopy and the diagnostic portion shows a rotator cuff tear. Which of the following patterns would be best treated with a tendon repair over debridement?

## Options

1. Upper border fraying of the subscapularis
2. Bursal-sided supraspinatus tear 3mm thick with an intact anterior cable
3. Bursal-sided supraspinatus tear 4mm thick involving the anterior cable
4. Bursal-sided supraspinatus tear 4mm thick with an intact anterior cable
5. Deep articular-sided supraspinatus tear with 5mm of the footprint exposed

## Correct option

**3. Bursal-sided supraspinatus tear 4mm thick involving the anterior cable**

## Answer explanation

Of the options above, partial supraspinatus tears involving the rotator cable would most likely need a tendon repair.

The supraspinatus tendon insertion on the greater tuberosity measures 20mm from anterior to posterior, and 12mm from medial to lateral. The rotator cable is a continuation of the coracohumeral ligament fibers that crosses the tendon perpendicularly and is thought to create a suspension bridge-type phenomenon to reinforce the tendinous attachments. Rotator cuff tears (RCTs), either partial or complete, with disruption of the cable destabilizes the tendon and allows propagation of tears. Partial rotator cuff tears represent a spectrum of disease that both Ellman and Snyder classified based on location (bursal, articular, or interstitial) and depth (in mm) of tearing. Though it is unclear exactly what degree of tear involvement requires repair, it is generally accepted that amongst the general population tears >3mm thickness on the bursal side or >7mm thickness on the articular side respond best to surgical repair. Even more debatable is the technique for tendon repair, with some authors advocating for in-situ repair whereas others routinely complete the tear and repair the tendon.

*Wolff et al. reviewed the pathogenesis, diagnosis and management of partial RCTs.* The articular surface of the rotator cuff has decreased vascularity and tensile strength compared to the bursal surface. Therefore, articular-sided partial RCTs are often due to degenerative tendinopathy in older patients and tensile failure in younger patients, particularly overhead throwing athletes. Bursal-sided tears are most commonly associated with extrinsic impingement of the acromion and the coracoacromial ligament.

*Cordasco et al. retrospectively reviewed 162 patients who underwent arthroscopic acromioplasty and debridement for shoulder impingement syndrome.* Overall, patients with partial RCTs involving < 50% of the tendon (< 6mm thickness) had equivalent postoperative outcomes compared to patients without RCTs. However, patients with bursal-sided partial RCTs > 3mm in depth had a significantly higher failure rate (38%) compared to articular-sided tears of the same depth (5%). This led the authors to believe bursal-sided tears are less tolerated than articular-sided tears of the same thickness following debridement.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1- Fraying of the subscapularis without tendon retraction does not require tendon repair. However, if fraying is seen during arthroscopy, these need to be investigated as tears are commonly missed.
Answers 2 and 4- Bursal sided tears around 3-4mm in thickness with an intact rotator cable may be considered for repair but are less likely to require repair than if the cable was disrupted.
Answer 5- Partial articular-sided tears under 7mm in thickness generally can be treated with a simple but thorough debridement.
