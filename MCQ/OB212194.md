## Question

Figure A is the radiograph of a 76-year-old female who presents to your office with complaints of right shoulder pain for the past 6 years. She has tried several course of physical therapy and cortisone injections without relief. On examination, her active and passive forward elevation are 50 and 150 degrees, respectively. She has active and passive external rotation with her arm at her side of -10 and 40 degrees, respectively. She has a positive hornblower's sign. She undergoes a reverse shoulder arthroplasty with a tendon transfer (labeled A in Figure B). Which nerve is most at risk when performing the tenotomy for transfer?

## Options

1. Axillary
2. Lower subscapular
3. Radial
4. Suprascapular
5. Thoracodorsal

## Correct option

**3. Radial**

## Answer explanation

This patient underwent a reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) with latissimus dorsi transfer. The radial nerve is most at risk when performing the tenotomy during a latissimus dorsi transfer.

The RSA is a type of shoulder arthroplasty that uses a convex glenoid (hemispheric ball) and a concave humerus (articulating cup) to reconstruct the glenohumeral joint by moving the center of rotation inferiorly and medial, allowing the deltoid muscle to act on a longer fulcrum and provide a mechanical advantage. The indications for utilization of the RSA are several but include cuff tear arthropathy, pseudoparalysis, antero-superior escape, proximal humerus fractures in the elderly, failed arthroplasty, and rheumatoid arthritis. With the Grammont (medialized) RSA, latissimus dorsi tendon transferred were routinely utilized to improve pre-operative external rotation deficits. In recent years, there has been a decline in latissimus dorsi tendon transfers due to improvement in external rotation with the more lateralized designs. Nonetheless, it is imperative to understand the pertinent anatomy and structures at risk if a latissimus dorsi tendon transfer is performed.

*Cuff et al. reviewed 96 shoulders who underwent RSA for end-stage rotator cuff deficiency. with a minimum follow-up of 10 years.* **They report** that implant survivorship was 91%. **They concluded** that at a minimum follow-up of 10 years, patients continue to maintain their improved outcome scores and range of motion.

*Mulieri et al. reviewed the indications for and outcomes of RSA used in patients with massive rotator cuff tears without glenohumeral arthritis.* **They report** that all measured outcomes improved postoperatively. **They concluded** that RSA provides reliable pain relief and return of shoulder function in patients with massive rotator cuff tears without arthritis who have failed non-arthroplasty treatments.

*Bacle et al. reported on the long-term results of the Grammont-style RSA after a minimum of 10 years.* **They found** that 73% of shoulders exhibited scapular notching and an overall 29% complication rate was recorded. The 10-year overall prosthetic survival rate using revision as the end point was 93%. **They concluded** that despite a high arthroplasty survival rate and good long-term clinical results, RSA outcomes showed deterioration when compared with medium-term results. The cause of this decrease is probably related to patient aging coupled with bone erosion and/or deltoid impairment over time.

*Pearlet et al. reviews the combined latissimus dorsi and teres major musculotendinous transfer described for the treatment of massive rotator cuff deficits.* *In this cadaveric study, they examined surgically relevant relationships for latissimus dorsi and teres major tendon transfers.* **They found** that the radial nerve passed directly anterior to the tendons at an average of 2.9cm medial to the superior aspect and 2.3cm medial to the inferior aspect of the humeral insertions. **They noted** that the posterior branch of the axillary nerve was noted to cross superficially over the transferred tendons as they were tunneled under the posterior deltoid. **They concluded** that multiple steps of the combined latissimus dorsi and teres major musculotendinous transfer place local neurologic structures at risk.
