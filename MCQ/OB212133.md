## Question

Figure A shows the AP radiograph of a 52-year-old female who presents to your clinic with complaints of medial foot pain and states she thinks her “arch is collapsing.” After examination, you recommend flexor digitorum longus (FDL) tendon transfer, calcaneal slide osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, and medial cuneiform osteotomy. Which of the following physical exam findings would be a contraindication to this surgery?

![[q212133-a-ptti-ap-xr.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Failure to perform single leg heel raise
2. Forefoot varus
3. Forefoot abduction
4. Pes planus
5. Rigid hindfoot valgus

## Correct option

**5. Rigid hindfoot valgus**

## Answer explanation

Surgery recommended for this patient consists of FDL tendon transfer and corrective osteotomies, which is consistent with recommendations for patients with stage 2B posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) and a flexible hindfoot. Rigid hindfoot valgus would be best treated with arthrodesis.

Posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) is common in middle-aged women and is notable for pes planus, hindfoot valgus, forefoot abduction and compensatory forefoot varus. It progresses through a predictable series of disease beginning with tenosynovitis, followed by bony deformity which eventually becomes rigid. Initial treatment of stage 1 PTTI includes AFO bracing and immobilization, with stages 2A and 2B (with forefoot abduction) classified based on their flexible hindfoot deformity, which is treated with osteotomies and tendon transfers. Rigid hindfoot valgus deformity seen in Stages 3 and 4 would be treated with arthrodesis +/- deltoid ligament reconstruction in stage 4.

*Stein et al. review the algorithm for treatment of PTTI.* *They describe tips for early, accurate diagnosis of PTTI and conclude this is the most important consideration in guiding joint-sparing treatment including osteotomies and tendon transfers.*

*Deland et al. describe the various deformities and progression of PTTI.* *They again note that early recognition and management is important in slowing progression and treating symptoms.* **They conclude** that if surgery is necessary, the primary goal is maintaining motion and achieving proper foot alignment.

*Tankson et al. specifically discuss stage 2 PTTI,* **noting** that it is characterized by an incompetent posterior tibial tendon that causes flexible planovalgus deformity. They also describe the compensatory mechanisms that lead to forefoot varus in these patients. **They conclude** that recognition of forefoot varus and appropriate surgical management with cotton osteotomy are vital for successful management.

Figure A demonstrates an AP foot radiograph showing increased talo-first metatarsal angle and significant talonavicular uncoverage consistent with stage 2B PTTI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: In stage 2 or greater PTTI, patients have difficulty performing a single leg heel raise due to posterior tibial dysfunction. This is not a contraindication in this patient.
Answer 2: Forefoot varus is a common compensatory deformity in patients with hindfoot valgus due to PTTI. This occurs as the foot attempts to maintain a plantigrade foot despite the valgus hindfoot deformity and may require medial cuneiform plantarflexion osteotomy to correct. This is not a contraindication for the procedures listed.
Answer 3: Forefoot abduction, as seen in Figure A, is seen in stage 2B PTTI and is noted on physical exam with the, “too many toes sign.” It is typically addressed surgically with a lateral column lengthening. This is not a contraindication for the procedures listed.
Answer 4: Pes planus is commonly seen in PTTI and worsens with increasing severity. Patients often describe this as their arch collapsing. FDL tendon transfer and medial slide calcaneal osteotomy help correct this issue. This is not a contraindication for the procedures listed.
