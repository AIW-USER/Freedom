## Question

A 17-year-old, skeletally mature, high school pitcher presents to your clinic with complaints of right elbow pain. He is a right-hand dominant starting pitcher. He notes that early in the season one of the team’s starting pitchers sustained a season-ending injury, so he and the other starting pitchers were forced into a more rigorous pitching rotation with less rest and more innings pitched. He reports that he was having difficulty maintaining velocity and control of his fastball and notes that his pain was most pronounced during the end of his wind-up. On physical examination, he has mild paraesthesias in the ulnar nerve distribution, and his pain is reproduced when a valgus forced is applied to his elbow during active flexion. He has painless active motion of his wrist. What is the most likely diagnosis for this patient?

## Options

1. Medial epicondyle stress fracture
2. Strain of the flexor-pronator mass
3. Isolated ulnar neuropathy
4. Attenuation of the anterior band of the anterior oblique bundle
5. Attenuation and inflammation of the common insertion of the flexor-pronator mass

## Correct option

**4. Attenuation of the anterior band of the anterior oblique bundle**

## Answer explanation

The presentation of this young pitcher with increased pitch count, pain in late cocking/early acceleration, and a positive valgus stress examination is consistent with medial ulnar collateral ligament (MUCL) injury. The anterior band of the anterior oblique bundle is part of the MUCL.

Injury to the medial ulnar collateral ligament is a common cause of elbow pain among high school and amateur pitchers. While originally described among track and field throwers, it is now encountered much more frequently in baseball pitchers. The injury occurs secondary to the significant valgus stress experienced by the medial elbow during the pitching cycle. While skeletally immature patients often experience injuries along the spectrum of little leaguers’ elbow, isolated injury to the MUCL is much more common in young but skeletally mature pitchers. The most common risk factors for injury include exceeding pitch count and inning restrictions, higher pitch velocity, and altered pitching biomechanics. It is imperative that coaches of adolescent and high school pitchers closely monitor pitch counts and rest periods in order to minimize the incidence of this injury.

*Lyman et al. investigated the effect of pitch type, count, and mechanics on the risk of elbow and shoulder pain in youth baseball pitchers.* *This was a prospective cohort study of 476 pitchers (ages 9 to 14 years) who were followed for a single season.* **The authors found** that 50% of patients experienced either shoulder or elbow pain during the season. Curveballs were associated with the development of shoulder pain and sliders were associated with the development of elbow pain. There was a direct correlation between the number of pitches thrown per game and during the season and the rate of the development of shoulder and/or elbow pain. **The authors recommended** that young pitchers avoid curveballs and/or sliders, and that strict pitch counts be enforced.

*Kerut et al. provide a review on injury prevention in youth baseball pitchers.* **They note** that the single most important component of injury prevention is limiting pitch count and pitches per week, especially for skeletally immature patients. In addition, **they recommend** avoiding the pitching of breaking balls until skeletal maturity, that coaches emphasize meticulous pitching mechanics, and that pitchers are allowed at least three months of rest from pitching at the conclusion of a season.

*Bruce and Andrews performed a review of ulnar collateral ligament injuries in the throwing athlete.* **They postulate** that these injuries, especially among young athletes, represent an epidemic. They too emphasize the importance of pitch count and meticulous pitching mechanics. **They recommend** an initial trial of nonoperative management with physical therapy and pitching cessation in young athletes and stress that rehabilitation should focus on mechanics, kinematics, and core and lower body strengthening. **They note** that MUCL reconstruction in patients with acute tears or in those who failed nonoperative management provides a return to play rate which exceeds 80% and that a variety of surgical techniques have been described.

*Wilk et al. provide guidelines on the rehabilitation of the thrower’s elbow.* **They note** that optimal rehabilitation follows a sequential and progressive approach, with the ultimate goal being a safe and timely return to sport. **They emphasize** that throwing should be avoided early on, and initial management should focus on periscapular strengthening and stretching, kinematic chain development, and core and lower body strengthening.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This would be consistent with little leaguers’ elbow, which is much less common in skeletally mature patients when compared to a MUCL injury.
Answers 2 and 5: While this might cause medial elbow pain, we would expect the patient to have pain with wrist resisted wrist flexion and/or pronation.
Answer 3: The patient’s ulnar nerve paraesthesias are secondary to ulnar nerve stretch during late cocking/early acceleration. An isolated ulnar neuropathy does not explain his moving valgus stress examination.
