## Question

You are discussing the surgical plan with a patient during a pre-operative visit for a planned rotator cuff repair. The patient is an otherwise healthy 53-year-old male. Preoperative advanced imaging is concerning for a 2-centimeter full-thickness tear of the supraspinatus. There is no evidence of any fatty atrophy. The patient, who always comes to your clinic with a litany of questions, states that he has been reading about the various rotator cuff repair configurations, and asks you about the benefits of each. Which of the following is true regarding the constructs shown in Figure A?

![[q217891-a-cuff-configurations.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Construct B has the overall highest re-tear rate at one year
2. Construct C has the overall lowest re-tear rate at one year
3. Patients repaired with constructs A and B demonstrate equivalent post-operative outcome scores at one-year
4. Patients repaired with construct A demonstrate the lowest post-operative outcome scores at one year
5. Patients repaired with construct B demonstrate the highest post-operative outcome scores at one year

## Correct option

**3. Patients repaired with constructs A and B demonstrate equivalent post-operative outcome scores at one-year**

## Answer explanation

Figure A demonstrates three rotator cuff repair constructs. Construct A is a traditional single-row repair, construct B is a traditional double-row repair and construct C is a double-row suture-bridge construct. Based on the size of the patient's tear, there is no difference in patient-reported outcomes at one year between any of the three constructs pictured (Answer 3).

Rotator cuff tears are an incredibly common injury and are a hallmark of the aging shoulder girdle. 28% of individuals over the age of 60, and 65% of patients over the age of 70, have a full-thickness tear. Aside from advancing age, tobacco use, hypercholesterolemia and family history serve as risk factors for the development of rotator cuff tears. While older patients are typically the victims of overuse and attrition, young patients are at risk for acute trauma. First-line treatment for most tears is physical therapy, NSAIDs and corticosteroid injection. In patients with symptoms recalcitrant to conservative management, surgical treatment generally consists of rotator cuff repair. Intraoperatively, the tendon can be repaired through a variety of suture-anchor constructs. There has been much research into the risks and benefits of each construct. While the effect of construct on re-tear rates remains an area of debate, best available evidence suggests that outcome scores are independent of the repair construct utilized, especially for tears smaller than 3 cm.

Millett and co-authors performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of level I randomized controlled trials comparing single-row with double-row repair. Their review and analysis included 7 studies. Overall, patients treated with single-row repair experienced higher re-tear rates compared to double-row repairs. This increased re-tear rate, however, had no effect on outcome scores.

*Schmidt et al. provide a comprehensive review on the treatment of rotator cuff tears.* Specific to this question, **the authors note** that the literature regarding single-row versus double-row is vast and inconclusive. **They note** that while some studies have shown differences in re-tear rates when comparing the two constructs, patient-reported outcomes do not differ between the two.

*Gerhardt et al. compared the modified Mason-Allen single-row repair versus a double-row suture bridge configuration.* *Their study included 20 patients in each cohort.* *Overall, at 16.8 months follow-up, patient outcomes and re-tear rate did not differ between the two constructs.* The mode of failure, however, was significantly different.

Figure A demonstrates three different rotator cuff repair constructs. A is a traditional single-row repair, B a traditional double-row repair and C a double-row suture-bridge construct.

Incorrect Answers
Answers 1 and 2: constructs B and C are both double-row configurations, and have equivalent re-tear rates. Some evidence suggests that construct A has a higher re-tear rate than B or C.
Answers 4 and 5: all three constructs demonstrate equivalent patient-reported outcomes at one-year postoperative.
