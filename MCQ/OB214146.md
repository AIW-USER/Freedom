## Answer explanation

The patient has sustained a syndesmosis injury with a posterior malleolus fracture. Fixation of the posterior malleolus has been shown to contribute the most to the stability of the syndesmosis in these injury patterns.

Syndesmosis injuries are very common in patients that sustain pronation-external rotation (PER) ankle injuries. These injuries generally involve suprasyndesmotic fibula fractures (Weber C) as well as injuries of the posterior inferior tibiofibular ligaments (PITFL) or posterior malleolus. In the majority of cases, the PITFL is still attached to the posterior malleolar fragment and anatomic reduction of this will indirectly reduce the syndesmosis and form a stiffer and more stable syndesmosis construct than isolated syndesmosis fixation.

*Fitzpatrick et al. performed a cadaveric study on posterior malleolus ankle fractures to determine the effect of its lack of reduction or malreduction on the anteroposterior and medial-lateral reduction of the syndesmosis.* **The authors noted** that clamp reduction of the syndesmosis increased medial translation of the distal part of the fibula in the specimens with an unfixed or an anatomically fixed posterior malleolus fracture fragment and caused lateral displacement of the distal part of the fibula in the specimens with gap malreduction of the posterior malleolus fracture. **The authors concluded** that medial-lateral syndesmotic reduction was affected by the conditions of the posterior malleolus fixation, with mal-reduction of the posterior malleolus leading to syndesmotic malreduction.

*Baumbach et al. performed a retrospective study of 236 patients with a trimalleolar ankle fracture.* *Patients were grouped with respect to the treatment of the posterior malleolus fracture (PMF).* **The authors noted** that ORIF of the PMF significantly reduced the frequency of trans-syndesmotic fixation (25%) compared to closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF, 61%) or untreated PMFs (63%) with no significant influence of the PMF size (≤25%/>25%). Neither the frequency of trans-syndesmotic fixation nor the quality of reduction differed significantly between untreated PMF and CRIF. **The authors concluded** that all posterior malleolus fragments, independent of their size, should be treated by ORIF, as this restores syndesmotic stability significantly more often than untreated PMF or CRIF.

*Clanton et al. performed a cadaveric study to compare screw fixation, suture-button, or combination, for the treatment of syndesmosis injuries.* *24 cadavers with syndesmosis injuries were repaired using 1 of 3 randomly assigned techniques: (1) one 3.5-mm syndesmotic screw, (2) 1 suture-button construct, and (3) 2 divergent suture-button constructs.* **The authors noted** no significant differences between repair techniques in resistance to internal and external rotation with respect to the intact syndesmosis. **The authors concluded** that while all repairs provided comparable rotational stability to the syndesmosis, no repair technique completely restored rotational stability and tibiofibular anatomic relationships of the preinjury state.

*Gardner et al. review the contribution of posterior malleolus fracture fixation with respect to syndesmotic stability.* *The authors reviewed 15 patients who sustained PER4 ankle fractures that involved the posterior malleolus with MRIs and noted no complete tears of the posterior-inferior tibiofibular ligaments.* *Furthermore, a PER fracture pattern with a posterior malleolar fragment was created in 10 cadaver specimens with random fixation of the posterior malleolus or the syndesmosis.* Compared with the intact specimens, stiffness was restored to 70% after fixation of the posterior malleolus, and to 40% after syndesmosis stabilization. **The authors concluded** that syndesmotic stability may be obtained more effectively by the fixation of the posterior malleolus rather than by using a syndesmotic screw.
