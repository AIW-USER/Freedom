## Answer explanation

Unicameral bone cysts (UBC) are benign bone lesions with histology significant for fluid-filled, fibrous-lined cavities with occasional giant cells (Answer 1).

Unicameral bone cysts are benign, serous-filled bone lesions that are most commonly seen in the proximal humerus and proximal femur of patients under the age of 20 years. Plain radiographs typically demonstrate a metaphyseal, central, and lytic lesion. Occasionally, a “fallen leaf sign” may be present, which represents a cortical fragment that has fallen into the cyst in the setting of a pathologic fracture. Histology, if obtained, demonstrates fluid-filled, fibrous-lined cavities with occasional giant cells.

*Noordin et al. reviewed the epidemiology, presentation, differential diagnoses, and treatment for UBCs.* **They report** that this predominantly pediatric lesion presents as an intramedullary, centrally located cyst with thin sclerotic margins and occasional “fallen fragment” when in the presence of a fracture or “rising bubble” sign on plain radiographs. Though most UBCs heal by skeletal maturity, patients may be indicated for intervention such as injection, decompression, and surgery to address pathologic fractures, prevent recurrence, or promote cyst healing. **They concluded** that optimal management of these patients requires balancing the risk profile of various treatment options with the severity of symptoms and risk of pathologic fracture.

*Rosenblatt et al. reviewed both unicameral and aneurysmal bone cysts (ABC) in the pediatric population.* **They highlight** that ABCs can be distinguished from UBCs due to their tendency to be wider than the width of the physis and “bubbly” appearance on plain radiographs. Both lesions can be associated with pathologic fractures, which may be an indication in some cases for more invasive treatment. **They concluded** that the management of pediatric bone cysts can be complex and warrants referral to orthopaedic subspecialists to optimize outcomes, especially in the presence of a pathologic fracture.

Figure A demonstrates a pathologic fracture of the left proximal humerus through a lytic lesion with a cortical fragment suspended within the lesion itself (falling leaf sign)
Figure B demonstrates a cyst-like structure with fibrinous material in the cyst wall, characteristic of a unicameral bone cyst.
Figure C demonstrates pleomorphic neoplastic cells with surrounding lacy osteoid characteristic of osteosarcoma.
Figure D demonstrates pleomorphic neoplastic cells in a background of abundant cartilaginous matrix characteristic of chondrosarcoma.
Figure E demonstrates cyst-like structures filled with blood, which is more consistent with an aneurysmal bone cyst than a unicameral bone cyst.
Figure F demonstrates a low-power histology slide with numerous giant cells characteristic of a giant cell tumor of the bone.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer B: Osteosarcoma is unlikely to present with radiographs significant for a “fallen leaf” sign. Typical radiographic findings include periosteal reaction, cortical destruction, and a mixed lytic–sclerotic lesion, often with a soft tissue mass and Codman triangle on radiographs.
Answer C: Chondrosarcoma is unlikely to present with radiographs significant for a “fallen leaf” sign. Radiographic findings usually consist of rings-and-arcs calcification within a lucent lesion, endosteal scalloping, and cortical thickening or expansion, often with minimal periosteal reaction.
Answer D: Radiographs of aneurysmal bone cysts demonstrate expansile, eccentric, and lytic lesions that tend to be wider than the width of the physis. Radiographs in patients with this condition are unlikely to be significant for a “fallen leaf” sign.
Answer E: Radiographs of giant cell tumors of the bone are unlikely to be significant for a “fallen leaf” sign. These lesions are also seen in patients who are older (20 to 40 years old) than the one presented in the above question.
