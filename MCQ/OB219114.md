## Question

A 62-year-old female presents for initial evaluation with long-standing right foot pain. Her examination reveals a collapsed medial arch and inability to perform a single-heel raise. There is no forefoot abduction and the deformity present is flexible. Her sensation is intact to Semmes-Weinstein monofilament testing. She has not had any treatment to this point. What would be the next best step of treatment for long-term management of her condition?

## Options

1. Physical therapy and orthotics with lateral hindfoot posting and cutout for first ray
2. Physical therapy and orthotics with medial hindfoot posting
3. Total contact casting
4. Flexor digitorum longus transfer, calcaneal osteotomy, medial cuneiform plantarflexion osteotomy, and gastrocnemius recession
5. Triple arthrodesis

## Correct option

**2. Physical therapy and orthotics with medial hindfoot posting**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with posterior tibial tendon insufficiency (PTTI) with a flexible hindfoot deformity that would be most appropriately treated conservatively initially with physical therapy and medial hindfoot post orthotics (Answer 2)

Posterior tibial tendon insufficiency is a common cause of flatfoot deformity, especially in middle-aged females. Treatment for PTTI is stage-dependent and based on the degree of rigidity of the deformity. Initial treatment in flexible deformities should be conservative with NSAIDS, physical therapy, and orthotics with a medial hindfoot post. If conservative management fails and the deformity is flexible, transfer of the flexor digitorum longus (FDL), medializing calcaneal osteotomy, +/- medial cuneiform osteotomy and lateral column lengthening can be performed. In rigid deformities, hindfoot arthrodesis should be considered, usually in the form of a triple arthrodesis.

*Chao et al. looked at 49 patients (average age 66) with posterior tibial tendon dysfunction treated non-operatively with orthoses.* The average period of orthosis use was 14.9 months (range, 1.5-29 months), with an average length of daily orthosis wear of 12.3 hours. **They showed** that orthoses were well-tolerated and particularly useful for elderly patients with a sedentary lifestyle or patients at high surgical risk because of medical problems.

*Kulig et al. randomized 62 patients with stage I and II posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) to (1) orthoses wear and stretching, (2) orthoses wear, stretching, and concentric exercises, or (3) orthoses wear, stretching, and eccentric exercises.* **They found** that all three groups improved with treatment; however, the group with the eccentric exercises improved the most. **They concluded** that patients in the early stages of PTTD benefit from orthoses, stretching, and exercise.

*Alvarez et al. followed 47 patients with stage I and II PTTD prospectively over 3 years and treated them with a nonoperative protocol of orthoses and therapy.* **They found** that after four months of therapy, 83% of patients saw subjective and functional improvements, and 89% were satisfied. **They concluded** that patients with early PTTD can be successfully treated with physical therapy and orthoses.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Lateral hindfoot orthotic posting with a first ray cutout would be used to treat a cavovarus foot deformity and would worsen this patient's pain/deformity.
Answer 3: Total contact casting would be most useful for patients presenting with insensate feet/Charcot foot.
Answer 4: FDL transfer and calcaneal osteotomy would be most appropriate in stage II PTTI, for which conservative management has failed.
Answer 5: Triple arthrodesis would be most appropriate in a rigid hindfoot deformity (stage III or IV PTTI).
