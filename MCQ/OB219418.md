## Question

There has been much investigation into head and cervical spine injuries in collision athletes. Which of the following, if present, would be an absolute contraindication to return to playing collision sports, due to risk of future spinal cord injury?

## Options

1. Asymptomatic patient with continued T2-signal changes after a solid 1-level ACDF for a prior cervical spine injury
2. Asymptomatic patient with no T2-signal changes following a solid 3-level ACDF for a prior cervical spine injury
3. Asymptomatic patient with no T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 9 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved
4. Asymptomatic patient with resolved T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 14 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved
5. Asymptomatic patient with resolved T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 11 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved

## Correct option

**2. Asymptomatic patient with no T2-signal changes following a solid 3-level ACDF for a prior cervical spine injury**

## Answer explanation

Anywhere from 9% to 15% of the 12,000 new cases of spine injury per year are attributable to contact/collision sports. Asymptomatic patients with no T2-signal changes following a solid 3-level ACDF are at a high risk of future spinal cord injury, and should never be allowed to return to contact/collision sports (Answer 2).

Injuries to the cervical spine can occur in all sports and range from soft tissue injuries to quadriplegia. These types of injuries are most common among football and rugby players, although the continued evolution of protective gear has decreased the incidence of these injuries. Most injuries in contact sports occur during tackling of another player, and the "spear tackle", in which the player leads with the crown of the head, have been identified as the most common mechanism of neck injury in football. Repetitive "spear tackling" can lead to the development of "spear tackler's spine", a clinical spectrum defined as developmental cervical stenosis, straightening or reversal of normal cervical lordosis and post-traumatic radiographic abnormalities.

Much research surrounding cervical spine injuries in contact athletes has revolved around establishing safe return-to-play (RTP) protocols. RTP guidelines are based on a number of factors including, but not limited to, the severity of cervical stenosis (based on cervical canal diameter/space available for cord (SAC) (Illustration A, line b)), the presence/absence of MRI signal changes, the presence/absence of symptoms and, if treated surgically, the length of the fusion construct. Critical assessment of RTP safety is of paramount importance, as inappropriate RTP markedly increases an individual's risk of sustaining future spinal cord injury.

*Schroeder et al. performed a modified Delphi consensus study of members of the cervical spine research society and NFL team physicians to build consensus on RTP recommendations after spinal cord injury in collision sports athletes.* While the strength of consensus across the provided scenarios varied, the following recommendations achieved a super majority and strong consensus:
-Following an episode of transient paralysis, asymptomatic athletes with resolved T2-signal changes and a SAC <10mm should not RTP. Those with no history of T2-signal change can be considered for RTP on a case-by-case basis.
-Asymptomatic athletes with no T2-signal change after a solid 1- or 2-level ACDF are allowed to RTP, but a 3-level ACDF should not RTP.
-Asymptomatic athletes with continued T2-signal change after a solid 2- or 3-level ACDF should not RTP.

*Puvanesarajah et al. provide a narrative review of traumatic sports-related cervical spine injuries.* *While the authors provided RTP guidelines are based on older data, they mostly align with the newer data provided by Schroeder et al.* Specifically, patients with evidence of a spear tackler spine on radiography, with a clinical history of cervical myelopathy and any patient with ongoing T2-signal change on MRI should not be allowed to return to sport.

Illustration A is a lateral cervical spine radiograph. A represents the vertebral body width, and B represents the canal width (SAC). Normal SAC is approximately 17 mm, and a value < 13 mm is typically associated with cervical spinal stenosis. Dividing B/A gives the Torg-Pavlov ratio (average value 1.0), with a value < 0.8 suggestive of stenosis. However, this value is not accurate in larger athletes, is technique-dependent, and has little predictive value.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: An asymptomatic patient with continued T2-signal change after a solid 1-level ACDF can be considered for RTP on a case-by-case basis.
Answer 3: A patient with no T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 9 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved, can be considered for RTP on a case-by-case basis.
Answer 4: A patient with resolved T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 14 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved, can return to play.
Answer 5: A patient with resolved T2-signal changes and a space available for the cord of 11 mm following an episode of tackling-induced transient quadriplegia, which has resolved, can be considered for RTP on a case-by-case basis. A patient with a space available for the cord < 10 mm with the same clinical scenario should not RTP.
