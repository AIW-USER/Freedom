## Question

A 61-year-old male presents to your office for evaluation of his right shoulder. He sustained the injury shown in Figure A three weeks ago after trying to catch himself as he fell off a dock. He was subsequently treated in the emergency department and discharged home. Which of the following provocative maneuvers indicates the most common associated pathology in this age cohort?

![[q213998-a-gh-dislocation.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. O'Brien's test
2. Apprehension sign
3. Drop arm test
4. Bear hug test
5. Spurling's maneuver

## Correct option

**3. Drop arm test**

## Answer explanation

The drop arm test is indicative of supraspinatus pathology. In patients >60-years-old, 80% will have rotator cuff tears, most commonly involving the supraspinatus, following anterior glenohumeral dislocations.

Patients with glenohumeral dislocations can be broken out into two groups; younger patients who have a higher incidence of recurrent instability, and older patients who have a higher incidence of associated rotator cuff pathology. The likelihood of rotator cuff pathology following traumatic shoulder dislocation increases with age and is present in 30% and 80% of patients older than 40 and 60 years, respectively. These traumatic tears in patients >40 years often involve the “SIT” tendons – supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and teres minor. Patients who present at least 2 weeks after glenohumeral dislocation with persistent pain or dysfunction should be evaluated further for concomitant rotator cuff tears.

*Robinson et al review the injuries associated with traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocations.* **They report** that of the 3633 patients reviewed with glenohumeral dislocations 33.4% had either a rotator cuff tear or greater tuberosity fracture. Patients >60-years-old were most likely to have concomitant rotator cuff tears or greater tuberosity fractures following glenohumeral dislocation.

*Atef et al review the prevalence of associated injuries following anterior shoulder dislocations.* **They report** that 60% of patients had associated lesions following dislocations including rotator cuff tears, axillary nerve injuries or greater tuberosity fractures. **They conclude** that associated injuries, especially rotator cuff tears, are more common than previously expected and a thorough clinical exam is needed when assessing these patients.

*Gombera et al performed a systematic review evaluating rotator cuff tears and glenohumeral instability.* **They report** that patients with persistent pain or dysfunction following anterior shoulder dislocations often had concomitant rotator cuff tears. Surgical repair of these lesions resulted in improved pain and patient satisfaction compared to nonoperative management. **They conclude** that in patients >40-years-old, persistent pain or dysfunction following anterior shoulder dislocation should prompt evaluation of the rotator cuff.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: O’Brien’s test is used to evaluate for SLAP lesions, which are not highly associated with anterior glenohumeral dislocations.
Answer 2: The apprehension sign is more common in younger patients with glenohumeral dislocations who are at a higher risk for recurrent instability. Further, it is highly suggestive of concomitant glenoid bone loss, not rotator cuff tears.
Answer 4: The bear hug test evaluates the integrity of the subscapularis. The majority of rotator cuff tears in the setting of dislocation involve the “SIT” tendons (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and teres minor).
Answer 5: Spurling’s maneuver is used to evaluate for cervical radiculopathy, which is not associated with a glenohumeral dislocation.
