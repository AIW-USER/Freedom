## Question

A 68-year-old man who underwent a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) 10 weeks ago sustains a fall and now has anterior knee pain. A lateral radiograph is shown in Figure A. His extensor mechanism is not intact on examination with a significant lag. The decision is made to undergo surgery. Intraoperatively, the patellar component is stable with good bone stock. How would you treat this patient's injury?

![[q214846-a-42.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF)
2. Patellar component resection arthroplasty
3. Patellar component revision arthroplasty
4. Patellectomy
5. Conversion to metal backed patella

## Correct option

**1. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF)**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a Type II periprosthetic patella fracture. Of the available answer choices, he would be best served with ORIF given good bone stock and well fixed patellar component. In the periprosthetic setting, this ORIF can be augmented with extensor mechanism augmentation such as a mesh reconstruction.

Periprosthetic patella fractures are treated based on the Ortiguera and Berry classification. Type I fractures have a stable implant and intact extensor mechanism; these fractures can be treated nonoperatively with casting/bracing in extension. Type II fractures have a stable implant but a disrupted extensor mechanism; these fractures can be treated with extensor mechanism repair with partial or complete patellectomy versus ORIF (if going to remove the patella need to do reconstruction with allograft or mesh which was listed in answer 2). Type III fractures have a loose implant and are subclassified as IIIa (good bone stock) and IIIb (poor bone stock); IIIa is treated with component revision versus component resection arthroplasty, and IIIb is treated with patellar component removal with patelloplasty or patellectomy.

*Ortiguera and Berry performed a retrospective review of 78 periprosthetic patella fractures in which* **they found** a prevalence of periprosthetic fractures to be 0.68%. **They found** 38/78 fractures to have a stable implant + an intact extensor mechanism, 12/78 had extensor mechanism disruption, 28/78 had a loose patellar component. **They concluded** that this is a rare complication after total knee arthroplasty and that treatment can be guided by three main criteria: integrity of the extensor mechanism, fixation status of the patellar component, and quality of the remaining bone.

*Parvizi et al. performed a case series of 12 periprosthetic patella fractures.* *Seven of 12 cases were nondisplaced and treated nonoperatively; these patients all had good to excellent outcomes.* Surgical treatment was selected for the remaining patients (all type II or III); these patients all had fair to good outcomes. **They conclude** that the management of patella fracture after total knee arthroplasty is challenging with modest outcomes.

Image A shows a lateral radiograph with a displaced periprosthetic fracture of the patella. Illustration A shows the Ortiguera and Berry classification for periprosthetic patella fractures.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 3: Patellar component revision versus resection would be appropriate in the setting of type III fractures with loose patellar implants. Patellar removal can be considered in poor bone quality situations but must be replaced with a formal extensor mechanism reconstruction such as with a non-resorbable mesh or extensor mechanism allograft.
Answer 4: In the setting of a repairable fracture with adequate bone stock, patellectomy would not be appropriate.
Answer 5: Metal backed patella is not an appropriate solution for this injury.
