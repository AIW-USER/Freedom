## Question

A 14-year-old basketball player collides head first with an opposing player during a scramble for a loose ball. A timeout is called and during the timeout the player has difficulty focusing and complains of some dizziness. The coaches decide to remove him from the game. What would be the proper time frame or sequence for this athlete to safely return to basketball?

## Options

1. Athlete may return to play after head CT is negative
2. After patient passes ImPACT testing and scores have returned to baseline
3. Athlete may return later in the game if passes graduated return-to-play protocol
4. Athlete may return to play the next day if symptoms resolved and passes return-to-play protocol
5. Athlete may return after passing a one week minimum graduated return-to-play protocol

## Correct option

**5. Athlete may return after passing a one week minimum graduated return-to-play protocol**

## Answer explanation

This athlete has suffered a concussion and the most appropriate and safe return to play would be after completing a one-week minimum graduated return-to-play protocol.

Concussions result in acute, transient impairment of neurological function secondary to a direct impact or force transmission to the head. This results in abnormal cerebral metabolism and loss of the normal neurometabolic cascade. Symptoms can include headaches, vision changes, inability to focus, emotional lability and dizziness. Proper treatment involves immediate removal from play and a graduated return-to-play protocol. The return-to-play protocol consists of a step-wise progression, with each step requiring 24 hours. These steps include starting with daily activities that do not provoke symptoms and progressing to light aerobic activity, sport-specific exercise, non-contact training, full-contact practice, and finally return to play. At any step, if symptoms return, the athlete is required to return to the previous step.

*Matuszak et al. reviewed physical examination tests for concussions.* **They found** some of the important tests include cranial nerves, muscle testing, reflexes, general head and neck inspection, vision, mental status, vision, concentration, psychiatric screen, and many others. **They concluded** that these tests are useful for evaluating patients with concussions, but their validity does not have great evidence.

*Putukian reviewed the evidence regarding the clinical assessment of sport-related concussion on the sideline with the sensitivity and specificity of sideline testing, and ongoing assessment and follow-up.* **She found** that the sensitivity and specificity of sideline assessment to be good to very good if used within the first 48 hours of injury and are improved with available baseline testing. **She concluded** that a standardized assessment is crucial and clinicians should err on the side of caution for return-to-play decisions.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This athlete does not meet criteria to warrant a head CT and a negative head CT does not qualify an athlete to return to play
Answer 2: While ImPACT testing is useful to establish a baseline to guide treatment and return-to-play. The ImPACT test is NOT designed to be a stand-alone assessment tool, and should always be used as part of a comprehensive clinical assessment. A return to baseline alone does not qualify an athlete to return to play.
Answer 3: An athlete with a suspected or confirmed concussion should NEVER be allowed to return to play on the same day.
Answer 4: Appropriate return-to-play protocol involves a minimum of one week with each stage requiring a minimum of 24 hours.
