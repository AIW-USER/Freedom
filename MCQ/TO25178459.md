Chapter: Pediatric Orthopedics MCQs — Cerebral Palsy (CP)

## Question

A 9-year-old child with spastic quadriparetic Cerebral Palsy (GMFCS Level IV) presents with a Migration Percentage (MP) of 65% and an Acetabular Index of 34 degree on the right hip. Intraoperatively, after performing a Varus Derotation Osteotomy (VDRO) with a final neck-shaft angle of 115 degree and correction of anteversion to 15 degree, the surgeon notes that the femoral head remains "uncovered" posterosuperiorly during a trial reduction. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to ensure long-term stability and prevent recurrent subluxation?

## Options

1. Increase the varus correction of the VDRO to 105 degree to achieve deeper medialization of the femoral head.
2. Perform a Salter Innominate Osteotomy to redirect the entire acetabulum anteriorly and laterally.
3. Perform a Dega-type Acetabuloplasty, ensuring the osteotomy cut is deeper posteriorly to allow for increased posterosuperior coverage.
4. Perform an isolated Psoas tenotomy at the lesser trochanter to reduce the superior "tethering" force on the femoral head.

## Correct option

**3. Perform a Dega-type Acetabuloplasty, ensuring the osteotomy cut is deeper posteriorly to allow for increased posterosuperior coverage.**

## Answer explanation

Unlike Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH), where the deficiency is primarily anterolateral, the deficiency in Cerebral Palsy is typically posterosuperior.

 - The Dega Osteotomy is a "volume-reducing" or "pedicled" acetabuloplasty. Because the osteotomy is incomplete (hinging on the triradiate cartilage), the surgeon can differentially place the bone graft.

 - By placing a larger graft in the posterior aspect of the iliac cut, the surgeon "bends" the acetabular roof down and backwards, specifically addressing the posterosuperior void common in spastic hips.

While varus helps with containment, dropping the neck-shaft angle to 105 degree (extreme varus) creates significant abductor insufficiency. This results in a severe Trendelenburg gait (in ambulatory patients) and can lead to "proximal femoral physis" issues or even a "valgus-producing" stress fracture over time. In CP, we aim for a "sweet spot" of 110 degree to 115 degrees.

The Salter Osteotomy is a "redirection" osteotomy that hinges at the pubic symphysis. It moves the entire acetabulum as a single unit. While excellent for DDH, it provides mostly anterior and lateral coverage. In a CP patient, whose primary instability is posterior, a Salter osteotomy can actually leave the posterior head more exposed, leading to early failure.

A psoas release is an adjunctive soft-tissue procedure. While it reduces the dynamic "pull-up" force, it cannot compensate for a bony structural deficiency (the steep Acetabular Index). Attempting to fix a MP of 65% with soft tissue alone is a common error that leads to a "rebound" dislocation within 12–24 months.

Concepts of hip deformity correction in CP

The primary drivers are the spastic Iliopsoas
and Adductor groups. These muscles create a constant force vector that promotes:

 - Persistent Femoral Anteversion:
 Failure of the normal reduction of anteversion during growth.

 - Coxa Valga: Increased neck-shaft angle due to lack of normal weight-bearing and muscle pull.

 - Acetabular Dysplasia: Secondary shallowing of the acetabulum (especially posterosuperiorly) because the femoral head is not seated deeply.

A. Soft Tissue Realignment

Soft tissue surgery is often "preventative" in younger children (Reimers' Migration Percentage < 30-40%) or "adjunctive" to bony surgery in older patients.

Iliopsoas Lengthening

 - In ambulatory patients (GMFCS I-III), the psoas is lengthened fractionally at the pelvic brim (releasing the tendon over the muscle belly).

 - Rationale: Complete release from the lesser trochanter can cause a "hip flexor void," leading to a compensatory crouch gait. We aim to weaken the internal rotation and flexion force without destroying the power needed for swing-phase initiation.

Adductor Longus/Brevis Tenotomy

 - Targeted to reduce the "scissoring" gait and medial force.

 - The Gracilis: Often lengthened if there is a concomitant knee flexion contracture.

 - Warning: Excessive adductor release in GMFCS IV/V can lead to "Abduction Contractures," making sitting and perineal care difficult.

B. Bony Reconstruction: The "Gold Standard"

When the Migration Percentage (MP) exceeds 40% or the hip is clinically unstable, bony surgery is mandatory.

Varus Derotation Osteotomy (VDRO)

This is a three-dimensional correction performed at the subtrochanteric or intertrochanteric level.

 - Varus Component: Reduces the neck-shaft angle (aiming for a post-op angle of ~110° to 115°). This "tucks" the femoral head under the acetabular roof.

 - Derotation Component: Corrects excessive anteversion. By rotating the distal fragment externally (and thus the proximal fragment internally), the femoral head is centered in the acetabulum.

 - Shortening: In cases of high subluxation or dislocation, the femur is shortened (1–3 cm) to reduce the "tension" on the soft tissues and neurovascular structures (Sciatic nerve) during reduction.

Addressing the "Socket": Acetabuloplasty

If the acetabulum is dysplastic (Acetabular Index > 25-30°), correcting the femur alone will fail because the "roof" is too steep to hold the head.

Dega Osteotomy / San Diego Acetabuloplasty

 - These are incomplete trans-iliac osteotomies.

 - Technique: The cut is made above the acetabulum, leaving the pelvic brim and sciatic notch intact. The roof is then "hinged" downward and laterally over the femoral head.

 - Advantage: It allows for specific coverage (anterior, lateral, or posterior) depending on where the deficiency lies. In CP, the deficiency is usually posterosuperior, so the bone graft is placed to push the roof down and backward.

Pemberton Osteotomy

 - Similar to Dega but hinges at the Y-symphysis (triradiate cartilage). It changes acetabular volume and is highly effective in younger patients with open triradiate cartilage.

 Salvage Procedures (GMFCS IV & V)

When the hip is chronically dislocated and the femoral head is deformed (Head of Femur is no longer spherical), reconstruction is often impossible. The goal shifts to pain relief.

 - Schanz Osteotomy: A pelvic support osteotomy to provide a stable sitting platform.

 - McHale Procedure: Femoral head resection (Girdlestone) combined with a subtrochanteric valgus osteotomy and muscle interposition. This prevents the "proximal migration" of the femur into the pelvis, which causes pain during sitting.

 - Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA):
 Emerging as a viable option for high-functioning CP patients with painful hip arthritis, though it carries a higher risk of dislocation and heterotopic ossification.
