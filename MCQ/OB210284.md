## Question

It does not cause pain and has not been growing in size overall but does enlarge towards the end of the day. ## Options

1. Spindle cells in a wavy arrangement with positive S100 staining, wide resection and radiation
2. Vascular dilations with erythrocytes and some striated muscle cells, observation
3. Small round monotonous blue cells in pseudo-rosettes, chemotherapy followed by marginal excision
4. Angiofibroblastic hyperplasia with disorganized collagen, observation
5. Hemosiderin stained multinucleated giant cells, marginal excision

## Correct option

**2. Vascular dilations with erythrocytes and some striated muscle cells, observation**

## Answer explanation

The presentation and imaging of this patient are consistent with a benign hemangioma of soft tissue. A biopsy would show multiple vascular dilations filled with erythrocytes and notably, no malignant cells. The first line of treatment for benign hemangiomas is observation, especially given this patient's age and lack of symptoms.

Hemangiomas of soft tissue are common benign masses of both the upper and lower extremities that arise from capillaries. Histology typically shows vessel lumens filled with erythrocytes and a notable lack of malignant cells. Superficial hemangiomas present with mostly cosmetic issues and do not require treatment. Deeper intramuscular hemangiomas, on the other hand, may cause pain due to a mass effect. They often abut vital muscles or neurovascular structures, causing most attempts at excision to be intra-lesional, which accounts for their high recurrence rates. Traditionally, sclerotherapy using ethanol had been employed but was limited to more superficial lesions. Adjunctive use of ultrasound has allowed access to deeper intramuscular lesions and has greatly improved injection accuracy.

*Greenspan et al. reviewed the imaging characteristics of confirmed hemangiomas in the extremities.* A majority of hemangiomas showed phlebolith formation on radiographs and consistently demonstrated a high signal intensity on MRI. Based on this, **they recommend** that all patients get radiographs followed by MRI, with ultrasound and angiography reserved for equivocal cases.

*Nelson et al. evaluated the effectiveness of MRI compared with contrasted CT studies and arteriography for extra-neural hemangiomas.* *MRIs from 10 patients with biopsy-proven hemangiomas were reviewed and with the addition of T2 fluid-sensitive sequences, masses showed signal intensity higher than the surrounding tissues and allowed a clear delineation of mass extent.* **They recommended** the use of MRI when working up suspected hemangiomas.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph showing phleboliths within the hemangioma. Figures B-D are various MR sequences showing a high signal mass contained in the deep posterior compartment of the calf. The heterogeneity within the mass is due to blood flow differences. Illustration A shows the typical histology for a hemangioma, which includes capillaries fills with erythrocytes.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This is characteristic of a peripheral nerve sheath tumor.
Answer 3: This would be seen in Ewings sarcoma or other PNET tumors.
Answer 4: This would be seen with lateral epicondylitis.
Answer 5: This is the classic histology for pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS).
