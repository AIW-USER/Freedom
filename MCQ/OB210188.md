## Question

A 12-year-old boy falls while playing football. He is found to be neurologically intact with imaging shown in Figure A. Closed reduction is performed in the emergency department using the Roberts maneuver. Figures B and C show corresponding post-reduction radiographs. What is the next best step in treatment?

## Options

1. Immobilization for one to five days followed by early range of motion
2. Immobilization for three to four weeks followed by early range of motion
3. Repeat closed reduction using the Patterson technique
4. Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning
5. Open reduction and internal fixation

## Correct option

**5. Open reduction and internal fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an elbow dislocation with an associated medial epicondyle fragment that remains incarcerated following closed reduction. The next best step in treatment is open reduction and internal fixation.

Medial epicondyle fractures occur secondary to valgus stress at the elbow often following a dislocation. Closed reduction should be attempted with postreduction radiographs obtained to evaluate for incarceration of the medial epicondyle fragment. Closed reduction may be performed using the Roberts maneuver, which consists of placing the forearm in supination with the wrist and fingers in full extension and then applying a valgus load to the elbow. A persistent incarcerated medial epicondyle fragment in the joint is an absolute indication for open reduction and internal fixation.

*Dodds et al. retrospectively reviewed 11 consecutive cases of incarcerated medial epicondyle fractures following elbow dislocations.* **They found** the average final arc of motion to be 4-140 degrees and a 27% (3/11) percent chance of ulnar nerve symptoms at 14 months follow-up. **They concluded** that open reduction and internal fixation for incarcerated medial epicondyle fragments led to good motion and functional outcomes but with a high rate of complications, particularly involving the ulnar nerve.

*Gottschalk et al. reviewed pediatric medial epicondyle fracture diagnosis, management, and outcomes.* **They stated** that medial epicondyle fragments are incarcerated in the joint in 15-25% of elbow dislocations. They described a step-by-step guide to open reduction internal fixation using a single 4.0-mm cannulated screw following anatomic reduction.

Figure A shows an AP and lateral radiograph of a pediatric elbow showing a dislocation with an associated medial epicondyle fracture. Figure B shows an AP image demonstrating reduction of the elbow with an incarcerated medial epicondyle fragment. Figure C shows a lateral image following closed reduction with an incarcerated medial epicondyle fragment. Illustration A shows a post-operative image following open reduction internal fixation.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 and 2: Immobilization alone would fail to address the incarcerated medial epicondyle fragment that remains in the joint.
Answer 3: The Patterson technique is used for reducing pediatric radial neck fractures. Repeat closed reduction is not indicated given the retained incarcerated fragment.
Answer 4: The incarcerated fragment should be removed from the joint through an open approach and anatomically reduced prior to fixation.
