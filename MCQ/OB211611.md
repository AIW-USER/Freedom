## Question

On an away rotation, a fourth-year medical student interested in orthopaedic surgery examines a patient with spastic hemiplegic cerebral palsy, who complains of a lack of hand function and hygiene issues. The chief resident hints that the patient may ultimately need a wrist arthrodesis with proximal row carpectomy (PRC). The medical student misses a key portion of the physical exam. Which of the following likely represents the missed physical exam finding?

## Options

1. With the fingers extended, the wrist is brought from flexion to extension, but the wrist cannot extend to within 45 degrees of neutral.
2. With the fingers flexed, the the wrist is brought from flexion to extension, but the wrist cannot extend to neutral.
3. The shoulder is held in internal rotation and the elbow is locked in extension.
4. The thumb has a deficient and unstable carpo-metacarpal joint
5. Tight constricting bands around the bilateral upper extremities

## Correct option

**1. With the fingers extended, the wrist is brought from flexion to extension, but the wrist cannot extend to within 45 degrees of neutral.**

## Answer explanation

Assessing a wrist flexion contracture is done by extending all the fingers with the wrist in maximal wrist flexion, then extending the wrist. The degree to which the wrist cannot fully extend is the Volkmann angle.

The typical position of the upper extremity in cerebral palsy (CP) is elbow flexion, forearm pronation, wrist flexion, and adduction of the thumb. Patients often have wrist flexion contractures caused by weak wrist extensors, spastic wrist flexors, and/or volar wrist capsule contracture. This condition weakens grip strength and leads to hygiene problems. Mild flexion contractures may be improved by soft tissue releases of the flexor tendons. However, fixed deformities are best treated by a wrist fusion. A PRC, in addition to the arthrodesis, effectively shortens the flexors and may improve finger motion after the arthrodesis.

*Carlson et al reviewed the treatment of the hand and wrist in CP.* Dynamic deformities may not need any formal treatment. However, those with a static deformity, particularly when the Volkmann angle is 45 degrees may require tendon lengthening (FDS, FDP, FPL) or tendon transfer (FDS to FDP, or FCU to EDC). An arthrodesis may weaken hand strength but should improve hygiene.

*Donadio et al. reviewed 20 adolescents with spastic deformities of the wrist who underwent PRC with wrist arthrodesis.* *Additional soft tissue release was performed in 8/20 patients.* The subjective outcome scores improved and average flexion deformity improved from 66 to 10 degrees. All patients achieved bony union by 6 months although 4 patients required hardware removal. **The authors advocated** PRC with wrist fusion as a reliable and safe treatment in the adolescent spastic wrist.

*Van Heest et al. reviewed 41 cases of wrist spasticity treated with arthrodesis.* Union was achieved in 98%, however, 44% required hardware removal and 10% sustained a metacarpal fracture. DASH and VAS scores improved significantly, and ease of daily care and hygiene were also reported to have been improved post-operatively. 94% of the patients were satisfied with their outcome.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The fingers need to be extended during the Volkmann test to put the extrinsic finger flexors on stretch.
Answer 3: Locked shoulder internal rotation and elbow extension is typical of arthrogryposis. The solution may a triceps muscle transfer.
Answer 4: This exam would be present in a patient with thumb hypoplasia (Blauth Stage IIIB) and indicated for a pollicization.
Answer 5: This would be more typical of constriction band syndrome.
