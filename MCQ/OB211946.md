## Question

A 23-year-old female avid runner with a history of recurrent ankle sprains sustained an acute inversion injury to her right ankle while running 7 weeks ago. At that time, she was able to bear partial weight with crutches. She now returns to the office with complaints of persistent lateral ankle pain and swelling, with the inability to resume running despite the use of an ankle brace and having completed a course of physical therapy. On examination, she has pain with resisted ankle eversion. Weight-bearing radiographs of the ankle are unremarkable. What is the best next step?

## Options

1. Below knee immobilization for 4 weeks
2. Continue therapy with the addition of eversion ankle strengthening exercises
3. Corticosteroid injection into the right ankle joint for diagnostic purposes
4. CT scan of the right ankle
5. MRI of the right ankle

## Correct option

**5. MRI of the right ankle**

## Answer explanation

This patient has chronic lateral ankle instability after an acute inversion injury despite the completion of a course of physical therapy. Her history and examination is concerning for concomitant peroneal tendon pathology and an MRI is warranted.

Pain with eversion of the foot after an acute inversion injury should raise concern for associated peroneal tendon pathology. An MRI should be obtained to help identify intra-articular pathology in addition to injury of the peroneal tendons. Continued instability, despite bracing and compliance with a functional ankle rehabilitation program, may require surgical intervention. Imbrication of the anterior talofibular and calcaneofibular ligaments with advancement of the inferior extensor retinaculum is most commonly performed; concomitant peroneal tendon pathology should be addressed in the same setting.

*Maffulli et al. reviewed chronic lateral ankle instability following untreated acute lateral ankle ligament injuries.* **They report** that failed nonsurgical management after appropriate rehabilitation is an indication for surgery, with anatomic repair of the ATFL and CFL recommended. **They concluded** that anatomic reconstruction with autograft or allograft should be performed when the ruptured ligaments are attenuated, and tenodesis procedures are not recommended because they may disturb ankle and hindfoot biomechanics.

*Philbin et al. reviewed peroneal tendon injuries in patients with chronic lateral ankle pain.* **They report** that addressing concomitant peroneal tendons tears, subluxing or dislocating tendons, and peroneal tenosynovitis are of great importance in achieving a favorable outcome. **They conclude** that low-demand patients do well with a nonsurgical approach but high-demand patients may benefit from surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Early protected range of motion and weight bearing, limiting ankle inversion and plantarflexion has been shown to be superior to prolonged immobilization for acute lateral ankle sprains.
Answer 2: This patient has persistent symptoms despite completion of formal therapy with clinical findings suggestive of peroneal tendon pathology. An MRI is indicated at this time.
Answer 3: A corticosteroid may provide some temporary relief in symptoms if there is intra-articular pathology, but would provide little benefit in diagnosing the suspected peroneal tendon pathology.
Answer 4: Presumed peroneal tendon pathology is best evaluated with an MRI rather than CT scan.
