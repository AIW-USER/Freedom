## Question

A 19-year-old female presents with vague worsening pain in her left knee that has worsened over the past 3 months. She has refrained from sports and running because of the pain. She denies any inciting traumatic event or any recent illness. Radiographs are obtained and shown in Figure A. Which of the following diagnostic tests serves as the most appropriate next step in management?

![[q219444-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Skeletal survey
2. CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis
3. MRI of tibia with knee/ankle joints included
4. Bone marrow biopsy
5. Performing a biopsy utilizing fine needle aspiration

## Correct option

**3. MRI of tibia with knee/ankle joints included**

## Answer explanation

This patient demonstrates concerning symptoms and radiographic findings consistent with a proximal tibia osteosarcoma. Diagnostic workup should involve obtaining an MRI of the entire involved bone with adjacent joints (Answer 3), a chest CT scan to rule out pulmonary metastases, and a core needle versus open biopsy to establish a tissue diagnosis.

Osteosarcoma is a rare disease that displays a bimodal age distribution; the first peak incidence occurs during years of adolescent growth spurts, and the second occurs among the elderly population. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard imaging modality to assess soft tissue components of osteosarcoma, its intramedullary extension, and its overall relation to pertinent surrounding neurovascular structures. Importantly, the MRI should include the entire involved bone and neighboring joints to rule out skip metastases (i.e., intramedullary tumor foci without direct contact with the primary lesion). Additional metastatic workup should be pursued at the time of presentation to include a chest CT scan as well as a 99mTc bone scan.

Ritter and Bielack provided a comprehensive review of the multi-disciplinary approach to the treatment of osteosarcoma. In addition to the aforementioned diagnostic imaging, they also discuss the importance of obtaining pertinent labs for prognostic purposes (alkaline phosphatase/lactate dehydrogenase) and obtaining baseline values before chemotherapy induction (kidney and liver toxicity). The authors conclude by discussing the current standard chemotherapy regimen (MAP; Methotrexate, Adriamycin/doxorubicin, and Cisplatin) and advances in limb salvage surgery.

Biazzo and De Paolis provided a similar review of the multi-disciplinary approach to the treatment of osteosarcoma. They discuss various reconstructive treatment options, including allograft prosthetic composites (APCs), expandable endoprosthetic implants, and even vascularized autografts. The authors conclude by emphasizing the importance of regular, long-term follow-up to monitor for disease relapse (associated with dismal <20% survival rates).

*Alexander and colleagues provided an overview of osteosarcoma, including contemporary etiology, genetics, staging classification systems, diagnostic tests, and treatment options.* *They discuss the updates to chemotherapy and advanced surgical approaches, which have collectively resulted in 5-year survival rates of up to 80% for localized disease.* Unfortunately, patients presenting with metastatic disease to the lung have a much poorer prognosis, with approximately 30% 5-year survival rates. **They conclude** that further research into the molecular pathogenesis driving osteosarcoma and its metastatic potential may translate into a meaningful effect on patient survival.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A skeletal survey is a systematic series of radiographs to cover the entire skeleton and observe for any relevant changes. It is commonly employed to rule out nonaccidental trauma, workup for multiple myeloma, or rule out certain skeletal dysplasia variants. Bone scans are cheap, cost-effective, and more sensitive for detecting osteosarcoma skeletal metastases over skeletal surveys (newer studies endorse PET-CT scans over bone scans).
Answer 2: A CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis would be a part of the appropriate workup for an adult (age 40+) with an unknown lytic bone lesion. This workup is one diagnostic component to determine if the lytic lesion is a metastasis, myeloma, or lymphoma. Osteosarcomas do not require CT of the abdomen and pelvis, as they would be atypic areas of metastatic spread.
Answer 3: Bone marrow biopsy is a regular component of the diagnostic workup for Ewing sarcoma to rule out bone marrow metastases (independent risk factor distinct from overt bone metastases). However, osteosarcoma does not share this same predilection for bone marrow metastases, and therefore bone marrow biopsy is not a regular component in the workup for osteosarcoma.
Answer 5: Fine needle aspiration has yielded poor diagnostic results when utilized in the setting of sarcomas, particularly primary bone sarcomas (i.e. does not obtain enough tissue volume, high rate of failure to obtain tumor tissue over normal tissue, cannot penetrate bone well, etc.). For this reason, core needle biopsy (thicker gauge needle) and, in certain circumstances, open biopsy are encouraged in the setting of primary bone sarcomas.
