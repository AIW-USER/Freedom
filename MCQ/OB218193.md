## Question

The patient states he fell while hiking and went to an urgent care center where radiographs were taken and he was told there were no fractures. ## Options

1. Apply a short arm cast allowing thumb range of motion
2. Apply a long arm cast to include thumb immobilization
3. Open reduction internal fixation using a single screw via a dorsal approach
4. Open reduction internal fixation using a single screw via a volar approach
5. Four corner fusion

## Correct option

**4. Open reduction internal fixation using a single screw via a volar approach**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with a displaced scaphoid fracture (i.e. “humpback deformity”) with a notable delay in diagnosis. This injury should be treated with open reduction, deformity correction, and internal fixation (+/- bone grafting) via the volar approach.

Acute scaphoid fractures are notoriously missed injuries that result in continued wrist pain and decreased functionality. Providers should be aware that radiographs may take 1-2 weeks for fracture remodeling to display an apparent fracture line. If left untreated, acute fractures persist to nonunion with resultant deformity, as seen in this case. The humpback deformity (shown on this patient’s CT scan) occurs when the proximal pole extends via its attachment to the lunate through the scapholunate ligament. At the same time, the distal pole remains flexed through its attachment to the trapezoid and trapezium via the scaphotrapezial ligament. The primary fulcrum through which these fracture fragments hinge is the stout volar radioscaphocapitate ligament (RSC). To adequately reduce these fragments and provide internal fixation, cases involving the humpback deformity specifically require a volar approach. The technique involves booking open the fracture fragments, bone grafting the bony defect, and placement of internal fixation to maintain the reduced scaphoid position.

*Li and colleagues provided an updated review article regarding the management of acute scaphoid fractures.* **They discuss** that nondisplaced and minimally displaced scaphoid waist and distal pole fractures can be treated with below elbow casts without the inclusion of the thumb. **They note** that grossly displaced fractures, those with dorsal intercalated segmental instability (DISI), and persistent nonunions should be treated with operative intervention. **The authors conclude** that fracture stability and patient-related factors ultimately determine the best treatment modality.

*Lee and colleagues performed a retrospective case series in which they treated humpback deformity scaphoid fractures using a combined cancellous and cortical allograft from the distal radius (hybrid Russe technique).* **They reported** 100% union at an average 32-month follow-up with improved radiographic findings and patient-reported outcomes. **The authors concluded** their straightforward technique provides excellent outcomes and recommend its use in any nonunion with humpback deformity and dorsal intercalated segmental instability.

*Cohen and colleagues performed a retrospective case series in which they treated humpback deformity scaphoid fractures using non-structural cancellous autograft from the distal radius metaphysis (modified Russe).* **The authors argued** that the single screw fixation provided adequate stability and that cancellous autograft could easily be molded into the bone defect without the technical nuances of placing a structural cortical graft. **The authors report** 100% union with a 2-year follow-up and concluded that structural autografts are not required for the treatment of humpback deformities.

Figure A-C contains radiographic imaging of this patient’s left wrist involving the scaphoid fracture. The lateral image demonstrates an extended lunate, suggesting a chronic injury involving dorsal intercalated segmental instability (DISI). Figure D displays this patient’s underlying humpback deformity which is hinged along the robust volar radioscaphocapitate ligament fulcrum.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1&2: There have been various studies, including randomized control trials, demonstrating that there is no difference in scaphoid union rates when managed conservatively between different casting models (i.e. above versus below elbow cast, including a thumb spica component or not). Wrist immobilization is all that appears to be required for appropriately indicated fracture patterns.
Answer 3: Open reduction internal fixation using a single screw via a dorsal approach would be appropriate in the majority of scaphoid waist fractures. However, the humpback deformity requires a volar approach to correct the deformity and graft in the collapsed volar osseous defect.
Answer 5: While a four-corner fusion could be considered in this injury pattern with the development of scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse (SNAC), this patient is notably young and has not developed significant arthritis. Less aggressive surgical options should be sought first. Further, younger patients with scaphoid fracture nonunion have been treated with both vascularized and non-vascularized bone grafts with notably high (85-100%) union rates.
