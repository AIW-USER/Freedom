Chapter: Nerve Injury MCQs — Brachial Plexus Injuries

## Question

A 30 years old male comes to you with complaints of difficulty in using his forearm. He gives history of fall on his shoulder after a road traffic accident one month ago. On examination a tinel sign can be elicited in the infraclavicular fossa. A sensory deficit is present over small area of the anterolateral aspect of the forearm. Shoulder x ray shows glenohumeral subluxation. What is the most appropriate diagnosis based on this history and examination?

## Options

1. lower brachial plexus injury
2. medial cord injury
3. lateral cord injury
4. upper brachial plexus injury

## Correct option

**3. lateral cord injury**

## Answer explanation

Answer: 3
Cord injuries in brachial plexus :
When the lesion is at the level of the cords in the plexus, the long thoracic nerve, the suprascapular nerve, and the pectoral nerves are intact. A cord lesion may be suspected if a Tinel Hoffmann sign is located in the infraclavicular fossa.

Lateral cord injuries
Injuries of the lateral cord cause motor and sensory deficits in the distribution of the musculocutaneous nerve (paralysis of the biceps), the lateral root of the median nerve (paralysis of the flexor carpi radialis and pronator teres), and the lateral pectoral nerve (clavicular head of the pectoralis major). Glenohumeral subluxation may result. An aggressive program of rehabilitation of the remaining intact musculature may prevent this subluxation. Sensory deficit can be detected over the anterolateral aspect of the forearm in the relatively small autonomous zone of the musculocutaneous nerve.

Posterior cord injuries
• Injuries of the posterior cord cause motor and sensory deficits in the distribution of the following nerves:
1. Subscapular (paralysis of the subscapularis and teres major)
2. Thoracodorsal (paralysis of the latissimusdorsi)
3. Axillary (paralysis of the deltoid and teres minor)
4. Radial (paralysis of extension of the elbow, wrist, and fingers)
• The disability consists mainly of inability to internally rotate the shoulder, elevate the limb, and extend the forearm and hand.
• Sensory loss most often is apparent only in the autonomous zone of the axillary nerve overlying the deltoid muscle.
Medial cord injuries
• Injuries of the medial cord produce the motor deficit of a combined ulnar and median nerve lesion (except for the flexor carpi radialis and pronator teres) and extensive sensory loss along the medial aspect of the arm and hand.
