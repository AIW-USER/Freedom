## Question

A 31-year-old female smoker was involved in a skiing accident approximately 9 months ago and underwent open reduction internal fixation of the radius and ulna at the time of injury. She now returns to the clinic complaining of increasing pain with range of motion and activity. Radiographs from her most recent follow-up can be seen in Figure A. Laboratory tests show ESR, CRP and WBC count to be within normal limits. Which of the following options is the most appropriate next step in management?

![[q3958-a-bbff2.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Bone scan
2. Above elbow cast
3. Removable splint
4. Reamed intramedullary nail
5. Iliac crest bone grafting + compression plating

## Correct option

**5. Iliac crest bone grafting + compression plating**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with an atrophic non-union of the ulna after open reduction internal fixation for a both-bone forearm fracture nine months ago. The next appropriate step in management would be iliac crest bone grafting and compression plating of the ulna.

The primary issue with an atrophic nonunion is biological. The blood supply is poor and, therefore, incapable of purposeful fracture healing. Smokers, as in this vignette, are at high risk for nonunion. Treating an atrophic nonunion involves improving biology at the fracture site through autologous bone graft (e.g., iliac crest) and providing mechanical stability using compression plating (e.g., 3.5 mm LC-DCP).

*dos Reis et al. reported excellent results of 31 diaphyseal forearm fracture non-unions treated with autologous bone grafting and compression plating.* Thirty of thirty-one patients went on to bony union within 3.5 months of revision surgery.

*Nadkarni et al. presented a case series of 11 patients with non-unions of various long bones initially managed with intramedullary (IM) nail fixation.* *The authors successfully used locking compression plates while retaining the IM nails in treating the nonunion in all cases.*

Figure A shows an AP radiograph of a both-bone forearm fracture with an atrophic nonunion. Illustration A shows a lateral x-ray of a fully healed radius and ulna after hardware removal one year after revision surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: With normal WBC, ESR, and CRP levels, there is little reason to suspect an infectious non-union; thus, there is no indication for a bone scan.
Answer 2 & 3: Immobilization will not treat the underlying issue of biology. Autologous bone grafting is necessary in the treatment of atrophic non-unions.
Answer 4: A reamed IM nail does not provide adequate mechanical stability and is not used to treat radius or ulna non-unions.
