## Question

A 60-year-old female patient that is a 1 pack-per-day smoker with well-controlled diabetes presents for follow-up of lower back, buttock, L5 radicular pain, and decreased walking tolerance due to neurogenic claudication. Her current radiographs are in figure A. She has undergone 8 months of physical therapy, three lumbar epidural steroid injections, and scheduled dosing of acetaminophen and ibuprofen. Despite these efforts, she reports significant impairment in her daily function and 8/10 axial back pain at rest. The physical exam is unremarkable for neurologic deficits. She denies any bowel or bladder symptoms or saddle numbness. What is the next best step in management?

![[q214126-a-degenerative-spondy.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Continued physical therapy with smoking cessation
2. L4-L5 ALIF
3. L4-L5 posterior instrumented fusion
4. Psychiatry referral to optimize depression treatment
5. Repeat epidural steroid injection

## Correct option

**1. Continued physical therapy with smoking cessation**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with persistent symptoms of neurogenic claudication secondary to degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) despite adequate nonoperative treatment but is a current heavy smoker. The patient should undergo smoking cessation to improve her chances of a good surgical outcome.

Degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) is characterized by anterior subluxation of a vertebral body due to disc and facet degeneration and typically occurs at L4-5. In contrast to isthmic spondylolisthesis, there is a high rate of spinal stenosis associated due to the intact lamina encroaching the central canal with further anterior displacement. High-grade slips (Myerding >2) are uncommon due to the preservation of the posterior elements. Symptomatic patients are often successfully treated with a course of physical therapy, corticosteroid injections, NSAIDs, and activity modifications. Patients that are refractory to six months of nonoperative treatment are considered surgical candidates. However, active smokers should undergo smoking cessation to enhance the chance of surgical success and decrease operative complications.

*Pearson et al. evaluated patient and disease characteristics that influenced the treatment effect of surgical and nonsurgical care for spinal stenosis.* **The authors reported** greater treatment effect with surgical treatment in patients with baseline Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)≤56, nonsmokers, lack of neuroforaminal stenosis, a predominant complaint of leg pain, no lifting at work, and lack of neurologic deficit. **The authors concluded** that treatment decisions should be based on patient and disease characteristics and smokers should be counseled on smoking cessation prior to surgery.

*Abdu et al. performed a combined randomized trial and an observational study comparing the 8-year outcomes of surgical and nonsurgical treatment for lumbar DS.* In the as-treated analysis, there was a significantly greater improvement in Short Form-36 (SF-36) bodily pain and physical function scores and Oswestry Disability Index score with surgical treatment at all time points in the study. **The authors concluded** that surgical treatment appears to have superior results in patients presenting with lower back pain and leg pain from DS.

*Glassman et al. performed a retrospective cohort study of 357 patients undergoing lumbar fusion at either L4-5 or L4-S1.* **The authors reported** a 26.5% pseudoarthrosis rate in smokers compared to 14.2% in nonsmokers, which was not affected by the number of cigarettes consumed preoperatively or the duration of preoperative smoking cessation. **The authors concluded** that postoperative smoking cessation can improve union rates in lumbar spinal fusion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 2 and 3: Surgical treatment for DS is indicated in patients with persistent debilitating symptoms despite appropriate nonoperative treatment for 6 months. However, patients that are active smokers must undergo smoking cessation prior to surgical treatment to enhance the chance of surgical success and reduce complications.
Answer 4: The patient has a history of depression that is well-controlled. Further treatment of her depression would likely not affect the success of surgical or nonsurgical treatment.
Answer 5: A repeat epidural steroid injection is an option for this patient for further nonoperative care, but addressing their continued smoking habit would be the most prudent choice.
