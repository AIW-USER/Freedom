## Options

1. Aspirate the shoulder under fluoroscopic guidance to rule out indolent infection with Cutibacterium acnes
2. Cease all therapy and mandate rest in a sling for 6 weeks to allow acromial stress fracture to heal
3. Follow up in 3 months for a one-year post-operative visit with repeat radiographs of the shoulder
4. Revise the baseplate to an augmented baseplate with increased inferior inclination to prevent further notching
5. Provide a new prescription for physical therapy given suboptimal internal rotation on exam

## Correct option

**3. Follow up in 3 months for a one-year post-operative visit with repeat radiographs of the shoulder**

## Answer explanation

The patient has radiographic evidence of scapular notching (Illustration A), but is asymptomatic and should be treated expectantly with appropriate follow up only (Answer 3).

Scapular notching is a common complication of reverse shoulder arthroplasty and is caused by repeated contact between the humeral component and/or medial humerus and the inferior pillar of the scapular neck. The overall incidence of notching has been reported to be between 51% and 96% and has been attributed to implant positioning, altered glenoid and humeral anatomy, and duration of implantation. Importantly, notching can result in the creation of particulate debris, causing osteolysis and ultimate implant failure, thus, it should be monitored with serial radiographs to ensure that it is neither worsening nor causing additional complications. Recent studies have shown that increased lateral offset, increased glenosphere size, and inferior positioning of the base plate with increased inferior tilt can reduce the incidence of scapular notching.

*Cheung et al. reviewed complications in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty.* **The authors note** that the increased use of primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty has led to reports of associated problems unique to the procedure, with the most common complications include neurologic injury, periprosthetic fracture, hematoma, infection, scapular notching, dislocation, mechanical baseplate failure, and acromial fracture. **They conclude** that little information has been published regarding best practices for managing these complications.

Lévigne et al. reviewed scapular notching in reverse shoulder arthroplasty. The authors looked at 326 consecutive patients (337 shoulders) undergoing RSA between 1991 and 2003 and found that at last follow-up (average, 47 months; range, 24-120 months) 62% had scapular notching. They concluded that positioning of the baseplate definitely influences scapular notching and that high positioning of the baseplate and superior tilting must be avoided.

Gutiérrez et al. reviewed the evaluation of abduction range of motion and avoidance of inferior scapular impingement in a reverse shoulder model. The authors placed custom implants in three glenosphere diameters (30, 36, and 42 mm), with 3 different centers of rotation offsets (0, +5, and +10 mm), into a Sawbones scapula in 3 different positions: superior, center, and inferior glenoid. Humeral sockets, meanwhile, were manufactured with a 130 degrees, 150 degrees, and 170 degrees neck-shaft angle. They found that center of rotation offset had the largest effect on range of motion, followed by glenosphere position. Neck-shaft angle had the largest effect on inferior scapular impingement, followed by glenosphere position. They concluded that this information may be useful to the surgeon when deciding on the appropriate reverse implant and positioning.

Figure B is an interval AP radiograph showing evidence of scapular notching with bony erosion of the inferior scapular pillar evident to the level of the inferior stabilization screw of the glenosphere. This is depicted more clearly by the yellow line shown in Illustration A.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is no evidence in the vignette that should concern the provider for possible infection and there are no radiographic signs of loosening.
Answer 2: There is no evidence of acromial or scapular spine stress fracture on the provided imaging. Furthermore the patient is asymptomatic and has no pain.
Answer 4: Given the minimal notching and the fact that the patient is asymptomatic, a revision surgery is not necessary at this time.
Answer 5: The patient actually has excellent internal rotation in the setting of a reverse total shoulder arthroplasty given that he is able to get his hand up his back to the level of L5 at 9 months post-op.
