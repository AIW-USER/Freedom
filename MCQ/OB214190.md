## Options

1. High rate of neurologic deterioration
2. High nonunion rate due to vascular watershed
3. Airway compromise
4. Progressive cervical kyphosis
5. High mortality rate

## Correct option

**2. High nonunion rate due to vascular watershed**

## Answer explanation

The patient is presenting with a type II odontoid fracture which has a high risk of nonunion due to the fracture occurring at a vascular watershed region.

Odontoid fractures can be classified based on the anatomic position of the fracture within the dens itself. Type II fractures are injuries that occur at the base of the odontoid as it attaches to the body of C2. These fractures are associated with higher nonunion rates due to the fracture occurring at a vascular watershed between the internal carotid artery and the vertebral artery. Risk factors for nonunion include >6 mm of fracture displacement, age > 50 years, fracture comminution, fracture gap >1 mm, current smoker, and delay in treatment greater >4 days.

*Shilpakar et al. reviewed operative techniques for the treatment of type II odontoid fractures.* **They recommended** the use of anterior odontoid screw fixation in acute type II fractures as well as rostral type III fractures. In cases of chronic type II fractures or with transverse ligament disruption, **they recommended** posterior transarticular facet screw fixation supplemented with bone graft and interspinous C1-2 wiring.

*Subach et al. retrospectively studied patients treated with anterior odontoid screw fixation that presented with acute type II and rostral type III odontoid fractures.* **They found** at 30-months follow-up there was a 96% fusion rate with no complications related to the surgical technique. Of the included patients, **they reported** one patient requiring external immobilization due to poor screw placement and one requiring posterior stabilization due to insufficient fixation of the anterior screw. **The authors concluded** that single screw anterior odontoid screw fixation is an effective surgical treatment option with high fusion rates and low complication rates in type II and rostral type III odontoid fractures.

*Fountas et al. retrospectively studied the long-term fusion rates of patients undergoing anterior odontoid screw fixation for patients with type II and shallow type III odontoid fractures.* **They reported** a fusion rate of 87.1% with the remaining 12.9% of patients developing a pseudoarthrosis. Of the 12.9% that developed pseudoarthrosis 9.6% were stable and 3.2% with instability. **The authors concluded** that anterior odontoid screw fixation is a safe and effective therapeutic option for type II and shallow type III odontoid fractures.

*Molinari et al. performed a retrospective study of 58 geriatric patients treated for type II odontoid fractures that were treated with rigid collar immobilization and posterior spinal fusion (PSF).* **They reported** that patients with less than 50% displacement of type II odontoid fractures treated with rigid collar immobilization had lower complication rates and no differences in outcomes with respect to Neck Disability Index scores, analog pain scores compared to PSF.
Further, they observed that fracture healing was higher in the PSF group, but was associated with higher mortality rates than rigid collar immobilization, 20% and 12.5% respectively. However, fracture healing was not indicative of pain, functional, and satisfaction outcomes. They concluded that mortality and complication rates following nonoperative management of type II odontoid fractures with less displacement can be associated with lower complication and mortality rates, which remains a controversial topic in the treatment of these injuries in the geriatric population.

Figures A and B are the AP and lateral radiographs of the cervical spine with a type II odontoid fracture. Figures C and D are the coronal and sagittal CT scans of the cervical spine demonstrating a displaced type II odontoid fracture.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Neurologic deterioration in these patients is rare due to the large space for the spinal cord in the upper cervical spine.
Answer 3: Airway compromise can occur if there is sufficient swelling in the neck. However, this is uncommon in odontoid fractures. More commonly it is associated with dysphagia due to the adjacency of the esophagus to the retropharyngeal hematoma.
Answer 4: Progressive cervical kyphosis has not been reported to occur secondary to a type II odontoid fracture.
Answer 5: High mortality rates occur in patients >65 years treated non-operatively, however, this patient is 35 years of age.
