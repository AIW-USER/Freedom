## Question

A 40 years old lady presented with a sudden onset severe headache with focal neurological deficits. NCCT head shows perimesencepahlic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). CT angiogram shows some abnormal vessels. Gold standard diagnostic test for the scenario would be:

## Options

1. Ct angiography
2. Digital subtraction angiography
3. Mr angiography
4. Lumbar puncture

## Correct option

**2. Digital subtraction angiography**

## Answer explanation

Ans: b (digital subtraction angiography)

Explanation:

- Work up of suspected SAH:

1. Tests to diagnose SAH –

- non-contrast high-resolution CT scan head
- if CT is negative: lumbar puncture in suspicious cases

2. Tests to identify source of SAH – options include: CT Angio (CTA), MR Angio (MRA), or catheter angiography. The choice needs to take into account the patient’s age, renal function, and even best guess of where an aneurysm might be located

- MRA: no radiation, and 2d-tof MRA does not use contrast. Poor sensitivity for aneurysm detection early after SAH.
- CTA vs Catheter Angiogram: one needs to balance the risk of the procedure and ease of obtaining it against the information expected to be obtained. CTA is ideally the first investigation done. It permits reliable assessment of feasibility of endovascular treatment in most cases. Catheter angiography may be necessary after a positive CTA to better delineate the anatomy, or to determine dominant filling and cross flow, or in highly suspicious cases with a negative CTA.

3. If CTA/catheter angiogram is negative: the entity is called as SAH of unknown etiology and work up is done accordingly.

Catheter Angiogram procedure:

- Injection of radio-opaque (iodinated) contrast (“dye”) into selective vessels using a catheter typically inserted into the femoral artery at the upper thigh, while taking serial x-rays to obtain a “video-like” representation of the vasculature.
- It is considered “The gold standard” for evaluation of cerebral aneurysms. Current state of the art uses digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Demonstrates source (usually aneurysm) in ≈ 80–85%; remainder are so-called “SAH of unknown etiology”. Shows if radiographic vasospasm is present – clinical vasospasm almost never occurs < 3 days following SAH – and assesses primary feeding arteries, collateral flow in case of a need for arterial sacrifice.
