## Answer explanation

Figure B demonstrates the "arcuate sign," which is an avulsion fracture of the proximal fibula by the lateral collateral ligament (LCL), indicative of a posterolateral corner injury.

Injuries to the posterolateral corner complex occur in around 10-15% of all ligamentous knee injuries and are often missed due to sometimes subtle findings on examination and imaging. Three main structures comprise the posterolateral corner, including the LCL, popliteus tendon, and popliteofibular ligament. The LCL primarily works to resist excess varus movement, while the popliteus and popliteofibular ligament work more dynamically to control tibial rotation and translation. Due to the LCL attachment on the proximal fibula, avulsion fractures of the ligament may sometimes be seen instead of mid-substance ruptures, and this has been termed the "arcuate sign." The treatment of posterolateral corner injuries depends on the severity and presence of other injuries, but repair and reconstruction are both viable options.

*Lee et al. looked at the arcuate sign in posterolateral corner injuries and how the anatomic, radiographic, and MRI data related to different injury patterns.* **They noted** that injury to the arcuate ligament and popliteofibular ligaments often caused a smaller styloid process avulsion fracture compared to injury to the LCL or biceps attachments which caused a larger avulsion fragment. **They concluded** that the imaging results helped identify the specific injury patterns seen.

*Huang et al.* **noted** that avulsion fractures of the fibular head (i.e. the arcuate sign) were predictive of injuries to the posterolateral corner and PCL. **They noted** that in 13 patients included in the study with fibular head avulsion fractures, there was an injury to the posterolateral complex, which most commonly affected the LCL compared to the popliteus. **They also noted** that there was often some associated disruption of the posterior cruciate ligament with this injury pattern.

Figure A is a knee radiograph demonstrating a segond fracture which is pathognomonic for an ACL tear. Figure B is a radiograph showing an arcuate avulsion fracture from the proximal fibula which is representative of an avulsion of the LCL. Figure C is a radiograph showing a lateral tibial plateau fracture. Figure D is a lateral radiograph demonstrating a posterior cruciate ligament (PCL). Figure E is a radiograph showing a tibial spine avulsion fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This radiograph shows a segond fracture which is seen in cases of anterolateral ligament disruption in combination with ACL tear.
Answer 3: This radiograph demonstrates a Schatzker II tibial plateau fracture. While soft tissue injury can occur concomitantly, medial plateau injuries are more common with the varus stress seen in posterolateral corner injuries.
Answer 4: This radiograph demonstrates a PCL avulsion fracture. PCL injuries can occur with injuries to the LCL but based on the radiographs alone, Figure B is more likely associated with injury to the LCL.
Answer 5: This radiograph demonstrates an ACL avulsion fracture. ACL injuries can also occur with injuries to the posterolateral corner but the image in Figure E does not show any direct evidence of this.
