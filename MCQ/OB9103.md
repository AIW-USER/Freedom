## Question

Which of the following intramuscular compartment pressure (IMCP) measurements is most consistent with the diagnosis of chronic exertional compartment syndrome?

## Options

1. Resting IMCP = 10 mmHg
2. IMCP 1 minute post-exercise = 15 mmHg
3. IMCP 1 minute post-exercise = 25 mmHg
4. IMCP 5 minutes post-exercise = 15 mmHg
5. IMCP 5 minute post-exercise = 25 mmHg

## Correct option

**5. IMCP 5 minute post-exercise = 25 mmHg**

## Answer explanation

Intramuscular compartment pressure (IMCP) measurements that are diagnostic of chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) include: resting IMCP >/= 15 mmHg, IMCP 1 minute post-exercise >/= 30 mmHg, IMCP 5 minutes post-exercise >/= 20 mmHg. Therefore, an IMCP = 25mmHg 5 minutes post-exercise would be most consistent with the diagnosis of CECS.

CECS occurs when exercise elevates the IMCP to a point that muscle perfusion is reduced and ischemic pain results. The anterior and lateral compartments of the leg are most commonly affected, followed by the thigh and forearm. Etiology is related to limited osseofascial expansion, increased incidence of fascial hernias and arteriole dysregulation. Patients experience cramping pain and transient neurologic symptoms. Unlike acute compartment syndrome (ACS), symptoms tend to be bilateral and resolve with rest. Nonsurgical treatment is only successful if a patient completely ceases the activity that is causing symptoms. Therefore, most patients are managed surgically with fasciotomies of the involved compartments.

*Fraipont et al. reviewed the diagnosis and management of CECS.* Diagnosis is most often confirmed by measurement of resting and post-exercise IMCP. Measurement of muscle oxygenation during and after exercise by infrared spectroscopy is another diagnostic tool and reveals greater deoxygenation and delayed reoxygenation in patients with CECS compared to controls. MR imaging shows increased T2 signal intensity in the affected compartment post-exercise.

*Roscoe et al. performed a prospective cohort study evaluating the utility of intramuscular compartment pressure (IMCP) measurement for diagnosis of CECS.* Study patients with CECS symptoms demonstrated higher IMCP upon standing at rest compared to asymptomatic controls (35.5 mmHg v. 23.8 mmHg, p = 0.006). This elevation persisted through exercise, with the greatest difference noted at maximal tolerable pain (114 mmHg v. 68.7 mmHg, p < 0.001). **The authors suggested** using a threshold value of 105 mmHg during maximal tolerable pain leads to improved diagnostic accuracy.
