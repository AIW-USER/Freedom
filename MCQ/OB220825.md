## Question

A 7-year-old female is referred to your office for a second opinion. She was recently diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic scoliosis. She has a right thoracic curve measuring 75 degrees and a left lumbar curve measuring 45 degrees. The MRI does not show any abnormalities in the spinal cord. You begin talking with her parents about surgical treatment options. Of the options listed below, which requires more than two surgical procedures?

## Options

1. Lumbar vertebral body tethering and posterior spinal fusion of the thoracic spine
2. T3-L3 instrumented posterior spinal fusion
3. Traditional growing rods
4. Instrumented anterior and posterior spinal fusion
5. Halo traction and T3-L2 instrumented posterior spinal fusion

## Correct option

**3. Traditional growing rods**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with severe juvenile idiopathic scoliosis. Given the magnitude of her curve and her age, traditional growing rods can be used to help guide the growth of her spine in the correct direction. The downside to traditional growing rods is that they require repeat lengthening procedures, on average, every 6 months until definitive spinal fusion (answer 3).

Juvenile idiopathic scoliosis is a coronal plane spinal deformity that most commonly occurs in children aged 4-10 years old. This accounts for approximately 15% of all idiopathic scoliosis cases, with females more frequently affected. Given there is a higher propensity (18-25%) for neural axis abnormalities in this age group (e.g., syringomyelia, Arnold-Chiari malformation, tethered cord, dysraphism, and spinal cord tumor), MRI is indicated in these children <10 years old with a curve >20 degrees. Treatment options in this age group include observation in curves <20 degrees, bracing in those with 20-50 degree curves, and surgery for curves >50 degrees. Surgical treatment options include growing rods or VEPTR (Vertical Expandable Prosthetic Titanium Rib), anterior and posterior spinal fusion (for younger children), posterior spinal fusion (for older patients near skeletal maturity), and anterior spinal fusion (best suited for thoracolumbar and lumbar curves). Also, Halo traction (intraoperatively or staged) and vertebral body tethering (normally a 2-stage procedure) can be used in conjunction with a spinal fusion procedure.

*Howard et al. assessed prospectively collected data evaluating deformity correction and HRQOL 5 years post spinal fusion in 20 patients (17 females and 3 males).* *The mean age was 9 years old, and 85% had spastic cerebral palsy (GMFCS V).* *Posterior spinal fusion was performed in 18 patients, with distal fixation to the ilium (80%) or to L4-S1 (20%).* *At 5-year follow-up, there were no significant changes in major curve magnitude (p = 0.638), thoracic kyphosis (p = 0.09), or pelvic obliquity (p = 0.28).* Quality of life, mobility, and comfort significantly improved from preoperative to 5 years postoperatively (p < 0.05). **They concluded** that performing a definitive spinal fusion demonstrates good results at 5 years postoperatively.

*Miller et al. reviewed prospectively collected data evaluating factors associated with an improvement in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) after spinal fusion surgery for nonambulatory patients with cerebral palsy.* *157 patients with nonambulatory cerebral palsy (GMFCS IV and V) were included.* Meaningful improvement in CPCHILD scores were reported in 36.3% (57/157) at 2 years postoperatively. These patients had significantly lower preoperative total CPCHILD scores (43.8 vs. 55.2, p<0.001). Also, only the preoperative comfort, emotions, and behavior domains of the CPCHILD were predictive of meaningful improvement after surgery (p≤0.001). **They concluded** that patients with lower preoperative quality of life scores had more room for improvement, but curve correction was surprisingly not associated with meaningful improvement after surgery.

*Jackson et al. compared the outcomes in severe neuromuscular scoliosis patients with cerebral palsy who were treated with either intraoperative halo traction and posterior spinal fusion (PSF) or anterior and posterior spinal fusion (APSF).* Twenty-three patients (18 traction + PSF versus 5 anteroposterior spinal fusion) were included with > 100-degree curves. **They found** greater correction in pelvic obliquity (33.5 vs. 14 degrees of correction, P=0.007) in the traction group. The anteroposterior spinal fusion group demonstrated longer OR time (669 vs. 415 min, P=0.005), but similar hospital and ICU length of stay, intubation time, estimated blood loss, cell saver, and red blood cells used. **They concluded** that intraoperative traction may be a reasonable alternative to an anterior release in patients with cerebral palsy and severe neuromuscular scoliosis.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer 1: Typically, combined vertebral body tethering and posterior spinal fusion is performed in 2 stages.
Answer 2: T3-L3 instrumented posterior spinal fusion is typically performed in one stage.
Answer 4: Instrumented anterior and posterior spinal fusion can be performed in one or two stages.
Answer 5: Halo traction can be performed first with posterior spinal fusion 3-4 weeks later or performed in a single stage with intraoperative halo traction removed at the end of the procedure.
