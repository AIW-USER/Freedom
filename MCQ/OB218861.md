## Question

## Answer explanation

This patient has sustained a medial condyle fracture, a rare type of pediatric humerus fracture. During the exposure of the medial fragment, the ulnar nerve (supplied by the C8-T1 nerve roots) is often encountered. If accidentally injured, you would expect the patient to complain of postoperative numbness on the palmar and dorsal aspect of the medial 1.5 digits (Answer C).

Medial condyle fractures of the humerus are rare elbow fractures in pediatric patients. They represent 1-2% of all pediatric elbow fractures, and the management of these injuries is fraught with complications. Most importantly, like lateral condyle fractures, these represent intra-articular injuries and are prone to non-union. In addition, these injuries are often Salter-Harris type IV injuries and can be associated with physeal injury. Furthermore, the blood supply to the medial condyle arises posteriorly, is terminal, and has no collateral flow; disruption of the blood supply either at the time of injury or fixation can lead to avascular necrosis. Given these characteristics, much like a lateral condyle fracture, treatment often revolves around anatomic reduction, stabilization, and subsequent immobilization until healing is observed. One must use caution during operative fixation, as the ulnar nerve is intimately associated with the medial condyle, and is often encountered during fracture reduction and/or fixation.

*Saglam et al. provide a case series and review of medial condyle fractures in adolescent children.* *Their series included six patients with a mean age of 14.8.* Three patients presented with signs of ulnar nerve dysfunction at the initial presentation. These findings contrast sharply with prior published reports, in which only a single case of ulnar nerve injury was reported across 19 patients. **The authors postulate** that the higher rate observed in their series could be due to the older patient age. Promisingly, no gross injury to the nerve was seen in any of their patients, and all resolved spontaneously during observation.

*McCarthy et al. present a case report of a 30-month-old boy with a displaced medial condyle fracture and entrapment of the median nerve.* **The authors highlight** the importance of an accurate and complete neurological examination throughout patient care and note that given the risk of entrapment of both the median and ulnar nerves, percutaneous fixation techniques (i.e. CRPP) should be avoided.

*Rubin et al. provide a review of tardy ulnar nerve palsy following injury to the elbow.* **The authors note** that while this injury is typically associated with non or malunited lateral condyle fractures and a subsequent cubitus valgus deformity, this clinical syndrome can also be encountered in patients with malunited supracondylar humerus fractures, medial condyle fractures, and/or radial head +/- Monteggia fracture/dislocations. *Much like McCarthy et al., the authors stress the importance of a complete and accurate neurologic examination throughout patient care.*

Figure A represents a displaced medial condyle fracture in a skeletally immature patient. Figure B is a diagram depicting the sensory innervation of the upper extremity, with the following labeled sensory distributions:
A: Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (C5-C6)
B: Dorsal sensory branch of the radial nerve (C6-C8)
C: Palmar digital branch of the ulnar nerve (C8-T1)
D: Median nerve (C5-C8)
E: Medial brachial cutaneous nerve (T1-T2)

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, a branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, innervated by C5-C6, is not encountered posterior to the medial condyle.
Answer 2: The dorsal sensory branch of the radial nerve, innervated by C6-C8, is not at risk of injury during the fixation of a medial condyle fracture.
Answer 4: The median nerve, innervated by C5-C8, can become entrapped at the time of injury, but is not innervated by C8-T1.
Answer 5: The medial brachial cutaneous nerve, innervated by T1-T2, does not course posterior to the medial condyle fragment.
