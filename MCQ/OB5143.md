## Question

An 8-year-old boy sustains the injury demonstrated in Figure A. It is a closed, isolated injury. After closed reduction and casting, post-reduction imaging demonstrates anatomic reduction. X-rays at 1 week follow-up demonstrate some loss of reduction, now with approximately 12 degrees of dorsal angulation. With continued observation and maintenance of this alignment, long term outcome is most likely to include:

![[q5143-a-bbff1.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Decreased pronation/supination.
2. Malunion.
3. Decreased elbow flexion.
4. No additional sequelae.
5. Nonunion.

## Correct option

**4. No additional sequelae.**

## Answer explanation

Although the fracture has developed angulation since the reduction, the magnitude of the deformity is still acceptable. Complications are unlikely as long as alignment is maintained.

BBFF are one of the most common fractures in pediatrics. Initial management of closed injuries includes CR and immobilization with monitoring of alignment on weekly plain radiographs. Operative intervention is considered if the alignment is unacceptable following CR. For children less than 10 years old, complete displacement with bayonet apposition, angulation less than 15 degrees, and rotation less than 45 degrees are acceptable and lead to no additional sequelae.

*Bowman et al performed a retrospective review of 282 pediatric patients that underwent CR and casting of a BBFF to identify factors associated with failure of nonoperative treatment and the time frame in which failure is most likely.* **They concluded** that children age 10 years old or greater with proximal-third fractures seem to be at highest risk for failure when treated nonoperatively for both-bone forearm fractures. Paradoxically, ulna angulation <15 ∘ were also predictive of failure. Regarding time of failure, 55% failed by the end of the first week, and 95% failed by 3 weeks, **emphasizing** the importance of early follow-up.

*Noonan et al provide guidelines for acceptable displacement of the radius and ulna.* In fractures in any level in children less than 9 years of age, complete displacement, 15 degrees of angulation, and 45 degrees of malrotation are acceptable. In children age 9 or older, 30 degrees of malrotation is acceptable, with 10 degrees of angulation for proximal fractures and 15 degrees for more distal fractures. Complete bayonet apposition is acceptable as long as angulation does not exceed 20 degrees and the patient has at least 2 years of growth remaining.

Figure A demonstrates a both bone forearm fracture in a skeletally immature patient.

Answers 1, 2, 3, and 5 are incorrect because the patient is 8 years old with angulation and rotation within acceptable limits; therefore, if he maintains this alignment, he should have no additional sequelae.
