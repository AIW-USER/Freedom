## Question

A 42-year-old male weightlifter presents to your clinic with complaints of left shoulder pain after an injury sustained while bench pressing 3 days ago. He states that since the injury, the shoulder has become swollen and ecchymotic. On physical examination, there is a loss of axillary contour and pain/weakness with resisted adduction. His neurovascular status is intact distally. You order an MRI, which confirms your suspected diagnosis and the most common pattern of injury for the injured structure. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Electromyography (EMG)
2. Sling for 1-2 weeks then physical therapy
3. Primary repair of the injured structure
4. Reconstruction of the injured structure
5. Tendon transfer

## Correct option

**3. Primary repair of the injured structure**

## Answer explanation

This weightlifter has a tear of the pectoralis major for which primary repair of the tendon should be recommended.

Pectoralis major tendon tears are relatively uncommon but occur mostly in a young, male population who participate in heavy weightlifting activities. Typically, the tendon avulses from its attachment on the lateral aspect of the bicipital groove and retracts medially which causes loss of axillary contour and the classically described, "dropped nipple sign." MRI is helpful in confirming this diagnosis and evaluating for partial vs. complete tear as well as the degree of retraction and any associated muscle atrophy in a chronic setting. In acute tears, repair is recommended in an athletic population trying to return to their activities. Reconstruction with allograft tissue may be necessary in chronic tears or those for which adequate tissue is not present for repair such as a tear at the myotendinous junction. Outcomes of primary repair of the pectoralis major have demonstrated excellent outcomes in this population.

*Brown et al. review pectoralis major injuries including their presentation, diagnosis, and management.* **They note** that treatment options should be heavily influenced by injury severity and patient activity/goals. Patients undergoing surgical repair are typically satisfied with their treatment and return to full activity around 4-6 months, with some exceptions.

*Kowalczuk and Elmaraghy published a JAAOS review article discussing pectoralis major ruptures,* **noting** their increasing frequency in young, high-demand, male patients secondary to eccentric contraction. Physical examination is particularly helpful in these cases and attention should be paid to loss of axillary contour and orientation of the chest wall/nipple. Surgical treatment is recommended by the authors compared to nonsurgical management, as it results in improved functional outcomes and high levels of return to sport.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is no evidence of nerve injury in this patient that would require an EMG.
Answer 2: In the setting of a high-demand weightlifter, operative repair of the injured pectoralis major tendon is recommended to restore strength and function.
Answer 4: Reconstruction of the injured pectoralis tendon would not be required in an acute injury such as this one. Reconstruction would be indicated in the setting of a chronic injury without adequate tendinous excursion or in tears that occur at the myotendinous junction requiring augmentation.
Answer 5: The pectoralis major can be used as a source of tendon transfer, especially in the case of subscapularis insufficiency, but a tendon transfer to substitute for an acute pectoralis tendon tear would not be indicated.
