## Question

A 68-year-old female presents with persistent pain around her right ankle. She had right ankle surgery 18 months ago with another surgeon, and has the implant shown in Figure A. She reports increasing discomfort over the past six months, accompanied by occasional swelling and warmth. Her C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are elevated. Aspiration of the joint reveals a white blood cell count of 25,000/µL with 85% polymorphonuclear cells. What is the next best step in treatment?

![[q220884-a-salto.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Oral antibiotics for 6 weeks
2. SPECT scan
3. Polyethylene exchange
4. Two component explantation and antibiotic spacer fabrication
5. Below knee amputation

## Correct option

**4. Two component explantation and antibiotic spacer fabrication**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a prosthetic joint infection (PJI) involving a total ankle arthroplasty (TAA). She is indicated for implant explantation and antibiotic spacer placement, with plans for future reimplantation or arthrodesis (Answer 4).

Prosthetic joint infection after TAA is a complex and serious complication with poor outcomes. Initial diagnostic testing involves a physical exam, radiographic exam, and serum laboratory studies, including blood counts, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Joint aspiration and synovial fluid analysis are typically utilized to confirm PJI. The gold standard for treatment of PJI is two-stage revision of all components, including explantation and cement spacer fabrication.

*Myerson et al. conducted a retrospective study between 2002 and 2011 to evaluate the management of infections in patients who underwent TAA and subsequently required prosthesis removal.* *Of 613 TARs performed, 19 cases of infection were treated, including 15 late chronic infections, 3 early postoperative infections, and 1 acute hematogenous infection.* *Treatment outcomes varied, with only 3 patients successfully undergoing revision arthroplasty, 6 receiving arthrodeses, 7 retaining a permanent antibiotic spacer, and 3 requiring transtibial amputation.* *The study concluded that successful joint-preserving revision arthroplasty post-infection is limited, with hindfoot arthrodesis being a reliable alternative.* The research emphasizes the need for accurate diagnosis and timely intervention in managing deep infections following TAA.

*Alrashidi et al. provide a comprehensive overview of the diagnosis and treatment of infection in TAA.* *They identify risk factors such as prior surgery, low preoperative scores, diabetes, and wound healing issues.* *The authors detail the clinical variability in presentation and emphasize the importance of initial evaluation with blood tests, joint aspiration, and synovial fluid analysis to confirm infection.*

*Hsu et al. provide a comprehensive overview of the challenges and considerations in managing painful TAA.* **They highlight** the importance of patient selection, implant design, and surgical technique in preventing TAA failure. The authors discuss the various causes of TAA failure, including aseptic loosening, subsidence, impingement, arthrofibrosis, and infection, and emphasize the need for a thorough clinical evaluation to determine the underlying etiology. Surgical management options for failed TAA include arthrodesis, revision surgery, or below-knee amputation, with the choice of intervention depending on factors such as the extent of bone loss, alignment, and the condition of the soft tissues.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Oral antibiotic treatment alone has not been shown to have clinical efficacy in treating PJI.
Answer 2: A SPECT scan is not indicated in the diagnosis of PJI.
Answer 3: Polyethylene exchange alone has not been shown to have good success in treated PJI in TAA. Additionally, the system shown in Figure A does not have a modular polyethylene liner.
Answer 5: Below knee amputation is indicated only in non-salvageable scenarios. This patient has not undergone an attempt at treatment of PJI.
