## Question

A 64-year-old male presents to the clinic with concerns about apical toe ulceration of his second digit as seen in Figure A. He has been strictly managing his glucose levels and lost weight since his prior toe amputation a year prior. On physical examination, there is no purulence at the ulceration and his claw toes are passively correctable. In addition to wound care, his surgeon is planning to perform a percutaneous flexor tenotomy as part of his treatment plan. Which of the following is true regarding the treatment of diabetic toe ulcerations with adjuvant flexor tenotomy?

![[q218593-a-figure-a.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. The flexor tenotomy incisional site has a very high risk (>50%) of wound complications
2. Flexor tenotomies performed for ulcerations at the base of the metatarsal heads are similarly effective
3. Underlying osteomyelitis does not affect the expected time to resolution or healing rates
4. Patients can develop a similar ulceration to an adjacent toe after tenotomy
5. Patients require inpatient observation for the flexor tenotomy procedure

## Correct option

**4. Patients can develop a similar ulceration to an adjacent toe after tenotomy**

## Answer explanation

Diabetic toe ulcerations with passively correctly toe deformities benefit from flexor tenotomies via offloading the ulceration site. While complications are scarce, transfer ulceration to adjacent toes is a well-described complication.

Superficial ulcerations over the tip (apex) of the toe are often secondary to deformities that increase pressure points experienced with normal shoe wear. In the diabetic population, these altered pressures initially result in callous formation, ulceration, and then infection. Wound care, antibiotics (if an infection is present), and wide-toe or offloading shoe wear is the mainstay of treatment. Flexor tenotomy has been increasingly utilized as an adjuvant modality serving to correct the toe deformity and offload the area of ulceration. The risk of complications is low (<15%), however development of an ulcer developing in an adjacent digit (i.e. "transfer lesion") in the first few months after tenotomy is well described. It has been postulated to be secondary to tendon retraction resulting in increasing clawing of adjacent digits or due to increased loading of adjacent toes with correction of the originally involved toe.

*Bonanno and colleagues performed a systematic review of the outcomes of flexor tenotomies for the treatment of diabetes-related toe ulcerations.* **They found** that overall healing rates were exceedingly high (>95%) with a low risk for complications. **They also reported** that patients who underwent prophylactic flexor tendon tenotomy effectively remained ulceration free at the final follow-up. **They concluded** that flexor tenotomy to address toe deformity is safe, effective, and can be used as a preventative measure in cases with heavy callous formation.

*Tamir and colleagues retrospectively reviewed their patient cohort outcomes after performing percutaneous tenotomy for the treatment of diabetic toe ulcerations.* **They reported** a low complication rate, however, noted transfer lesions in 8% of patients requiring additional adjacent toe flexor tenotomy. Additionally, **they showed** that plantar metatarsal head ulcers persisted despite flexor tenotomies and recommended gastrocnemius recession procedures for these types of ulcerations.

Figure A shows apical ulceration of the patient’s second toe. The prior third toe amputation can be appreciated as well.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The flexor tenotomy incisional site has a very LOW risk (<5%) of developing wound complications. However, if the infection becomes apparent at the incisional site, it can be a harbinger of spreading infection and the eventual need for amputation.
Answer 2: Flexor tenotomies performed for ulcerations at the base of the metatarsal heads are not effective, as shown in the Tamir article. All patients treated with flexor tenotomy for these ulcerations had persistent ulceration findings at follow-up.
Answer 3: Underlying osteomyelitis certainly affects the expected time to resolution or healing rates. The majority of patients who reportedly failed tenotomy and wound care management had osteomyelitis on presentation.
Answer 5. Patients do not require inpatient observation for the flexor tenotomy procedure. In fact, one of the many benefits is that they can be performed on an outpatient basis in the clinic.
