## Question

A 75-year-old man with a previous history of a cemented total hip arthroplasty presents with left hip pain and a limp for the past 2 months. His laboratory markers are unremarkable. Radiographs reveal good bone stock with a loose cemented femoral stem with subsidence, varus alignment, and the tip of the femoral stem eroded through the lateral cortex distally. What would be the most appropriate treatment?

## Options

1. Proximal femur replacement
2. Strut allograft augmentation of the femur with cable fixation
3. Use of a cemented femoral stem revision without strut allograft
4. Use of a cemented femoral stem revision with strut allograft
5. Use of a cementless femoral stem revision

## Correct option

**5. Use of a cementless femoral stem revision**

## Answer explanation

An uncemented long-stem revision prosthesis would be most appropriate in this setting.

Cementless fixation utilizing an uncemented long-stem revision prosthesis allows for engagement of the femoral shaft and provide immediate axial and rotational stability of an impending fracture. Cemented fixation may lead to extrusion and interdigitation of cement and is not recommended.

*Harris et al. performed a radiographic study of cemented total hip replacements to assess the incidence of loosening of the femoral component in older adult patients.* *They defined three categories of loosening: definite (radiographic evidence of migration of the component or the cement), probable (complete 100% radiolucent zone around the cement mantle on one or more radiographs without migration), and possible (a radiolucent zone that occupied more than 50% but less than 100% of the cement-bone interface on one or more radiographs).* **They report** that the total incidence of definite, probable, and possible loosing of the femoral components to be 1%, 0%, and 4%, respectively. **They concluded** that there is a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of definitely loose cemented femoral components compared with prior reports.

*Callaghan et al. reviewed revision cemented hip arthroplasty due to mechanical failure.* **They report** that complications associated with revision included perforation of the femoral cortex (13%), postoperative deep infection (3%), postoperative dislocation (8%), trochanteric complications (6%), and sciatic nerve palsy (11%). After revision, progressive radiolucencies (29%), femoral subsidence (18%), and acetabular migration (9%) were noted. **They concluded** that mechanical failure and progressive radiolucencies were associated with poor quality of bone and inadequate anatomical reconstruction.

*Harris et al. reviewed whether THA femoral component loosening predisposes to periprosthetic femoral fracture.* *Using synthetic and paired cadaveric femora with either well-fixed or loose cemented stems,* **they reported** that for synthetic specimens, torque to failure was reduced by 38%, whereas stiffness was decreased 54% for the loose group compared with the well-fixed group; conversely, for the cadaveric specimens, torque to failure was reduced by 58%, whereas stiffness decreased 70% for the loose group compared with the well-fixed group. **They noted** that fracture patterns were similar between synthetic and cadaveric femora with a proximal spiral pattern in loose specimens and more distal fracture patterns with well-fixed stems. **They concluded** that patients with loosened femoral components are at risk for fracture at a substantially lower torque than those with well-fixed components.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Proximal femoral replacement is a viable option in the setting of significant bone loss
Answer 2: This would not be a reasonable option in the setting of a loose femoral stem with an impending fracture
Answers 3&4: Revision of a cemented femoral stem may lead to extrusion and interdigitation of cement and is not recommended
