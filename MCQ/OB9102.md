## Question

## Options

1. Bracing with a thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO)
2. Observation with repeat radiographs of the scoliosis in 3 months
3. Continued anti-inflammatory medications
4. Removal via surgical excision
5. Phenol injection

## Correct option

**4. Removal via surgical excision**

## Answer explanation

The clinical presentation is consistent with an osteoid osteoma that has failed the first line of treatment with NSAIDs. The axial CT shows the lesion is adjacent to the spinal canal with a possible breach of the cortex to the canal. Therefore, the most appropriate treatment at this time would therefore be removal via surgical excision rather than radiofrequency ablation.

Osteoid osteomas of the spine are benign but painful lesions, sometimes associated with scoliosis, that are characterized by pain that is often worse at night and that is responsive to NSAIDs. Treatment therefore focuses on the management of symptoms and begins with NSAIDs. In ~50% of patients, the lesions "burn out" and become asymptomatic over time, though this can take several years. If nonoperative treatment fails then either surgical excision or CT-guided radiofrequency ablation is indicated depending on the location of the lesion. If the lesion is not in proximity to the spinal canal or neurologic elements, than RFA is indicated. If the lesion is close to the spinal canal, and there is concern for communication with the spinal canal or neural elements than surgical excision may be indicated.

*Hadjipavlou et al. published a case report comparing the effectiveness of two different minimally invasive surgical techniques for the management of osteoid osteoma of the spine.* **They found** that radiofrequency thermal ablation can be an effective and safe treatment of osteoid osteoma in the spine when the nidus is not adjacent to the neural elements.

*Wang et al. performed a retrospective review to determined the efficacy and safety of percutaneous radiofrequency ablation for spinal osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma.* **They concluded** that CT-guided percutaneous RFA is a safe and effective treatment for spinal osteoid osteomas and osteoblastomas, especially in cases with no neurological deficits and an intact cortical bone to the spinal canal. **They report** that cerebrospinal fluid around the lesion is an appropriate indication for percutaneous RFA.

*Volkmer et al. discussed the use of radiofrequency ablation for musculoskeletal tumors such as osteoid osteomas.* **They report** that osteoid osteomas are usually a self-limiting phenomenon and that the first line of treatment should be observation and NSAID administration. Relative indications for radiofrequency ablation of osteoid osteomas include failed medical management, painful scoliosis resulting from spinal lesions, or periarticular lesions with an increased risk of cartilage injury and premature degenerative disease.

Figures A and B are AP and lateral radiographs of the spine that demonstrate a mild scoliosis. Figure C is a bone scan showing intense uptake at the site of the osteoid osteoma. Figure D is a CT scan demonstrating the classic "target" lesion of an osteoid osteoma adjacent to the spinal canal. In this axial image there is concern for the integrity of the cortex to the spinal canal.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Bracing would be indicated for spinal deformity such as idiopathic scoliosis > 25° but is not required for a mild curvature.
Answer 2: The AP lumbar radiograph demonstrates spinal curvature < 25 degrees. Bracing is not required at this time and the curve may even improve with treatment of the osteoid osteoma.
Answer 3: Pain has persisted despite use of NSAIDs in the past, thus alternatives should be considered.
Answer 5: Ablation of osteoid osteomas with radiofrequency ablation and alcohol has been described but not with phenol.
