## Question

A 39-year-old female presents to your office with a persistently painful right shoulder and limited motion despite 4 months of physical therapy and a corticosteroid injection by her PCP. On exam there is 20 degrees of internal rotation and 50 degrees of external rotation with pain noted throughout the arc. She has full and symmetric rotator cuff strength with no tenderness over the AC joint. Radiographs and MRI are seen in Figures A-C. ## Options

1. Comprehensive arthroscopic release
2. Percutaneous core decompression
3. Resurfacing hemiarthroplasty
4. Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty
5. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

## Correct option

**3. Resurfacing hemiarthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient has humeral head avascular necrosis (AVN) which has not improved with conservative measures. Given the extent of chondral involvement and loss of sphericity, a resurfacing hemiarthroplasty would be of most benefit and requires the least bone removal.

Humeral head AVN is a common condition among sickle cell individuals, those on chronic steroids, and/or those with a long history of alcohol dependence. Treatment is dictated by the degree of symptoms and chondral surface involvement. Once the cartilage has collapsed and symptoms become debilitating, arthroplasty should be considered. While traditional, stemmed-hemiarthroplasties with or without glenoid arthroplasty (TSA) have been used, stemless designs are becoming more in favor with data showing reasonable survivability with preservation of the metaphysical bone stock.

*Harreld et al. provided a review of joint preservation options for humeral AVN.* In pre-collpase stages of AVN, core decompression can be performed in isolation or with arthroscopic assistance. Additionally, bone grafting may also be added to the subchondral void left following decompression. In post-collapse stages of AVN, arthroplasty should be pursued, with resurfacing being an optimal option if the underlying epiphyseal bone is sufficient.

*Levy and Copeland described an early series of 285 cementless humeral resurfacing arthroplasties.* While the indications for these varied, **they showed** exceptional improvement in range of motion, pain relief, and patient-reported outcomes. This data helped spur interest in humeral resurfacing as opposed to implants requiring more bone removal.

*Hawi et al. described a more recent case series of stemless humeral resurfacing using the Arthrex Eclipse implant system.* *At an average follow-up of 9 years,* **they showed** significant improvements in Constant score, strength, and range of motion, without instances of loosening or implant failures. This series shows promising results at intermediate follow-up, but long-term data is still needed.

Figures A and B are standard shoulder radiographs depicting collapse of the chondral surface of the humeral head and loss of sphericity. Figure C is a fluid-sensitive coronal MRI showing the complete separation of the ischemic osteochondral fragment, with edema within the metaphysis, which is very common and normal.
Illustration A demonstrates post-operative radiographs following a stemless humeral resurfacing arthroplasty. Illustration B shows the Cruess classification for humeral AVN. Note that Stage III represents chondral collapse, for which arthroplasty should be considered.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1- Arthroscopic management would be unlikely to yield any sustained benefit as the AVN will likely continue to progress.
Answer 2- The role of core decompression in pre-collapse stages of AVN is highly debated, but is equivocally contraindicated in post-collapse stages.
Answers 4 and 5- The glenoid shows little involvement, and placing a glenoid component carries the risk for loosening, which this patient's age puts her at very high risk.
