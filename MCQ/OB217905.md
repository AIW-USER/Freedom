## Question

A 16-year-old male presents as a trauma transfer 12 hours after an ATV rollover in the desert with a grossly contaminated injury to his right hand as seen in Figures A and B. The patient has no other injuries and is brought back emergently to the operating room for attempted revascularization of his small and ring fingers. The wound was thoroughly irrigated and debrided with sand and dirt found within the carpal tunnel. The ring finger digital arteries were successfully repaired, and the remaining bone stock stabilized. The digital arteries to the small finger show a ribbon sign. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

*Figure A*

![[q217905-b-radiographs-hand-ray-resection.jpg]]

## Options

1. Metacarpal phalangeal joint fusion
2. Open reduction percutaneous pinning alone
3. Ray resection of the small finger
4. Replantation of the small finger
5. Transposition of revascularized ring finger to small finger

## Correct option

**3. Ray resection of the small finger**

## Answer explanation

Replantation following a mixed avulsion/crush injury is problematic with revision amputation often being necessary. In this setting, a ray resection of the small finger is prudent given the zone of injury, segmental fractures, poor soft tissue envelope, and contamination of the wound.

The ability to perform revascularization is often technically challenging and may require vein grafting or other reconstructive efforts. The ribbon sign refers to the corkscrew appearance of the digital artery resulting from avulsing disruption of the vessel wall layers. A ribbon sign is a poor prognostic indicator for attempting primary repair of the vessel and would require segmental arterial resection with vein grafting if revascularization is pursued. Mangled limbs often preclude the ability to perform replantation and necessitate revision amputations. While ray resection results in decreased grip and pinch strength, it also allows for early return of hand function and would be the best option in this setting for this patient’s small finger.

*Blazar et al. reviewed the indications and outcomes of ray resections of the hand.* *They focus on operative techniques with leaving the metacarpal base in border digit ray resections while considering transposition of adjacent digits in central ray resections.* **They conclude** dexterity, function, patient satisfaction, and cosmetic results of ray resection are generally as good as or better than amputations at the proximal phalanx level.

*Gupta et al. reviewed the management of mangled hands and forearms.* *They stress the importance of adequate irrigation and debridement with broad-spectrum antibiotics to avoid deep wound infections.* *They prioritize vascular restoration, stable skeletal fixation, and provision of adequate skin coverage of the mangled extremity.* **They conclude** by acknowledging the limits of replantation and the necessity to perform secondary reconstructive efforts in many cases.

Figure A displays a mangled right hand with near complete amputation of the ring finger and complete amputation of the small finger with loss of web space. Figure B displays the same patient’s radiographic imaging with multiple metacarpal fractures and a segmental boney injury to the fifth digit.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Metacarpal phalangeal joint fusions are seldom utilized in the acute trauma setting and would not be appropriate when the proximal phalanx is dysvascularized.
Answer 2: Open reduction internal fixation alone would not address the vascular compromise to the digit.
Answer 4: While this is a child and replantation should be the goal, there is a reality behind the limitations of replantation procedures. Figure A shows the combined avulsion/crush injury with loss of webspace of the fifth digit. The wound was grossly contaminated with a risk of deep wound infection and the digital arteries showed the ribbon sign. Unfortunately, these collective injuries preclude the ability to perform replantation surgery.
Answer 5: Transposition of adjacent digits is often utilized when there is a ray resection of a central digit (middle finger or ring finger) in order to prevent the gap formation and scissoring that can occur between digits.
