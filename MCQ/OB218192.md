## Question

A 72-year-old female presents to your office having undergone cruciate retaining total knee arthroplasty (TKA) at an outside hospital 9 months ago. For the last several months, she has been having stiffness, pain anterolaterally, and sensations of patellar subluxation with flexion and extension of the knee. On exam, there is no posterior tibial sag or clunk with extension of the knee but there is a subtle J-sign. Radiographs are unrevealing and inflammatory labs are within normal limits. She undergoes a CT scan which leads to you recommending revision TKA. What was the most likely cause of TKA failure?

## Options

1. Elevation of joint line
2. Indolent infection
3. Osteolysis
4. Internal rotation of components
5. Patellar clunk

## Correct option

**4. Internal rotation of components**

## Answer explanation

This patient is having abnormalities of patellar tracking, which are likely secondary to internal rotation of the tibial and/or femoral components.

Patellar maltracking is one of the most common complications after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and the most common reason for a secondary surgery after TKA. Patellar tracking is effectively altered with any alterations in coronal plane alignment (medial/lateral placement of femur/tibia implants) or rotational alignment (femoral implant or tibial tray rotation). An increase in the Q-angle causes increased lateral pull on the patellar implant and causes it to subluxate or dislocate. This can lead to a "J-sign," where the patellar component subluxes laterally in extension and then reduces as the knee is flexed and the patellar component engages the trochlear groove. In order to ensure alignment is appropriate, the tibial and femoral components should be placed laterally and internal rotation of both components should be avoided. The tibial tray should be aligned with the medial 1/3 of the tibial tubercle in order to prevent excessive internal rotation of the component. The patellar button is the only implant that should be placed more medially, as this effectively lateralizes the patella and decreases the Q-angle.

*Nicoll and Rowley* **noted** that internal rotation of the tibial component is a major cause of pain after TKA. They used CT scans to evaluate the rotational alignment of 39 painful TKAs and 26 painless TKAs and noted the painful group had an average of 4.3 degrees internal tibial rotation vs. the painless group which was, on average, 2.2 degrees externally rotated. **They concluded** that over half of the patients in the painful TKA group had internal rotation malalignment of the femoral, tibial, or both prostheses.

*Young et al. looked at whether rotational malalignment was a cause of unexplained pain following TKA.* *They compared 71 patients with unexplained pain after TKA to 41 well-functioning patients based on CT scans.* Interestingly, in contrast to the prior study, **they noted** that there were no statistically significant differences in the degree of malrotation between the two groups with 59% of patients with pain having some degree of internal malrotation vs. 49% in the control group.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Elevation of the joint line may lead to mid-flexion instability due to abnormal ligamentous tension and implant stability. While it can cause periarticular pain, patellar instability would be unlikely.
Answer 2: Inflammatory labs are normal in this patient, indicating a low likelihood of infection.
Answer 3: With normal radiographs cited, and only being 9 months post-op, osteolysis would be an uncommon finding, especially with the advent of highly cross-linked polyethylene.
Answer 5: Patellar clunk does not usually occur in cruciate retaining total knees.
