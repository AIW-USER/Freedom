## Question

A 25-year-old woman presents to the clinic after sustaining a Grade II lateral ankle sprain. Although her pain should be improving, she reports worsening pain that is disproportionate to the injury. On examination, the foot is exquisitely tender to light touch, warm, mildly erythematous, and subtly diaphoretic compared to the contralateral foot. She is reluctant to move the ankle due to pain. You suspect she is in the early stages of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS). Given the suspicion of early-stage CRPS following a minor trauma, what is the most appropriate initial management step?

## Options

1. Initiate a trial of oral gabapentin and refer for pain management consultation for steroid injections
2. Order a Lumbar Sympathetic Block (LSB) to immediately interrupt sympathetic outflow
3. Prescribe a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) and restrict all physical therapy until pain subsides
4. Recommend a three-phase bone scan to confirm the diagnosis of CRPS
5. Refer for immediate physical therapy focusing on desensitization and active range of motion, combined with close, multidisciplinary follow-up

## Correct option

**5. Refer for immediate physical therapy focusing on desensitization and active range of motion, combined with close, multidisciplinary follow-up**

## Answer explanation

The most effective initial treatment for early CRPS is an aggressive, multidisciplinary approach centered on maximizing function through active physical therapy and desensitization.

Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) is a neuro-inflammatory disorder where prompt diagnosis and aggressive treatment are critical to preventing progression to a chronic, intractable stage. Since early CRPS does not typically have radiographic changes and is primarily a clinical diagnosis, the focus should be on immediate functional recovery. Physical therapy (PT) is the cornerstone of early management, emphasizing desensitization and maintaining an active range of motion to combat the stiffness and disuse cycle. Combining this with close follow-up with a specialist (e.g., pain management or rehabilitation) ensures multidisciplinary intervention is ready if the condition progresses.

Shah and Kirchner provided a review of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) relevant to foot and ankle injuries. They noted that early diagnosis is challenging but crucial, and stressed that immobilization and delayed diagnosis lead to poor outcomes. They concluded that early, aggressive physical therapy, focusing on range of motion and weight-bearing, is paramount to initial management.

*Hogan and Hurwitz reviewed the treatment principles for CRPS in the lower extremity.* **They noted** that the best results are achieved when intervention is initiated early with a multidisciplinary approach, including both physical therapy and pharmacological agents. **They concluded** that early mobilization and an aggressive, collaborative effort are essential to interrupting the cycle of pain and disuse associated with CRPS.

*Harden et al. released the 5th Edition of practical diagnostic and treatment guidelines for Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS).* **They specifically emphasized** that physical and occupational therapy, aimed at normalizing function and desensitization, is the initial cornerstone treatment for CRPS. **They concluded** that these non-pharmacological therapies are the critical first-line interventions to mitigate the long-term functional decline characteristic of CRPS.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: While appropriate later, medication is an adjunct to, not the primary focus of, immediate therapy.
Answer 2: This is a high-level, invasive procedure typically reserved for cases that fail initial conservative management or present more severely.
Answer 3: Immobilization and rest are contraindicated in CRPS and promote the progression of stiffness and disuse.
Answer 4: This test has low specificity and is not needed for immediate diagnosis or management, which should be based on clinical suspicion.
