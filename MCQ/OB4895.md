## Question

A 66-year-old Caucasian female presents to the ER with severe new low back pain after she slipped on the curb yesterday night. You see her 3 hours after admission, and two hours after she was given 5 mg of oxycodone. She states she had no back pain prior to the fall, and a CT scan of the abdomen obtained 2 months earlier for abdominal pain demonstrated no evidence of a fracture. Her past medical history is significant for hypertension and hyperlipidemia. On physical exam she has 5/5 strength in all major muscle groups, normal sensation, 1+ patellar and achilles reflexes, no clonus and a down-going Babinski sign. While it causes her significant pain, she is able to stand and walk to the exam room door and back. A lateral radiograph is seen in Figure A. What would be the most appropriate first line of treatment.

*Figure A*

## Options

1. Kyphoplasty
2. Thoracolumbar Custom Orthosis (clamshell brace)
3. Opioids for pain control with gradual activity such as walking with no restrictions
4. NSAID therapy alone, strict bed rest for 7 days, followed by structured physical therapy
5. Calcitonin therapy alone, strict bed rest for 7 days, followed by structured physical therapy

## Correct option

**3. Opioids for pain control with gradual activity such as walking with no restrictions**

## Answer explanation

The patient has an acute osteoporotic compression fracture without neurologic deficits. The patient is able to ambulate with opiates treatment. Therefore, the first line of treatment includes opioids for pain control with gradual activity such as walking with no restrictions.

Vertebral compression fractures are the most common fragility fracture, with over 700,000 occurring yearly in the United States. Treatment, including the role of calcitonin and kyphoplasty, has been controversial over the past decade. Medical management is the mainstay of treatment for these fractures and remains the first line of treatment in patients that are ambulate to ambulate short distances independently. If patients pain levels are so severe, they are unable to ambulate despite opiates therapy, kyphoplasty with cement augmentation may be indicated after a short trial of nonoperative treatment.

*Savage et al. in a review, report that kyphoplasty provides the added advantage of vertebral body expansion and correction of kyphosis prior to the injection of cement.* Kyphoplasty may also provide with pain relief for a longer duration. Complications of cement augmentation relate to cement extravasation, embolization of cement, and new fractures. Increased stiffness from cement augmentation leads to higher loads on adjacent segments and may increase the risk of fracture to these segments.

*Esses et al. authored the AAOS clinical practice guideline on the treatment of symptomatic osteoporotic spinal compression fractures.* The only 'strong' recommendation was against the use of vertebroplasty for treatment of these fractures. There was a moderate recommendation in favor of calcitonin for four weeks following diagnosis.

*Kallmes et al. performed a multi-center trial randomized controlled trial of 131 patients with one to three painful osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures to undergo either vertebroplasty or a simulated procedure (placebo).* **They found** no difference in improvements in pain and pain-related disability scores between the two groups at one month. At one month vertebroplasty does not improve clinical condition compared to placebo.

Figure A is a lateral lumbar spine radiograph which shows a compression fracture of L4.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: The AAOS Clinical practice guideline reports that Kyphoplasty may be an option for this patient, which is based on weak evidence. It is not however first line treatment in this setting.
Answer 2: A clamshell brace is not required for this injury, but can be used for comfort.
Answer 4: Strict bedrest is not required. Pain control can include a course of anti-inflammatory or opioid.
Answer 5: Calcitonin use is controversial, however the AAOS clinical practice guideline reports moderate evidence supporting its use for 4 weeks after acute injury. It may have a role in decreasing pain associated with the injury.
