## Question

A very functional 18-year-old male with cerebral palsy presents to your office for evaluation of his upper extremity. On examination, he has no active supination but maintains full passive supination. He is able to determine position and sensibility without visual input. His radiographs are unremarkable. Which muscle transfer would likely be of benefit in this patient?

## Options

1. Anconeus
2. Brachialis
3. Brachioradialis
4. Pronator teres
5. Supinator

## Correct option

**4. Pronator teres**

## Answer explanation

Transferring the pronator teres will allow this muscle to act primarily as a forearm supinator rather than as a pronator.

Upper extremity disorders in cerebral palsy are caused by spasticity and can take the form of shoulder, elbow, wrist, or hand contractures. Diagnosis is made clinically with restriction or limitations in movement of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, hand, or fingers, secondary to spasticity. Treatment can range from bracing to surgical soft tissue release or osteotomy depending on the severity of the deformity, limitations in the ability to maintain hygiene, and functional deficits. A pronator teres muscle transfer to an anterolateral position is indicated with a forearm pronation deformity.

*Cobeljic et al. reviewed the effects of three surgical procedures in the treatment of pronation deformities of the forearm in cerebral palsy patients; namely the transposition of pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis muscle; and rerouting of the pronator teres muscle with or without pronator quadratus muscle myotomy.* **They reported** that surgery is very effective in the management of pronation deformities of the forearm in patients with cerebral palsy. **They concluded** that isolated pronator teres rerouting is probably the most effective and simple technique.

*Van Heest et al. reviewed the surgical treatment of the upper extremity involved with cerebral palsy over a 25-year period.* *They reported on surgical treatments that were based on the following principles: soft tissue releases of deforming spastic muscles, tendon transfers to augment antagonistic activity, and joint stabilization.* **They concluded** that patients with adequate and good voluntary control had significantly greater improvement in functional use scores than those with poor voluntary control.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1-3,5: Transfer of the pronator teres will allow this muscle to act primarily as a supinator of the forearm rather than as a pronator, hopefully improving his activities of daily living. The other muscles listed would not be appropriate transfers for this patient to regain supination
