## Question

A 3-year-old child is referred by her pediatrician for a fixed flexion deformity of the bilateral little finger. She has been wearing splints for the last few months. Mother gives a history of slow progressive onset of the deformity and lack of history of trauma.
On examination there is a fixed flexion deformity of PIP joint of both little finger, asymmetrical. There is no swelling or pain. No concomitant deformities of DIP and MCP joint. There is no triggering. What is the diagnosis?

![[TO17141286-1.jpg]]

## Options

1. Clinodactyly
2. Dupuytren contracture
3. Congenital absence of extensor mechanism
4. Compatodactyly

## Correct option

**4. Compatodactyly**

## Answer explanation

The word "camptodactyly" is derived from a Greek term meaning "bent finger." It is commonly used to describe a nontraumatic flexion deformity of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint of one or several fingers.

The reported incidence of camptodactyly varies greatly, but generally it is felt to occur in less than 1 % of the population. In familial cases it usually follows a simple autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance with variable penetrance, but most cases are sporadic in occurrence.

Clinical findings

 The flexion deformity of camptodactyly may be present at birth or first noticed later in life

- a congenital type - in which the little finger is flexed from early life, and which affects boys and girls equally
- an acquired or adolescent type - which is more common and which is usually seen in female patients and appears gradually in the early teens

The deformity characteristically occurs at the PIP joint of the little finger. There is a high incidence of bilaterality, but the degree of contracture is not necessarily symmetrical. Other fingers may be affected occasionally, but the incidence of involvement and the degree of the flexion contracture usually is higher in the fingers on the ulnar side of the hand.

Etiology

The cause of camptodactyly is unknown, although a great variety of abnormalities have been invoked to explain this condition

One theory is due to abnormal tightness of the flexor digitorum superficialis or to "slow retraction" of the flexor tendons.

Anomalous tendons also have been felt to play a role, including an abnormal lumbrical insertion onto the flexor digitorum superficialis, abnormal shortening of flexor digitorum profundus and by faulty development of the dorsal extensor aponeurosis over the PIP joint.

Jones, Marmor, and Lankford attribute this deformity to "poor lumbrical muscles" which insert into structures in the lumbrical canal rather than into the extensor hood, and they liken it to an intrinsic minus digit

Smith and Kaplan have aptly summed up this progression of theories by stating that virtually every structure about the base of the finger has been implicated as a deforming factor in camptodactyly. With the variety of deforming factors that have been reported, it is likely that camptodactyly represents a final common expression of flexion-extension imbalance at the PIP joint due to several potential causes.

Differential diagnosis

Camptodactyly may present as an isolated finding or may be only one part of a multisystem syndrome. When present as an isolated finding, it must be differentiated from a boutonniere deformity, Dupuytren's contracture, a trigger digit, and congenital absence of the extensor mechanism

Boutonniere deformity - The slow progressive onset, the lack of history of trauma, the absence of swelling or pain, or of any hyperextension deformity of the distal interphalangeal joint all help to exclude a boutonniere deformity

Dupuytren’s disease - Dupuytren's disease can be ruled out by lack of concomitant flexion contracture of the MCP joint and the absence of palmar skin dimpling and palmar fascial involvement.

Trigger digit and congenital absence of the extensor mechanism - Trigger digits and congenital absence of the extrinsic extensor mechanism primarily affect the MCP joint rather than the PIP joint.

Treatment

For mild cases, nonoperative measures such as active exercises, dynamic splints, serial plaster casts, and passive stretching have been recommended.

Surgical treatment has been directed at rebalancing the flexion-extension forces at the PIP joint. These include release of various volar deforming factors and occasionally reinforcement of the extensor mechanism.

For contracture of the flexor digitorum superficialis - tenotomy of the flexor digitorum superficialis is required.

For anomalous lumbricals, recommended intervention is excision of the anomalous lumbrical or reinsertion into the lateral band when possible, Z-plasties of the volar skin and active post-operative splinting.

For flexor-extensor imbalance, combination of volar release with transfer of the divided superficialis through the lumbrical canal to the dorsal side of the finger where it is sutured into the extensor aponeurosis. And in extreme cases lengthening of the profundus tendon and capsulotomy of the PIP joint are done.

Clinodactyly - Camptodactyly should not be confused with a deviation of the PIP joint in a radioulnar plane which is termed "clinodactyly".

Congenital absence of the extensor mechanism - Trigger digits and congenital absence of the extrinsic extensor mechanism primarily affect the MCP joint rather than the PIP joint

Answer: 4
