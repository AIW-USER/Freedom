## Question

An 17-year-old football player is brought to the emergency room following a tackling injury 90 minutes prior. On arrival to the emergency room he is alert and oriented. On physical exam he has tenderness in his posterior midline cervical spine. He reports intact sensation in his upper and lower extremities. He motor exam shows he has some motion in his arms and legs, but is unable to lift his legs or arms against gravity. His bulbocavernosus reflex is absent. Which of the following statements is true regarding the administering a dose of Methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg bolus followed by a 5.4 mg/kg/hr infusion x 24 hours?

## Options

1. It is not supported by current literature - recent studies have shown that while there are no associated complications, there is not significant benefit.
2. It is not supported by current literature - recent studies have shown an increased risk of complications with no clear evidence of benefits
3. It is supported by current literature - recent studies have demonstrated few harmful side effects, which are outweighed by the large potential benefits
4. It is supported by current literature - recent studies have shown only a mild benefit, but due to the high safety profile, the benefits still outweigh the risks
5. There is no clear evidence to recommend for or against the administration of Methylprednisolone

## Correct option

**2. It is not supported by current literature - recent studies have shown an increased risk of complications with no clear evidence of benefits**

## Answer explanation

The patient presents with a possible acute spinal cord injury (SCI) and evidence of spinal shock. While the use of Methylprednisolone Sodium Succinate (MPSS) has been controversial, the current guidelines of the American Association of Neurological Surgeons advise that MPSS not be used for the treatment of acute SCI due to lack of evidence of clinical benefit, and evidence of potential complications of its use.

The National Acute Spinal Cord Injury Study (NASCIS) II and III trials were performed to evaluate the utility of MPSS infusion for acute traumatic SCI. These randomized, double-blinded, placebo controlled studies demonstrated early evidence of improved neurologic recovery with the use of MPSS if started within 8 hours of injury. Critical appraisal of these studies however called the results into question due to issues with their methodology. Current evidence does not support the use of MPSS.

*Gupta et al. review advances in the treatment of spinal cord injuries and controversies regarding the use of MPSS in spinal cord injury.* The NASCIS trials were initially promising, however recent studies have been critical of the results.

*Hurlbert et. al. report the current guidelines and supporting literature for phamarcological treatment of acute SCI.* **They report** there is no class I or II evidence supporting the use of MPSS in acute SCI, and advise against its use due to the lack of evidence of clinical benefit and the potential for harm. **They report** the increasing risk of complications of MPSS administration include pneumonia, ventilated days, and length of stay (among others).

*Evaniew et. al. performed a propensity score-matched cohort study with data from a multi-center registry evaluating the NASCIS-II regimen of MPSS use for treatment of acute spinal cord injury.* Importantly, in their analysis they controlled for anatomic level and severity of injury. **They found** that MPSS did not improve motor recovery in this cohort, and that those who did receive an infusion had higher rate of total complications including urinary tract infections, decubitus ulcers, and pneumonias. The authors were in agreement with guidelines against routine administration of MPSS in acute SCI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: There is good evidence of an increase in infectious complications after MPSS use as well as increasing length of stay and number of ventilated days.
Answer 3-5: Based on current evidence, MPSS administration should be avoided.
