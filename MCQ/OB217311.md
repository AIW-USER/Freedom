## Question

A 25-year-old male was involved in a motor vehicle collision where he sustained a closed humeral shaft fracture. At presentation, he was noted to have a radial nerve palsy. He underwent open reduction and internal fixation the following morning based on a discussion of risks and benefits regarding nonoperative and operative management. At his 12-month follow-up appointment, he continues to complain of the inability to extend his wrist or fingers. He is particularly bothered by his lack of wrist extension, as it is limiting his job duties. What is the next best step in management with regards to improving his wrist motion and function?

## Options

1. Physical therapy
2. Radiocarpal arthrodesis
3. Pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis transfer
4. Pronator teres to extensor digitorum communis transfer
5. Flexor carpi ulnaris to extensor digitorum communis transfer

## Correct option

**3. Pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis transfer**

## Answer explanation

In order to restore wrist extension in cases of persistent radial nerve palsy, the most appropriate strategy is to perform a pronator teres (PT) to extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon transfer.

Radial nerve palsy occurs commonly in association with humeral shaft fractures, with distal third spiral patterns more likely to be associated (up to 20%). Radial nerve injury also occurs more frequently in open fractures, with a tendency for more severe, lasting consequences. The majority (85-90%) of associated radial nerve injuries improve over the course of 3 months, with full recovery at an average of 6 months. If no improvement is noted, an EMG/NCS can be obtained at 2-3 months to further characterize the injury. Based on these findings, surgical exploration can be performed or further observation may be undertaken. If clinical improvement continues to lag, tendon transfers can be performed, typically at or after 1 year. To improve wrist extension, the PT is transferred to the ECRB, as it most closely imitates its line of pull. To correct thumb extension, the palmaris longus (PL) can be transferred to the extensor pollicis longus (EPL) and to correct finger extension the flexor carpi radialis (FCR)/flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) can be transferred to the extensor digitorum communis (EDC).

*Bishop et al. describe management strategies for humeral shaft fractures with an associated radial nerve palsy.* Using a decision tree analysis, **they note** that initial observation is preferred in these cases over immediate surgical intervention. In cases where there is a low initial likelihood of regaining radial nerve function, the patient may benefit from more expedient surgical intervention.

*Sammer et al. review the principles of tendon transfers in the forearm and hand after a radial nerve palsy.* *They discuss the initial indications and appropriate transfer couples at length.* Ultimately, **they recommend** the following transfers: wrist extension (PT to ECRB), finger extension (FCR to EDC), and thumb extension (PL to EPL).

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The patient lacks wrist extension due to a persistent radial nerve palsy, therefore, physical therapy will not help recover this function.
Answer 2: Radiocarpal arthrodesis may position the wrist more appropriately but would not be most appropriate initially for trying to improve his motion and function as the question asks.
Answer 4: PT to EDC transfer is not used, as the PT is usually transferred to the ECRB to improve wrist extension and the FCR is transferred to the EDC to improve finger extension.
Answer 5: FCU to EDC transfer is a described technique, but addresses finger, not wrist extension. Additionally, FCR to EDC transfer is preferred because FCU transfer can cause a profound loss in ulnar deviation power with the wrist flexed.
