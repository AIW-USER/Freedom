## Question

A 24-year-old male presents for evaluation of right shoulder weakness for the past 3 weeks. He reports that the pain is deep and most noticeable in the posterolateral shoulder. He has weakness with both forward elevation and external rotation at 0 degrees of abduction. What is the most common location at which a compressive neuropathy would result in these signs and symptoms?

## Correct option

**1. A**

## Answer explanation

The patient's examination shows weakness to both external rotation and forward flexion, which is concerning for a compressive neuropathy of the suprascapular nerve.

Suprascapular nerve compression at the suprascapular notch will lead to weakness of both supraspinatus and infraspinatus, while entrapment at the spinoglenoid notch will result in weakness of the infraspinatus only. The suprascapular nerve (C5,C6) emerges off superior trunk (C5,C6) of brachial plexus and travels across posterior triangle of the neck to the scapula and innervates both the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles. Ganglion cysts (often associated with labral tears), transverse scapular ligament thickening, or fracture callus can all result in nerve compression. As in this patient, presentation often includes symptoms of deep, diffuse, posterolateral shoulder pain. An MRI is important to identify compressive masses of paralabral cysts. A trial of activity modification and organized shoulder rehab program should be sought initially, and if these fail then surgical nerve decompression of the suprascapular notch is indicated.

*Momaya et al. systematically reviewed suprascapular neuropathy.* **They reported** that the most common symptom was deep, posterior shoulder pain (98%), with a mean duration of symptoms before decompression of 19 months. **They found** that 92% of athletes were able to return to sport following decompression. **They concluded** that surgical decompression in the setting of suprascapular neuropathy leads to satisfactory outcomes and low complications rates.

*Schroeder et al. systematically reviewed whether cyst management is necessary in the setting of SLAP lesions with concomitant paralabral ganglion cysts.* *They reported on 66 patients treated with repair alone and 94 patients with additional cyst decompression or excision.* **They concluded** that both groups have excellent results. The results do not show any advantages from performing decompression.
