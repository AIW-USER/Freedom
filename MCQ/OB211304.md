## Question

A 30-year-old active-duty woman presents to your clinic with right knee pain and without any history of trauma. She is an avid runner and is part of the Army 10-miler team. Her pain is located directly over her medial femoral condyle (MFC). Additionally, she describes intermittent episodes of an inability to fully extend her knee. Her clinical mechanical alignment, patellar tracking, meniscal examination, and ligamentous examination are all equivocal on physical examination. Your diagnostic imaging workup with pertinent findings is represented in Figures A through E. You perform a diagnostic arthroscopy to confirm that the pathology is isolated to the medial compartment and you note that there is not any evidence of ligamentous or meniscal pathology. Based on your findings which definitive surgical options would you recommend to the patient?

## Options

1. Proximal tibia valgus osteotomy, microfracture to the MFC, and removal of loose osteochondral bodies
2. Distal femur varus osteotomy and autologous chondrocyte implantation to the MFC
3. Osteochondral autograft transplantation to the MFC
4. Removal of loose osteochondral bodies and osteochondral allograft transplantation to the MFC
5. Patellofemoral realignment osteotomy, removal of loose osteochondral bodies, and autologous chondrocyte implantation to the MFC

## Correct option

**4. Removal of loose osteochondral bodies and osteochondral allograft transplantation to the MFC**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an osteochondral defect of the right knee MFC measuring 1.7 cm x 1.3 cm = 2.21 cm2 with loose osteochondral bodies. Surgical treatment should include removal of the loose osteochondral bodies and reconstruction of the MFC using osteochondral allograft transplantation due to the presence of the osseous defect and the size of the defect (>2 cm2)

The root cause of the patient’s pathology is due to her increased intensity and frequency of running which resulted in focal damage to the articular cartilage and underlying bone of the MFC. The volume and thickness of articular cartilage increase with weight-bearing activity in a dose-response linear relationship. If the intensity and/or the frequency of the weight-bearing activity travels beyond a certain threshold, then there can be disruption of osteochondral homeostasis. This leads to osteochondral changes as a result of chondropenia, chondrocyte apoptosis, subchondral osseous bruising, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral osseous fracture, and/or necrosis.

*Murray et al. performed a review of the management of articular cartilage injuries in athletes.* *The authors identify that the meniscal, ligamentous, and mechanical alignment must be without pathology or corrected at the time of or prior to addressing any focal articular cartilage defects.* **They recommend** for full-thickness articular cartilage defects of the tibia or femur:
• <1 cm2, no osseous defect: Microfracture
• <1 cm2, osseous defect: Osteochondral Autograft Transplantation
• 1-2 cm2, no osseous defect: Microfracture or Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation
• 1-2 cm2, osseous defect: Osteochondral Autograft Transplantation
• >2 cm2, osteochondral defect or avascular necrosis: Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation or Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation with bone grafting

*Yanke et al. completed a comprehensive clinical and surgical review for chondral injuries specific to the patellofemoral joint.* Adding to the difficulty of addressing cartilage injuries, the patellofemoral joint adds another layer of complexity due to its inherent and unique biomechanical loading environment and articular topography. Osteochondral allograft transplantation is an option in the patellofemoral joint for unipolar lesions with bone loss in young patients albeit with less favorable outcomes when compared to its use in the tibiofemoral joint. Improved surgical outcomes for chondral injuries in the knee are dependent on low BMI, pain for <1 year, objective effusions, and no prior surgery.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: A realignment osteotomy is not indicated because of the neutral mechanical axis with it passing between the medial and lateral tibial spines. In the setting of an osseous defect and surface >1 cm2, microfracture is not indicated.
Answer 2: A realignment osteotomy is not indicated because of the neutral mechanical axis with it passing between the medial and lateral tibial spines. In the setting of an osseous defect of this size, autologous chondrocyte implantation would require the addition of bone grafting. This option is less predictable and has a longer recovery than osteochondral allograft transplantation.
Answer 3: In the presence of a full-thickness cartilage lesion of the tibiofemoral joint with osseous involvement, osteochondral autograft transplantation is indicated for lesions <2 cm2. Some data supports performing an osteochondral autograft transplantation for lesions 2-4 cm2; however, for larger lesions, this is contraindicated due to donor site morbidity. Regardless, the loose osteochondral bodies need to be removed.
Answer 5: The patient does not have any evidence of patellofemoral pathology (malalignment, instability, or focal chondral defect) therefore a patellofemoral realignment osteotomy is not indicated. In the setting of an osseous defect of this size, autologous chondrocyte implantation would require the addition of bone grafting.
