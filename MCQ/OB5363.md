## Question

A 6-year-old girl sustains a fall from a slide and presents with the injury pictured in Figure A. She undergoes successful closed reduction and application of a long arm cast in the emergency room. At her follow up appointment 5 days later, radiographs demonstrate loss of ulnar reduction and radiocapitellar joint dislocation. You discuss surgery with her parents. What is the best operative plan for this patient?

![[q5363-a-fx.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Open radiocapitellar joint reduction, annular ligament repair, closed reduction of ulna fracture, long arm cast in supination.
2. Open radiocapitellar joint reduction, annular ligament repair, lateral collateral ligament repair, closed reduction of ulna fracture, long arm cast in neutral.
3. Reduction and internal fixation of ulna fracture, closed radiocapitellar joint reduction, long arm cast in supination.
4. Reduction and internal fixation of ulna fracture, open radiocapitellar joint reduction, annular ligament repair, long arm cast in neutral.
5. Reduction and internal fixation of ulna fracture, open radiocapitellar joint reduction, annular ligament repair, lateral collateral ligament repair, long arm cast in supination.

## Correct option

**3. Reduction and internal fixation of ulna fracture, closed radiocapitellar joint reduction, long arm cast in supination.**

## Answer explanation

This patient has a Bado type I Monteggia fracture that has failed nonoperative management. She would benefit most from reduction and surgical fixation of the ulna fracture with closed reduction of the radiocapitellar joint and immobilization in supination. Open reduction of the radiocapitellar joint and/or ligamentous repair is not often necessary.

Successful management of acute Monteggia fractures hinges on the restoration of ulnar length and alignment. Those with incomplete ulna fractures (buckle or greenstick) are length stable and often successfully treated with closed reduction alone. Conversely, those with complete ulna fractures are at risk for loss of radiocapitellar reduction. These complete fractures require surgical stabilization to maintain ulna length and radiocapitellar alignment.

*Nakamura et al evaluated 22 children with a missed radial head dislocation after a Monteggia fracture.* *All patients underwent open radial head reduction, posterior bending elongation ulnar osteotomy and annular ligament reconstruction.* Better clinical and radiographic outcomes were seen in patients less than 12 years of age or within 3 years post-injury.

*Tan et al surgically explored the radial heads of 35 children with type I and III Monteggia fractures treated operatively.* *The annular ligament was intact in all cases, while the radiocapitellar capsule had been avulsed from the radial neck in 18 cases.* Intraoperative angulation of the ulna fracture at increasing degrees resulted in distal subluxation of the radial head from the annular ligament, **highlighting** the important of reestablishing ulnar length and alignment for radiocapitellar stability.

Incorrect Responses:
Answers 1, 2, 4, 5: The ulna fracture should be surgically stabilized to maintain length and alignment. Open reduction of the radiocapitellar joint and ligamentous repair is rarely needed in pediatric patients.
