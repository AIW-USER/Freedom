## Question

A 14-year-old male presents with a progressive, rigid thoracic kyphosis. He complains of an unattractive posture and mild back pain. A physical exam reveals a rigid kyphosis that does not correct with hyperextension. Standing lateral radiographs show a thoracic kyphosis measuring 90 degrees, with endplate irregularities and disc space narrowing at multiple adjacent levels from T7 to T11. He is at skeletal maturity, with a Risser stage 4. Given this patient's presentation and radiographic findings, what is the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Bracing with a thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO) for 1 year
2. Close observation with serial radiographs every 6 months
3. Referral to physical therapy for core strengthening and posture exercises
4. Surgical treatment with posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation
5. Vertebral body tethering (VBT)

## Correct option

**4. Surgical treatment with posterior spinal fusion and instrumentation**

## Answer explanation

A rigid Scheuermann kyphosis greater than 75 degrees in a skeletally mature patient is a definitive surgical indication due to cosmetic deformity and back pain (Answer 4).

The patient has a rigid Scheuermann kyphosis with a Cobb angle of 90 degrees, which exceeds the operative threshold. For skeletally mature patients with large, rigid deformities causing pain or cosmetic concerns, non-operative treatments like observation, bracing, and physical therapy are generally ineffective. The definitive treatment is posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation, which provides excellent correction and a durable long-term outcome. This approach addresses the underlying deformity and its associated symptoms, offering a predictable solution.

*Debnath et al. evaluated the long-term outcomes of surgical treatment for Scheuermann's kyphosis.* **They noted** that surgical correction with posterior-only approaches using pedicle screw instrumentation resulted in excellent and sustained improvements in kyphosis angle and quality of life. **They concluded** that surgery is a highly effective treatment for severe Scheuermann's kyphosis with lasting benefits.

*Horn et al. studied trends in the treatment of Scheuermann kyphosis over a 10-year period.* **They noted** a significant increase in the use of posterior-only approaches and instrumentation for surgical correction, reflecting a shift away from combined anterior-posterior procedures. **They concluded** that a posterior-only fusion with instrumentation has become the dominant surgical method for treating Scheuermann kyphosis.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Bracing is not effective in a skeletally mature patient with a rigid, high-magnitude kyphosis.
Answer 2: Observation is inappropriate given the magnitude of the curve and the patient's symptomatic status.
Answer 3: Physical therapy is a part of non-operative management and is not effective for a rigid, severe deformity at this stage.
Answer 5: VBT is a growth-sparing technique and is not indicated in a skeletally mature patient with no remaining spinal growth.
