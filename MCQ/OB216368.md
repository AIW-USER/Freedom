## Question

A 62-year-old male presents with a seven week history of right knee pain and swelling. He underwent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) 14 years ago, and reports he had been doing well until the past seven weeks. He denies antecedent trauma, recent illness, fevers, malaise, or paresthesias. On exam, he is afebrile, well-appearing, and non-toxic. He tolerates nearly full ROM but notes discomfort with terminal flexion and extension. Serum labs demonstrate a CRP of 15 mg/L, ESR of 35 mm/hr, and WBC count of 12,000. Arthrocentesis is performed and demonstrates a synovial WBC count of 4,200, and 83% PMN. Gram stain, culture, and synovial alpha defensin are pending. Given the patients most likely diagnosis, what is the next best step in management?

## Options

1. Perform a CT scan
2. Perform a bone scan
3. Treat with 6 weeks of antibiotic therapy
4. Trial of physical therapy and anti-inflammatory medication
5. Plan for two-stage revision arthroplasty

## Correct option

**5. Plan for two-stage revision arthroplasty**

## Answer explanation

This patient presents with multiple laboratory markers that suggest prosthetic joint infection (PJI) including elevated serum CRP (>10), ESR (>30), elevated synovial WBC count (WBC > 3,000), and elevated PMN's (>80%). The next best step in management would be to perform stage one of a two-stage revision arthroplasty.

Prosthetic joint infections occur in approximately 1-2% of primary total knee arthroplasties. Patients with a TKA who present with pain and swelling should be worked up for infection. Standard workup includes radiographs, serum laboratory markers (e.g. CRP and ESR), and arthrocentesis. Two-stage replacement arthroplasty is the gold standard for prosthetic joint infection that occurs more than 4-6 weeks after the index surgery.

*In 2018, Parvizi et al. created new criteria in order to better define periprosthetic hip and knee infection.* *They generated a scoring system, as an adjunct to the MSIS criteria, to help with the diagnosis of PJI as well as guide treatment.* This includes major criteria, minor criteria, and inconclusive criteria. A pre-operative score of 0-1 is considered not infected, 2-5 is inconclusive, and 6 or greater is considered infected. When compared to the MSIS criteria, this new criteria has a higher sensitivity (98% vs. 79%) and similar specificity of 99.5%.

*Lee et al. performed a systematic review of synovial fluid analysis to determine the diagnostic utility and accuracy of various biomarkers for PJI.* **They found** that leukocyte count, PMN%, CRP, alpha defensin, LE, IL-6, and IL-8 all demonstrated a sensitivity and specificity >80% and >90%, respectively, in diagnosing PJI. **They also found** that alpha defensin had the highest DOR in diagnosing PJI.

*Schinsky et al. evaluated the utility of laboratory test in determining the presence of prosthetic joint infection, particularly synovial WBC.* **They found** a synovial WBC count level of 3000 cells/microliter yielded the highest combined sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV compared to other synovial WBC levels. **They determined** a synovial WBC of 3000 cells/microliter was a highly accurate perioperative test, especially when combined with a serum ESR >30 and CRP >10.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: CT scan is not routinely indicated in the diagnosis and management of PJI but may be useful in the setting of periprosthetic fracture around a TKA.
Answer 2: Bone scan is not necessary to aid in diagnosis and management of PJI but may be beneficial to supplement radiographs in diagnosing aseptic loosening .
Answer 3: Given the positive serum and synovial biomarkers, this patient's management should include revision surgery in addition to antibiotic therapy.
Answer 4: This patient's findings are consistent with PJI and should undergo two-stage arthroplasty.
