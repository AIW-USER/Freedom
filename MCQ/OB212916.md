## Question

An 8-year-old sustains the injury shown in Figure A after falling downstairs. He is able to cross his fingers, flex and extend the IP joint of his thumb, and has intact sensation. His fingers have a brisk capillary refill but there is no palpable or dopplerable pulse. Injury films are shown in Figure A. This patient should undergo:

![[q212916-a-screen-shot-2019-04-13-at-1-30-27-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Emergent vascular exploration
2. Open reduction and internal fixation
3. Close monitoring for compartment syndrome perioperatively and urgent surgery
4. Closed reduction and casting
5. Delayed surgical intervention to allow for soft tissue rest

## Correct option

**3. Close monitoring for compartment syndrome perioperatively and urgent surgery**

## Answer explanation

Patients with a "floating elbow," or concomitant fractures both the humerus and forearm, have been found to have as high as a 33% risk of developing compartment syndrome within the perioperative period. As a result, this patient should be monitored closely for acute compartment syndrome and taken urgently for attempted closed reduction and percutaneous fixation of one of both of these injuries.

Floating elbow injuries often result from higher-energy mechanisms than those seen with isolated supraconylar of both-bone forearm fractures. In addition to an increased risk for compartment syndrome, these patients have been found to have a greater inclidence of associated neurologic injury as well as loss of reduction due to the inherent instability of the contiguous fractures. Therefore a thorough neurologic exam is required at the time of evaluation. These injuries require close monitoring and often surgical fixation of at least one if not both injuries.

*Blakemore et al. reviewed 33 patients with ipsilateral supracondylar humerus and forearm fractures.* **The authors found** that overall, 7% of patients developed acute compartment syndrome, necessitating emergent surgical management. However, among patients in this study with displaced forearm fractures in association with a supracondylar humerus fracture, one-third went on to developed compartment syndrome. **They advocated** for increased vigilance with these patients.

*Livingston et al. review the diagnosis of pediatric acute compartment syndrome (PACS).* **They note** that the presentation in a child is different than that of adult and recommend the 3 A's in evaluation (ie, anxiety, agitation, analgesic requirement). **They noted** that overall, outcomes after fasciotomy in children tended to be excellent; however, poor outcomes were diagnostic delays secondary to unfamiliar clinical scenarios.

Figure A shows injury radiographs of a skeletally immature patient with an ipsilateral angulated both-bone forearm fracture and displaced supracondylar humerus fracture. Illustration A shows postoperative radiographs of a patient who underwent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning for this floating elbow injury.

Incorrect answers:
Answer 1: Emergent vascular exploration is unnecessary in the setting of a patient with a displaced supracondylar humerus fracture and a pink, pulseless hand.
Answer 2: An attempt should be made a closed reduction and percutaneous fixation prior to open reduction.
Answer 4: Closed reduction and casting may be implemented in Gartland type I and some type II supracondylar humerus fractures but is not the optimal treatment for a type III supracondylar humerus fracture.
Answer 5: This patient should undergo operative intervention on an urgent basis. Soft tissue swelling is not a contraindication to acute surgical management of this injury.
