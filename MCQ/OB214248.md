## Question

A 78-year-old male presents to the emergency department with the isolated injury shown in Figure A after falling off a curb. There are no breaks in the skin or blister formation. His medical history consists of insulin-dependent diabetes, asthma, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. He is admitted for pre-operative work-up and pain control. Initial vitals are within normal limits and his labs show hemoglobin of 13.4 g/dL and creatinine of 0.79 mg/dL. His electrocardiogram does not show evidence of ischemia. What is the best next step in management?

![[q214248-a-bimal.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Obtain brain naturetic peptide levels
2. Proceed with surgical fixation
3. Perform an echocardiogram
4. Pharmacologic cardiac stress test
5. Proceed with surgical fixation using only a neuraxial anesthetic

## Correct option

**2. Proceed with surgical fixation**

## Answer explanation

This patient has an isolated extremity injury requiring orthopaedic intervention. He has no EKG evidence of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and is at low risk for a cardiac event, thus no further pre-operative workup is necessary at this time.

Peri-operative major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) are devastating and often avoidable. Much effort has been spent at standardizing pre-operative cardiac evaluation to optimize patient cardiac status. The Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) and NSQIP surgical risk were formulated to stratify patients into high- or low- risk cohorts. These provide a crude risk percentage for cardiac events and deem whether further cardiac testing is warranted.

*Fleisher et al. formulated the ACC/AHA guidelines for cardiac risk management for non-cardiac procedures.* This states that in the low-risk patients (<1% RCRI), no further testing is needed and surgery may proceed. For those high-risk patients (>1% RCRI), further assessment of functional capacity using the Duke Activity Status Index should be performed. For those with poor functional capacity, a discussion should be held on the merits of going forward with surgery versus further cardiac work-up with a stress test.

Figure A is an AP radiograph of an unstable, bimalleolar ankle fracture. Illustration A shows the variables and associated risk percentages that comprise the RCRI. Illustration B is the ACC/AHA guidelines showing a stepwise approach for preoperative cardiac assessment.

Incorrect Answers;
Answers 1 and 3- Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels and echocardiograms are warranted if the patient has signs/symptoms of congestive heart failure.
Answer 4- Cardiac stress testing should be done if this patient was at elevated risk for MACE and a very poor functional capacity.
Answer 5- While neuraxial anesthesia is advantageous in most circumstances, general anesthetics are safe for patients at low-risk for MACEs.
