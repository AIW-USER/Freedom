## Question

A patient has come with an AV malformation located in frontal lobe of brain. What option will you offer as treatment

## Options

1. Embolization
2. Stereotactic surgery
3. Embolization + stereotactic surgery
4. All

## Correct option

**4. All**

## Answer explanation

Ans: D

EXPLANATION: -

ARTERIOVENOUS MALFORMATION [AVM]

 AVM is an abnormal tangle of dilated arteries and veins with dysplastic vessels. Arterial blood flows directly between them with no capillary bed and no intervening neural parenchyma in nidus. These usually are congenital lesions with a lifelong risk of bleeding of ≈ 2–4% per year.
 AVMs are medium-to-high pressure and high-flow
 The average age of patients diagnosed with AVMs is ≈ 33 yrs, which is ≈ 10 yrs younger than aneurysms
 Presentation: Brain hemorrhage (most common) - 50%, seizures from mass effect, ischemia-by steal, focal neurologic deficits, increased ICP etc. Peak age for hemorrhage is between 15–20 yrs. An estimate is 10% mortality and 30–50% morbidity (neurological deficit) from each bleed.
 Small AVMs tend to present more often as hemorrhage than do large ones. Larger AVMs presented as seizure more often simply because their size made them more likely to involve the cortex. However, small AVMs are now thought to have much higher pressure in the feeding arteries. Conclusion: small AVMs are more lethal than larger ones.
 Hemorrhage rate is based on nidus depth, venous drainage and prior bleed.
o S-M grade 1–3: annual risk of hemorrhage is 3.5%.
o S-M grade 4–5: annual risk of hemorrhage is 2.5%.
 7 % of patients with AVMs have aneurysms. 75% of these are located on major feeding artery.
 The Spetzler-Martin arteriovenous malformation (AVM) grading system allocates points for various angiographic features of intracranial arteriovenous malformations to give a score that predicts the morbidity/mortality risk of surgery.
Grading: The grading system requires correlation between CT (site of the nidus) or MRI scans (size and site of the nidus) with cerebral angiography (size of the nidus and pattern of venous drainage; the size being defined by the largest diameter of the nidus on angiography). The grade (denoted in Roman numerals) is equal to the sum of points in 3 categories as mentioned below, for a minimum of grade I and maximum of grade V (with a special designation of grade VI). A special category, grade VI, is defined as "inoperable," in which surgical resection would almost certainly cause total disability or death.
Three categories (for grading)
o Size of nidus: small (<3 cm) = 1, medium (3–6 cm) = 2, large (>6 cm) = 3
o Eloquence of adjacent brain: non-eloquent = 0. eloquent = 1
o Venous drainage: superficial veins only = 0, deep veins = 1

Evaluation:
 CT
 MRI - Characteristics of AVM on MRI:
 1. flow void on T1WI or T2WI within the AV
 2. feeding arteries
 3. draining veins
4. significant edema around the lesion may indicate a tumor that has bled rather than an AVM
5. gradient echo sequences (GRASS…) help demonstrate surrounding hemosiderin which suggests a previous significant hemorrhage
6. a complete ring of low density (due to hemosiderin) surrounding the lesion suggests AVM over neoplasm

 Angiography - Characteristics of AVM on angiography:
 1. tangle of vessels
 2. large feeding artery
 3. large draining veins
4. draining veins are visualized in the same images as arteries (arterial phase)

 Spetzler has published a 3-tiered management recommendation scheme as follows:
- Class A (S-M Grade I & II): surgical resection
- Class B (S-M Grade III): multimodality treatment
- Class C (S-M Grade IV & V): follow clinically and repeat angiogram every 5 years. Treatment only for progressive neurologic deficit, steal-related symptoms, or aneurysms identified on surveillance angiograms
Treatment
 Options and some pros and cons of each include:
1). Surgery: the treatment of choice for AVMs. When surgical risk is unacceptably high, alternative procedures (e.g. SRS) may be an option
a) pros: eliminates risk of bleeding almost immediately. Seizure control improves
 b) cons: invasive, risk of surgery, cost.

2). Radiation treatment
a) conventional radiation: effective in ≈ 20% or less of cases. Therefore, not considered effective therapy
b) stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS): accepted for some small (≤ 2.5–3 cm nidus), deep AVMs.
pros: done as an outpatient, non-invasive, gradual reduction of AVM flow, no recovery period
cons: takes 1–3 years to work (latency period). During that time there is a risk of bleeding, limited to lesions with nidus ≤3 cm.

3). Endovascular techniques: e.g. embolization
a) pros: facilitates surgery
b) cons: sometimes inadequate by itself to permanently obliterate AVMs, induces acute hemodynamic changes, may require multiple procedures, embolization prior to SRS reduces the obliteration rate from 70% (without embolization) to 47% (with embolization) .
4). Combination techniques: e.g. embolization to shrink nidus then stereotactic radiosurgery.

 When satisfactory complete angiographic obliteration of an AVM has been accomplished, recommended follow-up is with catheter angiogram (not CTA or MRA) at 1 & 5 years post treatment.
 Basic tenets of AVM surgery: -
1. wide exposure
2. occlude feeding (terminal) arteries before draining veins (lesions with a single draining vein can become impossible to deal with if premature blockage of the draining vein occurs, e.g. by kinking, coagulation).
3. excision of whole nidus is necessary to protect against rebleeding (occluding feeding arteries is not adequate)
4. identify and spare vessels of passage and adjacent (uninvolved) arteries
5. dissect directly on nidus of AVM, work in sulci and fissures whenever possible
6. in lesions that are high-flow on angiography, consider preoperative embolization
7. lesions with supplies from multiple vascular territories may require staging
 8. clip accessible aneurysms on feeding arteries.
