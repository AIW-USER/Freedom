Chapter: Regional Orthopedics MCQs — Iliotibial Band Friction Syndrome

## Question

You are a team physician of a hockey team and an athlete complains of pain on the lateral aspect of the knee every time he runs during a match. During assessment of his running practice, which risk factor could cause this pain?

## Options

1. Increased maximal foot eversion
2. . increased knee external rotation
3. Increased knee flexion at heel strike
4. Increased hip abduction

## Correct option

**3. Increased knee flexion at heel strike**

## Answer explanation

Answer: 3

Iliotibial band friction syndrome (ITBS) is a common cause of pain on the lateral aspect of the knee in field athletes. It was first seen in US Marine Corps recruits during their training in 1975 and has been diagnosed frequently in long-distance runners, cyclists, skiers, and participants of hockey, basketball, and soccer since then. Today, ITBS is the most common etiology of lateral knee pain in runners and cyclists. Its slightly more common in women than men.

The iliotibial band (ITB) is the distal fascial continuation of the tensor fascia lata, gluteus medius, and gluteal maximus. It traverses superficial to the vastus lateralis and inserts on the Gerdy tubercle of the lateral tibial plateau and partially to the supracondylar ridge of the lateral femur. There is also an anterior extension called the iliopatella band that connects the lateral patella and prevents medial translation of the patella. The ITB functions as a knee extensor when the knee is less than 30 degrees of flexion but becomes a knee flexor after exceeding 30 degrees of flexion. The ITB has been postulated to acquire a more posterior position relative to the lateral femoral epicondyle with increasing degrees of flexion.

The pathogenesis is poorly understood. Most acceptable is that repetitive friction of the ITB and the lateral epicondyle during repetitive flexion and extension lead to inflammation of the contact area of the ITB (or more precisely inflammation of a highly innervated fat pad under the ITB insertion). Contact between the ITB and lateral epicondyle occurs at 30 of flexion, which is the degree of knee flexion at foot strike. This contact area has the name of the "impingement zone”.

Running on tilted surfaces, hilly regions and abrupt changes in training intensity are thought to contribute. Following are some of the risk factors for Iliotibial band syndrome to develop: -

- Increased hip adduction
- Increased knee IR
- Increased knee flexion at heel strike
- Increased velocity of knee IR
- Increased maxim al foot inversion

Diagnosis is mainly clinical. Palpation of the distal ITB can be painful, and the clinician may feel crepitus with range of motion. Special tests for ITBS include the Noble and Ober tests. To perform the Noble test, the clinician first palpates the lateral femoral epicondyle and then extends the knee from 90 degrees of flexion to 0 degrees of extension. Reproducible pain at 30 of flexion is a positive test. The Ober test involves placing the patient in the lateral recumbent position with the knees flexed to 90. The clinician stands behind the patient and abducts and extends the affected hip while supporting the knee. A positive test is indicated by restriction and pain to the lateral knee while the clinician adducts the hip in this extended position. Radiography mainly has a role to rule out other pathology although MRI/ USG may show ITB thickening.

Management initially is always conservative with activity modification, a brief period of rest and use of intermittent cryotherapy. Physical therapy is focused on stretching of the ITB and abduction strengthening can also be very effective at lengthening the ITB and reducing tension. Furthermore, manual therapy such as myofascial release with a foam roller has been used to break up adhesions and is an integral part of the rehabilitation process. Corticosteroid injections at painful site are used in non responders. Most patients will have complete symptom relief and able to return to activity within 6 to 8 weeks with nonoperative management alone.

The recommended progression back to activity starts with a week of running on alternating days on a flat surface. The following weeks are focused on fast-paced running daily and avoid downhill running. After 3 to 4 weeks, the patient can gradually increase the distance and frequency. Introducing hills and cambered surfaces should only take place if there is no pain or symptoms on a flat surface. If the patient relapses, then they will need to start their activity progression over again and may require a period of rest beforehand.

Surgical intervention is only for refractory cases despite nonoperative management for more than 6 months. There are several surgical options including percutaneous or open ITB release (or resecting a triangular portion of ITB overlying LFC), ITB lengthening with a Z-plasty, ITB bursectomy (excising bursa and fat pad under the ITB), and arthroscopic ITB debridement. Most modern approaches focus on resecting the lateral synovial recess that connects the ITB to the lateral femur and innervated synovial fat.
