## Question

A 25-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with a right ankle grade III inversion injury. She is immobilized in a boot and made weight bearing as tolerated. At her 4-week follow up, she complains of severe right ankle burning pain and inability to bear weight. On exam, she has no swelling but exquisite tenderness to palpation over the anterolateral ankle and dorsolateral foot that is exaggerated by plantarflexion and foot inversion. Repeat radiographs are unchanged. MRI demonstrates a tear of the anterior talofibular ligament. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## Options

1. Stretch neurapraxia of the superficial peroneal nerve
2. Stretch neurapraxia of the deep peroneal nerve
3. Persistent tear of the deltoid ligament
4. Stretch neurapraxia of the sural nerve
5. Stretch neuropraxia of the saphenous nerve

## Correct option

**1. Stretch neurapraxia of the superficial peroneal nerve**

## Answer explanation

Anterolateral ankle pain with MRI evidence of anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injury is most likely caused by a stretch neurapraxia of the superficial peroneal nerve (SPN).

Inversion ankle sprains are extremely common and associated with a high incidence of residual symptoms (up to 40%), including prolonged gastrocnemius or peroneal weakness, anterolateral ankle pain, and localized tenderness and burning. Greater than 80% of patients with a grade III inversion ankle sprain have electromyographic (EMG) evidence of tibial or common peroneal nerve injury. Due to its anatomic position, the SPN is vulnerable to traction injury during inversion ankle sprains, particularly when there is associated injury of the ATFL, which is an important lateral stabilizing structure of the ankle and the most commonly involved ligament in low ankle sprains.

Gabapentin, pregabalin, or antidepressants like amitriptyline can be utilized for treatment for these residual neuritic symptoms.

*Nitz et al. performed EMG evaluation on 66 ankles after Grade II or Grade III inversion ankle injuries.* **They reported** peroneal and tibial nerve injuries in 17% and 10% of Grade II ankle sprains, respectively. **They also reported** peroneal and tibial nerve injuries in 86% and 83% of Grade II injuries.

*O'Neill et al. performed a cadaveric study on 16 ankles measuring excursion and strain of the SPN with simulated inversion ankle injuries of different force.* **They found** that inversion ankle injuries with as little as 4.5 kg can demonstrate sufficient strain and excursion to lead to peripheral nerve injury. The strain and excursion on the SPN both increase significantly with laceration of the ATFL.

Illustration A shows the classification scheme of ankle sprains. Complete ligamentous disruption constitutes a Grade III sprain. Illustration B demonstrates the dermatomes of the ankle and foot. The SPN provides sensory innervation to the anterolateral ankle and dorsolateral foot.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Stretch neuropraxia of the deep peroneal nerve would result in paresthesias of the 1st dorsal web space and might include weakness of the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus.
Answer 3: Persistent tear of the deltoid ligament would be evident on MRI.
Answer 4: Stretch neuropraxia of the sural nerve would result in paresthesias of the posterolateral ankle and lateral foot. Anterolateral ankle symptoms are more consistent with superficial peroneal nerve injury.
Answer 5: Stretch neuropraxia of the saphenous nerve would result in paresthesias over the anteromedial leg.
