## Question

A 22-year-old college hockey player complains of persistent right sided groin pain. The pain is made worse with vigorous skating and on examination he has tenderness over the right pubic tubercle and inguinal region, which is worsened when performing a sit-up but not with hip rotation. Radiographs of the pelvis and left hip are obtained and are read as normal. An MRI is obtained and shown in Figure A. Which repetitive motion likely contributed to this injury?

![[q215514-a-screen-shot-2021-05-04-at-7-57-32-am.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Hip extension and abduction
2. Hip extension and internal rotation
3. Hip flexion and internal rotation
4. Hip adduction
5. Moving from hip flexion and external rotation to hip extension and internal rotation

## Correct option

**1. Hip extension and abduction**

## Answer explanation

Athletic pubalgia, better known as a "sports hernia," occurs most commonly with repeated hip extension and abduction.

Athletic pubalgia is most common in hockey and soccer athletes that are repeatedly performing hip extension, abduction, and external rotation. The injury to the common aponeurosis of the adductor longus and rectus abdominis tendons is thought to occur because of an imbalance of the relatively stronger hip adductors and weaker abductors and usually results from eccentric contractions of the adductors. Patients complain of pain over the pubic rami and many have difficulty performing a sit-up, given the disruption of the rectus aponeurosis. MRI is helpful in definitive diagnosis and will often show edema at the site of injury adjacent to the aponeurosis. Nonoperative management is usually attempted before surgical repair is considered. A careful physical exam is critical in these patients to ensure the pathology is an isolated sports hernia as some of these patients may present with concomitant hip impingement.

*Hopkins et al. review the anatomy, diagnosis, and management of sports hernias.* *They discuss a comprehensive list of differential diagnoses and note that pain with sports hernias is typically more localized to the inguinal region.* **They suggest** core stability should be the initial focus of nonoperative management and should be attempted for at least two months before any open repair is considered.

*Lynch et al provide an overview of different hip injuries that occur in athletic populations.* *They go into detail describing the, "sports hip triad," which consists of adductor strains, osteitis pubis, and athletic pubalgia and discuss the diagnostic algorithm best used in these injuries.*

*Emblom et al. review athletic pubalgia and its outcomes regarding surgical management in a large group of athletes.* *All patients initially failed nonoperative treatment and underwent rectus/adductor aponeurotic plate repair.* *96% of patients were able to return to sport, with a high degree returning to previous level of function.*

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2-5: None of these answer choices would place repetitive, eccentric stress on the adductor aponeurotic attachment.
