## Question

A 42-year-old woman presents with a firm, nonmobile bump in the distal anterolateral thigh. She first noticed this in adolescence. She reports mild aching after kneeling but no night pain, no neurologic symptoms, and no recent change in size. This does not bother her during normal day-to-day activities and exercise. Radiographs demonstrate a pedunculated bony metaphyseal outgrowth with cortical and medullary continuity with the femur. MRI shows a lobulated cartilage cap measuring 14 mm without an adjacent soft-tissue mass. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

## Options

1. Image-guided fine-needle aspiration of the mass
2. CT scan of the chest for metastatic evaluation
3. Whole-body bone scan
4. Serial imaging and physical examination
5. En bloc surgical resection with wide margin

## Correct option

**4. Serial imaging and physical examination**

## Answer explanation

Serial imaging and evaluations are most appropriate because the lesion is a benign-appearing osteochondroma with a thin cartilage cap (<2 cm) and minimal symptoms in an adult with no documented growth; these features do not raise strong concern for malignant transformation (answer choice 4).

Chondrosarcoma is a malignant cartilage tumor that more commonly presents in older adults and is generally managed with surgical resection; prognosis and treatment strategy correlate with histologic grade and anatomic site. Secondary chondrosarcoma can arise from a preexisting osteochondroma, and a cartilage cap thickness ≥2 cm on MRI/CT is highly concerning for malignant transformation; by contrast, thin caps favor benignity and reduce the need for immediate resection. A bone scan can be “hot” in chondrosarcoma and may aid in assessing metastatic disease, but it is not required for a benign-appearing osteochondroma without worrisome features. Grade 1 acral lesions may be treated with intralesional curettage, whereas grade 2/3 and any pelvic lesions are indicated for wide surgical excision. Except in cases with mesenchymal subtypes, chemotherapy and radiation are not typically utilized in the treatment of chondrosarcomas, as these lesions are relatively resistant to both of these treatment modalities.

*Bernard et al. reviewed standardized cap-thickness measurements on CT/MRI in 101 path-confirmed osteochondromas and secondary chondrosarcomas.* **They found** that a 2-cm cap-thickness cutoff distinguished benign osteochondromas from secondary chondrosarcomas with very high sensitivity and specificity, and supported using this threshold to guide resection decisions. **They concluded** that caps ≥2 cm strongly indicate secondary chondrosarcoma, whereas thinner caps favor benignity and help avoid unnecessary surgery.

*Tsuda et al. conducted a multicenter retrospective review of 50 patients with peripheral pelvic chondrosarcoma to assess disease-specific and local recurrence–free survival, the association between surgical margin width and outcomes, preoperative biopsy grade accuracy, and complications.* **They found** 10-year disease-specific survival of 90% and local recurrence–free survival of 69%; no patient with a ≥1-mm histologic margin experienced local recurrence, metastasis, or disease-related death, and biopsy grading matched final grade in only 37%. **They concluded** that surgeons should aim for a negative margin at the index resection, and that a margin of at least 1 mm appeared sufficient in this cohort.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: Biopsy (especially limited sampling, such as the case with fine-needle aspiration) is often unreliable for grading cartilage tumors and is unnecessary when imaging/clinical features favor a benign osteochondroma.
Answer choice 2: Chest staging is reserved for suspected malignancy; this lesion lacks worrisome features and does not warrant metastatic workup at this time.
Answer choice 3: Bone scans can be hot in chondrosarcoma and can help identify metastasis, but they are not indicated for a benign-appearing osteochondroma without malignant features.
Answer choice 5: Wide excision is appropriate when secondary chondrosarcoma is suspected (e.g., cap ≥2 cm, new pain or growth, pelvic location), not for a thin-cap, minimally symptomatic osteochondroma.
