## Question

You are performing a lumbar interbody fusion for a 55-year-old-male with multilevel spinal stenosis recalcitrant to conservative management. Intraoperatively, you determine that you will need additional autologous bone graft to fully pack the fusion cage. You perform a mini-open approach to the posterior iliac crest in preparation for graft harvest. As you are subperiosteally elevating along the iliac crest, your key elevator slips off caudally, and you encounter brisk bleeding that does not stop with electrocautery. What structure did your elevator most likely injure?

## Options

1. Arterial anastomosis between the Obturator and External Iliac Arteries
2. Deep Circumflex Iliac Artery
3. Inferior Gluteal Artery
4. Superior Gluteal Artery
5. Superior Gluteal Vein

## Correct option

**4. Superior Gluteal Artery**

## Answer explanation

The posterior iliac crest is a reliable source of autologous bone graft, and as such is used in conjunction with a variety of orthopedic procedures. Aberrant instrument travel in a caudal direction will cause the instrument to enter the superior aspect of the greater sciatic notch, which puts the superior gluteal artery (Answer 4) at risk.

The iliac crest is the most common site of harvest for autologous bone graft. It provides a graft with both cortical and cancellous components and provides a higher concentration of stem cells when compared to anterior harvesting. While anterior harvesting does portend a higher complication rate, there is still a 2-36% rate with a posterior approach and harvest. The most common complications include blood loss and hematoma, nerve and vessel injury, pain, and poor cosmetic results.

*Shin et al. provide a series of 2 cases documenting superior gluteal artery injury during posterior iliac bone graft harvest.* **They note** that due to the tethering of the superior gluteal artery to the superior aspect of the greater sciatic notch, aberrant caudally-directed instrument travel could injure the artery, and cause brisk, difficult-to-control bleeding.

*Banwart et al. reported on a series of 261 patients undergoing posterior iliac crest bone graft harvest.* *They use a modified technique in which subperiosteal dissection is avoided, and meticulous, layered wound closure is utilized.* As a result of this technique, they had no instances of superior gluteal artery injury, which they associate with their limiting of subperiosteal dissection along the crest.

*Arrington et al. reported on a consecutive series of 414 patients undergoing iliac crest bone graft harvesting.* *3 patients sustained an injury to the superior gluteal artery, all during posterior bone graft harvest.* All three injuries were secondary to retractor placement in the superior aspect of the greater sciatic notch. No other vascular injuries were identified in their series.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the corona mortis, which is at risk with anterior intrapelvic approaches. The corona mortis is not at risk with a posterior approach to the iliac crest.
Answer 2: The deep circumflex iliac artery is at risk with anterior, not posterior, iliac crest bone graft harvesting.
Answer 3: The inferior gluteal artery enters the sciatic notch caudal to the piriformis. It is unlikely to be injured during posterior iliac crest bone graft harvest.
Answer 5: The superior gluteal vein is not tethered to the superior aspect of the notch like the artery, and bleeding from this injury to this structure has not been described with posterior iliac crest bone graft harvest.
