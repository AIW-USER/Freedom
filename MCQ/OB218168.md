## Question

A 65-year-old male is transferred to a tertiary care facility with concerns of sepsis secondary to periprosthetic joint infection. Interventional radiology performs an aspiration (Figure A) which is positive for chronic periprosthetic joint infection. He undergoes explant, antibiotic-imbued cementation of the resultant bone defect, and spacer placement with adjunctive antibiotic beads (Figures B & C). Over the next 6 months, the patient completes a prolonged course of IV antibiotics followed by an antibiotic holiday. He obtains updated labs demonstrating a serum ESR of 10 mm/hr (normal 0-22 mm/hr) and CRP of 0.4 mg/dL (normal <0.5mg/dL). A hip aspiration is performed (Figure D) resulting in negative alpha defensive, negative leukocyte esterase, synovial WBC of 1,000 cells/mL, and 74% neutrophils. A CT scan confirms a subtle fracture nonunion between his anterior and posterior columns beneath the cemented pelvic defect. He continues to endorse groin and thigh pain limiting his ability to perform transfers or walk. He would like to return to a reasonable level of independent functioning. Which of the following would be the most appropriate next step in management?

## Options

1. Remove current implants, place an antibiotic spacer with a cemented constrained liner followed by 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics
2. Remove current implants, revise the femoral stem, utilize bulk allograft to fill the pelvic defect, and cement a constrained liner into the bulk allograft
3. Remove current implants, revise the femoral stem, place an independent acetabular cage to bridge the defect, and cement a constrained liner into the cage
4. Remove current implants, revise the femoral stem, utilize a custom triflange acetabular component
5. Explant current implants as a Girdlestone procedure

## Correct option

**4. Remove current implants, revise the femoral stem, utilize a custom triflange acetabular component**

## Answer explanation

This patient has chronic pelvic discontinuity with associated massive bone loss. A custom triflange acetabular component (Answer 4) can be 3D printed to fill the anticipated bony defect and bridge the pelvic discontinuity to provide a stable construct for independent ambulation (Illustrations A&B).

Pelvic discontinuity involves the separation of the superior ilium from the inferior ischiopubic segment and may be encountered in the setting of failed prior total hip arthroplasty cases. Achieving long-term cup stability is difficult, particularly if there is concurrent bone loss. The three primary factors influencing outcomes are (1) the remaining bone stock, (2) the potential for biological ingrowth, and (3) the potential for healing. Younger, healthy patients with adequate bone stock with biological potential to heal, may be treated with pelvic open reduction internal fixation with additional fixation achieved using a revision acetabular shell with multiple screws. Chronic cases with concomitant bone loss require implants that can bridge the ilium and ischiopubic segments while also addressing bone loss. This can include the distraction method using a revision acetabular shell +/- metal augments, a cup cage construct +/- metal augments, or a custom triflange acetabular component.

*Abdel and colleagues provided a review article on the management of pelvic discontinuity associated with total hip arthroplasty.* *They discuss potential causes for this condition and various treatment options, each with unique pros and cons.* *They focus primarily on techniques involving posterior column plating with a hemispheric acetabular component, cup-cage constructs, pelvic distraction, or custom triflange constructs.* **The authors conclude** by highlighting that the most successful treatments are those with high rates of cup fixation and simultaneous pelvic discontinuity healing or unitization.

*Hasenauer and colleagues provided a similar review of treatment options for chronic pelvic discontinuity.* **They specifically highlight** their preferred treatment of acetabular distraction, a relatively new technique with promising early reported outcomes. **The authors conclude** that multiple other treatment options are also available with satisfactory outcomes.

Figure A demonstrates fluoroscopic imaging of a loosened and mal-rotated acetabular cup, which collectively are concerning for an underlying periprosthetic joint infection. Figures B and C are radiographic images of this patient’s stage 1 revision THA arthroplasty with a cemented constrained liner and significant pelvic bone loss filled with antibiotic cement. Figure D demonstrates fluoroscopic localization for arthrocentesis of the stage 1 spacer and subsequent synovial fluid analysis. Illustrations A and B are radiographic images after the removal of all implants, placement of a custom triflange acetabular component, and femoral stem revision to a modular diaphyseal engaging system.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Given his lab values and synovial aspiration, this patient does not meet the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria to be diagnosed with an active periprosthetic joint infection. Therefore, pursuing a 2-stage approach is not indicated.
Answer 2: Historically, massive bulk allograft, used in conjunction with a cemented liner in the setting of chronic pelvic discontinuity had a 50% failure rate. Additionally, there is concern for this type of reconstruction due to graft resorption and infection risks associated with the allograft.
Answer 3: Using an independent acetabular cage as a bridging device to span the ilium and the ischium also has poor outcomes with high rates of complication with reported failures of up to 50–60%. When there is inadequate bone to support a cage construct, bulk allograft or a revision acetabular cup can be used in conjunction with a cage. With the advent of trabecular metal implants, bulk allografts are used less frequently, due to improved biologic ingrowth of tantalum-backed cups and negated concerns for implant resorption.
Answer 5: While performing a Girdlestone procedure would likely provide significant pain relief, the expected outcomes would not be consistent with the patient’s goals to return to independent ambulation.
