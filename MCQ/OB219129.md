## Answer explanation

Hip dislocations can present as isolated injuries or with concomitant femoral head fractures, acetabular fractures, or both. Patients who sustain an infra-foveal femoral head fracture (i.e. Figure A - Pipkin 1) with maintained joint congruity may be treated with non-operative management.

Femoral head fractures are rare injuries that most commonly occur in the setting of hip dislocation. They are characterized based on the Pipkin classification (Illustration A). These injuries incur several potential long-term complications including avascular necrosis, post-traumatic arthritis, and heterotopic ossification. For this reason, prompt reduction of the hip joint (<6 hours) is recommended followed by open reduction internal fixation of most femoral head fractures. Fractures that occur in the non-weight-bearing portion (infra-foveal) of the femoral head with maintained joint congruity can be considered for nonoperative treatment. Operative intervention is indicated for fractures that are grossly displaced with non-concentric joint reduction, fractures that involve the weight-bearing portion (supra-foveal) of the femoral head, any fracture involving the femoral neck, or if there are incarcerated bone fragments within the joint space. These cases require specialized approaches to avoid disrupting the posterior-based medial femoral circumflex artery (i.e. Smith Peterson anterior hip approach or Gibson surgical hip dislocation). Older patients may be treated with acute arthroplasty as well.

*Del Core and colleagues performed a single institution retrospective review of cases involving femoral head fractures with an average of 18 months follow-up.* *Of the 22 patients included, 18 (82%) required operative intervention with less than half of those patients having an uneventful postoperative course (41%).* The majority of patients experienced a complication including post-traumatic arthritis (23%), heterotopic ossification (18%), avascular necrosis (9%), persistent sciatic nerve palsy (9%), or anterolateral thigh numbness (9%). **The authors conclude** that early diagnosis and concentric reduction are the prerequisites for successful treatment.

*Scolaro and colleagues performed a retrospective review of their institutional prospectively collected data regarding the treatment and outcomes of 164 femoral head fractures.* Their most common treatment modalities included ORIF in 78 (53.1%), incarcerated fragment excision in 37 (25.1%), and non-operative in 28 (19%). **They reported** primarily positive results with 90% of ORIF cases healing with uneventful union and predominantly asymptomatic heterotopic ossification despite its high prevalence (40.6% of patients). All Pipkin III cases failed open reduction internal fixation and required conversion to early arthroplasty. **The authors concluded** that Pipkin III fractures represent catastrophic injuries, asymptomatic heterotopic ossification is common, and that avascular necrosis and post-traumatic arthritis are less common than often believed.

Figure D demonstrates a femoral head fracture (infra-foveal) with a concomitant acetabular fracture classified as Pipkin IV. Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: Pipkin II fractures involve the supra-foveal, weight-bearing portion of the femoral head and should be treated with operative fixation. In this case, the anterior fragment is displaced and could be fixed using the anterior Smith-Peterson approach.
Answer 3: Pipkin III fractures involve a femoral head and ipsilateral femoral neck fracture. These are always operative with an exceedingly high risk for avascular necrosis leading to the need for early hip replacement.
Answer 4: Pipkin IV fractures involve a femoral head and ipsilateral acetabular fracture. The femoral head fracture and acetabulum are grossly displaced (i.e. nonconcentric) and require operative fixation.
Answer 5: After performing a closed reduction of hip dislocation, a CT scan must be obtained to evaluate for incarcerated fragments in the joint space. In this case, there is a large posterior wall fragment incarcerated in the joint displacing the femoral head laterally. These fragments must always be removed (and potentially fixed) unless they are small enough to fall into the cotyloid fossa and the joint space is concentrically reduced.
