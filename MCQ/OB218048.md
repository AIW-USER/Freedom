## Question

A 59-year-old male undergoes a robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA). His initial postoperative course was uncomplicated, and he was discharged home the same day his procedure was performed. He presents to the emergency room three weeks later with the findings in Figure A. With respect to performing a robotic-assisted TKA, which of the following represents the greatest risk for the complication this patient has suffered?

![[q218048-a-screenshot-2023-06-19-at-1-15-05-pm.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Placement of metaphyseal tracking pins
2. Smaller tracking pin diameters (3mm)
3. Tracking pins placed with transcortical purchase
4. Use of a posterior stabilized implant with navigation
5. Use of self-drilling, self-tapping tracking pins

## Correct option

**3. Tracking pins placed with transcortical purchase**

## Answer explanation

When performing a robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA), placement of pins in the femur is required to secure the navigation tracker (Illustration A). Placement of larger diameter, bicortical or transcortical, diaphyseal pins has been associated with an increased risk of femur fracture post-operatively (Answer 3).

Computer navigation and robotic-assisted systems for total knee arthroplasty are becoming more widely used given the potential for more reproducible bone cuts and more reliable component alignment. However, the technology is not without risk. In order for the technology to be used, threaded tracker pins must be placed into the distal femur and the proximal tibia, which ultimately remove bone and result in stress risers when they are removed at the end of the case. Biomechanical studies have shown that drill holes can diminish the bending strength and energy absorption potential of cortical bone by as much as 40%. A single, unicortical 5mm pin would be expected to remove 2.78 times more bone than a corresponding 3mm pin; thus, it is recommended that smaller diameter, unicortical pins are used in order to reduce torsional stresses and prevent post-operative femur fractures.

Bonutti, et al. published a case report of two patients who suffered femoral shaft fractures from femoral tracker placement after navigated TKA. The authors concluded that the use of smaller pins with double pin-fixated trackers placed in the metaphyseal areas of the distal femur appears to be the safest method for tracker anchorage. Based on limited anecdotal experience, they also suggest that females may be at greater risk.

Wysocki, et al. published another case report of two patients who sustained femur fractures through previous pin sites after computer-assisted total knee arthroplasty. The authors concluded that the risk of this complication may be theoretically reduced by placing the femoral pins through the wider and more cancellous bone of the metaphysis rather than the diaphysis.

Jung, et al. published a single case of delayed femur fracture through a tracker pin site at 12 weeks after navigated TKA. The authors assert that all similar, previously reported fractures have occurred within 3 months of surgery, and thus, any patient with unexpected thigh pain during the early postoperative period should be carefully monitored for a potential fracture and receive appropriate follow-up care. They concluded that transcortical drilling is a risk factor and that surgeons should be aware of the possibility of thermal necrosis from the drilling, resulting in delayed cortical remodeling and increased risk of fracture, even in the late postoperative period.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of the knee status-post total knee arthroplasty with a femoral shaft fracture through the stress riser created by navigation tracker pin insertion.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Placement of tracker pins in the metaphyseal bone of the distal femur has been suggested to decrease the risk of fracture as compared to insertion in the more proximal femoral shaft.
Answer 2: Placement of two, small-diameter (3mm) pins in a unicortical fashion is the preferred method of insertion for tracker pins and reduces the risk of post-operative femur fracture.
Answer 4: The use of a posterior stabilized implant with navigation has not been shown to be a risk factor for femur fracture.
Answer 5: The use of self-drilling, self-tapping tracking pins has been shown to be protective against femur fracture in navigation-assisted TKA.
