## Question

A 45-year-old female presents to your office complaining of diffuse anterior and medial knee pain which is hard to pinpoint. You obtain radiographs in the office which demonstrate progressive degenerative changes in the medial and patellofemoral compartments. She does report having a prior car accident where she injured her knee in her twenties. If you were to obtain an MRI in this patient, what might it show that has contributed to her radiographic findings?

## Options

1. Posterior horn medial meniscus tear
2. ACL rupture
3. PCL rupture
4. Type C trochlear dysplasia
5. Medial femoral condyle osteochondral defect

## Correct option

**3. PCL rupture**

## Answer explanation

With a history of degeneration in the medial and patellofemoral compartments, this patient likely had a previous injury to her posterior cruciate ligament (PCL).

Injury to the PCL can occur in several mechanisms, with one being a posteriorly directed force to a flexed knee, or a so-called, "dashboard injury." With complete rupture of the PCL there is increased posterior tibial translation as well as some increase in varus instability of the knee. For this reason, the medial and patellofemoral compartments see more stress and can degenerate more quickly. This pattern is important to recognize in patients with prior PCL injuries, whether diagnosed or previously undiagnosed.

*Van de Velde et al looked into knee cartilage deformation in the PCL deficient knee using MRI after PCL injury.* *They had patients perform squats and measured compartment pressures at different flexion angles,* **noting** that the location and peak cartilage deformation were more anterior and medial on the tibia in the PCL deficient knee when flexed beyond 75º. No significant changes were found in the lateral compartment and the patellofemoral compartment was not directly evaluated in this study.

*Skyhar et al. evaluated the effects of PCL and posterolateral corner sectioning on knee articular cartilage pressures.* *In this study,* **they noted** that patellofemoral contact pressures and quadriceps load were significantly increased after PCL sectioning, in addition to the increase in medial compartment pressure. **They concluded** that these cadaveric results help to support and explain the findings being seen in people with PCL injuries.

*Shelbourne et al. reviewed the natural history of PCL injuries treated nonoperatively.* *They evaluated 133 patients,* **noting** no change in laxity from their initial injury to follow-up. This said, the degree of laxity did not affect patient outcomes or the ability for patients to return to sport if desired.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Injury to the posterior horn of the medial meniscus may cause some degeneration and wear in the medial compartment but would not concomitantly affect the patellofemoral compartment.
Answer 2: Anterior cruciate ligament rupture increases anterior tibial translation, which places more force through secondary stabilizers such as the medial meniscus. However, the pattern of arthritic changes presents differently than detailed above for the PCL.
Answer 4: Trochlear dysplasia can lead to patellofemoral dysfunction and arthritis but would not affect the medial compartment.
Answer 5: Medial femoral condyle osteochondral defect (OCD) is typically localized to the condyle and does not necessarily infer significant loss of medial joint space or contribution to patellofemoral arthritis.
