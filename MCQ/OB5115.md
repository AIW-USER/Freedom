## Question

Which of the following injuries is associated with highest incidence of osteonecrosis?

## Options

1. 69-year-old male with a valgus impacted three-part proximal humerus fracture
2. 89-year-old female with a valgus impacted (Garden I) femoral neck fracture
3. 14-year-old male with a displaced distal femoral physeal fracture
4. 13-year-old female with a displaced transcervical femoral neck fracture
5. 42-year-old male with a closed 5th metatarsal fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction

## Correct option

**4. 13-year-old female with a displaced transcervical femoral neck fracture**

## Answer explanation

A displaced transcervical femoral neck fracture in an adolescent has the highest rate of osteonecrosis of the listed options.

Osteonecrosis is the most common complication of hip fractures in children. The overall risk of osteonecrosis after a pediatric femoral neck fracture is approximately 30%. This risk is further stratified by considering the Delbet classification as seen in Illustration B. Type I transphyseal femoral neck fractures are at the greatest risk of osteonecrosis at rate of 38%. Type Ib fractures (dislocation of epiphysis) have osteonecrosis rates approaching 100%. Type II-IV fractures have osteonecrosis rates of 28%, 18%, and 5%, respectively. The risk of osteonecrosis is increased with increased displacement and proximity to the physis and the risk can be decreased with prompt anatomic reduction and intracapsular decompression.

*Ju et al. retrospectively reviewed 58 pediatric patients with femoral neck fractures that underwent either open reduction and internal fixation or closed reduction and internal fixation more than 24 hours after injury.* The incidence of avascular necrosis was significantly lower in those treated with open reduction (5/37 (open) vs. 6/21 (closed)) and in those that achieved anatomic reduction (3/41 (anatomic) vs. 8/17 (acceptable)). Therefore, **the authors recommend** treating patients that present greater than 24 hours after the injury with open reduction and internal fixation.

*Spence et al. retrospectively reviewed 70 pediatric patients that underwent surgical treatment for a femoral neck fracture.* Osteonecrosis occurred in 29% of the patients and time to treatment > 24 hours was a positive predictor of this complication. **The authors recommend** prompt treatment of this injury.

Illustration A shows a transcervical femoral neck fracture. Illustration B shows the Delbet classification of pediatric femoral neck fractures.

Incorrect Answers:
1) A valgus impacted proximal humerus fracture is generally protective against disruption of the medial vascular sleeve of the proximal humerus. AVN rates are lower than in a pediatric femoral neck fracture.
2) A valgus impacted femoral neck fracture is not associated with high rates of AVN due to the lack of disruption of the lateral circumflex vessels (AVN rate reported as less than 10%).
3) Distal femoral physeal fractures are typically monitored for limb length discrepancy and angular deformity. AVN is possible, but not common.
5) 'Jones' fractures are more likely to develop nonunion, not AVN.
