## Question

A 12-year-old baseball player is seen in your clinic. He has recently completed the season with his summer travel team, and individual workouts are set to begin for his fall showcase league. The patient is right-hand dominant and throws right-handed. He complains of right shoulder pain, which has progressively worsened over the past month. Physical examination of his right shoulder demonstrates 20° less internal rotation than his left shoulder, with a 25° smaller total arc of rotational motion. Radiographs of his right and left shoulders are available for review in Figures A and B, respectively. Which of the following is true regarding the patient's expected clinical course?

## Options

1. His physical examination findings have no association with the expected likelihood of recurrence.
2. The presence of pain following two months of physical therapy is not correlated with the likelihood of recurrence.
3. His physical examination findings are associated with an increased likelihood of recurrence.
4. The patient is unlikely to complain of concomitant ipsilateral elbow pain.
5. The patient is likely to be a pitcher who throws primarily non-breaking/curving pitches

## Correct option

**3. His physical examination findings are associated with an increased likelihood of recurrence.**

## Answer explanation

This patient with glenohumeral internal rotation deficiency (GIRD) has symptoms and radiographic findings consistent with Little Leaguer's shoulder (proximal humeral epiphysiolysis). Compared to patients without GIRD, this patient is three times more likely to experience recurrence, which occurs at a mean of 6.2 - 7.6 months after presentation (Answer 3).

Little leaguer's shoulder (proximal humeral epiphysiolysis) is an overuse injury occurring in young baseball pitchers. The diagnosis is typically made clinically and radiographically, with radiographs demonstrating a widened proximal humeral physis and metaphyseal bony changes, although 17% of patients may have normal radiographs. Patients typically complain of decreased pitched velocity and accuracy, and report diffuse arm and shoulder pain with throwing. While all overhead athletes are at risk for the development of little leaguer's shoulder, this pathology classically occurs in pitchers who throw breaking pitches and do not adhere to pitch counts and/or appropriate rest periods. The development of Little Leaguer's shoulder has been associated with the presence of glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD), classically defined as an internal rotation loss of 20-25°, with a decreased total rotational arc of motion, compared to the non-throwing shoulder. The treatment of Little Leaguer's shoulder is nonoperative and is based on cessation of throwing, followed by physical therapy and a return-to-throwing program once asymptomatic. Most surgeons recommend a three-month refrain from pitching, and it is important to correct any kinetic chain imbalances and/or glenohumeral stiffness during the rehabilitation process.

Once rehabilitated, prevention of future episodes is of paramount importance. Proper pitching mechanics are critical, and patients should be discouraged from throwing breaking balls until skeletal maturity is reached. Furthermore, pitch counts and days off for shoulder rest should be enforced by coaches, and patients should avoid year-round pitching. The presence of GIRD at the time of diagnosis of little leaguer's shoulder has been identified as a risk factor for recurrent and/or persistent symptoms. As a result, therapy must focus on restoration of normal shoulder motion.

*Heyworth et al. provide an overview of the presentation, management and outcomes of Little League Shoulder.* *Their study includes 95 patients with a mean age of 13.1 years.* **The authors note** that concomitant elbow pain was observed in up to 13% of patients. Promisingly, following rest and physical therapy, recurrence rates were low (7%), and typically occurred 7.6 months after initial diagnosis. The authors did highlight, however, that the rate of recurrence was three times higher in patients with GIRD.

*Harada et al. investigated factors related to incomplete return to baseball and recurrence following conservative treatment of little leaguer's shoulder in baseball players.* *Their study reported on 87 patients with a mean age of 12.1 years.* *Overall, 20 patients (25%) experience recurrent symptoms, which typically occurred 6.2 months after initial presentation.* **The authors note** that the presence of continued pain following two months of appropriate rehabilitation and poor shoulder motion were predictors of more frequent and/or rapid recurrence.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Patients with GIRD are more likely to experience recurrent symptoms.
Answer 2: Continued pain following two months of therapy is an independent predictor of persistent/recurrent symptoms.
Answer 4: Patients with GIRD typically experience ipsilateral elbow pain, due to an altered throwing motion.
Answer 5: The development of little leaguer's shoulder has been linked to pitchers who throw primary breaking pitches.
