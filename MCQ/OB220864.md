## Question

An 11-year-old female presents to the emergency department for evaluation of a left upper extremity injury after falling while performing aerial maneuvers at a local gymnastics camp. Radiographs are obtained, which demonstrate a Bado Type I Monteggia injury. An attempted reduction is performed, and the ulna is anatomically reduced; however, the radial head remains dislocated despite repeated attempts. The function of the most likely interposed structure involves

## Options

1. Innervation to the dorsal forearm musculature
2. Stabilizing the elbow with varus angulation
3. Providing stability to the elbow with valgus stress
4. Stabilizing the proximal radioulnar joint
5. Assisting with forearm supination

## Correct option

**4. Stabilizing the proximal radioulnar joint**

## Answer explanation

This 11-year-old female sustained a Monteggia fracture-dislocation with most likely an interposed annular ligament, which serves as the most common intervening structure. This structure is essential to the stability of the proximal radioulnar joint by keeping the radial head located within the joint (Answer 4).

Monteggia injuries are rather uncommon injuries sustained in the pediatric population, with a predilection for those aged between 4 and 10 years old. When proximal one-third ulna fractures are encountered, intense scrutiny of the radiocapitellar joint must be made. The radial head should bisect the capitellum on all radiographic views, and subluxation suggests a Monteggia fracture-dislocation. The mainstay of treatment includes closed reduction and casting. If anatomic reduction of the ulna is obtained, examine the radial head in both pronation and supination to ensure the joint is well reduced. If the head remains subluxated/dislocated, soft tissue interposition is likely present (most likely the annular ligament). In these scenarios, open reduction is indicated.

*Abe et al. provide a retrospective review detailing 17 pediatric patients who sustained Monteggia fractures with irreducible radial heads requiring open reduction.* All but one suffered Bado type I fractures, and 15 of the 17 (88%) had the annular ligament as the interposing structure. In contrast, one patient had the biceps tendon, and the other had the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) interposed. **The authors concluded** that clinicians must pay close attention to the possibility of subluxated/dislocated radial heads, as they can appear reduced in supination but dislocated in pronation.

Dr. Bae discusses the treatment strategies for Monteggia fractures in the pediatric population. In the setting of proximal one-third ulna fractures, close examination of the radial head bisecting the capitellum on all views is essential, and any deviation from this alignment suggests a Monteggia fracture-dislocation. Obtaining anatomic reduction is key to reducing the radial head, and persistent subluxation/dislocation of the radial head in the setting of anatomic reduction of the ulna, suggesting soft tissue interposition, which is most commonly the annular ligament.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: The posterior interosseous nerve, which has been reported as a possible interposing structure in rare instances, innervates the dorsal forearm musculature
Answer 2: The lateral ulnar collateral ligament is the essential part of the lateral collateral ligament complex and has not been shown to be an interposing structure
Answer 3: The anterior portion of the medial ulnar collateral ligament provides valgus stability to the elbow, and has not been reported to be an interposing structure with Monteggia fractures
Answer 5: The biceps, which has been reported as an interposed structure although far less commonly, assists with forearm supination
