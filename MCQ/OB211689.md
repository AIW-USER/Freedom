## Question

A 29-year-old man presents with atraumatic left deep posterior hip pain for the last 7 months. His pain is worst after running long distances. Plain radiographs were interpreted as negative and his MRI is depicted in Figure A. On examination, his pain is exacerbated by passive left hip extension in the right decubitus position. ## Correct option

**1. Decreased native femoral offset may predispose to this condition**

## Answer explanation

This patient is presenting with deep posterior hip pain and MRI findings of quadratus femoris tendonitis, consistent with his diagnosis of ischiofemoral impingement (IFI). IFI occurs secondary to abnormal contact between the lesser trochanter and the lateral border of the ischium, and may result from decreased native femoral offset.

IFI is a distinct pathology with abnormal osseous contact between the ischium and the lesser trochanter of the femur, observed in patients without antecedent trauma or previous surgery. Patients with IFI generally present with deep posterior hip pain that is unresponsive to diagnostic hip joint diagnostic injections. On physical examination, pain may be elicited with palpation over the area, or passive extension of the affected hip while in the contralateral decubitus position (IFI test). Plain radiographs may demonstrate narrowed space between the ischium and lesser trochanter with MRI often exhibiting signal intensity in the quadratus femoris muscle/tendon. The treatment for IFI is often nonoperative, including stretching and corticosteroid injections in the ischiofemoral space. In recalcitrant cases, partial resection of the lesser trochanter may be considered.

*Gómez-Hoyos et al. investigated the accuracy of two clinical tests (long-stride walking [LSW] and IFI tests) for IFI.* **They reported** that the IFI test had a sensitivity of 0.82 and specificity of 0.85, whereas the LSW test had a sensitivity of 0.94, and specificity of 0.85. **They concluded** that in patients with posterior hip pain and negative lumbosacral spine involvement, the IFI and LSW tests are highly accurate to help identify those with or without IFI.

*Backer et al. evaluated the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided steroid injection of the quadratus femoris as a treatment of IFI syndrome when compared to a control group without injection.* **They reported** a statistically significant pain reduction after injection over the 2-week period in favor of the injection group. **They concluded** that ultrasound-guided steroid injection of the quadratus femoris muscle is an effective treatment of IFI syndrome, but recommended other studies to evaluate its role in the diagnosis.

*Hatem et al. investigated the clinical and radiographic presentation of patients with IFI and to assess the outcomes of endoscopic treatment with partial resection of the lesser trochanter.* **They observed** significant increases in the Harris Hip Score, reduction in VAS pain scores, and no complications. **They concluded** that the endoscopic treatment of IFI is an effective method in treatment of IFI.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 2: The most sensitive physical examination finding is pain with flexion, adduction, and internal rotation in femoral-acetabular impingement (FAI), not IFI. Patients with IFI often experience with passive hip extension and external rotation.
Answer 3: MRI will often demonstrate decreased ischiofemoral (not iliofemoral) space.
Answer 4: The muscle/tendon (quadratus femoris) most commonly involved is innervated by the nerve to the quadratus femoris, not the femoral nerve.
Answer 5: This refers to the Cam deformity in FAI, and not IFI.
