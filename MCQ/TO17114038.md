Chapter: Arthroscopy MCQs — Elbow Arthroscopy

## Question

Which is not a maneuver to avoid nerve injury in elbow arthroscopy? (FNB Pattern Paper 2018)

## Options

1. Pronation and 90-degree flexion to protect PIN
2. Joint distension
3. More distal lateral portal
4. Low flow infusion

## Correct option

**3. More distal lateral portal**

## Answer explanation

Explanation: Elbow arthroscopy:

Indications: (1) removal of loose bodies; (2) osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum; (3) osteochondral lesions of the radial head; (4) excision of osteophytes from the humerus and olecranon;

(5) Rheumatoid arthritis (partial synovectomy) (6) stiff elbow (debridement and lysis of adhesions) (7) tennis elbow release; and (8) evaluation of a painful elbow (9) debridement of inflamed olecranon bursae (10) treatment of intra articular fractures

Contraindications: (1) bony ankylosis (2) severe fibrous ankylosis that prevents safe introduction of the arthroscope (3) active infection, and (4) Previous surgery that alters normal elbow anatomy, such as anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve, (relative contraindication)

Patient positions: Elbow arthroscopy can be done with the patient supine, prone, or in the lateral decubitus position.

Portals:

- The direct lateral portal is located in the lateral soft spot formed by the lateral epicondyle, radial head, and tip of the olecranon. This spot can be precisely located by palpating the posterior articulation of the radio capitellar joint. The portal is made just posterior and proximal to this joint. This is used for infusion of the joint.
- The anterolateral portal, traditionally the standard diagnostic portal, usually is the first established after elbow distention. Anterolateral portals may include (1) the traditional distal anterolateral portal 2 to 3 cm distal and 1 cm anterior to the lateral epicondyle, (2) the mid anterolateral portal just proximal and approximately 1 cm anterior to the palpable radio capitellar joint, and (3) the proximal anterolateral portal 2 cm proximal and 1 cm anterior to the lateral epicondyle. Distal anterolateral portal puts the radial nerve at maximum risk and rarely used.
- The anteromedial portal is located 2 cm distal and 2 cm anterior to the medial epicondyle. The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve and basilic vein are vulnerable with an incision deeper than the skin layer.
- The proximal medial portal (or supracondylar anteromedial portal): This portal is located 2 cm proximal and anterior to the medial epicondyle. The posterolateral portal is located 2 to 3 cm proximal to the olecranon tip and just lateral to the border of the triceps tendon along the lateral supracondylar ridge. The posterior antebrachial and the lateral brachial cutaneous nerves are at risk with deep incisions.
- The straight posterior portal is placed 2 to 3 cm proximal to the olecranon tip and approximately 2 cm medial to the posterolateral portal.

Complications: Neurologic problems are the most commonly reported complications. Most of them are transient injuries.

Factors that decrease the risk of neurovascular injury:

- 90 degrees of elbow flexion and joint distention to move the radial nerve anteriorly out of the path of the trocar entering the joint.
- Avoid deep penetration of the knife to protect the lateral and posterior antebrachial cutaneous nerves. A small hemostat is used to spread down to the capsule before using a blunt trocar to enter the joint.
- Keep the lateral portal more proximal to avoid injury to the radial nerve (PIN).
- Carefully draw anatomic landmarks and palpate the ulnar nerve location.
- Don’t use pressurized infusion (low flow of 40 to 45 mL/min or gravity can be used safely),
- Protect the posterior interosseous nerve by pronation, and by making lateral portal more proximal.
- Keep instrument tips in view at all times, and avoid suction around nerves.

While complications after elbow arthroscopy are rare, the most common permanent nerve palsy post- operatively is the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) followed by the ulnar nerve.

Answer: 3
