## Question

A 23-year-old male suffers a noncontact knee injury while pivoting during a pickup basketball game. Four weeks later, he presents to clinic reporting continued knee instability when walking. What finding on the physical exam would be classified as a 2B Lachman exam?

## Options

1. 7 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with a soft endpoint with the knee in 90 degrees of flexion
2. 9 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with a soft endpoint with the knee in 30 degrees of flexion
3. 4 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with a firm endpoint with the knee in 30 degrees of flexion
4. 11 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with no end point with the knee in 90 degrees of flexion
5. 12 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with a firm endpoint with the knee in 30 degrees of flexion

## Correct option

**2. 9 mm of anterior translation of the tibia with a soft endpoint with the knee in 30 degrees of flexion**

## Answer explanation

A grade 2B Lachman test demonstrates anterior translation of 6-10 mm of the tibia with no firm endpoint with the knee in 20-30 degrees of flexion (Answer 2)

The Lachman exam is considered the best test for clinically diagnosing anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures, with a sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 93%. The test is performed by placing the knee in 20-30 degrees of flexion, stabilizing the distal femur, and anteriorly translating the tibia with manual force. Grading Lachman exams follow a classification where Grade I is 0-5 mm of translation, Grade II is 6-10 mm translation, and Grade III is >10 mm translation. Subclassifications are A for a firm endpoint and B for a soft or no endpoint. It is also important to compare the injured knee to the contralateral side because some patients have physiologic laxity.

Other physical exam maneuvers are less reliable for diagnosing ACL tears, especially in the acute setting. The pivot shift has a sensitivity of 61%, but a specificity of 97%. The low sensitivity is likely due to patient guarding in the acute setting. The anterior drawer is similar to the Lachman, except that the knee is flexed to 90 degrees, but has a much lower sensitivity at only 48%. Overall, the Lachman and pivot shift maneuvers are the most clinically useful combination of exams in order to detect ACL ruptures.

*Van Eck and colleagues compared common physical exam tests, including the Lachman, anterior drawer, and pivot shift maneuvers.* **They found** that the Lachman test is the most sensitive method for detecting ACL ruptures, particularly in an acute setting, with accuracy increased under anesthesia due to patient relaxation and reduced guarding. While highly specific, the pivot shift test was less sensitive in conscious patients, as it relies on detecting instability often masked by muscle tension. Their meta-analysis confirms that combining physical exam maneuvers, particularly the Lachman test, provides the most reliable diagnostic accuracy for acute ACL ruptures.

*Benjaminse and colleagues performed a meta-analysis on the clinical diagnosis of ACL ruptures, evaluating the accuracy of physical examination techniques in diagnosing ACL injuries without imaging.* Results indicated that the Lachman test is the most reliable, with high sensitivity and specificity, making it the preferred method for clinicians to diagnose ACL rupture. The pivot shift test also demonstrated good specificity but had lower sensitivity, **suggesting** it may be more useful for confirming a diagnosis rather than screening. Overall, the meta-analysis underscores the effectiveness of these clinical tests, particularly the Lachman test, as essential tools for diagnosing ACL ruptures and helping to guide timely and accurate treatment.

Incorrect answers:
Answers 1 and 4: Examination of the knee in 90 degrees of flexion with anterior translation of the tibia with manual manipulation describes the anterior drawer test.
Answer 3: This would be classified as a 1A Lachman.
Answer 5: This would be classified as a 3A Lachman.
