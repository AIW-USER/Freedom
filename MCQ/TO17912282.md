## Question

65 years old man has come with complaint of chronic back pain. X ray and CT image of the spine is given. Marked structures can best be described as

![[TO17912282-1.png]]

## Options

1. Shiny corners
2. Syndesmophytes
3. Claw osteophytes
4. Bamboo spine

## Correct option

**3. Claw osteophytes**

## Answer explanation

![[TO17912282-2.png]]

Ans: C

Osteophytes (aka bone spurs) are bony protuberances seen in degenerative arthritis. Osteophytes can occur anywhere in the body, but they are most commonly found along the spine. They may be present in approximately 80% of men and 60% of women by age 50 and 95% in both genders by age 70. The distribution of osteophytes within the spine is greatest at L3-4 and T9-10, which is intuitive because these levels are at or near the sites of maximum sagittal thoracic and lumbar curvatures.

Formation: Degeneration of the annulus fibrosis of the disc renders it unable to withstand the stresses of axial loading, causing it to bulge outward. Over time, this bulging of the annulus on the vertebral edge leads to formation of an osteophyte at the site of Sharpey fibers attachment between the anulus fibrosus and the anterior margin of the vertebral body just above or below the end-plate margin from where they grow outward. An osteophyte eventually grows and meets an osteophyte on the other side of the disc space and may be called a bridging osteophyte. The latter have to be differentiated form a Syndesmophyte. Syndesmophytes are ossifications of the anulus fibrosus and are more vertically oriented, attaching right at the end-plate margin.

DISH in the spine, however, is an ossification of the anterior longitudinal ligament and, similar to an osteophyte, is also seen originating away from the end-plate margin. It has a characteristic undulating nature.

The osteophytes that are small and parallel to the endplate are often referred to as traction osteophytes while those that are larger, curved, and even bridging two adjacent vertebra are sometimes called as claw osteophytes. A common belief is that claw osteophytes are a natural progression from traction osteophytes. They believe that traction osteophytes are the initial stage of an ossification process at an unstable degenerated disc, and that their conversion to claw osteophytes indicates an attempt to increase stability by ossification of annular fibers.

It has traditionally been thought that osteophytes are not painful themselves, but can cause low back pain because of compression of the spinal canal, neural foramen, and/or lateral recess.
