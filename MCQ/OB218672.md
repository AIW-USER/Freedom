## Question

A 4-month-old female born with a neurocognitive syndrome is brought to the emergency department by her parents who state she fell out of her bouncer earlier in the evening. Her right thigh is quite swollen and she has been crying inconsolably. Radiographs obtained of her right leg are shown in Figure A. What is the most important next step in the management of this patient?

![[q218672-a-spiral-femur-peds.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Place long leg cast in emergency department and schedule follow up for 7-10 days
2. Place her in pavlik harness and schedule re-evaluation 2-3 days later
3. Obtain additional imaging and social work evaluation
4. Obtain laboratory workup including CBC, CRP, ESR, vitamin D
5. Perform a sedated MRI of right femur

## Correct option

**3. Obtain additional imaging and social work evaluation**

## Answer explanation

In a 4-month-old with a femur fracture, a non-accidental trauma (NAT) workup must be performed and includes social work evaluation and additional imaging.

Pediatric child abuse affects over 1 million children a year and orthopedic injuries are commonly seen at presentation. Risk factors for NAT include those with disabilities, those born premature or as a result of an unplanned pregnancy, or those with parents who are unemployed, involved in drug use, and who have a lower socioeconomic status. Up to 50% of fractures in children <1 year old and up to 30% of fractures in children <3 have been attributed to NAT, so orthopedic surgeons must have a high degree of suspicion. In addition to appropriately treating the fracture(s), these patients should be admitted to the hospital for social service evaluation and additional imaging such as a skeletal survey.

*Leaman et al. looked at ways to identify non-accidental fractures in children <2 years old, reiterating that fractures are the second most common presentation of child abuse behind soft tissue bruising and burns.* *They analyzed 115 consecutive patients <2 years old with fractures in the ED over two years,* **finding** that 17% were ultimately due to NAT, with 32% of all fractures in children <1 year old being attributed to NAT. Other predictors for non-accidental injuries were multiple fractures, corner fractures, transverse fractures, and children on Medicaid.

*Greenhill et al. reviewed the treatment of pediatric femoral shaft fractures in a JAAOS article,* **suggesting** that NAT should be considered in any child <3 with a femur fracture. They discuss management options with close consideration of patient age, weight, fracture pattern, other injuries, and social setting. Pavlik harnesses, spica casting, internal fixation with plates, flexible nails, and rigid nails are the primary surgical options depending on the aforementioned factors.

Figure A is a lateral radiograph of a right leg in a pediatric patient demonstrating a spiral, midshaft femur fracture.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This fracture pattern would not be appropriately treated with a cast, as it would not stabilize the fracture well and would likely slide off given the size of a 4-month-old. This said, before sending the patient home a NAT workup should be performed.
Answer 2: While a Pavlik harness is the appropriate treatment, this patient should not be sent home without further workup for NAT, as their social situation may place them at risk for further abuse.
Answer 4: Laboratory workup would be appropriate in the setting of metabolic bone disease, however, the first thing that should be performed in this case is a NAT evaluation.
Answer 5: An MRI of the femur might be done if there is concern for pathologic fracture or osteomyelitis. However, in this clinical scenario, this would be very uncommon and certainly would not occur before a NAT workup.
