## Question

A 28-year-old active male undergoes hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) with labral repair and femoroplasty. The procedure is technically challenging, requiring significant traction and manipulation. Postoperatively, in the recovery room, the patient reports severe groin pain and a sensation of the hip "giving out." On examination, the hip is found to be in a position of extension and external rotation, and attempting to internally rotate the hip elicits marked pain and resistance. Radiographs confirm a hip dislocation. Given this patient's presentation, what is the MOST appropriate immediate management strategy?

## Options

1. Emergent open reduction and internal fixation due to high risk of iatrogenic injury.
2. Immediate closed reduction followed by a period of restricted hip flexion and internal rotation.
3. Immediate closed reduction followed by a period of restricted hip extension and external rotation.
4. Placement of the patient in continuous traction for 24 hours to allow for muscle relaxation before reduction.
5. Urgent consultation with a vascular surgeon due to the high risk of femoral artery injury.

## Correct option

**3. Immediate closed reduction followed by a period of restricted hip extension and external rotation.**

## Answer explanation

Immediate closed reduction is the primary treatment for hip dislocations, and the subsequent restriction of hip extension and external rotation helps prevent recurrence of the most common type of iatrogenic hip dislocation seen after arthroscopy (answer 3).

Hip instability, specifically dislocation, is a recognized but serious complication of hip arthroscopy, often due to the forces applied during the procedure. The most common pattern of dislocation after hip arthroscopy is anterior dislocation, which typically occurs when the hip is positioned in extension and external rotation. Therefore, the immediate and most critical step is to perform a prompt closed reduction to restore the joint's alignment, minimize further damage to articular cartilage, and reduce the risk of neurovascular compromise. Following successful reduction, it's crucial to implement postoperative precautions that specifically avoid the position of instability (i.e., further hip extension and external rotation) to promote capsular healing and prevent recurrent dislocation.

*Duplantier et al. conducted a systematic review to investigate hip dislocation or subluxation following hip arthroscopy.* **They noted** that anterior dislocations were the most common instability pattern reported in the literature and frequently occurred with the hip in extension and external rotation. **They concluded** that, while rare, hip instability is a significant complication of hip arthroscopy, often presenting as an anterior dislocation.

*Wylie et al. presented a case report of an anterior hip dislocation following hip arthroscopy for Cam-type femoroacetabular impingement.* *They described the clinical course and subsequent development of progressive arthritis after the dislocation,* **highlighting** the potential long-term consequences of this complication. **They concluded** that hip dislocation after arthroscopy can be a severe complication with significant implications for joint health.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Open reduction is typically reserved for irreducible dislocations or those with associated fractures, not as the immediate first step for an acute dislocation.
Answer 2: This identifies the correct immediate management (closed reduction) but specifies precautions appropriate for a posterior dislocation, not the more common anterior dislocation seen post-arthroscopy in this context.
Answer 4: This approach delays definitive reduction and is not appropriate for an acute hip dislocation, which is a time-sensitive emergency.
Answer 5: While neurovascular assessment is crucial, the immediate priority for hip dislocation is reduction; severe vascular injury is rare and not the primary concern to delay reduction.
