## Question

A 43-year-old obese male (145kg, BMI 43), underwent an uncomplicated right UKA one year ago. He presents to your office today for an evaluation of insidious onset right knee pain that began 1 month ago. Pain is worse when he first begins ambulating and is improved with rest. Radiographs demonstrate a loose tibial component in varus. Laboratory evaluation reveals an ESR of 18 mm/hr (Reference range, 0-20mm/hr) and a CRP of 8 mg/L (Reference range, 0-10mg/L). Which of the following is the best next step in management?

## Options

1. Joint aspiration
2. Explant UKA and placement of an antibiotic spacer
3. Revision UKA tibial component only
4. Revision UKA both components
5. Revision UKA to TKA

## Correct option

**5. Revision UKA to TKA**

## Answer explanation

This patient is experiencing aseptic loosening of the tibial component. In the setting of a normal inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP), a revision to TKA can be performed without further workup.

When compared to TKA, UKA results in a faster recovery, offer more native knee mechanics, and are less expensive. UKA are typically contraindicated in obese patients (>82kg), those with inflammatory arthritis, and those with large deformities or multiple compartment disease. Numerous modes of failure have been identified including tibial loosening, persistent pain, infection, and progression of disease in the other compartments. Aseptic loosening represents the most common cause of early failure (<5 years) following UKA and is present in between 25-45% of patients. Loosening in this patient is likely related to the varus alignment of the tibial component and his increased weight (>82kg). Nevertheless, infection must be ruled out before revision surgery can be performed. In the setting of a normal ESR and CRP, no joint aspiration is needed, and the surgeon may proceed with revision if indicated. Conversely, if the inflammatory markers were elevated, joint aspiration and cell count would be warranted prior to revision surgery.

*Borus et al. review the indications and outcomes of UKA.* **They report** that despite the generally well accepted and stringent selection guidelines, many have expanded the indications to include heavier and younger patients. While some studies report good outcomes in this subset of patients, others report predictive failure in patients with a BMI >32. Furthermore, placing the tibial component in varus places undue stress on the proximal tibial cancellous bone, leading to early failure. **They conclude** that strict surgical indications, patient selection, and meticulous surgical execution are the most important factors to optimizing outcomes.

*Barrett et al. review the revision of failed UKA.* **They report** aseptic loosening to be the most common cause of failure occurring in 55% of patients. Additionally, the study included 3 patients who had a weight of >90kg at the time of surgery, all of which went on to failure. Outcomes of revised UKA to TKA were good or excellent in all but 4 patients in the current study. **They conclude** that proper patient selection and avoidance of technical errors are the keys to success in performing UKA.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: Joint aspiration is not necessary in the setting of normal inflammatory markers in a patient at risk for aseptic loosening.
Answer 2: This patient has aseptic loosening, likely due to poor patient selection and not an infected UKA.
Answers 3 and 4: Revision of UKA should be to TKA, not another UKA, especially given his weight.
