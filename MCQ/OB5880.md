## Question

A patient presents for further evaluation and management of an incidentally discovered lesion pictured in Figure A. Which of the following is true regarding this diagnosis in the pediatric population?

![[q5880-a-oc.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. MRI will likely demonstrate a thin cartilage cap <1 mm.
2. Secondary transformation occurs in approximately 20% of cases.
3. Secondary transformation often results in a low-grade tumor, typically cured with surgery alone.
4. Treatment should consist of radiotherapy alone.
5. Treatment should consist of chemotherapy and excision.

## Correct option

**3. Secondary transformation often results in a low-grade tumor, typically cured with surgery alone.**

## Answer explanation

Secondary transformation of an osteochondroma to a chondrosarcoma is extremely rare; however, when it does occur, it commonly results in a low-grade tumor (~90%) that can most often can be cured with surgery alone.

Osteochondroma is a benign chondrogenic exophytic lesion commonly located juxtacortical in the metaphysis. It is a common lesion in adolescents and young adults. Osteochondromas are not inherently painful and symptoms are generally caused by irritation of overlying soft tissue. Symptomatic lesions can undergo marginal resection for pain relief, while asymptomatic lesions can be safely observed. Secondary transformation to a chondrosarcoma is very rare, occurring in <1% of cases. These secondary chondrosarcomas are often low-grade lesions and can be cured by wide resection alone.

*Wodajo et al reviewed the presentation and radiographic appearance of common benign lesions including lipoma, enchondroma, nonossifying fibroma, Paget disease and osteochondroma.* With regards to osteochondromas, **the authors recommend** annual radiographs in young children to observe the expected growth associated with skeletal development, as well as monitor for the occurrence of musculoskeletal symptoms.

*Nystrom et al detailed the case of an 11-year-old patient with secondary chondrosarcoma arising from a solitary osteochondroma.* This example of malignant transformation of a solitary exostosis is extremely rare in the pediatric population. This patient was treated with wide resection without adjuvant therapy and is doing well without recurrence five years post-operatively.

Figure A is an anteroposterior radiograph of the right knee in a skeletally immature patient demonstrating a pedunculated exophytic lesion arising from the distal femoral metaphysis, most consistent with an osteochondroma.

Incorrect Responses:
Answer 1: MRI of an osteochondroma in the pediatric patient will demonstrate a larger cartilage cap (1-3cm) compared to adult patients. In the adult population, osteochondromas can be distinguished from secondary chondrosarcomas by a thin (< 2cm) cartilage cap. Therefore, MRI of this lesion is not as useful in the pediatric population.
Answer 2: Secondary transformation is rare, thought to occur in <1% of cases.
Answers 4 and 5: Treatment of depends on symptoms. Asymptomatic patients can be observed while symptomatic patients may under simple excision. There is no role for adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation.
