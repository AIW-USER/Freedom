## Question

A 13-year-old boy twists his ankle while playing soccer and is unable to bear weight. He has focal tenderness at the anterolateral joint line. A radiograph of the injured ankle is shown in Figure A, and CT scan confirms a Salter–Harris III fracture of the anterolateral distal tibia. Which injury mechanism most likely produced this fracture pattern?

![[q220927-a-img-0643-preview.jpg]]
*Figure A*

## Options

1. Pronation/eversion–external rotation
2. Supination/eversion–external rotation
3. Supination–plantarflexion
4. Axial compression
5. Supination–Inversion

## Correct option

**2. Supination/eversion–external rotation**

## Answer explanation

Supination/eversion–external rotation is correct, as this mechanism tensions the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL), avulsing the anterolateral distal tibial epiphysis in adolescents with a partially open physis, producing a Tillaux fracture (answer choice 2).

Tillaux fractures are Salter–Harris (SH) III injuries of the anterolateral distal tibial epiphysis that occur near skeletal maturity during the transitional period of physeal closure. Standard three-view ankle radiographs are the initial step in diagnostic evaluation, while CT is typically employed to help define articular displacement. Treatment is typically nonoperative if <2 mm of residual displacement remains after closed reduction and operative if >2 mm of residual displacement remains after closed reduction. The distal tibial physis closes in a predictable sequence (central, anteromedial, posteromedial, lateral), leaving the lateral physis last to fuse and therefore vulnerable to avulsion by the AITFL in this age group. Mechanistically, these fractures result from AITFL avulsion when the foot is in a supination/eversion position and an external rotation force is applied.

*Wuerz and Gurd reviewed pediatric physeal ankle fractures, describing the juvenile Tillaux fracture as an SH-III injury of the anterolateral epiphysis produced by everting a supinated foot, where external rotation transfers force through the anterior tibiofibular ligament to avulse the epiphysis at the open physis.* *They discussed a variety of mechanisms and resulting injury patterns, outlining their respective evaluation, management, and complications.* **They concluded** that management is ultimately determined by the amount of growth remaining, with the goal of limiting physeal damage and joint incongruity to optimize outcomes.

*Lurie et al. retrospectively studied 57 Tillaux and triplane fractures with 2–5 mm of articular gap to compare outcomes of surgical versus nonoperative treatment.* **They found** worse functional outcomes with a greater residual gap and with nonoperative treatment. Surgical management conferred the greatest functional benefit when the gap exceeded about 2.5 mm at the plafond. **They concluded** that a greater gap after closed reduction, nonoperative treatment, and complications were negative predictors of functional outcome at a mean follow-up of 4.5 years.

Figure A is an AP ankle radiograph of a skeletally immature individual, demonstrating a sagittal-plane fracture line extending from the lateral tibia physis to the tibiotalar joint, consistent with a Tillaux fracture. Illustration A, from the Wuerz and Gurd review, summarizes the Saltar–Harris classification: (A) Type I, physeal separation; (B) Type II, physeal fracture with metaphyseal extension; (C) Type III, physeal fracture with epiphyseal extension; (D) Type IV, fracture traversing the epiphysis, physis, and metaphysis; (E) Type V, crush injury to the physis. Illustration B, also from the Wuerz and Gurd review, summarizes the Dias-Tachdjian classification of pediatric ankle fractures: (A) Supination-inversion; (B) Pronation/eversion-external rotation; (C) Supination-plantarflexion; (D) Supination-external rotation. Of note, the supination-external rotation mechanism can produce the fracture pattern illustrated in the Dias-Tachdjian classification, or the classic Tillaux pattern.

Incorrect answer choices:
Answer choice 1: As described in the Dias–Tachdijan classification, pronation/eversion–external rotation typically leads to SH 2 fractures of the lateral tibia, with an associated suprasyndesmotic fibula fracture.
Answer choice 3: Supination–plantarflexion injuries typically lead to fracture extension through the posterior distal tibia.
Answer choice 4: Vertical axial compression mechanisms typically lead to an SH V fracture pattern, or damage to the tibiotalar cartilage surfaces.
Answer choice 5: Supination–inversion is the typical mechanism for ankle sprains, rather than Tillaux fractures.
