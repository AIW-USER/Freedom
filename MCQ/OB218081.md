## Question

A 65-year-old male is being seen for follow-up for a chief complaint of digit pain. The patient's symptoms have been recalcitrant to appropriate nonoperative management and he is interested in surgical intervention. Which of the following is best treated with arthrodesis?

## Options

1. Thumb carpometacarpal arthritis with sclerosis and < 2mm of osteophyte formation
2. Index finger proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) contracture with no radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis
3. Ring finger PIPJ arthritis with joint space narrowing and no evidence of joint instability
4. Ring finger distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ) arthritis with marked joint space narrowing
5. Thumb carpmetacarpal arthritis with slight joint-space narrowing and evidence of joint hypermobility and 14° of hyperextension

## Correct option

**4. Ring finger distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ) arthritis with marked joint space narrowing**

## Answer explanation

Arthrodesis is the appropriate surgical management for patients DIPJ arthritis of lesser digits (Answer 4). The other options listed are best treated with other surgical procedures.

Arthritis of the DIP and PIP joints are very common forms of osteoarthritis seen in the hand and are often associated with pain and deformity. The diagnosis is typically made radiographically, with joint space narrowing of the DIP and PIP joints routinely observed. It is not uncommon for mucous cysts of the DIP joint to be present. The DIPJ is the most common location for hand osteoarthritis, followed by the thumb CMC, then the digital PIP, and finally the digital MCP. The DIP is susceptible to osteoarthritis as this joint is exposed to the highest joint forces in the hand.

The initial treatment is observation if the patient is minimally symptomatic. In patients with more profound disease, a variety of surgical interventions are available. DIPJ arthritis is typically treated with fusion. PIPJ arthritis of the border (index and small) digits or the central digits with significant angulation, ligamentous instability, or poor bone stock are also treated with fusion, whereas central digits (long and ring) with good bone stock and no angulation and/or deformity are treated with silicone arthroplasty.

*Wu et al. provide a review of arthritis of the thumb IPJ and finger DIPJ.* **The authors note** that due to the DIP joints being subjected to the highest joint forces in the hand, at least 60% of individuals over the age of 60 have signs of DIPJ arthritis. Promisingly, however, **the authors conclude** that independent of the fusion method, DIP fusions heal reliably, are well tolerated, and provide excellent pain relief for patients.

*Kocak et al. provide a case series of 64 patients who underwent DIPJ fusions with the Herbert headless compression screw.* Overall, 61/64 (95%) of patients achieved successful union, with a majority reaching successful arthrodesis within 3 months. Three patients required revision arthrodesis, and five patients required hardware removal due to symptomatic hardware.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: sedentary patients with stage II basilar thumb arthritis are best treated with trapeziectomy +/- ligament reconstruction.
Answer 2: patients with an isolated contracture and no radiographic arthritis can be treated with collateral ligament excision, volar plate release and (if present) osteophyte excision.
Answer 3: patients with ring finger PIPJ arthritis and no ligamentous instability are best treated with silicone arthroplasty.
Answer 5: patients with basilar thumb hypermobility and a hyperextension deformity, in the setting of mild osteoarthritis, are best treated with a metacarpal osteotomy.
