## Question

A 38-year-old athlete undergoes MRI evaluation for chronic knee pain, revealing early cartilage degeneration in the weight-bearing region of the femoral condyle. During your discussion of cartilage biomechanics, you explain how different extracellular matrix components contribute to the tissue's mechanical properties. Which of the following best describes the primary biomechanical function of type II collagen in articular cartilage?

## Options

1. Generates osmotic swelling pressure through fixed negative charges to resist compressive loads
2. Provides tensile strength and reinforcement to the proteoglycan-rich matrix
3. Regulates hydraulic permeability during dynamic loading and unloading cycles
4. Anchors chondrocytes to the extracellular matrix through integrin-mediated adhesion
5. Distributes compressive forces from the articular surface to subchondral bone

## Correct option

**2. Provides tensile strength and reinforcement to the proteoglycan-rich matrix**

## Answer explanation

Type II collagen fibrils provide the primary tensile reinforcement of articular cartilage’s highly hydrated proteoglycan gel (Answer 2).

While proteoglycans generate osmotic swelling pressure, allowing cartilage to resist compressive loads, the type II collagen network supplies the structural restraint that limits excessive swelling and prevents failure under tension. In the superficial zone of cartilage, where collagen fibers are oriented parallel to the articular surface and primarily loaded in tension, the collagen network can bear approximately 20% of the applied load across various strain rates. This tensile reinforcement is critical for tissue integrity, as degradation of the collagen network leads to surface fibrillation and progressive cartilage degeneration.

*Fox et al. describe articular cartilage as a specialized connective tissue composed primarily of type II collagen and proteoglycans, arranged in a depth-dependent zonal architecture that enables load bearing and low-friction joint motion.* Compressive stiffness is generated by proteoglycan-driven osmotic swelling pressure, while tensile strength is provided by the collagen fibril network, together forming a viscoelastic tissue that is approximately 70% water. **The authors emphasize** that this structure–function relationship is central to cartilage repair, as the tissue’s avascularity and limited healing capacity make restoration of its complex hierarchical organization particularly challenging.

*Wang and Ateshian* **demonstrated** that articular cartilage exhibits a velocity-dependent “normal stress effect” during frictional shear, in which sliding increases compressive normal stress and effectively raises compressive stiffness by up to 3.1-fold. **They showed** that the equilibrium friction coefficient is both velocity- and strain-dependent, increasing with sliding velocity but decreasing with greater compressive strain due to non-proportional increases in normal and shear stresses. **The authors concluded** that this behavior reflects intrinsic, biphasic properties of cartilage, which distinguish it from conventional solid materials and have important implications for cartilage biomechanics and lubrication.

*Korhonen et al.* **demonstrated** that collagen orientation and depth-dependent fixed charge density shape the chondrocyte mechanical environment by controlling cell deformation, stress, and fluid flow in a zone-specific manner. Their modeling showed that collagen stiffness mainly governs cellular stresses and fluid flow, whereas fixed charge density primarily influences cell shape, **highlighting** the importance of realistic depth-dependent cartilage architecture for predicting chondrocyte mechanobiology.

Incorrect Answers:
Answer 1: This describes the function of proteoglycans (particularly aggrecan), not type II collagen. The negatively charged glycosaminoglycan side chains of proteoglycans create the fixed charge density that generates osmotic swelling pressure.
Answer 3: While the collagen network does influence fluid flow through the tissue, hydraulic permeability is primarily a property of the composite tissue structure as a whole (the porous solid matrix and interstitial fluid), not a primary biomechanical function of type II collagen itself.
Answer 4: This is the function of other matrix proteins, particularly fibronectin and type VI collagen in the pericellular matrix, which bind to chondrocyte integrins. Type II collagen can bind integrins, but this is not its primary biomechanical function.
Answer 5: This is more attributable to the water content of articular cartilage, which is structured by proteoglycans and collagen, and enables effective distribution of compressive forces from the articular surface to subchondral bone.
