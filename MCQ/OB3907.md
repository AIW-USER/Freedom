## Question

A 70-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes presents with an erythematous, swollen, and warm left foot, as depicted in Figure A. Her radiograph is depicted in Figure B. What is her diagnosis and a common clinical examination finding associated with the diagnosis?

*Figure A*

![[q3907-b-charcot-midfoot.jpg]]

## Options

1. Cellulitis; erythema decreases after elevation
2. Cellulitis; abnormal Semmes-Weinstein monofilament testing
3. Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS); erythema decreases after elevation
4. Charcot arthropathy; erythema decreases after elevation
5. Charcot arthropathy; erythema increases after elevation

## Correct option

**4. Charcot arthropathy; erythema decreases after elevation**

## Answer explanation

This patient's clinical history, exam, and radiographic findings are consistent with Charcot arthropathy of the foot. A classic finding often observed in patients with Charcot arthropathy of the foot and ankle is dissipation of the dependent erythema after minutes of elevation.

Charcot arthropathy is a progressive, non-infectious, destructive disease of the bones and joints in patients with sensory neuropathy. Diabetic neuropathy is the most common cause of Charcot neuroarthropathy, with the foot and ankle the most commonly affected locations. Common clinical findings of Charcot arthropathy almost always include inflammation, with significant unilateral swelling, warmth, and erythema, often mimicking infection. The disappearance of erythema with elevation is the key finding differentiating Charcot arthropathy from cellulitis.

*van der Ven et al. published a review article on Charcot neuroarthropathy of the foot and ankle.* **They reported** that disease progression prevention is the mainstay of treatment, with few promising pharmacologic therapies directed toward decreasing bone resorption. **They recommend** surgical intervention be reserved for refractory cases.

*Thompson et al. reviewed Charcot neuropathic osteoarthropathy in the diabetic foot.* **They reported** that a delay in diagnosis and treatment results in progression and ulcer formation in nearly half of patients, ultimately increasing amputation risk and decreasing overall survival. **They concluded** that health care providers should institute a multidisciplinary approach in managing these patients, ensuring compliance with weight-bearing restrictions, therapeutic footwear, soft-tissue protection, and prolonged immobilization to decrease complications and improve outcomes.

Figure A depicts an erythematous foot, which is often confused with cellulitis and may result in misdiagnosis of Charcot arthropathy. Figure B is a radiograph depicting disruption of the bony architecture of the midfoot, which is the hallmark of Charcot arthropathy. Illustration A depicts the Eichenholtz classification of Charcot arthropathy with predictable clinical and radiographic patterns.

Incorrect Answers:
Answers 1 & 2: The radiographic findings make isolated cellulitis unlikely. Furthermore, the disappearance of erythema with elevation is the key finding differentiating Charcot arthropathy from infection (cellulitis).
Answer 3: CRPS is caused by an aberrant inflammatory response and typically do not exhibit radiographic findings depicted in Figure A.
Answer 5: In Charcot arthropathy, the erythema decreases after elevation.
