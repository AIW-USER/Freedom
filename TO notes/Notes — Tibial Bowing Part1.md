Tibial Bowing Part 1 – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Tibial bowing in children is a broad diagnostic spectrum rather than a single entity.
Correct interpretation depends on identifying the apex of deformity in coronal and
sagittal planes. This lecture focuses on the most important causes of tibial bowing
encountered in pediatric orthopedics and exams: - Congenital Pseudoarthrosis of Tibia
(CPT) - Posteromedial Tibial Bowing - Tibia Vara (Blount disease) - Overview of
hemimelias (introduction only)
Early differentiation is critical, as some conditions remodel spontaneously while others
are limb-threatening.

Principle of Apex Identification
Any tibial bowing must be described by: - Apex on AP view (medial or lateral) - Apex on
lateral view (anterior or posterior)

### Diagnostic Clues

| Apex Combination | Likely Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Anterolateral | Congenital pseudoarthrosis of tibia |
| Posteromedial | Posteromedial tibial bowing |
| Anteromedial | Fibular hemimelia |

Exam Pearl: Always describe tibial bowing by apex direction, not by visual
appearance alone.

Congenital Pseudoarthrosis of Tibia (CPT)
Definition
A rare, challenging condition characterized by anterolateral bowing of tibia with a high
risk of fracture and development of a mobile pseudoarthrosis.

### Epidemiology and Associations
- One of the most difficult problems in pediatric orthopedics
- 6% of patients with NF‑1 develop CPT
- 50–55% of CPT cases are associated with NF‑1
- Associated with fibrous dysplasia in ~15%

<!-- page 2 -->

### NF‑1 Clinical Clues
- Café‑au‑lait spots (coast of Maine borders)
- Axillary freckling
- Neurofibromas
- Lisch nodules
- Optic glioma
- Skeletal involvement (scoliosis, CPT)
Always perform a head‑to‑toe examination in suspected CPT.
### Pathophysiology of CPT
- Disease is periosteal, not a simple nonunion
- Presence of fibrous hamartoma between bone ends
- Abnormal periosteum with poor osteogenic potential
- Reduced BMP production and increased osteoclast activity
- Medullary canal often sclerosed
Treating CPT as a routine nonunion leads to failure.
Radiological Types and Classifications
### Crawford Classification
- Type I: Anterolateral bowing without fracture
- Type II: Cystic lesion
- Type III: Pseudoarthrosis
- Type IV: Sclerotic segment
### Boyd Classification (High Yield)
- Type II: Hourglass constriction, called high‑risk tibia
- Strong association with NF‑1
### Paley Classification
- Type I: Mobile pseudoarthrosis, no prior surgery
- Type II: Pseudoarthrosis after failed surgery
- Type III: Fracture without frank mobility

Indications for Surgery in CPT
Absolute indication - Development of pseudoarthrosis or fracture

<!-- page 3 -->

Not indications by themselves: - Severity of bowing - Failure of brace to correct
deformity
Role of Bracing - Prevents fracture - Does not correct deformity
### Principles of CPT Management
- Complete excision of fibrous hamartoma
- Removal of abnormal periosteum
- Recanalization of medullary canal
- Rigid fixation (preferably intramedullary)
- Large cross‑sectional union mass
- Long‑term protection until skeletal maturity

Four‑in‑One Osteosynthesis Concept
Components
1. Excision of fibrous tissue
2. Acute docking and compression
3. Tibio‑fibular cross‑union
4. Circumferential corticocancellous bone graft
Adjuncts
- Intramedullary telescopic rod
- Plate fixation if required
- Pharmacologic stimulation (IV Zoledronate pre and post op)
Cross‑union reduces refracture rates significantly.
### Complications of CPT
- Refracture (most common)
- Ankle valgus due to fibular growth disturbance
- Limb length discrepancy
- Infection

Posteromedial Tibial Bowing
### Key Features
- Congenital deformity
- Apex posterior on lateral view, medial on AP view

<!-- page 4 -->

- Often associated with calcaneovalgus foot
- Must be differentiated from positional calcaneovalgus
### Natural History
- Bowing remodels spontaneously
- Fractures are rare
- Persistent problem is limb length discrepancy
### Long‑Term Outcome
- Tibial and fibular shortening of 3–7 cm
- Shortening proportional to initial bowing
Management
- Observation in infancy
- No corrective osteotomy for bowing
- Later management focuses on limb length equality

Tibia Vara (Blount Disease)
Etiology
- Dysplastic proximal medial tibial physis
- Associated with:
  - Early weight bearing
  - High BMI
  - Certain ethnic groups
### Deformity Components
- Varus
- Internal tibial torsion
- Recurvatum
- Limb length discrepancy
- Progressive nature

Radiological Assessment in Blount Disease
### Metaphyseal‑Diaphyseal Angle (Drennan)
- <11°: Normal
- 11–16°: Observe closely
- 16°: Blount disease
### Langenskiöld Staging
- Stage I–II: May resolve

<!-- page 5 -->

- Stage III–VI: Progressive, surgical

### Management Overview in Blount Disease
- Infantile type (early stages): Bracing
- Progressive or advanced stages:
  - Corrective osteotomy
  - Growth modulation
  - Medial tibial plateau elevation (selected cases)
Goal is correction of mechanical axis, not cosmetic alignment.
Faculty Quote
“Congenital pseudoarthrosis of tibia is not a bone problem. It is a periosteal disease.
Unless you remove the pathology completely, union will never occur.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- Always define tibial bowing by apex direction
- Anterolateral bowing suggests CPT
- 6% of NF‑1 patients develop CPT
- 50% of CPT cases have NF‑1 association
- Surgery in CPT is indicated only after fracture or pseudoarthrosis
- Posteromedial bowing remodels, limb length discrepancy persists
- Bracing in CPT prevents fracture, does not correct deformity
- MD angle >16° confirms Blount disease
