### Overview / Key Concepts
- Adult scoliosis: a coronal deformity with a Cobb angle of more than 10° in a skeletally mature patient.
- Primary degenerative ("de novo") scoliosis: a curve that develops in a previously straight spine because of asymmetric disc and facet degeneration from middle age.
- In degenerative scoliosis the coronal curve itself matters little; sagittal imbalance (stooping forward) drives pain and disability.
- Most patients do well with conservative care (analgesics, osteoporosis treatment, flexion exercises, blocks). Surgery has high complication rates and is the last resort.
- Surgery is chosen by symptoms and balance: decompression alone, decompression + short fusion, or long fixation with osteotomy.

Classification
Aebi classification (aetiology)

| Type | Name | Features |
|---|---|---|
| I | Primary degenerative (de novo) | Starts late in life from UNEVEN wear of discs and facets; mostly lumbar or thoracolumbar; presents with back pain and spinal stenosis (radicular or claudication pain) |
| II | Progressive idiopathic | Childhood or adolescent curve (untreated AIS) that keeps progressing into adulthood; degenerative changes are less than in type I |
| III | Secondary adult curves | Caused by something outside the spine: pelvic tilt or limb length discrepancy, osteoporosis with fractures, metabolic bone disease, asymmetric joint disease |

- Only describes aetiology; says nothing about presentation, severity or treatment.

Schwab classification
- Links radiological parameters with clinical outcome: lumbar lordosis, apex level and lateral subluxation.
- Lower apex, less lordosis and more lateral subluxation mean a poorer outcome.
- Modifiers: curve location (types I–V by apex level, K = sagittal only), lordosis modifier, subluxation modifier (0, 1–6 mm, more than 6 mm), global balance (C7 offset: normal 0–4 cm, positive 4–9.5 cm, very positive more than 9.5 cm).
- Drawback: does not take pelvic parameters and global sagittal balance into account properly.

SRS classification
- Copies the adolescent curve types: single thoracic, double thoracic, double major, triple major, thoracolumbar, lumbar (de novo or idiopathic), and primary sagittal plane deformity.
- Purely radiological; ignores symptoms; does not guide treatment.

(SVA)
Pelvic tilt (PT) Less than 20° 20–30° More than 30°

- Pelvic incidence (PI): angle between the line from the centre of the femoral heads to the midpoint of the S1 end plate and the perpendicular to the end plate at that point. It is fixed for life; normally lumbar lordosis roughly equals PI.
- PI minus LL: shows how much lordosis has been lost. In surgery, aim to restore lordosis close to the PI.
- SVA: horizontal distance of the C7 plumb line in front of the posterosuperior corner of S1 on a standing X-ray.
- Pelvic tilt: rises as the patient compensates for imbalance; PT more than 30° signals significant imbalance.
- Lumbar curves and primary sagittal deformity cause more disability than thoracic or double curves.
- Higher sagittal modifier grades predict the need for osteotomy, iliac fixation or decompression; the classification reflects severity and guides treatment.

Pathophysiology
- Asymmetric degeneration of the disc and facets (end-plate erosion and disc narrowing on one side), like the varus knee in medial OA.
- Asymmetric loading then speeds up the curve.
- Osteoporosis and sarcopenia add to it; more common in women (osteoporosis); onset after about 50 years and incidence rises with age.
- Also involved: inflammation, increased angiogenesis and extracellular matrix degradation.

Risk factors for progression
- Curve more than 30°.
- Asymmetric (wedged) discs above and below the apical vertebra.
- Lateral subluxation of the apical vertebra more than 6 mm.
- L5 lying above rather than below the intercrestal line (a less "deep-seated" L5).

Clinical Features
Back pain (60–80%)
- Usually on the CONVEX side.
- Causes: degeneration, and muscle fatigue from imbalance.
- Curve size does not correlate with pain; sagittal imbalance does.

Radicular pain and neurological symptoms
- Spinal stenosis is seen in about 90% of degenerative scoliosis vs about 30% of adult idiopathic scoliosis.
- Concave side (more common): foraminal stenosis from facet hypertrophy, narrowing of the distance between pedicles (pedicle kinking) on lateral bending, and degeneration.
- Convex side: nerve root stretching.

Deformity
- Unlike adolescents, these patients rarely complain of the coronal curve. The main problem is stooping forward (loss of lordosis), even when there is hardly any scoliosis.

Radiology
- Standing full-length X-ray (AP and lateral): location, extent and Cobb angle of the curve, degeneration, balance parameters.
- Look closely at the lumbosacral junction for listhesis and root compression.
- MRI for stenosis; CT when planning surgery (pedicle size).

Treatment
Conservative (first line; most patients do well)
- Asymptomatic or incidental curves: observe.
- Analgesics and local applications.
- Treat osteoporosis (calcium, bisphosphonates, teriparatide); improving bone density helps the symptoms.
- LS belt for support.
- Spinal flexion exercises: open the foramina and relieve radicular symptoms; pregabalin for radicular pain.
- Injections: facet block for facet tenderness, selective nerve root block, epidural injection.

Indications for surgery
- Back pain failing conservative care.
- Progressive leg pain or neurological deficit.
- Muscle fatigue from spinal imbalance.
- Curve progression (often more than 3° per year).

- Progressive pulmonary compromise from the deformity.
- Severe deformity.

Choice of surgery

| Operation | When |
|---|---|
| Decompression alone | Mainly claudication or radicular leg pain; no instability, no listhesis, no imbalance. Selective (microscopic) decompression: partial medial facetectomy and ligamentum flavum removal at the stenotic level |
| Decompression + posterior instrumented fusion | Back pain + leg pain with instability or listhesis at |
| (short segment) | 1–2 levels and NO sagittal imbalance (e.g. L4–L5 degenerative listhesis with a mild curve: L4–L5 TLIF). Ignore the mild scoliosis |
| Long segment fixation | Sagittal imbalance (stooping, fatigue pain). Restore lordosis by multilevel TLIF (cages open the front, compression behind) or, in stiff curves, osteotomy (usually pedicle subtraction osteotomy) + long fixation. Think osteotomy when PI minus LL is more than 20° |

- Before long fusion, give conservative care and osteoporosis treatment first, and explain the risks.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Adult scoliosis = Cobb more than 10° in a skeletally mature spine; de novo = develops in a previously straight spine.
- Aebi: I de novo, II progressive idiopathic, III secondary.
- Degenerative vs idiopathic: older, smaller curve, no compensatory curve, rotation at apex, lateral subluxation, stenosis common.
- SRS-Schwab: curve types T, L, D, N + modifiers PI-LL, SVA, PT (0 / + / ++).
- Progression risks: curve more than 30°, asymmetric discs at the apex, lateral subluxation more than 6 mm, high L5 relative to the intercrestal line.
- Back pain on the convex side; radicular pain on the concave side.
- Stenosis in 90% of degenerative vs 30% of idiopathic adult curves.
- Surgery for progression more than 3° a year or SVA more than 4 cm; osteotomy if PI-LL more than 20°.

### Take-Home Summary
- De novo scoliosis is a degenerative, often osteoporotic, older-age problem in which sagittal balance matters more than the coronal curve.
- Classify with SRS-Schwab; check PI-LL, SVA and PT on a standing full-length film.
- Treat conservatively first; match surgery to the problem: stenosis (decompress), focal instability (short fusion), imbalance (long fusion + osteotomy). Expect high complication rates.
