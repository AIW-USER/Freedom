### Overview / Key Concepts
- Part 2 of energy systems (part 1: phosphocreatine, anaerobic and aerobic systems). Focus: how blood lactate tracks exercise intensity, the lactate and ventilatory thresholds, OBLA, MLSS, RER and performance tests. Recent exam questions have been graph-based.
- All three energy systems work together at all times; exercise intensity decides which one predominates.
- Core idea: blood lactate rises with intensity NOT simply because more lactate is produced, but because the BALANCE between lactate appearance (production) and disappearance (transport, uptake, oxidation, recycling) changes.

### Lactate Production and Fate
- Higher intensity = higher ATP turnover = more glycolytic flux; substrate shifts from fat (needs oxygen) to carbohydrate.
- Pyruvate has two fates: with oxygen it enters the mitochondria (pyruvate dehydrogenase, acetyl-CoA, TCA cycle, electron transport chain = oxidative ATP); with low oxygen it becomes lactate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH).
- Pyruvate + NADH + H+ to lactate + NAD+: this regenerates NAD+, which is essential for glycolysis to continue (substrate-level ATP). Lactate formation is therefore useful, not just a waste product.
- At physiological pH about 99% of lactic acid is dissociated into lactate anions and H+; in the body it exists as LACTATE. Mainly the L(+) form. Resting production about 20 mmol/kg/day. Lactate is produced and removed continuously, even at rest.
- Lactate is also an energy substrate, a gluconeogenic precursor and a signalling molecule.

- During intense exercise appearance exceeds removal, so blood lactate rises. In recovery production falls while muscle, heart and liver keep clearing it; active recovery (e.g. massage, foam rolling, light work) speeds this up.

Is lactate the cause of fatigue?
- Not on its own: fatigue is MULTIFACTORIAL. Rising H+ (lower intracellular pH) impairs actomyosin cross-bridges, calcium handling and excitation-contraction coupling; inorganic phosphate rises; phosphocreatine falls (lasts only seconds); ADP/AMP and other metabolites accumulate.
- The aim is to TRAIN so lactate accumulates only at a higher workload, rather than simply "flushing lactate" in recovery.

Lactate threshold vs ventilatory threshold
- Minute ventilation (VE) = tidal volume x respiratory rate.
- Extra H+ is buffered by bicarbonate: H+ + HCO3- to H2CO3 to CO2 + H2O. The extra CO2 drives ventilation up: VT1 (ventilatory threshold) coincides with LT1.
- At still higher intensity CO2 rises so much that the athlete hyperventilates: VT2 = respiratory compensation point, coinciding with OBLA/LT2.

### Exam Question Discussed
- Elite cyclist, incremental test: lactate stable early, then rises, then rises steeply. Best explanation: C, increasing intensity progressively alters fibre recruitment and glycolytic flux, while lactate production, transport and removal set the blood level. Wrong options: an ABRUPT aerobic-to-anaerobic switch; lactate produced only when oxygen delivery stops completely (it is produced even at rest); lactate equals oxygen debt.

### Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER)
- RER = VCO2 / VO2.

<!-- page 6 -->

- About 0.7 (0.70 to 0.80) at rest/low intensity = mainly fat; rises with moderate intensity as carbohydrate use increases; about 1.0 = pure carbohydrate oxidation (high intensity); above 1.0 near maximal exercise (bicarbonate buffering of H+ adds extra CO2, hyperventilation/respiratory compensation).

### Performance Testing
- Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET): integrated cardiovascular, respiratory and metabolic response; incremental exercise on treadmill or cycle ergometer, breath-by-breath gas analysis, continuous ECG and heart rate, blood pressure and SpO2.

| CPET variable | Normal value |
|---|---|
| Peak VO2 | More than 84% predicted |
| Ventilatory anaerobic threshold | More than 40% of peak VO2 (40 to 80%) |
| Maximum heart rate | More than 90% age predicted |
| Heart rate reserve | Under 15 beats/min |
| Blood pressure | Under 220/90 |
| O2 pulse (VO2/HR) | More than 80% |
| Ventilatory reserve | MVV minus VEmax more than 11 L, or VEmax/MVV x 100 under 85% |
| Respiratory rate | Under 60 breaths/min |
| VE/VCO2 at anaerobic threshold | Under 34 |

- Aerobic capacity (VO2 max/peak): CPET, 1.5-mile (Cooper) test, Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (VO2 max covered in a later lecture). Thresholds: LT1/VT1, LT2/VT2, lactate profile. Speed: 5, 10, 20, 30 m and flying sprints.
- Incremental lactate test: capillary blood from the earlobe or fingertip at each stage; training zones set from LT2:

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Blood lactate = balance of appearance and disappearance, not production alone.
- LDH reaction regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue.
- MCT4 exports lactate; MCT1 imports it (heart, brain, oxidative muscle, liver).
- LT1 = aerobic threshold (type IIa join); LT2 = anaerobic threshold/OBLA about 4 mmol/L (type IIx).
- VT1 coincides with LT1; VT2 (respiratory compensation point) with OBLA.

<!-- page 7 -->

- OBLA is a fixed 4 mmol/L criterion; MLSS is an individual workload that training raises.
- Training shifts the lactate curve to the right; it does not stop lactate production.
- RER 0.7 fat, 1.0 carbohydrate, above 1.0 near maximal exercise.

### Take-Home Summary
- Read lactate graphs as a balance story: as intensity rises, fibre recruitment and glycolytic flux increase until clearance cannot keep up (LT1, then LT2/OBLA), ventilation follows the CO2 load (VT1, VT2), and endurance training raises the workload at which these points occur by improving lactate clearance and oxidation.
