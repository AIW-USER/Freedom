Perthes Disease & Idiopathic Chondrolysis of Hip –
Dr. Rahul Upadhyay
Overview / Key Concepts
This lecture is a high-yield revision-oriented integration of Perthes disease and
idiopathic chondrolysis of the hip, focusing on definitions, classifications, prognostic
factors, and exam-relevant management principles. Special emphasis is laid on
classification utility, age-based decision making, and differentiation from similar
conditions.

Perthes Disease
Definition
Idiopathic avascular necrosis of the capital femoral epiphysis in a growing child,
leading to altered femoral head growth, deformation, and potential early osteoarthritis.
If an underlying cause is identified, it is not Perthes disease.
Epidemiology
- Common age: 4–12 years
- Male predominance: 4–5 : 1
- Bilateral involvement: 10–15%
- Accounts for ~3% of pediatric hip disorders

Etiology and Risk Factors
Perthes has multifactorial etiology. No single proven cause.

### Likely Associations
- Trauma in susceptible child
- Passive smoking (strongest correlation)
- Hyperactivity
- Coagulopathies
- Hereditary predisposition
### Unlikely Associations
- Endocrinopathies

- Urban environment
- Synovitis

Predisposed Child (High-Yield Concept)
Definition: Child with delayed bone age compared to chronological age.
- Bone age lags by 2–4 years
- Skeletal standstill during disease activity
- Bilateral disease shows more severe delay
### Commonly Affected Carpal Bones
- Unilateral disease: Lunate, Triquetrum
- Bilateral disease: Trapezoid

### Clinical Features
- Painless limp (most common)
- Activity-related hip or knee pain
- Restricted abduction and internal rotation
- Muscle wasting of thigh
- Antalgic + Trendelenburg gait
- Late: Limb length discrepancy

Imaging
### X-rays (Primary Modality)
- AP pelvis + Frog-leg lateral mandatory
- Sensitivity ~97%
- Changes may lag symptoms by up to 8 months
### MRI
- Gold standard for early diagnosis
- Best for extent of necrosis and cartilage status
### Bone Scintigraphy
- Early detection
- High sensitivity and specificity

### Staging Classification (Waldenström)

| Stage | Key Features |
|---|---|
| I | Increased density, joint space widening |
| II | Fragmentation, subchondral fractures |
| III | Reossification |
| IV | Healing and remodeling |

Prognostic Classifications (Active Stage)
### Catterall Classification

| Group | Head Involvement | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| I | Anterior epiphysis | Good |
| II | Central involvement | Fair |
| III | Lateral involvement | Poor |
| IV | Total head | Worst |

### Head-at-Risk Signs (Mnemonic: CGMLH)
  - Calcification lateral to epiphysis
  - Gage sign
  - Metaphyseal cyst
  - Lateral subluxation
  - Horizontal physis

### Herring Lateral Pillar Classification (Most Reliable)

| Type | Lateral Pillar Height | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| A | Maintained | Good |
| B | <50% loss | Intermediate |
| B/C | Borderline | Variable |
| C | >50% loss | Poor |

### Salter–Thompson Classification
  - Based on subchondral fracture
  - <50% head involvement: Good prognosis
  - 50% head involvement: Poor prognosis

<!-- page 4 -->

Outcome Classification (Healed Stage)
### Stulberg Classification

| Class | Head Shape | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| I | Spherical, congruent | Excellent |
| II | Spherical, minor deformity | Good |
| III | Aspherical, congruent | Fair |
| IV | Aspherical, incongruent | Poor |
| V | Flat head | Very poor |

### Radiological Signs
  - Sagging rope sign: Coxa magna, neck shortening
  - Dimple sign: Seen in normal opposite hip, not pathological
  - V sign: Medial epiphyseal defect due to pressure overload

Principles of Management
### Core Aims
  - Maintain hip ROM
  - Achieve containment
  - Reduce load on femoral head
  - Allow biological remodeling

### Age-Based Treatment Strategy

| Age | Lateral Pillar | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| <6–8 yrs | Any | Conservative |
| ≥8 yrs | A or C | Conservative |
| ≥8 yrs | B or B/C | Surgical containment |

### Non-Operative Management
  - Activity restriction
  - Physiotherapy
  - Traction if stiff hip
  - Abduction braces (Scottish Rite, Petrie, Nevington modification)
  - Snyder sling for weight relief

<!-- page 5 -->

Operative Options
### Femoral Procedures
- Varus derotation osteotomy (VDRO)
### Pelvic Procedures
- Salter osteotomy
- Shelf acetabuloplasty
- Steel, Chiari (salvage)
Advanced
- Arthrodiastasis
- Valgus osteotomy (hinge abduction)
- Trochanteric advancement

### Prognostic Factors
- Younger age = better outcome
- Extent of head involvement
- Degree of extrusion
- Hip ROM at presentation
- Stage at intervention

Idiopathic Chondrolysis of Hip
Definition
Idiopathic, rapid loss of articular cartilage of the hip without infection or trauma.

Key Differences from Perthes
Idiopathic

| Feature | Perthes | Chondrolysis |
|---|---|---|
| First symptom | Limp | Pain |
| Gender | Male | Female (3–4×) |
| Age | 4–12 yrs | 11–12 yrs |
| Pathology | AVN | Cartilage loss |

<!-- page 6 -->

### Clinical Features
- Sudden hip pain
- Progressive stiffness
- Limp appears later
- Severe restriction of ROM

### Imaging in Chondrolysis
- Early joint space narrowing
- Minimal head deformity initially
- Late osteoarthritic changes

### Management of Chondrolysis
- Initial non-ambulatory containment
- Gradual protected ambulation
- Serial imaging follow-up
- Late stages: Total hip arthroplasty often required

Faculty Quote
“A good classification must be easy to apply, guide treatment, and predict prognosis.
Perthes has many classifications because prognosis matters.” – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay

### Exam Pearls
- Herring classification is the most reliable prognostic system
- Surgery is indicated only after 8 years in lateral pillar B or B/C
- Head-at-risk signs indicate need for containment, not mandatory surgery
- Predisposed child shows delayed bone age
- Sagging rope sign indicates coxa magna
- Idiopathic chondrolysis presents with pain before limp
- Arthroplasty is often required in late chondrolysis
