Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Evaluation of Bone Loss in Shoulder
Instability
Faculty: Dr. Mukul Mohindra

### OVERVIEW
- Two-part lecture: Part 1 (Evaluation) and Part 2 (Management).
- Focus: Understanding the importance, quantification, and significance of bone loss in shoulder instability.
- Shoulder joint is inherently unstable due to shallow glenoid; labrum deepens the socket and stabilizes via concavity–compression effect.
- Failure of Bankart repair is often due to neglected bone loss. Key Point: “Every recurrent shoulder dislocation has some bone loss — your job is to find how much and whether it matters.” – Dr. Mukul Mohindra

BIOMECHANICS & BASIC CONCEPTS
### Concavity–Compression Effect
- Stability comes from labrum + capsule tension + intra-articular pressure maintaining humeral head in the glenoid concavity.
- When labrum tears → pressure leaks → loss of concavity compression → recurrent instability.
### Bone Loss Importance
- Bankart repairs often fail because of unrecognized glenoid and/or humeral head bone loss.
- Rovée (1978) – first described failure of soft-tissue repairs due to bone defects.
- Burkhart & De Beer later emphasized bone loss as the main predictor of recurrence.

- Incidence:

- Glenoid bone loss: 50–95% of recurrent instability cases.
  - Hill–Sachs lesion: ~93% cases (softer cancellous bone).
  - Bipolar lesions (both sides): 60%+ of recurrent dislocations.
Hill–Sachs described in 1940 (Fogler described earlier, 1861).

### WHO DEVELOPS SIGNIFICANT BONE LOSS?
- Young, athletic, contact-sport players (recurrent trauma).
- Hyperlax individuals (shoulder hyperabduction test positive).
- Failed Bankart repairs.
- Prolonged untreated instability or high-velocity first injury.
Clues from History / Examination
- Apprehension at lower degrees of abduction/external rotation.
- “Ease of dislocation” — happens during sleep or minor movement.
- “Catching/locking” sensation.
- High-energy first episode (may indicate bony Bankart).
- Recurrent instability after failed Bankart. Gage’s hyperabduction test: Stabilize scapula and abduct arm; asymmetry between sides → shoulder hyperlaxity.

RELEVANT ANATOMY
### Humeral Head
- Diameter: ~43 mm
- Neck–shaft angle: 130°
- Retroversion: 30°
- Inclination: 30° upward from shaft axis.
Glenoid
- Normal shape: Pear-shaped (wider inferiorly).
- Inclination: ~5° superior.
- Retroversion: ~7° relative to scapular plane.
- Scapula itself is in ~30° anteversion → effective glenoid version ~20–25° anteversion.
- Bare Spot: Central, cartilage-free zone at junction of lower 2/3 and upper 1/3 of glenoid —
  - Maximum AP width ≈ 25 mm (2.5 cm).
  - Serves as reference point for glenoid bone loss assessment.
Normal glenoid: Pear-shaped → with anterior bone loss becomes inverted
pear.

IMAGING STRATEGY
X-rays (for suspicion)

| View | Purpose |
|---|---|
| AP + Axillary | Reduction status only (miss bone loss) |
| West Point Axillary View | Detect anterior glenoid bone loss |
| Apical Oblique (Garth) View | Shows Hill–Sachs lesion |
| Bernage View | Alternative to evaluate anterior rim |

Limitation: Cannot quantify glenoid bone loss. Only suspect.
### CT Scan (Gold Standard)
- Mandatory for quantifying glenoid bone loss and distinguishing acute vs attritional.
- 3D CT with humeral subtraction → best visualization.
- Indications:
  - Recurrent dislocations, high-energy injury.
  - Failed Bankart or multiple episodes.
  - X-ray/MRI suggests bone loss.
### MRI
- Detects labral tears and presence of bone loss, but poor correlation with actual size.
- Always confirm extent on CT before surgery. CT = Quantify. MRI = Qualify (presence + soft tissue).
Arthroscopy
- Used intraoperatively for direct measurement using the bare spot as reference.
- Linear measurement method: Compare anterior vs posterior radius (B–A)×2/B×100%.
- Advantage → visual confirmation of bone loss and dynamic engagement testing.

HUMERAL HEAD BONE LOSS (Hill–Sachs Lesion)
### Radiographic Assessment

| View | Description |
|---|---|
| AP (internal rotation) | Best shows Hill–Sachs defect |
| Garth / Bernage view | Defines lesion depth & orientation |
| Striker’s Notch view | Excellent for humeral head defects |

Quantification Methods
### 1⃣ Hill–Sachs Quotient Method
- Measure width (x), depth (y), length (z) in cm on different X-rays.
- Multiply x × y × z.
- Value > 2.5 → significant lesion.
### 2⃣ Ratio Method
- Compare defect depth to head radius on true AP view.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Defect >20% of radius → significant. Significant humeral defect: >20% surface involvement.

CONCEPT OF SIGNIFICANT / ENGAGING LESIONS
### Engaging Lesion (Burkhart & De Beer, 2000)
- During abduction + external rotation, Hill–Sachs axis aligns with anterior glenoid rim, causing engagement → dislocation.
- Inverted-pear glenoid and engaging Hill–Sachs → high recurrence risk. Engaging = Hill–Sachs axis matches glenoid rim axis.
On-Track / Off-Track Concept (Yamamoto et al., Itoi, Giacomo, 2014)
- Final evolution of engaging concept.
- Glenoid Track: Portion of humeral head contacting glenoid during full ER — 83% of glenoid width.
  - Remaining 17% anterior margin = safety zone.
Calculations:
1. Glenoid Track (GT) = 0.83 × Glenoid width – Bone loss (D)
2. Hill–Sachs Interval (HSI) = Hill–Sachs width + Cuff–Hill–Sachs distance
- If HSI < GT → On-track (safe)
- If HSI > GT → Off-track (engaging) Hill–Sachs Interval = width of lesion + distance to rotator cuff insertion.

### CLINICAL PEARLS
- CT > MRI for quantification.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Bare spot = central reference (25 mm glenoid width).
- Inverted pear glenoid = ≥25% anterior bone loss.
- Engaging lesion: Hill–Sachs aligns with glenoid rim during throwing motion.
- Glenoid track = 83% glenoid width.
- HSI > GT → Off-track → surgical bone block.
- Burkhart & De Beer: Bankart fails in presence of significant bone loss.
- Saito: >21% glenoid defect → universal failure.
