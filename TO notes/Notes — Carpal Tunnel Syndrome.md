Carpal Tunnel Syndrome – Dr. Amit Mittal
Overview
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common compressive neuropathy of the
upper limb. Understanding the three anatomical zones, median nerve variations,
clinical tests, and electrodiagnostic caveats is crucial for accurate diagnosis and
safe surgical release.
Faculty Remark: “CTS surgery is simple only when anatomy is respected. A small
cut in the wrong place can create lifelong pain.”

Anatomy Essentials
### Borders of the Carpal Tunnel
- Ulnar border: Triquetrum and hook of hamate.
- Radial border: Scaphoid and trapezium.
### Three Zones of the Roof (Ligamentous Structures)
1. Proximal: Deep forearm fascia.
2. Central: Transverse carpal ligament (TCL).
3. Distal: Aponeurosis of thenar and hypothenar muscles.

Contents
- 9 flexor tendons.
- Median nerve (30 fascicles at wrist level).
### Median Nerve Fascicular Arrangement
- Motor fascicles: 2 fascicles in volar position forming recurrent motor branch.
- Sensory fascicles: radial, ulnar, and dorsal positions.
### Palmar Cutaneous Branch
- Lies radial to median nerve.
- Pierces volar carpal ligament or antebrachial fascia before becoming cutaneous.
- Must be protected during incision.

Variations of Median Nerve (Important for MCQs)
### Recurrent Motor Branch Variations
1. Extraligamentous (most common).
2. Subligamentous.

<!-- page 2 -->

3. Transligamentous (pierces TCL).
4. Origin from ulnar border of median nerve.
5. Motor branch lying on top of TCL.
These variations increase the risk of injury during TCL incision.

### Grade III Variation – High Division of Median Nerve
- Division occurs proximal to tunnel.
- May be separated by persistent median artery or aberrant muscle.
### Grade IV Variation – Thenar Branch Variations
- Thenar branch may arise proximal to tunnel.
- Accessory branches may exist.
- Injury causes permanent thenar weakness.

Pathophysiology
### Two Potential Compression Sites
1. Proximal edge of TCL
  - Wrist flexion increases compression → Phalen’s test positive.
2. Hook of hamate
  - Causes hourglass deformity of nerve.
  - Durkan’s test positive, Phalen negative.

### Initial Symptoms
- Intermittent numbness and paresthesia at night.
- Due to:
  - Wrist flexion during sleep.
  - Fluid shift causing endoneural edema.

Progression
- Daytime symptoms triggered by pinching and wrist flexion.
- Chronic edema → persistent symptoms.

### Clinical Tests
- Tinel’s sign: tapping over median nerve.
- Phalen’s test (passive wrist flexion for 1 minute).
- Durkan’s test (direct compression) – most sensitive.
Probability of CTS increases when all four are present:
1. Abnormal hand diagram.

<!-- page 3 -->

2. Abnormal Semmes Weinstein sensibility test.
3. Positive Durkan’s test.
4. Night pain.
When all four are negative → probability is extremely low.

Electrodiagnostic Caveats
### Early Findings
- Sensory abnormalities occur before motor abnormalities.
- Median nerve: 94 percent sensory fibers → early sensory latency prolongation.
If motor latency abnormal with normal sensory SNAP
Rule out: - C8 radiculopathy. - Anterior horn cell disorder. - Isolated recurrent motor
branch compression.

### NCV After Surgery
- NCV does not return to normal, even with full clinical recovery.
- Because of retrograde degeneration or incomplete remyelination.

Severity Classification
### Mild CTS
- Failed trial of splinting/NSAIDs.
- No sensory or motor loss.
- Normal 2PD.
- Normal EMG.
### Moderate CTS
- Failed splinting/injection.
- Daytime symptoms.
- 2PD >15 mm.
- Weak abduction, no wasting.
- Sensory slowing on NCV.
### Severe CTS
- Constant symptoms.
- Motor weakness, APB wasting.
- NCV: significant sensory and motor abnormalities.
- No role for conservative management.

### Non Operative Management
- Neutral wrist splint.
- Steroid injection (temporary relief in 80 percent but long term benefit in ~20 percent).
- Manage systemic causes (thyroid, diabetes).
Patients with symptoms <1 year respond best.

Surgical Management
### Open Release (Preferred Technique)
- Incision ulnar to palmaris longus.
- Avoid radial side → risk to palmar cutaneous branch.
- Release:
  - Forearm fascia.
  - TCL.
  - Distal aponeurosis.
### Not Indicated in Primary CTS Surgery
- Neurolysis.
- Epineurotomy.
- Synovectomy.
Contraindications
- Untreated hypothyroidism.
- Uncontrolled diabetes.
- Other metabolic neuropathies.

### Endoscopic Techniques
- Chow technique (two portal).
- Agee technique (single portal).
### Landmark Lines
- Index–pisiform line.
- Fourth metacarpal line.

### Dynamic Procedures for Thumb Abduction (If Thenar Wasting)
- FDS transfer (Bunnell, Royle Thompson).
- Extensor indicis.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Abductor digiti minimi (Huber transfer).
- Palmaris longus (Camitz).

### Exam Pearls
- Most sensitive test: Durkan’s test.
- Phalen’s becomes negative if compression is at hook of hamate.
- Motor branch variation → most common is extraligamentous.
- High division of median nerve = Grade III variation.
- NCV does not normalize post surgery.
- Best incision → ulnar to palmaris longus.
- Mild = good steroid response, severe = always surgery.

Final Faculty Insight: “Carpal tunnel release is safe only when anatomy is
respected. Every variation you miss is a complication you create.”
