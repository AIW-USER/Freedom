Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Cervical Spine Injuries
Faculty: Dr. C S Vishnu Prasath

### OVERVIEW
- Cervical spine injuries account for ~20% of all spinal injuries and are the most life-threatening due to proximity to the brainstem and vital neural pathways.
- Common causes: Road traffic accidents, falls, diving injuries, sports trauma.
- Understanding anatomy, stability, and early management is vital to prevent permanent neurological deficits. “The difference between paralysis and recovery often lies in the first few minutes of cervical spine handling.” – Dr. C S Vishnu Prasath

### Stability Components
- Bony: Vertebral bodies, facets, pedicles, lamina.
- Ligamentous: ALL, PLL, ligamentum flavum, interspinous & supraspinous ligaments.
- Special ligaments (C1–C2): Transverse ligament, alar ligament, tectorial membrane. Functional divisions:
- C0–C1 (Atlanto-occipital): Flexion-extension (nodding)
- C1–C2 (Atlanto-axial): Rotation (50% of cervical rotation)

INITIAL MANAGEMENT & IMMOBILIZATION
### At Accident Scene
- Cervical spine protection is mandatory in all polytrauma or unconscious patients.
- Hard cervical collar / Philadelphia collar for immobilization.
- Log roll technique during transfer (avoid twisting).
- Airway management using jaw thrust, not head tilt.
### In Emergency Room
- Follow ATLS (ABC) with cervical spine protection.
- Assess for neurogenic shock (bradycardia + hypotension due to sympathetic loss).
- Maintain MAP >85 mmHg for cord perfusion.

RADIOLOGICAL EVALUATION
### 1⃣ Plain X-rays (3-view Series)
- AP View: Alignment, lateral masses.
- Lateral View: Crucial – visualize all 7 vertebrae + C7-T1 junction.
  - If not visible → Swimmer’s view.
- Open Mouth (Odontoid) View: Assesses C1 lateral masses and dens alignment. Stepwise assessment mnemonic: ABCS
- A: Alignment (3 lines)
- B: Bones (fracture, height loss)
- C: Cartilage/discs (space narrowing)
- S: Soft tissue (prevertebral shadow <7mm at C2; <22mm at C6)

<!-- page 4 -->

### 2⃣ CT Scan
  - Best for bony detail and classification (Jefferson, Hangman, burst fractures).
  - Preferred for surgical planning.
### 3⃣ MRI
  - Best for DLC injury, cord compression, epidural hematoma, and cord edema.
  - Indicated when neurological deficit or suspected instability present.

SPECIFIC FRACTURE TYPES
### 1⃣ Jefferson Fracture (C1 Burst)
  - Mechanism: Axial loading (diving injury).
  - Radiology: Lateral mass displacement on open-mouth view.
  - Stability: Transverse ligament disruption → unstable.
  - Treatment: Halo vest (intact ligament), Fusion (ruptured ligament).
2⃣ Odontoid Fracture (C2 Dens)

### 3⃣ Hangman’s Fracture (Traumatic Spondylolisthesis of Axis)
  - Mechanism: Hyperextension + distraction.
  - Site: Bilateral pars fracture of C2.
  - Treatment: Conservative (stable); Fusion (unstable Type II/III).
### 4⃣ Flexion-Distraction (Chance) Injuries
  - Usually at C5–C6.
  - Findings: Bilateral facet dislocation if severe.
  - Treatment: Closed reduction + posterior fusion.
### 5⃣ Burst / Compression Fractures (Subaxial)
  - Mechanism: Axial compression / flexion.
  - Treatment:
  - Stable → Collar / Halo vest.
  - Unstable → Anterior corpectomy + plating / Posterior fixation.

### EXAM & VIVA PEARLS
- Most common injury level: C5–C6.
- Upper cervical: Jefferson (C1), Odontoid (C2), Hangman’s (C2 pars).
- Subaxial: C3–C7 (wedge, burst, dislocation).
- SLIC ≥5 = Surgery.
- Jefferson = burst C1; Hangman = pars C2.
- Odontoid Type II: Most unstable & nonunion-prone.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Facet dislocation: Always unstable; posterior approach preferred.
- Cord syndromes: Central > Brown-Sequard > Anterior.
- MAP >85 mmHg: Maintain for cord perfusion.
- Immobilization device: Philadelphia / Halo vest.
