Recent Advances in Orthopedics – 3D Printing in Orthopedics
and Its Various Applications – Dr. Daivik Shetty

### Key Concepts / Overview
- 3D Printing (Additive Manufacturing) has revolutionized orthopedic design, surgical planning, and implant fabrication.
- It enables precise anatomical modeling, customized implants, and patient-specific instrumentation.
- The concept, though decades old, is now a major technological leap in orthopedic practice.
Dr. Daivik’s Remark:
“3D printing may seem futuristic, but it’s already transforming how we plan, design, and
execute surgeries in orthopedics.”

### Additive vs Subtractive Manufacturing

| Method | Mechanism | Example | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subtractive | Cuts/mills from a solid block | CNC milling | Wasteful process |
| Additive | Builds layer by layer from raw material | 3D printing | Minimal waste; efficient |

Process of 3D Printing
### Step 1: Digital Image Acquisition
- CT, MRI, or X-ray data (DICOM format) are used.
- Creates a digital representation of the anatomical structure or implant.
### Step 2: Image Refinement – Segmentation
- Removes unnecessary structures to isolate the area of interest (e.g., proximal femur, pelvis, etc.).
- Generates a Standard Triangular Language (STL) file – the universal 3D model format.
### Step 3: Conversion to G-Code
- STL file translated into G-code – printer-readable format.
- Each G-code line defines a cross-section for the 3D printer to fuse.
### Step 4: Printing & Formation
- Object printed layer by layer using additive technology.
- Minimal waste, high accuracy.

### Applications in Orthopedics
1. Surgical Planning & Anatomical Models
- Used in complex fractures, deformities, tumors, and arthroplasty.
- Surgeons can simulate reductions, resections, and hardware placement.
Benefits
- Improved preoperative visualization.
- Better hardware selection and plate prebending.
- Enhances patient understanding during counseling.
### Mirror Imaging Technique

| Purpose | Description |
|---|---|
| Planning & Symmetry | 3D models of both normal and |
| Restoration | pathological sides printed. Contralateral model guides surgical reconstruction. |
| Applications | Clavicle, calcaneal, pilon, and ankle fractures; hip deformities. |

Dr. Daivik’s Insight:
“Mirror imaging gives you the ‘normal’ blueprint — it’s like operating with a perfect
reference in your hand.”

2. Prosthetics & Orthotics
- 3D-printed AFOs (Ankle-Foot Orthoses) have replaced traditional plaster molds.
- Enable customized, lightweight, patient-specific devices.
- Widely used for plantar fasciitis and post-stroke rehabilitation.

3. New Non-Custom Implants
- Used in hip and knee arthroplasty, spine, and foot surgeries.
- 3D-printed acetabular cups – thinner, more porous, cost-effective.
- Titanium trabecular cups and cages show >98% fusion rates in lumbar surgeries.
- Porous metal implants improve biological fixation and reduce stress shielding.

<!-- page 4 -->

4. Customized Implants & Screws
- Tailor-made implants for unique anatomy or bone loss (post-trauma, tumor resection).
- 3D-printed titanium screws show greater pullout strength and improved fixation.
- Reduced implant inventory – cost-effective and precise.

5. Patient-Specific Instrumentation (PSI)
- 3D-printed jigs and cutting guides enhance deformity correction accuracy.
- Particularly useful in osteotomies, valgus/varus knee corrections, and arthroplasty.
- Enables preoperative simulation and intraoperative precision.

6. Arthroplasty & Revision Surgery
- 3D printing allows precise prosthesis design and single-use instruments.
- Improves efficiency, reduces inventory and cost.
- Facilitates short-stem and smart titanium implants that reduce stiffness.

| Year | Implant Material Evolution |
|---|---|
| 1960 | Stainless steel |
| 1970 | Cobalt-chrome alloy |
| 1980 | Titanium milling |
| 2010 | 3D-printed titanium |
| 2020 | Smart titanium (porous-surface, optimized volume) |

Dr. Daivik’s Observation:
“3D printing doesn’t make surgery futuristic — it makes it efficient, affordable, and
patient-specific.”

### 3D Bioprinting
- Next-gen advancement: Printing living tissues and organ analogs using bioinks.
- Uses biomaterials + live cells in layer-by-layer fashion.

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 2003 | First cell-based printing (Thomas Boland, Clemson University) |
| 2009 | 3D-printed blood vessel (Organova) |
| 2013 | Human liver tissue (Organova) |
| 2016 | 3D-printed kidney tissue (Harvard, Lewis Lab) |

### Orthopedic Applications
- Cartilage Bioprinting – creation of native cartilage for arthritis/defect repair.
  - Shim et al. demonstrated multilayered constructs with human MSCs showing good take-up in 8 weeks.
- Bone Bioprinting – scaffold-based bone regeneration using calcium phosphate (biodegradable, osteoconductive).
Challenges
- Cell viability under shear stress.
- Insufficient vascularization.
- Standardization of density and composition still under research.

### 4D Printing – The Future Frontier
- Adds a fourth dimension: time – printed structures change shape in response to environmental cues.
- Enables self-assembling and adaptive implants responding to temperature, pH, or magnetic fields.
- Example: Shape-memory bone scaffolds that soften to mimic cancellous bone at 37°C.
Dr. Daivik’s Vision:
“The future isn’t just printing structures — it’s printing structures that think, adapt, and
evolve with the body.”
