Lower Limb Trauma in Children – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Lower limb trauma in children has age-specific injury patterns, treatment protocols,
and complication profiles. Unlike adults, the primary concern is not union but
malunion, growth arrest, and limb length discrepancy.
This lecture focuses on three high-yield areas: - Pediatric femoral shaft fractures - Distal
femoral physeal injuries - Pediatric and transitional ankle fractures

Pediatric Femoral Shaft Fractures
Epidemiology
- ~2% of all pediatric bony injuries
- Most common pediatric fracture requiring hospitalization
- High union rate due to rich periosteal blood supply
Major concern is shortening and malalignment, not non-union.
Key Classifications (High Yield)
### Fracture Stability (Most Important)
- Length stable: Transverse or short oblique fractures
- Length unstable: Long oblique, spiral, comminuted fractures
  - Defined as fracture length >2× bone diameter at that level
### Age and Weight
- <6 months
- 6 months–5 years
- 5–11 years
- 11 years
- Weight cut-off: 50 kg

### Treatment Algorithm for Femoral Shaft Fractures

| Age / Weight | Fracture Type | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| <6 months | Any | Pavlik harness or spica |

<!-- page 2 -->

| Age / Weight | Fracture Type | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 6 months–5 yrs | Most | Spica cast |
| 5–11 yrs (<50 kg) | Stable | Flexible intramedullary nails (TENS) |
| 5–11 yrs | Unstable | Submuscular bridge plate or external fixator |
| >11 yrs (>50 kg) | Any | Rigid trochanteric entry IM nail |

Length unstable fractures should not be treated with TENS alone.
Pavlik Harness for Femur Fractures
### Key Points
- Age <6 months
- More proximal fracture → more hip flexion
- Usually not >90° flexion
- Monitor for femoral nerve palsy
- Duration: ~2 weeks
- Always confirm radiographic union

Hip Spica Application
Principles
- Traction during application
- Valgus molding to prevent varus collapse
- Reinforce V-junction
- Hip and knee flexion equal
- Connecting bar between limbs prevents breakage

Implants in Femoral Fractures
### Flexible Nails (TENS)
- Nail diameter: 30–40% of canal
- Both nails same size
- Avoid multiple passes near physis
- Do not bend nail at entry site

<!-- page 3 -->

### Rigid Nails
- Only trochanteric entry, never piriformis
- Safe >11 years
- Preserves femoral head blood supply

Distal Femoral Physeal Injuries
Epidemiology
- Rare (~2%)
- Peak: 10–12 years
- Males 6× more common

### Complications (Very High Risk)
- Growth arrest: 30–40%
- Compartment syndrome
- Neurovascular injury
- Ligament injuries (ACL, meniscus)
Distal femur physis contributes: - 70% of femoral growth - ~37% of total limb growth

### Mechanism and Special Considerations
- High-velocity trauma in adolescents
- Neonates: birth injury or non-accidental trauma
Metaphyseal corner and bucket-handle fractures suggest child abuse.
### Reduction Principles (Distal Femur)
- Gentle traction is primary maneuver
- Knee flexion often required to relax gastrocnemius
- Always reassess neurovascular status after reduction

Management by Salter–Harris Type
### SH Type I
- Closed reduction if possible
- Open reduction if periosteum entrapped
- Fixation with smooth K-wires

<!-- page 4 -->

### SH Type II
- Use Thurston–Holland fragment for fixation
- Prefer 2 screws if fragment is large
- K-wires if fragment small
### SH Type III & IV
- Anatomic open reduction
- Screw fixation
- Treat older children like adult fractures

### Fixation Across Physis (Important Pearl)
- Smooth K-wires across physis are safe if:
  - Minimal attempts
  - Low torque
  - Central placement
  - Avoid perichondrial ring
Growth arrest is due to injury, not wires.

### Growth Arrest Surveillance
- Remove implants at 3–4 months
- MRI at 4–6 months
  - Use T1 spoiled gradient recall echo
- Follow till skeletal maturity

Pediatric Ankle Fractures
Transitional Fractures (12–16 years)
Due to asymmetric distal tibial physeal closure: - Central → medial → posterior →
lateral

Types of Transitional Fractures
### Juvenile Tillaux Fracture
- SH Type III
- Avulsion of anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament
- Anterolateral epiphyseal fragment

<!-- page 5 -->

### Triplane Fracture
- SH Type IV equivalent
- Fracture in three planes
- Appears SH III on AP, SH II on lateral

### Imaging in Ankle Trauma
- X-rays may underestimate injury
- CT scan mandatory in adolescents
- Triplane fracture shows classic Mercedes-Benz sign on axial CT

### Treatment of Triplane Fractures
- <2 mm displacement: Closed reduction + above-knee cast
- 2 mm displacement: CR or ORIF + screw fixation
Approach: - Lateral triplane: Anterolateral - Medial triplane: Anteromedial

### Treatment of Tillaux Fractures
- <2 mm displacement: Above-knee cast
- 2 mm displacement:
  - Closed reduction with internal rotation
  - Percutaneous or open screw fixation

Ankle Physeal Injuries (Dias–Tachdjian)
Classification based on mechanism, not X-ray alone: - Supination–inversion -
Supination–external rotation - Supination–plantar flexion - Pronation–external rotation

Key Diagnostic Tip
Always look at the fibula first: - No fibula fracture → think transitional fracture -
Isolated fibula fracture may hide distal tibial physeal injury

Faculty Quote
“In pediatric femur fractures, union is guaranteed. What you must prevent is shortening.”
– Dr. Shalin Shah

<!-- page 6 -->

### Exam Pearls
- Length stability guides femur fracture management
- TENS is contraindicated in length unstable fractures
- Distal femur physeal injuries have very high growth arrest risk
- Smooth K-wires across physis are safe when used correctly
- CT is mandatory in adolescent ankle fractures
- Triplane fractures are SH IV injuries
- Always evaluate for non-accidental trauma in infants
- Follow distal femur injuries till skeletal maturity
