Osteosarcoma – Dr. Rajat Gupta (Jan 2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Most common primary bone cancer of children and adolescents (first two decades).
- Second most common primary bone malignancy overall – the most common is multiple myeloma. MCQ trap: metastasis is the commonest bone malignancy but it is secondary, not primary.
- Incidence about 1 per million per year (US data); extrapolated to India ≈ 1,400 new cases a year.
- Age usually 2nd decade (10–20 years), but it can present at any age (e.g. 6 or 25 years).
- Hallmark presence of malignant osteoid (bone made by tumour cells), seen on X-ray.
- Sex male preponderance in all types except parosteal osteosarcoma (female preponderance).

Predisposing conditions (MCQ)
- Hereditary retinoblastoma
- Li-Fraumeni syndrome
- Rothmund-Thomson syndrome

Sites
- Commonest: around the knee (distal femur, proximal tibia) and proximal humerus.
- Also proximal femur, pelvis, distal tibia – virtually any bone can be affected.
- Usually metaphyseal; diaphyseal in about 8% of patients.

Radiological Evaluation (how to read the X-ray)
Dr. Rajat reads the X-ray on 7 parameters. Typical answers for conventional osteosarcoma:

| Parameter | Typical finding in osteosarcoma |
|---|---|
| Age | Skeletally immature (physis open) |
| Location of tumour | Metaphyseal (e.g. distal femur) |
| Type of lesion (pattern of destruction) | Permeative; lytic + sclerotic components |
| Matrix of lesion | Bone-forming / sclerotic (osteoid) matrix |
| Zone of transition | Wide (ill-defined) |
| Periosteal reaction | Codman's triangle + sunburst – both can be seen in the same X-ray |
| Soft tissue component | Yes – often with mineralisation inside it |

- Type vs matrix: type = pattern of destruction (geographic / moth-eaten / permeative); matrix = what the lesion makes (cartilaginous, osteoid, ground glass, or none).
- Sclerotic (osteoid) matrix → the first differential is osteosarcoma.

Classification

| Type | Grade | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional (intramedullary) | High | Subtypes osteoblastic, chondroblastic, fibroblastic – no difference in prognosis; chondroblastic responds less to chemotherapy (MRI: whorled pattern) |
| Telangiectatic | High | 3–10% of osteosarcomas; fluid levels on MRI; closest differential = aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) |
| Small cell | High | Mimics Ewing sarcoma on histology (small round blue cells); usually treated with Ewing protocol chemotherapy |
| High-grade surface | High | Least common, highly aggressive; can break the cortex and involve the medulla |
| Periosteal | Intermediate | Usually diaphyseal; still requires chemotherapy |
| Low-grade intramedullary | Low | Indolent; mimics fibrous dysplasia; MDM2 and CDK4 (IHC) positive – helps to differentiate |
| Parosteal | Low | Posterior aspect of distal femur; middle age; female preponderance; no role of chemotherapy – surgery alone |

Secondary osteosarcoma
- Seen in the elderly.
- Causes: Paget's disease (commonest), post-radiation, bone infarct, chronic osteomyelitis, fibrous dysplasia.
- Worse prognosis than conventional osteosarcoma – partly because elderly patients tolerate chemotherapy poorly.
- Case shown: Paget's pelvis/humerus with pathological fracture plated elsewhere → large soft- tissue mass = secondary osteosarcoma. Post-radiation case: pelvis after radiotherapy for carcinoma rectum.

<!-- page 4 -->

Radiation-induced sarcoma of bone – Arlen criteria (Cancer 1971)
- Microscopic/radiological evidence of non-malignancy, or of a malignancy of different histology, before radiation.
- Sarcoma develops in the path of the radiation beam.
- Long asymptomatic period between radiotherapy and the sarcoma (usually quoted ≥ 5 years; no strict cut-off – but not months).
- Histological proof of the secondary sarcoma.

### Prognostic Factors
- Stage – metastatic disease at presentation is worse; bone metastasis is worse than lung metastasis (the lung is the first filter for blood-borne cells, so reaching bone means a more aggressive tumour).
- Grade – higher grade, worse prognosis (e.g. parosteal does better than conventional).
- Size – larger tumour volume, worse.
- Location – axial/central (pelvis, spine) worse.
- Pathological fracture – worse.
- Response to chemotherapy – poor necrosis (< 90%) is worse.
- Margins of resected specimen – positive margins worse.
- Serum alkaline phosphatase – high ALP at presentation worse.
MCQ pearl: grade is NOT a prognostic factor in Ewing sarcoma (Ewing is always high grade),
but it IS for osteosarcoma. Asked which single factor matters most, Dr. Rajat would rank stage >
grade > size.

Huvos grading – response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy

| Huvos grade | Tumour necrosis | Viable cells | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | Little or no necrosis | > 50% | Poor |
| Grade 2 | 50–90% | 10–50% | Poor |
| Grade 3 | 90–99% | 1–10% | Good |
| Grade 4 | 100% | None | Good |

- "Pass mark" is 90% necrosis (not 50%): < 90% necrosis = poor response. Some pathologists report viable cells instead: ≤ 10% viable = good, > 10% = poor.
- Picci grading: grade 1 macroscopic viable tumour, grade 2 microscopic viable tumour (both poor), grade 3 no viable tumour (good). Huvos is the one usually followed.

### Diagnosis and Work-up
- X-ray – extent of bony destruction; obvious or impending fracture.
- MRI – extent in bone and soft tissue, relation to muscles, vessels, nerves; used to plan biopsy and surgery. Always image the WHOLE bone (MRI whole femur, not just distal femur; whole humerus, not shoulder) to pick up skip lesions.

<!-- page 5 -->

- PET-CT – staging (disease anywhere else) and response: PET before treatment and again after 2 cycles of chemotherapy, comparing the fall in SUV before surgery.
- Biopsy – needle (core) biopsy, after imaging.

Biopsy rules (from the Q&A)
- FNAC has NO role in any bone or soft-tissue tumour – these are paucicellular, FNAC shows only cells not morphology, and is done without regard to the surgical incision.
- Needle biopsy is preferred over open biopsy: less contamination, minimal scar. Take several cores through the SAME entry point.
- If there is a soft-tissue component, sample it – as representative, no decalcification needed, quicker report.
- Open biopsy only if your pathologist cannot report a needle core – better to refer such patients.
- Never assume a diagnosis from MRI alone. A "giant-cell-rich lesion" is not necessarily a giant cell tumour – a curettage for a giant-cell-rich osteosarcoma can be disastrous.
- Poorly placed biopsy scars (two scars, wrong line) can jeopardise limb salvage.

Pathological fracture (Q&A)
- Young patient: X-ray → MRI → needle biopsy of tumour tissue (not haematoma); splint and wait for the report; PET-CT while waiting. No plating/nailing "for biopsy".
- Elderly (> 50: metastasis, myeloma, lymphoma): PET-CT first. Known primary or obvious primary on PET (e.g. kidney mass) → may fix upfront; solitary lesion → biopsy first.
- Fixation for metastatic fracture: assume it is the last surgery for that bone, and the patient must be able to bear weight the next day.

Treatment
Treatment plan

| Step | What | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Neoadjuvant chemotherapy | 2–3 cycles (depends on protocol) |
| 2 | Surgery | Limb salvage (the norm today) |
| 3 | Adjuvant chemotherapy | 3–4 cycles – total 6 cycles (3 + 3 or 2 + 4) |

- Parosteal osteosarcoma: surgery is the only treatment – no chemotherapy.
- Small cell osteosarcoma: usually chemotherapy as per Ewing sarcoma protocol.
- Management is a team effort: orthopaedic oncologist, medical oncologist, radiation oncologist, pathologist, radiologist.

| Drug | Dose |
|---|---|
| Adriamycin (doxorubicin) – backbone of sarcoma | 75 mg/m² over 3 days |

chemotherapy

| Cisplatin | 100 mg/m² over 2 days |
|---|---|
| High-dose methotrexate | 12 g/m² over 4 hours (compare: 15 mg per WEEK in rheumatoid arthritis) |
| Ifosfamide | 9 g/m² over 5 days (1.8 g/m² per day) |

- Three-drug regimens are better than two-drug regimens.
- MAP (methotrexate, Adriamycin, cisplatin) = standard of care worldwide. Some centres use IAP (ifosfamide, Adriamycin, cisplatin).
- Two-drug options: Adriamycin + cisplatin, or ifosfamide + Adriamycin.
- Doses are calculated on body surface area (BSA) from height and weight.

MAP protocol – one cycle = 5 weeks (35 days)

| Day | Drug |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Adriamycin |
| 1–2 | Cisplatin |

| 28 | High-dose methotrexate |
| 35 | Next cycle starts (Adriamycin) |

Limb salvage surgery
Barriers to limb salvage (relative – not absolute contraindications):
- Poorly placed biopsy incision
- Major vascular involvement
- Encasement of a major motor nerve
- Pathological fracture of the involved bone
3-strike rule: of bone, nerve, vessel and soft-tissue envelope – if three are involved, limb salvage
is probably not worth considering (e.g. bone + nerve + vessel, or bone + nerve + soft tissue leaves
no useful motors).

Canadell's procedure

- ECRT: tumour bone resected, packed and sent to radiotherapy – 50 Gy in a single fraction (15–20 min; compare usual radiotherapy given over 5–7 weeks) kills every living cell; soft tissue stripped, canal augmented with cement or bone graft, and the sterilised bone fixed back with plates and screws.
- Cryotherapy: liquid nitrogen at –196 °C instead of radiation.
- Pedicled cryotherapy: proximal osteotomy only, tumour debulked and dipped in liquid nitrogen without cutting distally – the distal blood supply is preserved. Saves ~22–24 cm of natural bone that a megaprosthesis would replace.
- Rotationplasty: for young children with much growth left – knee tumour removed, leg rotated 180° so the ankle works as a knee; patient functions as a below-knee rather than above-knee amputee. Advantages: functional knee, longer lever arm, better socket tolerance, functional and psychological benefit, improves with time. Drawback: cultural stigma (foot faces backwards).
- Canadell's procedure: for children when the tumour is close to the growth plate – step 1: external fixator and physeal distraction for 10–15 days; step 2: intercalary resection through the growth plate, ECRT and refixation, keeping the epiphysis with most of the growth plate (case shown: equal femur lengths at 6 months).

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Commonest primary bone malignancy = multiple myeloma; osteosarcoma is 2nd overall but 1st in children/adolescents.
- Fluid levels on MRI are not only ABC – think telangiectatic osteosarcoma.
- MDM2/CDK4 positive → low-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma (vs fibrous dysplasia).
- Parosteal: posterior distal femur, middle-aged woman, low grade, no chemotherapy.
- Huvos: < 90% necrosis = poor response.
- Skip lesion vs satellite lesion: a satellite lesion lies in the reactive zone; a skip lesion has normal bone between it and the tumour – worse prognosis, removed at surgery; hence MRI of the whole bone.
- Osteosarcoma can cross the physis (especially if long-standing), although classically it is said to respect it.
- Chondrosarcoma and chordoma: surgery is the only treatment; chemotherapy does not work. Unresectable sacral chordoma → proton therapy; metastatic chordoma → imatinib (palliative).

<!-- page 9 -->

- Multiple myeloma: marrow plasma cells > 10% + CRAB (not 30%).

### Take-Home Summary
- Osteosarcoma = malignant osteoid; 2nd decade; around the knee and proximal humerus.
- X-ray on 7 parameters → MRI of the whole bone → needle biopsy (never FNAC) → PET- CT staging.
- High/intermediate grade: neoadjuvant chemotherapy (MAP) → limb salvage → adjuvant chemotherapy (6 cycles total). Low grade: surgery only.
- Response is judged by Huvos necrosis (≥ 90% = good). Stage is the strongest prognostic factor.
- Limb salvage is the norm; reconstruction can be non-biological (megaprosthesis) or biological (ECRT, cryotherapy, rotationplasty, Canadell).
