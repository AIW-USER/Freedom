Overview
Deformity correction relies on understanding axes, joint orientation, CORA,
osteotomy rules, and planning. Dr. Dror Paley’s principles form the foundation for
systematic evaluation and correction of deformities.
Faculty Remark: “Know your axes before you even think of cutting bone. Planning
is everything.”

## Definitions & Fundamental Concepts
What Is a Deformity?
A limb or joint that cannot be brought to normal anatomic position due to
abnormalities in: - Angulation - Translation - Rotation - Limb length discrepancy (LLD)

### X Ray Requirements for Deformity Assessment
- Long limb scanogram including hip, knee, ankle on one film.
- Patella facing forward regardless of deformity.
- Pelvis squared by shoe raise in LLD.

## Axes of the Limb
### Mechanical Axis
- Line connecting proximal and distal joint centers.
- Always straight.

### Anatomical Axis
- Defined for a bone or entire limb.

- Mid diaphyseal line.
- May be curvilinear (e.g., femur in sagittal plane).
### Joint Centers
- Hip: center of femoral head.
- Knee: midpoint of femoral notch or tibial spines.

- Ankle: center of talus or tibia fibula plafond.

## Joint Orientation Lines & Angles
### Orientation Lines
- Hip: neck axis or head to GT line.
- Knee: distal femoral condylar line, tibial plateau line.
- Ankle: plafond line.

## Mechanical Axis Deviation (MAD)
- Indicates joint alignment.
- Normal MAD: 1–15 mm.
- If mechanical axis passes lateral to knee center → limb valgus.
- If medial → limb varus.

## Malalignment Test (MAT)
To identify level of deformity: 1. Measure MAD. 2. Measure mLDFA. 3. Measure
mMPTA. 4. Evaluate JLCA. 5. Assess for condylar deficiency.

<!-- page 3 -->

Interpretation
- mLDFA abnormal → femoral deformity.
- mMPTA abnormal → tibial deformity.
- JLCA >2 → joint laxity or subluxation.
- Condyles non parallel → condylar deficiency.

## CORA – Center of Rotation of Angulation
- Intersection of proximal and distal axes.
- Always defines apex of deformity.
- CORA may lie outside bone in multi apical deformities.
### Functional CORA
- Bisector line divides deformity angle.
- Any point on bisector can serve as a functional CORA.

## Osteotomy Planning
### Before Osteotomy, Identify:
1. Location of CORA.
2. Correction axis.
3. Level of osteotomy.

## Osteotomy Rules
Rule 1 – CORA = Correction Axis = Osteotomy Level
- Pure angulation correction.
- No translation.
- Open wedge or closing wedge possible.
Rule 2 – CORA = Correction Axis, but Osteotomy ≠ CORA
- Angulation + relative translation.
### Rule 3 – CORA ≠ Correction Axis ≠ Osteotomy
- Angulation + iatrogenic translation.

## Types of Osteotomy
### Open Wedge Osteotomy
- Creates a wedge on one side.
- Advantages: simple to perform.
- Disadvantages: unstable, increases limb length, requires graft, risk to

Closed Wedge Osteotomy
neurovascular structures.

- Removal of a wedge from convex side.
- Advantages: stable, no graft required, safer for nerves.
- Disadvantage: causes shortening.
Dr. Anurag’s preference: Closed wedge osteotomy in exams.
### Dome Osteotomy
- Circular cut centered around a fixed point.
- Always results in some translation.
- Useful for multi plane corrections.

## Oblique Plane Deformity
Total deformity magnitude: Oblique = √(AP² + Lateral²)

### Exam Pearls
- Mechanical axis is always straight.
- Tibial mechanical axis is lateral to anatomical axis.
- MAD >15 mm indicates malalignment.
- JLCA >2 degrees indicates joint laxity.
- CORA defines apex of deformity.
- Rule 1 osteotomy → angulation only.
- Closed wedge preferred in exam situations.
- Oblique deformity uses AP² + Lat² formula.

Final Faculty Insight: “Do not cut bone unless you know exactly where the CORA
lies and what the axis is. Planning prevents problems.”
