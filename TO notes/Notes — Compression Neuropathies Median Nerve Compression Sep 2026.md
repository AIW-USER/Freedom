Compression Neuropathies: Median Nerve
Compression – Dr. Aviral Gupta (Sep 2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- First of a series on compressive neuropathies (ulnar and radial nerves in the next lectures): evaluation, sensory testing, carpal tunnel syndrome, and proximal median nerve compression (pronator syndrome, AIN syndrome).
- Risk factors (MCQ list): diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, acromegaly, excessive alcohol, tobacco, obesity, hereditary motor sensory neuropathy.
- Subjective (patient-reported) tools, know the names: Carpal Tunnel Syndrome questionnaire, DASH (Disabilities of Arm, Shoulder and Hand), Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire, PROMIS (Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System), Patient Specific Functional Scale.
- Evaluation = nerve-specific stress (provocative) tests + sensory testing (common to all nerves) + motor examination (MRC grading).

### Pathophysiology (Three Circles)

| Stage | Histopathology | Symptoms | Sensory testing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Blood-nerve barrier breakdown, endoneurial oedema (e.g. wrist flexion at night raises carpal tunnel pressure) | INTERMITTENT paraesthesia/aching (nocturnal) | Positive provocative manoeuvres |
| Intermediate | Connective tissue thickening inside the nerve; localised then diffuse demyelination | CONSTANT paraesthesia and/or weakness | Abnormal THRESHOLDS (more stimulus needed) |
| Late | Axonal degeneration (axonal death) | Numbness and atrophy | Abnormal TWO- POINT DISCRIMINATION (fewer innervated receptors) |

- Compression affects heavily MYELINATED fibres first (A-beta: touch, pressure); pain fibres are affected very late. So patients complain of numbness but keep protective (pain) sensation and do not get trophic ulcers or burns, unlike total denervation.

- Mnemonic for functions: "Prime Minister Pressurized To Sign The Contract Fast" (Proprioception Motor, Pressure Touch, Spindle, Touch Cold Fast pain).
- Fast-adapting receptors (Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles; A-beta): respond to MOVING touch; tested by VIBRATION thresholds and moving 2PD. They stop firing after a while (you stop feeling your clothes).
- Slow-adapting receptors (Merkel cell-neurite complex, Ruffini end organs; A-beta): respond to STATIC touch; tested by CUTANEOUS PRESSURE thresholds and static 2PD (you keep feeling a coin in your hand).
- Quality vs quantity: threshold tests (light moving touch, vibration, pressure) show the QUALITY of innervation (demyelination); 2PD shows the QUANTITY (innervated receptor density, axonal loss).

### Carpal Tunnel Syndrome
- Commonest nerve compression in the upper limb.
- Symptoms: paraesthesia in median distribution; NOCTURNAL paraesthesia is pathognomonic (wrist falls into flexion during sleep); symptoms in fixed wrist positions (reading a newspaper, using a mouse); chronic cases get abductor pollicis brevis (APB) atrophy.
- Electrodiagnostic studies: not needed for diagnosis (history and examination are enough); they stage the compression (demyelination vs axonal loss) and predict recovery time (greater latency = slower recovery).

Anatomy that matters
- Flexor retinaculum: three parts; the middle part is the transverse carpal ligament, attached to the scaphoid and trapezium radially and to the hamate ulnarly. Narrowest point about 2 cm from the proximal (leading) edge = site of morphological nerve changes. Although open at both ends, the tunnel keeps strict fluid pressures; a rise in pressure compresses the nerve.
- Palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve (PCBMN): arises proximal to the wrist, runs along the ulnar side of the FCR sheath and crosses SUPERFICIAL to the transverse carpal ligament (in the thenar crease region, radial to palmaris longus). So in true CTS sensation over the thenar eminence is SPARED; thenar numbness suggests a more proximal compression.
- Structures superficial to the flexor retinaculum: ulnar nerve and artery, PCBMN (FCU and FCR tendons relate to its proximal part).
- Recurrent motor branch: first branch after the median nerve leaves the tunnel; supplies abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis and the superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis; at risk in carpal tunnel release because of its variations.

Treatment
- Non-operative (mainly intermittent symptoms): custom-made wrist splint in NEUTRAL (not flexion or extension) especially at night; oral analgesics or low-dose steroids for acute symptoms; nerve gliding exercises.
- Steroid injection into the carpal tunnel: relief is temporary, but it is a good DIAGNOSTIC marker (relief confirms CTS rather than a cervical or proximal cause) and predicts a good result from surgery.
- Many patients (especially those presenting late with APB atrophy or constant numbness) need surgery straight away.
- Open vs endoscopic release: long-term outcomes similar; complications are more frequent (and more serious) with endoscopic release; endoscopic release avoids scar tenderness and allows earlier return to work.

Open carpal tunnel release
- Can be done under local anaesthesia.
- Incision: about 6 mm ULNAR to the thenar crease, in line with the radial border of the extended ring finger. This protects the PCBMN (avoids a painful neuroma), protects the recurrent branch (usually radial) and keeps the scar off the median nerve.
- Kaplan cardinal line: from the apex of the first web space (thumb abducted) to the hook of hamate; marks the DISTAL limit of the incision (superficial palmar arch lies beyond).
- Finding the hook of hamate: from the pisiform (trace FCU to it) go about 1.5 cm distally and 1.5 cm radially and press deeply.

### Proximal Median Nerve Compression (Elbow and Forearm)

| Site (proximal to distal) | Details |
|---|---|
| Ligament of Struthers | Present in about 1%; runs from a supracondylar process of the humerus (seen on X-ray) to the medial epicondyle; may trap the median nerve and brachial artery. |
| Lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis) | Fibrous extension of the biceps tendon; nerve passes beneath it in the antecubital fossa. |
| Between the two heads of pronator teres | 3 to 7.5 cm distal to the humeral epicondylar line. |
| Fibrous arch of FDS | Tendinous edge about 6.5 cm distal to the epicondylar line (arch between the radial and ulnar origins). |
| Rare | Accessory head of FPL (Gantzer muscle), palmaris profundus, flexor carpi radialis brevis. |

- Branching pattern: first branch to pronator teres (around the elbow); then branches from the ULNAR side to palmaris longus, FCR and FDS (two branches); the ANTERIOR INTEROSSEOUS NERVE is the only branch from the RADIAL side and supplies FPL, FDP to index and middle fingers, and pronator quadratus. Useful for localising any median nerve lesion.

Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy)
- Leading differential of AIN syndrome; not a compression neuropathy.
- Triggered by viral illness, vaccination, pregnancy, trauma, surgery or stress. Severe periscapular/shoulder PAIN first; as pain settles, WEAKNESS follows. May involve the whole plexus, part of it (e.g. C5-C6) or only the AIN.
- High-dose corticosteroids if diagnosed early (within 72 hours, rarely possible). Some spontaneous recovery usually occurs, but full recovery is not assured.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Myelinated (A-beta) fibres are affected first; pain sensation is preserved until late.
- Thresholds = quality of innervation (demyelination); 2PD = quantity (axonal loss); 2PD over 8 mm is non-functional.
- Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments test slow-adapting receptors; vibration tests fast-adapting receptors.
- Nocturnal paraesthesia is pathognomonic of CTS; Phalen (60 s) is better than Tinel.
- PCBMN runs superficial to the transverse carpal ligament: thenar sensation spared in CTS, affected in pronator syndrome.
- Recurrent motor branch: extraligamentous commonest; ulnar-side origin is most at risk.
- Incision 6 mm ulnar to the thenar crease; Kaplan line marks the distal limit (superficial palmar arch).
- Pronator syndrome: pain + sensory loss including palm, normal EDX. AIN syndrome: motor only (FPL, FDP index/middle, PQ), no sensory loss.
- Ligament of Struthers: 1%, from a supracondylar process to the medial epicondyle.

### Take-Home Summary
- Grade compression neuropathy by its pathology (oedema, thickening/demyelination, axonal loss) using provocative tests, threshold tests and 2PD; treat CTS with a neutral splint or injection early and a carefully placed open release when established; and separate proximal median nerve problems into pronator syndrome (pain and sensory, including palm) and AIN syndrome (pure motor, think Parsonage-Turner).
