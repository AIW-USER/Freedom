Flatfoot (Planovalgus) in Child – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Pediatric planovalgus foot is a common presentation and a frequent source of confusion
in exams and clinical practice. It is not merely a cosmetic problem but a functional
disorder causing lever arm dysfunction. Correct differentiation between flexible and rigid
flatfoot is critical, as management differs completely.
Planovalgus foot consists of: - Pes planus (loss of medial longitudinal arch) - Hindfoot
valgus - Forefoot abduction

### Functional Significance
- Planovalgus foot causes lever arm dysfunction.
- The CORA of the foot shifts medially.
- Decreased plantar flexion lever arm.
- Increased energy expenditure during push off.
- Altered knee flexion and ankle biomechanics.

Classification
### Flexible Flatfoot
- Arch reappears on non weight bearing.
- Usually asymptomatic.
- Most common type in children.
- Normal developmental variant.
### Rigid Flatfoot
- Arch absent in weight bearing and non weight bearing.
- Often painful.
- Always pathological.
- Requires evaluation and treatment.

History Taking
Key points to identify etiology: - Pain and its pattern - Morning stiffness suggests
inflammatory cause. - Activity related pain suggests coalition or accessory navicular. -
History of recurrent ankle sprains. - Family history - Familial flatfoot - Hereditary spastic
paraplegia - Progressive deformity suggests neurological cause.

Important causes to consider: - Tarsal coalition - Accessory navicular - Congenital
vertical talus - Neurological disorders

Clinical Examination
### Static Examination
- Toe walking test: arch reappears in flexible flatfoot.
- Toe dorsiflexion test (Jack’s test): arch reappears when great toe is dorsiflexed.
- Non weight bearing test: presence of arch offloading.
### Dynamic Examination
- Observe gait pattern.
- Assess heel valgus during stance.

Silfverskiöld Test
Used to differentiate: - Gastrocnemius contracture - Tendo Achilles contracture

Technique
- Dorsiflex ankle with knee flexed.
- Repeat with knee extended.
- Reduction of dorsiflexion >10° on knee extension indicates gastrocnemius tightness.
Subtalar joint must be locked during testing to avoid pseudo dorsiflexion.
### Subtalar Joint Examination
- Stabilize ankle.
- Invert and evert calcaneum.
- Normal ROM: 20–30°.
- Pain or restriction suggests tarsal coalition or rigid flatfoot.

### Motor Power and Neurological Examination
- Assess tibialis posterior function.
- Compare calf girth.
- Examine spine for neurocutaneous markers:
  - Sacral dimple
  - Sacral sinus

<!-- page 3 -->

- Always spread buttocks during examination.
Progressive unilateral flatfoot is a red flag for tethered cord.
### Developmental Flatfoot
- Infants and toddlers commonly have flatfeet.
- Causes:
  - Subcutaneous fat pad
  - Ligamentous laxity
Staheli Quote “Flatfeet are usual in infants, common in children, and unusual in
adults.”
Arch typically develops by 6 years of age.

Management of Flexible Flatfoot
Asymptomatic
- Reassurance only.
- No orthotics required.
- Corrective footwear does not change natural history.
### Causes of Pain in Flexible Flatfoot
- Obesity
- Tight tendo Achilles
### Management of Painful Flexible Flatfoot
- Weight reduction.
- Stretching of tendo Achilles with subtalar joint locked.

Surgical Options for Symptomatic Planovalgus Foot
Reserved for recalcitrant pain.

### Mosca’s Procedure (Evans Modification)
- Calcaneal lengthening osteotomy.
- Restores lateral column length.
- Corrects lever arm dysfunction.
- Uses tricortical iliac crest graft.

<!-- page 4 -->

Triple C Osteotomy
Addresses all three components: - Cuboid opening wedge osteotomy. - Closing wedge
medial cuneiform osteotomy. - Sliding calcaneal osteotomy.

Congenital Vertical Talus (CVT)
Definition
A rigid deformity with: - Hindfoot valgus and equinus - Midfoot dorsiflexion - Forefoot
abduction - Caused by fixed dorsal dislocation of navicular over talar head

### Key Features
- Rare (≈1 in 10,000)
- 50 percent associated with neurological abnormalities
- Rocker bottom appearance
- Palpable medial talar head

Radiology in CVT
Imaging
- USG in neonates
- X rays with PF and DF stress views
- Use 1st metatarsal as navicular surrogate below 2 years
- MRI spine to rule out cord anomalies
### Key Angles
- TAMBA >35° indicates CVT
- Talus–1st metatarsal axis non collinear in all views
### Differential Diagnosis

| Feature | CVT | Oblique Talus | Flexible Flatfoot |
|---|---|---|---|
| PF stress view | No reduction | Reduction | Reduction |
| TAMBA | >35° | <35° | <35° |
| Calcaneum | Locked | Locked | Unlocked |

Management of CVT
### Early Presentation
- Serial manipulation and casting (Dobbs method)
- Mini open talonavicular reduction

<!-- page 5 -->

- Tendo Achilles lengthening
### Late Presentation
- Grice Green arthrodesis
- Talectomy
- Triple arthrodesis

### Accessory Navicular Syndrome
- Accessory bone within tibialis posterior tendon.
- Presents with medial foot pain.
- Often mistaken for navicular fracture.
Management
- UCBL orthosis
- Cast immobilization
- Surgical excision for refractory cases

Tarsal Coalition
Definition
Congenital fibrous, cartilaginous or osseous connection between tarsal bones.

### Common Types
- Calcaneonavicular (younger children)
- Talocalcaneal (adolescents)
### Clinical Features
- Pain with activity
- Recurrent ankle sprains
- Rigid flatfoot
- Restricted subtalar motion

### Imaging in Tarsal Coalition
- Weight bearing AP, lateral, oblique views
- CT scan for bony coalitions
- MRI for fibrous coalitions and cartilage status
### Radiological Signs
- Anteater sign: calcaneonavicular coalition
- C sign: talocalcaneal coalition

<!-- page 6 -->

- Harris axial view for subtalar joint
- Saltzman view for hindfoot alignment

Management of Tarsal Coalition
Asymptomatic
- No treatment
Symptomatic
- NSAIDs
- Cast immobilization
- Stretching of tendo Achilles
### Surgical Indications
- Persistent pain
### Surgical Principles
- Coalition resection if:
  - <50 percent facet involvement
  - Posterior facet congruent
- Hindfoot valgus >16° requires concurrent calcaneal lengthening osteotomy
- Arthritis present → arthrodesis

Faculty Quote
“A painful flatfoot is never normal. Always look for rigidity, neurological signs, or coalition
before reassuring the parents.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- Flexible flatfoot is a normal developmental variant.
- Rigid flatfoot is always pathological.
- Jack’s test differentiates flexible flatfoot.
- Subtalar joint must be locked during Silfverskiöld test.
- Obesity and tight tendo Achilles cause pain in flexible flatfoot.
- TAMBA >35° confirms congenital vertical talus.
- Anteater sign is seen in calcaneonavicular coalition.
- MRI is gold standard for fibrous tarsal coalition.
