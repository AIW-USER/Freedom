### Rheumatic Inflammatory Diseases
- Ankylosing spondylitis and other SpA
- Familial Mediterranean fever (autosomal recessive, recurrent fever, polyserocytosis)
- Behçet disease (uveitis, genital ulcers, oral ulcers)
- SAPHO syndrome (synovitis, acne, pustulosis, hyperostosis, osteitis)
- Gout and pseudogout
### Rheumatic Non-Inflammatory Diseases
- Osteitis condensans ilii (self-limiting, pregnancy as aggravating factor)
- Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (hyperostosis of ALL and PLL)
- Osteoarthritis
- Multicentric reticulohistiocytosis (papulonodular lesions, histiocytic infiltration)
### Infectious Diseases
- Pyogenic sacroiliitis (Staphylococcus most common, methicillin sensitive predominates)
- Brucellosis
- Tuberculosis
- Whipple disease
### Other Diseases
- Hyperparathyroidism

### Imaging Modalities for Sacroiliitis
- Lymphoma and malignancy

Radiographs
- Changes visible only after 5 years of disease activity.
- Not useful for early disease detection.

<!-- page 2 -->

CT
- Best for erosions, sclerosis and ankylosis.
- Useful for screening early disease.
- Multiplanar oblique coronal and axial scans parallel and perpendicular to sacrum.
### MRI
- Most sensitive for early sacroiliitis.
- Protocol: T1-weighted fast spin echo and STIR T2-weighted sequences.
- Non-contrast MRI sufficient, contrast enhances synovitis, capsulitis, enthesitis.

### ASAS MRI Criteria
- Part of ASAS criteria.

MRI must show bone marrow edema in: - STIR hyperintensity or - T1 fat-suppressed
contrast enhancement
Additional requirements: - Site: periarticular or subarticular - Number: two or more
lesions on one slice, or one lesion clearly visible on two slices

Normal MRI Anatomy of SI Joint
Structural lesions alone do not satisfy criteria without bone marrow edema.

### Coronal STIR Images
- Ventral lower cartilaginous portion: smooth, parallel margins
- Dorsal upper ligamentous portion: irregular margins
### Axial T1 Images
- Upper portion: ligamentous
- Mid portion: anterior cartilaginous, posterior ligamentous
- Lower portion: cartilaginous
### Anatomical Variants
- Accessory SI joint (degenerative changes)

### Acute Sacroiliitis – MRI Findings
- Bipartite iliac bone (may appear in pregnancy)

1. Subchondral Bone Marrow Edema
- Earliest and most important sign
- Lower and posterior joint involvement
- Symmetric or asymmetric
2. Erosions
- Early on iliac side, later on sacral side

<!-- page 3 -->

3. Synovitis and Joint Effusion
- Hyperintensity in joint space
- Contrast MRI helps differentiate synovitis from effusion
4. Capsulitis and Enthesitis

### Chronic Sacroiliitis – MRI Findings
- Seen as STIR hyperintensity or contrast enhancement

1. Subchondral Fatty Deposition
- T1 hyperintensity, well demarcated, homogeneous
2. Subchondral Sclerosis
- T1/T2 hypointensity
- Must extend at least 5 mm from articular surface
3. Erosions
- Joint space narrowing
- Large erosions may cause pseudo-widening
4. Bony Bridges and Ankylosis

### Acute on Chronic Sacroiliitis
- Partial or complete fusion

- Fat deposition from chronic stage with interspersed active bone marrow edema
- Synovitis and mixed signal changes

### Associated Imaging Findings
- Both T1 and STIR essential to evaluate

- Facetal arthropathy: erosions, osteophytes, fusion
- Enthesitis: trochanter, ischial tuberosity
- MRI corner sign: edema in acute, fatty deposition in chronic
- Bridging syndesmophytes

### CT Findings
- Hip joint effusion

- Subchondral demineralization (early, nonspecific)
- Erosions (iliac early, sacral later)
- Subchondral sclerosis >5 mm
- Bony bridges and ankylosis
- Pseudo-widening from large confluent erosions

Infective Sacroiliitis
### Key Organisms
- Staphylococcus aureus (most common)
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa (most common gram-negative)
- TB and non-brucellar infections (rare)
### Clinical Example
- Pregnant woman, 34 weeks
- Lower back pain radiating to both legs
- Elevated ESR and CRP
- MRI: marrow edema, erosion, sclerosis and effusion
- Diagnosis: infective sacroiliitis in pregnancy
Treatment
- Cefuroxime IV for 10 weeks
- Ciprofloxacin IV for 4 weeks

### Diagnostic Criteria for SI Joint Dysfunction
- Clindamycin IV then oral for 8 + 8 weeks

IASP Criteria: - Pain 3 cm x 10 cm inferior to PSIS - Pain reproduced by provocative

SI Joint Dysfunction Algorithm
tests - Pain relieved by local anesthetic injection

1. Diagnose SIJ pain via history, exam and imaging.
2. Perform diagnostic SI joint injection.
3. If <50 to 70 percent relief → evaluate other causes.
4. If >50 to 70 percent relief → PT and NSAIDs for 4 weeks.
5. If persistent pain → therapeutic SI injection.
6. If relief >3 months → repeat as needed.

### Management of Sacroiliitis
7. If inadequate → radiofrequency ablation or surgery.

### Conservative Management
- Activity modification
- Physiotherapy and chiropractic manipulation
- Pelvic belts (pregnancy, hypermobility)
- Orthotic insoles for limb length discrepancy
- Topical and oral NSAIDs
### Interventional Management
- Prolotherapy (dextrose, PRP)

<!-- page 5 -->

- Corticosteroid injections (fluoroscopy, CT or ultrasound guided)
- Radiofrequency ablation of L5–S2 dorsal rami
- Pulsed RF (modulates c-Fos and ATF3 pathways)
### Surgical Intervention
- Indications: persistent moderate to severe pain after 6 months conservative therapy
- Procedures:
  - Open arthrodesis

### Biologic Therapy in Spondyloarthropathy
  - Minimally invasive percutaneous arthrodesis

### TNF-α Inhibitors
- Etanercept
- Infliximab (IV at 0, 2, 6 weeks then every 6 to 8 weeks)
- Adalimumab
- Golimumab
- Certolizumab
When to start TNF inhibitors: - Axial SpA: BASDAI >4, failure of 2 NSAIDs -
Peripheral SpA: failure of intra-articular steroids and DMARDs - Psoriatic arthritis:
moderate to severe disease - Enthesitis/dactylitis: severe or refractory cases

### Anti-IL17A Agents
- Secukinumab (IgG1κ) – weekly x5 then monthly
- Ixekizumab (IgG4) – monthly
- Bimekizumab, Netakumab (in trials)
### IL-23 Inhibitors
- Risankizumab (targets p19)
- Ustekinumab (targets p40)

### Faculty Quote
