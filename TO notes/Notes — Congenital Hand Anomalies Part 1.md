Overview / Key Concepts
Congenital hand anomalies arise due to disturbances during early embryological
development of the upper limb. Understanding embryology is essential to classify
anomalies, explain patterns of deformity, and plan treatment. Most congenital hand
anomalies develop between the 4th and 7th weeks of gestation.
Key principles: - Majority of anomalies are established early in pregnancy. -
Embryological timing explains the type of anomaly. - Classification systems are exam

Embryology of Upper Limb Development
favourites and guide understanding.

Timeline
- 4th week (≈27 days): Appearance of limb bud.
- By 30 days: Arm bud fully formed.
- 5th week: Limb elongation in three axes.
- 34–38 days: Formation of hand paddle.
- 38–40 days: Onset of finger separation.
- 50–52 days (7th week): Complete finger separation.
Failure at specific stages leads to predictable anomalies such as syndactyly.

### Axes of Limb Development
1. Proximodistal axis – shoulder to hand growth.
2. Radio-ulnar (anteroposterior) axis – thumb to little finger differentiation.
3. Dorsoventral axis – dorsal and palmar surface development.

### Key Embryological Zones
- AER (Apical Ectodermal Ridge): Controls proximodistal growth.
- ZPA (Zone of Polarizing Activity): Controls radio-ulnar differentiation.
- WNT signalling pathway: Controls dorsoventral development.

Classification Systems
Disruption of these pathways explains most congenital hand anomalies.

Swanson Classification (IFSSH Accepted)
A simple and widely used classification with seven categories:
1. Failure of formation – amelia, phocomelia.

<!-- page 2 -->

2. Failure of differentiation – syndactyly.
3. Duplication – polydactyly.
4. Overgrowth – macrodactyly.
5. Undergrowth – brachydactyly.
6. Constriction band syndrome – amniotic bands.
7. Generalized skeletal abnormalities and syndromes.

OMT Classification (Oberg, Manske, Tonkin)
Embryologically sound classification grouping anomalies into three major categories:
1. Malformation – abnormal formation before limb development completed.
2. Deformation – normal formation followed by external distortion.
3. Dysplasia – abnormal tissue organization after formation.

Malformations Based on Axis Involvement
This system correlates anomalies with embryological growth axes.

Syndactyly
- Early surgery preferred to avoid loss of adaptive function

### Key Facts
- Most common congenital hand anomaly.
- Incidence: 1 in 2000, males more commonly affected.
- Familial in 15–40 percent, autosomal dominant.
- Failure of apoptosis during finger separation.
- Commonest type: middle and ring finger (50 percent).
### Timing of Surgery
- Ideal age: 12–18 months.
### Types of Syndactyly
- Simple incomplete – soft tissue only, partial fusion.
- Simple complete – soft tissue fusion including nail.
- Complex – bony fusion present.

<!-- page 4 -->

- Complicated – abnormal skeletal arrangement.
### Principles of Surgical Release
- Staged release if multiple fingers involved.
- Protect neurovascular bundles.
- Release soft tissue and bone.
- Create deep web spaces.
- Cover lateral raw areas.
- Reconstruct nail folds.
### Management of Multiple Fingers
- Two staged release.
- Release lateral digits first.
- Do not release adjacent digits together.
- Prioritize digits with differential growth.
- Interval of 3–6 months between stages.
- Complete releases before school age.
### Surgical Technique Highlights
- Dorsal truncated flap for web creation.
- Volar anchor or triangular flap.
- Zigzag incisions.
- Buck-Gramcko flaps for nail folds.
- Full thickness skin grafts for residual defects.
Complication

### Syndactyly vs Symphalangism
- Web creep is the most common complication.

| Feature | Syndactyly | Symphalangism |
|---|---|---|
| Structure involved | Adjacent fingers | Same finger |
| Fusion | Soft tissue or bone | Longitudinal phalangeal fusion |
| Joint involved | Normal joints | PIP joint absent |
| Management | Surgical | Conservative |

Synostosis
- Metacarpal synostosis: commonly 4th and 5th metacarpals.
- Carpal synostosis: usually conservative.
- Radio-ulnar synostosis:
  - Presents after 3 years.
  - Excess pronation.

Polydactyly
  - Compensatory wrist and shoulder movements.

### Key Facts
- Second most common hand anomaly.
- Types:
  - Preaxial (radial or thumb duplication).
  - Postaxial (ulnar or little finger).
  - Central (rare).
### Wassel Classification (Thumb Duplication)
- Type I to VII based on skeletal duplication.
- Type IV (duplicated proximal phalanx) is most common.
### Principles of Repair
- Use most of both digits.
- Balance soft tissues.
- Repair collateral ligaments.
- Reinsert tendons.
- Create adequate web space.

### Special Anomalies
- Cover all articular surfaces.

### Mirror Hand (Ulnar Dimelia)
- Absence of radial ray.
- Duplication of ulnar side.
- Extremely rare.
- Treatment involves removal of extra digits and creation of a functional thumb.
Triphalangism
- Appears as five-fingered hand.
- Extra middle phalanx (delta phalanx).
- Hypermobile joints, unstable MCP.

Camptodactyly
pollicisation.

- Congenital PIP flexion contracture.
- Usually little finger.
- Managed with splinting at birth.
- Surgery for persistent deformity.

Clinodactyly
- Radial or ulnar curvature of finger.
- Commonly little finger.
- Associated with Down syndrome.

### Faculty Quote
- Managed with closed or open wedge osteotomy if symptomatic.

Exam Pearls
Sharma

- Upper limb development occurs between 4th and 7th weeks.
- Syndactyly results from failure of apoptosis.
- Most common syndactyly is middle and ring finger fusion.
- Ideal age for syndactyly release is 12–18 months.
- Web creep is the most common complication after syndactyly surgery.
- Type IV is the most common Wassel type.
- Symphalangism involves fusion within the same finger.
- Clinodactyly is associated with Down syndrome.
- Mirror hand is also called ulnar dimelia.
