Acute Infections in the Hand – Dr. Aviral Gupta (Mar
2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Same treatment principle for every hand infection: expose the whole infected area, debride thoroughly, leave the wound open, give antibiotics, mobilise early.
- What differs is the anatomy of each space and the pathogenesis; learn those.
- Topics: pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis (with radial and ulnar bursa and horseshoe abscess), paronychia, felon, deep palmar space and web space infections, necrotising fasciitis.

Pyogenic Flexor Tenosynovitis
Anatomy and pathogenesis
- A closed-space infection of the flexor tendon sheath, which runs from around the MCP joint (A1 pulley) to just beyond the DIP joint, inside the fibrous sheath.
- The synovial sheath has an outer parietal layer and an inner visceral layer (epitenon) that join at both ends to form a closed tube of synovial fluid, which nourishes the tendon.
- Bacteria feed on the synovial fluid, pressure rises in the closed sheath and compresses the vincula (vincula longa and brevia, superficialis and profunda), the second source of tendon nutrition: loss of vascularity, purulence, tendon necrosis, stiffness and sometimes amputation. Detect early.

Presentation and organisms
- History of penetrating trauma (thorn prick, animal bite); plump, swollen, red digit.
- Organisms: Staphylococcus aureus or beta-haemolytic streptococci; Pasteurella multocida after animal bites (MCQ); haematogenous cases are mostly gonococcal.

Investigations and differential diagnosis
- CBC, ESR, CRP to follow the disease; X-rays for foreign body, osteomyelitis, pyarthrosis.
- MCQ point: on a lateral X-ray, volar soft tissue at the proximal phalanx more than 7 mm thicker than the dorsal soft tissue predicted flexor tenosynovitis in one study.
- Non-septic tenosynovitis: gout, calcific tendinitis, rheumatoid arthritis, acute stenosing tenosynovitis: less acute, no excruciating pain. Aspirate the sheath; if no pus, treat with NSAIDs.

Radial and ulnar bursae, space of Parona, horseshoe abscess
- The thumb sheath continues as the radial bursa and the little finger sheath as the ulnar bursa, both reaching about 2 cm proximal to the transverse carpal ligament in the distal forearm (anatomically separate but usually continuous).
- Space of Parona: potential space in the distal volar forearm, deep to the flexor tendons and superficial to pronator quadratus (seen when approaching a distal radius fracture). The two bursae communicate here.
- Horseshoe abscess: infection from the thumb (or little finger) sheath tracks up the radial bursa, through Parona space, and down the ulnar bursa (or the reverse).

<!-- page 3 -->

- Bursal infections still show Kanavel signs but are subtler (more room to spread: auto- decompression).
- Hallmark tenderness: radial bursa at the junction of the wrist crease and thenar eminence; ulnar bursa at the junction of the wrist crease and hypothenar eminence.

Management
- Non-operative (narrow indication): only within 24 hours of onset: dorsal block splint, elevation, IV antibiotics. Operate if no improvement in 12 hours (judge by the Kanavel signs).

| Surgical option | Details |
|---|---|
| Limited exposure (Neviaser) | Incision at the A5 pulley distally and the A1 pulley proximally; catheter passed through the sheath for continuous antibiotic irrigation every 1– 2 hours. Cumbersome; only for very early cases |
| Open exposure: mid-lateral incision + palmar incision over A1 | Decompresses the pulley system and the digit; debride necrotic sheath; irrigate through a catheter from the palm until clear fluid exits |
| Open exposure: Brunner zig-zag incision | For advanced infection needing wider exposure and thorough debridement; more scarring |

- Leave wounds open for drainage; dorsal slab; heal by secondary intention once the infection settles.
- IV antibiotics 7–10 days, then oral to complete 4 weeks.
- Active movement within 12–48 hours (as comfort allows) to prevent adhesions and preserve tendon excursion.
Poor outcome factors (MCQ): age over 43, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease or renal
failure, subcutaneous purulence, digital ischaemia, polymicrobial infection, human bite.

Acute paronychia
- Infection of the soft tissue fold around the nail after disruption of the seal between nail fold and plate (nail biting, manicure, or no cause).
- Staphylococcus aureus is the commonest organism.
- Eponychia: infection of one nail fold and the eponychium. Runaround infection: pus travels from one nail fold to the other through the eponychium.
- Screen for diabetes; X-ray for osteomyelitis.
- Early (no pus point): first-generation cephalosporin + warm povidone-iodine soaks (1 part povidone-iodine to 10 parts warm saline, 10–15 minutes twice a day).
- Surgery: "follow the infection": one or two incisions, removal of the nail plate, or volar exposure if it has spread volarly.

### Felon (Pulp Space Infection)
- Subcutaneous abscess of the distal pulp; does not extend beyond the DIP joint unless the joint or sheath is involved. Infection limited to the skin of the pulp tip = apical infection.
- Anatomy: fibrous septa run from the pulp skin to the distal phalanx, dividing the pulp into compartments (each with its artery, sweat glands, nerve endings). They give a firm, non-slip grip; the pulp is "the second eye" because of its rich sensory supply.
- Pathogenesis: prick inoculates sweat glands: cellulitis and abscess: "compartment syndrome" of the pulp: ischaemia, possible sequestration of the distal phalanx, finally spontaneous decompression through the skin.
- Children rarely get epiphyseal necrosis because the epiphyseal artery arises proximal to the closed pulp space.
- Early: oral antibiotics, warm povidone-iodine soaks, immobilisation. Pus point: surgery.

Felon incision
- Keep the scar away from the pinch surface: radial side of the thumb and little finger; ulnar side of the index, middle and ring fingers.
- But incise over the most tender point, whichever side it is; do not enter the flexor sheath; leave the wound open.

### Deep and Superficial Space Infections
- Deep spaces: thenar, midpalmar, hypothenar (potential spaces between flexor tendons and the metacarpals/interossei). Superficial: dorsal subcutaneous, dorsal subaponeurotic, interdigital web space (less defined, can mimic deep infections).

| Space | Dorsal | Volar | Radial | Ulnar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thenar | Adductor pollicis fascia, volar 2nd metacarpal, first dorsal interosseous fascia | Index flexor sheath, radial palmar fascia | Adductor pollicis fascia and palmar fascia at the thumb proximal phalanx | Midpalmar (oblique) septum (3rd metacarpal to palmar fascia) |
| Midpalmar | Fascia over 2nd/3rd volar interossei; periosteum of 3rd –5th metacarpals | Flexor sheaths of middle, ring, little fingers; palmar aponeurosis | Midpalmar oblique septum | Hypothenar septum (5th metacarpal to palmar fascia) |
| Hypothenar | Periosteum of 5th metacarpal, deep hypothenar muscle fascia | Palmar fascia, superficial hypothenar fascia | Hypothenar septum | Hypothenar muscle fascia |

- Thenar drainage: volar and/or dorsal incisions (combined for extensive pus); protect the recurrent (motor) branch of the median nerve and the princeps pollicis artery; avoid a transverse dorsal first-web incision (web contracture).
- Hypothenar drainage: incision in line with the ulnar border of the ring finger; protect the deep (motor) branch of the ulnar nerve.

- Protect digital nerves, arteries and flexor tendons in every incision; length depends on the extent of pus.

Web space ("collar button") abscess
- Pus in the loose web-space tissue tracks both volarly and dorsally: hourglass (collar-button) swelling with little in between.
- The finger lies ABDUCTED (not seen in a dorsal subcutaneous infection, which has no volar pus).
- Chronic web-space infection = "barber's abscess" (MCQ).

### Necrotising Fasciitis
- Case: 42-year-old diabetic farmer, thorn prick 3 days earlier, rapidly increasing swelling and pain of the upper limb, no fever; within hours cyanotic patches, epidermolysis and bullae; anxiety, feeling of impending doom, tachycardia, hypotension, electrolyte imbalance.
- Pathogenesis: bacteria proliferate in the subcutaneous and suprafascial planes; endo- and exotoxins spread the infection LENGTHWISE along fascial planes (few barriers) rather than deep; microcirculation thromboses: ischaemia and necrosis. Skin turns red, purple, blue-grey, then bullae and necrosis.

| Type | Organisms |
|---|---|
| 1 (commonest, about 80%) | Mixed aerobic and anaerobic (facultative anaerobes and non-group-A streptococci) |
| 2 | Group A streptococcus alone or with staphylococci: toxic shock, severe sepsis, early death |
| 3 (fungal) | Mucormycosis in the immunocompromised (seen in the COVID period) |

Clinical clues (all potential MCQs)
- Risk: diabetes, IV drug use, alcoholism, peripheral vascular disease.

<!-- page 7 -->

- Diffuse erythema, swelling, tenderness around the inoculation site, but NON-pitting oedema beyond the area of cellulitis, peau d'orange beyond the swelling, hypo- or anaesthetic skin, tenderness beyond the erythema.
- Hallmark: a finger or probe dissects easily along the fascia beneath intact skin.
- X-ray: gas in the soft tissues.

| LRINEC criterion (standard values) | Points |
|---|---|
| CRP 15 mg/dL or more | 4 |
| WBC 15–25 / more than 25 (x10^3/mm³) | 1/2 |
| Haemoglobin 11–13.5 / less than 11 g/dL | 1/2 |
| Sodium less than 135 mmol/L | 2 |
| Creatinine more than 1.6 mg/dL | 2 |
| Glucose more than 180 mg/dL | 1 |

- Score 5 or less: low risk (under 50%); 6–7: intermediate (50–75%); 8 or more: high (over 75%).
- Bedside biopsy (rarely needed): proximal to the erythema over the oedema, down to fascia, 1 cm² of fascia for frozen section: thrombosis of perforating vessels and massive polymorphonuclear infiltration confirm it. Do not delay surgery waiting for it.

Treatment: surgical emergency
- Extensive debridement of all involved skin, subcutaneous tissue (and muscle if involved, a poor sign) beyond the visible margin; repeat as needed.
- Findings: "dishwater" fluid instead of pus; grey-green or liquefied fascia with normal-looking patches in between; fingers pass easily along normally tough planes.
- Spread to the shoulder and chest wall carries more than 75% mortality.
Factors increasing mortality (MCQ): age over 50, diabetes, truncal involvement, bacteraemia
at presentation, raised creatinine, hypoalbuminaemia, delay in diagnosis and treatment.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Kanavel: sheath tenderness (most reliable), fusiform swelling, pain on passive extension, flexed posture.
- Pasteurella multocida in animal bites; gonococcus in haematogenous tenosynovitis.
- Space of Parona: deep to flexors, superficial to pronator quadratus; horseshoe abscess.
- Felon incision away from the pinch surface; children spared epiphyseal necrosis.
- Thenar: protect recurrent median branch; hypothenar: protect deep ulnar branch.
- Web space abscess: abducted finger; chronic = barber's abscess.
- Necrotising fasciitis: oedema and tenderness beyond erythema, anaesthetic skin, easy finger dissection, dishwater fluid; LRINEC 8 or more high risk.

### Take-Home Summary
- Know the closed spaces of the hand and how pus spreads between them; that tells you where to cut.
- Operate early, leave wounds open, mobilise early; in necrotising fasciitis debride radically and urgently.
