Tendon Injuries of Hand – Dr. Amit Mittal
Overview
Flexor tendon injuries of the hand are common but challenging due to the intricate
anatomy of flexor tendons, pulleys, vincula, and neurovascular structures. Accurate
assessment, atraumatic surgical repair, and planned rehabilitation are critical for optimal
function.
Faculty Remark: “Straight finger alone does not diagnose a tendon injury. Proper
evaluation determines whether the injury is partial or complete.”

Anatomy Essentials
### Normal Flexor Cascade
- Progressive flexion from index to little finger in resting posture.
- Thumb shows mild flexion.
- Loss of cascade suggests tendon injury. turn36file1
### Tenodesis Effect
- Wrist extension → finger flexion if tendons intact.
- Wrist flexion → finger extension.
- Absent tenodesis effect suggests FDS/FDP or FPL discontinuity. turn36file0
### Functional Anatomy
- FDS splits into two slips at proximal phalanx, rotates 180 degrees, allowing FDP to pass through (chiasma). Injury near this level requires restoring rotation for proper gliding. turn36file0
- Vincula System:
  - Vincula brevia: short, attach near insertions.
  - Vincula longa: supply proximal tendon; FDP vincula longa arises from FDS tendon. turn36file0
### Pulley System
- Annular pulleys (A1–A5): maintain tendon-bone contact, improve force efficiency.
- Cruciate pulleys: flexible, collapse during flexion to prevent annular pulley deformation.
- Critical pulleys: A2 and A4 (must be preserved). turn36file1

Clinical Assessment
Skin
- Record number, location, severity of lacerations.
- Identify doubtful skin viability. turn36file1
### Tendon Examination
- Test FDS: Isolate finger, flex at PIP.
- Test FDP: Block PIP, flex DIP.
- Painful active flexion → partial injury.
- Painless full flexion → intact tendon.
- Active flexion does NOT rule out partial tear. turn36file0
### Neurovascular Exam
- Light touch and static two-point discrimination preferred.
- Delayed capillary refill → arterial injury. turn36file0
Imaging
- X-ray for fractures, foreign bodies. turn36file1

### Flexor Tendon Zones (Verdan Classification)
- Zone 1: Distal to FDS insertion.
- Zone 2: A1 pulley to FDS insertion – “No man’s land”.
- Zone 3: Lumbrical origin to A1 pulley.
- Zone 4: Carpal tunnel.
- Zone 5: Musculotendinous junction to proximal carpal tunnel. turn36file1

Surgical Principles
### When to Repair
- >60 percent tendon laceration: repair required.
- <50 percent: conservative or epitendinous suture.
- Do NOT attempt repair after 6 weeks; consider reconstruction. turn36file0
Timing
- Best within 2 weeks; acceptable up to 3 weeks before retraction complicates repair. turn36file1
### Incision Principles
- Cross flexion creases obliquely or transversely.

<!-- page 3 -->

- Prefer mid-lateral incision. turn36file1
### Atraumatic Handling
- Use non-toothed forceps to hold endotendon, not epitendon.
- Stabilize tendon with 25G, 5/8 inch needle. turn36file1
### Suture Technique
- 4-strand core repair adequate for early active motion.
- Examples:
  - Two-strand: Modified Kessler, Tajiama.
  - Multistrand: Strickland, Savage, Becker.
- Suture purchase: 10 mm from cut edge.
- Locking loops provide superior strength.
- Add 6-0 epitendinous running suture → improves gliding and adds 20 percent strength. turn36file1
### Pulley Management
- Preserve A2, A4, and thumb oblique pulley.
- Tendon repairs are weakest between post-op day 6 to 20. turn36file1
### Order of Repair (Practical Sequence)
1. FDP, FPL (deep tendons)
2. FDS
3. Artery
4. Nerve
5. Superficial tendons (FCR, FCU, PL) turn36file1

Specific Zones – Key Points
### Zone 1 – FDP Injuries
- If distal stump ≥1 cm, primary repair is possible.
- If <1 cm, avoid repair, perform tendon advancement or bone pull-out technique.
- Avoid over-advancement (>1 cm): causes Quadriga effect (loss of flexion in adjacent fingers). turn36file1
### FDP Avulsion Injuries
- Type I: Retracted to palm, no vincula supply → urgent repair.
- Type II: Retracted to A4 pulley.
- Type III: Large fragment blocks retraction.
- Type IV: Avulsion + small bony fragment → retracts into palm. turn36file1

<!-- page 4 -->

### Zone 2 – “No Man’s Land”
- Requires meticulous FDS and FDP repair.
- Restore FDS slip rotation to recreate chiasma.
- Pulley reconstruction often needed.
- Avoid sacrificing both FDS slips. turn36file0
### Zone 3 and 4
- Multiple tendon plus neurovascular injuries common.
- Repair tendons first, then nerves and vessels. turn36file0
### Zone 5
- Often combined injuries to FDS, FDP, FPL, FCR, FCU, PL, radial and ulnar arteries, and nerves.
- Requires systematic exposure and layered repair. turn36file1

Rehabilitation Principles
### Controlled Postoperative Regimen
- Aim: regulate force on repair site, promote tendon excursion, reduce adhesions.
- Required tendon excursion: Minimum 3–5 mm, optimal 6–9 mm. turn36file1
### Early Active Motion
- Dorsal blocking splint.
- “Place and hold” technique. turn36file1
### Early Passive Motion
- Duran protocol: Patient-assisted passive flexion and extension.
- Kleinert protocol: Dynamic traction-assisted passive flexion with active extension.
- Mayosynergistic splint: Low velocity, high excursion. turn36file1
Progression
- Splint discontinued at 6–8 weeks.
- Complex repairs may need passive-only protocols for first 4 weeks. turn36file1

Complications
- Rupture: Re-repair if <3 weeks; tendon graft if >3 weeks.
- Adhesions: Consider tenolysis at 4–6 months.
- Quadriga effect: Due to over-advancement of FDP. turn36file1

<!-- page 5 -->

### Reconstruction Ladder
1. Do nothing (if unsuitable candidate).
2. Tenolysis.
3. Tendon transfer.
4. Primary tendon graft.
5. Two-stage tendon graft (for scarred bed). turn36file1

### Exam Pearls
- Zone 2 is most challenging due to sheath and pulleys.
- Painful flexion → partial tear.
- Straight finger alone does not confirm complete tear.
- Preserve A2 and A4 pulleys.
- Locking loops are stronger than grasping loops.
- Tendon repairs are weakest from day 6 to 20.
- FDP advancement >1 cm → Quadriga effect.
- Duran vs Kleinert: passive-passive vs passive-active.

Final Faculty Insight: “Good repair needs good handling, correct suture technique,
and a disciplined rehab program. The first attempt must be the best attempt.”
