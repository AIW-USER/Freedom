Fractures of Trochanteric, Sub-Trochanteric and
Shaft Femur – Dr. Anurag Tiwari
Overview
These fractures encompass a wide spectrum of high and low energy injuries involving
the proximal femur and shaft. Their management depends on fracture stability,
biomechanical forces, soft tissue condition, and patient profile. High velocity injuries
commonly occur in young adults, while trivial trauma affects the elderly.
Faculty Remark: “Never fix a proximal femur fracture in varus. Varus is the enemy.”
### Anatomy and Pathomechanics
- Complex structure with both cortical and cancellous bone.
- Calcar femorale acts as the major compressive strut.
- Deforming forces:
  - Gluteus medius/minimus: abduction and external rotation of proximal fragment.
  - Iliopsoas: flexion of proximal fragment.
  - Adductors/hamstrings: medialization and shortening of shaft.
- Hip capsule: anterior attachment at intertrochanteric line; posterior capsule ends proximal to crest.

### Clinical Presentation
- Pain and inability to bear weight.
- Limb externally rotated and shortened.
- Ecchymosis may be present.
- Loss of transmitted movements.
- Occult fractures present with pain on weight bearing.

Imaging
### Standard Views
- X ray pelvis with both hips in 15 degree internal rotation.
- Lateral view for assessing comminution.
### CT Scan
- Determines posteromedial comminution.

- Evaluates lateral wall thickness.
- Identifies subtrochanteric extension.

Intertrochanteric Fractures
Stable vs Unstable Patterns
Stable: intact posteromedial cortex, intact lateral wall.
Unstable: - Posteromedial comminution. - Reverse oblique pattern. - Subtrochanteric
extension. - Incompetent lateral wall (<20.5 mm).

Classifications
### Boyd and Griffin
- Type I: stable.
- Type II: unstable, comminuted.
- Type III: reverse oblique.
- Type IV: intertrochanteric with subtrochanteric extension.
Evans
- Type 1: stable.
- Type 2: reverse oblique.
### Jensen Modification
- Accounts for GT/LT involvement and reverse obliquity.
### AO Classification
- 31A1: simple.
- 31A2: multifragmentary.
- 31A3: reverse oblique.

Subtrochanteric Fractures
### Common Features
- High deforming forces.
- Extension into piriformis fossa or LT region.
Classifications
### Russell and Taylor
- Based on piriformis fossa and lesser trochanter involvement.
Seinsheimer
- Grades 1 to 5, based on number and pattern of fragments.

Shaft Femur Fractures
Features
- Common in high energy trauma.
- Up to 1 to 1.5 liters of blood loss.
- Often associated with fat embolism.
### Winquist and Hansen
- Grade 0 to 4 based on comminution.

Management Principles
Non Operative
Reserved for: - Medically unfit. - Non ambulatory. - Undisplaced fractures.
Traction options: - Skin traction. - Russell’s traction (balanced traction). - Boot and bar
traction (derotation bar).

### Reduction Principles
- Always attempt closed reduction first.
- Avoid varus, internal rotation, or retroversion malreduction.
### Tips for Reduction
1. Perform reduction before making entry for nail.
2. Use blunt Steinmann pin for manipulation.
3. Correct posterior sag.
4. Use reamer as reduction lever.
5. Use temporary fixation with K wires.

Implant Options
Extramedullary
- DHS: for stable fractures.
- Trochanteric stabilization plate: for lateral wall deficiency.
- Blade plate: reverse oblique.
Intramedullary
- PFN: preferred in unstable patterns.
- Locking plates: for specific scenarios.

<!-- page 4 -->

Arthroplasty
- Considered in elderly with arthritis or failed previous fixation.
- GT must be fixed.

### DHS – Key Concepts
- Controlled collapse construct.
- Tip Apex Distance < 25 mm.
- Lag screw placement: center center.
- Contraindicated in unstable and reverse oblique patterns.
- Lag screw > 85 mm → long barrel.

Cephalomedullary Nails
Advantages
- Excellent biomechanical control.
- Acts as lateral buttress.
- Prevents medialization.
Complication
- Z effect: proximal migration of screws.
### Entry Points
- Piriformis fossa or greater trochanter tip.

Shaft Femur – Nailing Principles
### Antegrade Nailing
- Entry point: piriformis or greater trochanter.
- Reduction tips: bent guide wire, sheet, reamer manipulation.
Reaming
- Advantages: larger nail, better stability, bone graft deposition.
- Disadvantages: thermal necrosis, fat embolism.
Retrograde Nailing
Indications: - Bilateral shaft fractures. - Polytrauma. - Floating knee. - Ipsilateral
acetabulum / patella fracture.
Entry Point: - Just anterior and medial to PCL attachment. - Apex of Blumensaat line.
Nail Length: - Should reach up to or above lesser trochanter.

### Neck with Shaft Femur Fracture
- Combined fixation often required.
- Debate between single versus dual implant approach.

### Exam and Viva Pearls
- Lateral wall <20.5 mm → unstable IT fracture.
- Z effect is classic complication of PFN.
- TAD must be <25 mm.
- Reverse oblique fractures → PFN.
- Subtrochanteric fractures have strongest deforming forces.
- Never accept varus in proximal femur fixation.
- Retrograde nails: entry at Blumensaat line.

Final Faculty Insight
“Your reduction determines your result. Even the best implant fails if the reduction is
wrong.”
