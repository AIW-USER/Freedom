Introduction to Flaps and the Big 5 – Dr. Priyanka
Sharma (Oct 2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Follows the lecture on basics of microvascular surgery (equipment, instruments, techniques). This lecture covers flap definitions, the main classifications, special flap terms, free flap monitoring and the "Big 5" free flaps.
- Reconstructive ladder vs elevator: the old ladder went step by step (secondary intention/primary closure, skin graft, local or regional flap, distant pedicled flap, free flap last). Today the reconstructive ELEVATOR is used: if a free flap is the best option (and the patient agrees), go straight to it, even for a small defect.
- Dermal substitutes (e.g. AlloDerm, Biobrane: human acellular dermal matrix) can let a wound granulate and take a skin graft instead of needing a flap.
- For every flap know: the pedicle (blood supply), the size that can be harvested, the components (skin, muscle, bone, nerve), whether it can be neurotized, and the donor site morbidity.

Definitions
- Flap: tissue attached at one end and raised from the other sides (like a door on its hinge).
- Pedicled flap: carries its own blood supply on its neurovascular pedicle and is moved locally, regionally or distantly. Common lower limb examples: reverse sural artery flap, gastrocnemius muscle flap; also the pedicled groin flap.
- Free flap: completely detached; its donor vessels are anastomosed to recipient vessels (microvascular surgery).

### Special Flap Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Propeller flap | Island flap based on a single perforator that reaches the defect by axial rotation of up to 180 degrees (like a fan blade). Flap length must cover the distance from perforator to defect plus the defect. Donor site usually closed primarily. Perforator must be skeletonized and the flap fully islanded to avoid torsion/kinking. Common in lower limb cover (e.g. over the lateral malleolus). |
| Supercharging | Extra vascular source from an UNRELATED distant vessel anastomosed to the flap to boost inflow or outflow (e.g. superior unipedicled TRAM flap). |
| Turbocharging | Main vascular source connected to an additional pedicle FROM THE SAME flap (e.g. joining ipsilateral and contralateral deep inferior epigastric vessels of a TRAM flap). TRAM now largely replaced by the perforator-based DIEP flap (no rectus sacrifice). |
| Standard delay | Incise the periphery / partly elevate the flap, put it back, elevate fully after 10 to 14 days; "trains" the flap to survive. Used for pedicled flaps (e.g. groin flap), not free flaps. |
| Strategic delay | Divide selected (minor) pedicles so the flap survives on the main pedicle (e.g. gracilis), elevate after 2 weeks. |

Compound flaps (Hallock classification)
- Solitary compound flap: composite flap on a single source of circulation.
- Combined compound flaps (multiple sources): conjoined (at least two distinct territories, each with its own supply, joined by a common physical territory) and chimeric (independent flaps, each with its own supply, all linked to one large source vessel).
- Classic chimeric flap: scapular/parascapular system. Subscapular artery (from the axillary artery) gives the circumflex scapular artery (transverse and descending cutaneous branches: scapular and parascapular skin paddles; periosteal supply to the lateral border) and the thoracodorsal artery (latissimus dorsi; angular branch to the scapular tip). Skin, muscle and two bone segments can be raised on one pedicle.

### Principles and Monitoring of Free Flaps
- Indications: oncological reconstruction, trauma, burns. Needs teamwork, the right equipment (microscope), the right timing (critical in trauma) and "do no harm". Primary reconstruction is now usual in oncology; secondary reconstruction is still used.
- Clinical monitoring is the gold standard: warm to touch, normal (light pink) colour, not white and not congested, and a slow sustained bright red ooze on pricking or scratching.
- Other methods: handheld Doppler over the marked perforator/pedicle, laser Doppler, implantable devices, digital plethysmography, radioisotope clearance, fluorescein perfusion, transcutaneous oxygen tension, photoplethysmography (mainly when staff for clinical checks is limited).

### Other Commonly Used Flaps
- Groin flap: axial flap on the superficial circumflex iliac vessels; about 10 to 12 cm long and at least 8 cm wide; thin pliable tissue, hidden scar, no functional loss. Used for hand and upper limb cover when microsurgery is not possible. Drawback: hand kept "in the pocket" for 2 to 3 weeks until division, so hygiene is difficult.
- Scapular and parascapular flaps: on the circumflex scapular vessels (parascapular = ellipse along the inferolateral border); skin alone, with bone, or as a chimeric flap for complex head and neck reconstruction.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Reconstructive elevator has replaced the ladder.
- Random flap 1:1 in the lower limb, up to 3:1 in the face.
- Mathes-Nahai: I TFL/gastrocnemius, II gracilis/soleus, III gluteus maximus/serratus, IV sartorius/EHL, V LD/pectoralis major.
- Propeller flap: perforator island rotated up to 180 degrees.

<!-- page 10 -->

- Supercharging = unrelated distant vessel; turbocharging = vessel from the same flap.
- Standard delay 10 to 14 days; prefabrication flap raised at about 6 weeks.
- Best free flap monitoring = clinical (colour, warmth, bright red ooze on pricking).
- Allen test: under 6 s safe, 12 s or more do not take the radial forearm flap.
- Fibula: peroneal artery, 22 to 26 cm; peronea magna is an absolute contraindication; fibular head needs the anterior tibial artery.

### Take-Home Summary
- Choose the flap by what the defect needs (skin, muscle, nerve, bone) and know each flap by its pedicle, size, components, nerve and donor morbidity. ALT, radial forearm, latissimus dorsi, gracilis and fibula cover most reconstructions; monitor free flaps clinically.
