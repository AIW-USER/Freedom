### Overview / Key Concepts
- Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is a LOWER motor neuron lesion: areflexia, absent anal wink, bilateral leg weakness and sensory loss, but NEVER a positive Babinski (that is an UMN sign).
- There is no single defining criterion; it is a clinical AND radiological diagnosis that needs a high index of suspicion.
- Time-dependent recovery: delay causes permanent sphincter and sexual dysfunction and has major medico-legal consequences.
- Canal PRESSURE on the roots matters more than the size of the disc or the duration of compression.
- Spine is now a separate FNB exam, so expect more questions (several FNB-style MCQs were discussed in this lecture).

### Definition and Epidemiology
- Definition: neuromuscular and urogenital symptoms from simultaneous compression of multiple lumbosacral nerve roots below the level of the conus medullaris.
- Incidence: rare, 1 in 33,000 to 1 in 100,000; seen in 1–6% of lumbar disc cases. Because it is rare, it is easy to miss.
- Morbidity: sphincter and sexual dysfunction, physical disability, psychological distress, social disruption, lawsuits.

Anatomy
- The conus ends at the lower border of L1 (between L1 and L2). The roots that continue down to the coccyx form the cauda equina ("horse tail").
- Cauda equina = L2 to S5 and the coccygeal nerve; supplies the lumbar, sacral and coccygeal regions.

Why the cauda equina is vulnerable and why the bladder goes first
- The roots have no Schwann cell covering, so they are sensitive to compressive and tensile forces.
- They lack a regional segmental blood supply; the central part of the root bundle is relatively hypovascular, so compression causes ischaemia.
- The sacral roots lie centrally in the thecal sac at every level (L2–L3 down to L5–S1), exactly in the hypovascular zone. So bladder symptoms often appear before motor or sensory loss.

In CES (parasympathetic + somatic lost)
- Detrusor areflexia: denervation of both afferent and efferent supply (S2–S4), an LMN lesion.
- Autonomous or "sacral" bladder: flaccid, insensate, cannot initiate micturition; voiding problem with no urgency; overflow incontinence.
- Unsafe bladder: residual urine predisposes to infection.

Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Disc prolapse (commonest) | Large central disc; about 6% of lumbar disc prolapses (often L5–S1 or L4–L5) |
| Trauma | Burst fracture below L2 with a retropulsed fragment |
| Tumours | Metastases; intradural tumours (schwannoma, intradural lymphoma, cystic teratoma) |
| Infection | Epidural abscess (including after microdiscectomy), tuberculous granulation tissue |
| Iatrogenic / haematoma | Postoperative epidural haematoma; spontaneous haematoma in bleeding disorders |
| Degenerative | Spinal stenosis, high-grade listhesis (gradual, so less often) |

Pathophysiology
- Compression neuropathy stage: direct fibre injury with capillary haemorrhage and oedema; decompression gives good recovery.
- Ischaemic stage: when the compression pressure exceeds the mean arterial blood pressure the roots become ischaemic; even prompt decompression gives poor recovery.
- Electrophysiological dysfunction has a sigmoid response; compressive pressure has a larger effect than duration; the outcome depends most on the function left before decompression.

<!-- page 4 -->

- This explains why some patients operated on early do poorly while some operated on late recover well.
- Two mechanisms: mechanical compression reduces blood flow and CSF diffusion to the root; and an intraneural compartment syndrome (venous congestion and oedema reduce arterial perfusion further). Both end in root ischaemia.

Presentation
Types of onset (Tandon and Sankaran)

| Type | Presentation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Rapid onset of CES as the first symptom of a disc herniation, no previous back problems |
| 2 | Acute bladder or CES symptoms after a long history of back pain with or without sciatica |
| 3 | Long-standing back problems with gradual progression to numbness and urinary symptoms (often spinal stenosis) |

- Onset can be acute (hours) or gradual (weeks to months); complete (painless incontinence) or incomplete (some sphincter function).
- CES cannot be predicted: only about 19% of patients presenting with red-flag symptoms actually have CES on MRI.
- Obesity is the only physical factor linked to CES: odds 3.1 times higher.
- Usual sequence (222 cases): back pain, leg pain and paraesthesia, motor symptoms, sciatica, then perineal symptoms and bowel/bladder dysfunction.

Clinical features
- Severe low back pain, bilateral sciatica, saddle and genital sensory loss, bowel, bladder and sexual dysfunction.

- Urinary: loss of bladder-full sensation, retention, overflow incontinence (urine running down the thigh).
- Motor: LMN weakness, often multilevel.
- Reflexes: absent ankle jerk (S1), absent anal wink (S2–S4), absent bulbocavernosus reflex (S2–S4).

### Examination and Investigations
- Power, sensation and reflexes, plus a perianal examination: digital rectal examination (tone and voluntary squeeze), bulbocavernosus reflex, perineal sensation. Document all of it (medico-legal).
- Is DRE reliable? In a model-anus study (Spine 2015) doctors judged anal tone correctly only 64% of the time; the squeeze test was far more reliable. Still do a PR and test the squeeze.
- Post-void residual (PVR) bladder scan: PVR below 200 mL has a 98% negative predictive value for CES; PVR of 200 mL or more increases the odds of CES about 20-fold (Venkatesan, Spine 2019). Routine in the UK.

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| MRI (gold standard) | Emergency; add a whole-spine screening sequence to rule out tandem (cervical) stenosis |

### Classification by Stage

| Stage | Features | MRI | Surgery |
|---|---|---|---|
| CES-S (suspected) | Bilateral radiculopathy and/or subjective sphincter or perineal symptoms; no objective signs | Urgent (within 24 h) | Urgent (within 24 h) |
| CES-I (incomplete) | Objective signs; voluntary micturition kept: impaired urinary sensation, loss of desire to void, poor stream, straining, blunted perianal sensation; catheter-tug (trigone) sensation intact | Emergency | Emergency |
| CES-R (retention) | Painless neurogenic retention, overflow incontinence, complete saddle anaesthesia, absent trigone sensation | Emergency | Emergency |

Key message: CES should be diagnosed in the early stage, before sphincter loss (Sun et al, Spine
2014).

Treatment
Timing

| Surgery within | Normal bladder recovery |
|---|---|
| 24 hours | 88.9% |
| 24–48 hours | 79% |
| More than 48 hours | 44% |

<!-- page 7 -->

- British (BASS) standards: operate within 24 hours where possible (for example the first case next morning); beyond 48 hours is regarded as negligent.
- Ahn 2000 meta-analysis (322 patients): surgery within 48 hours gives better results; within 24 hours is no better than 24–48 hours.
- Qureshi and Sell 2007: no significant difference between under 24, 24–48 and over 48 hours; severity of bladder dysfunction at surgery is the main predictor.
- Srikandarajah 2015: surgery within 24 hours helps CES-I; timing makes no difference in CES-R.

Technique

| Procedure | Details |
|---|---|
| Microdiscectomy | Unilateral laminotomy + medial facetectomy + discectomy through a 2 cm incision (or tube); keeps the midline. For a massive soft disc in a younger patient |
| Laminectomy | Bilateral laminectomy + medial facetectomy + discectomy; spinous process can be kept (undercutting) or removed. Wider view, less dural retraction, better decompression; risk of later instability |
| Laminectomy + fusion | Rarely; when there is instability or listhesis |

- CES decompression is harder than routine microdiscectomy: the canal is tight and the root is hard to retract. Convert to laminectomy rather than cause iatrogenic instability.

Outcomes
- Motor recovery may continue up to 1 year; bladder function up to 16 months; residual bladder deficits may persist.
- In a 147-patient review, sexual dysfunction improved least (residual in about 29% at follow- up).
- No study compares microdiscectomy with wide decompression; both are acceptable.

Complications

| Complication | Points |
|---|---|
| Dural tear (1–4%) | Postural headache, pseudomeningocele. Primary repair (Prolene) with a fat graft or sealant; bed rest 4–7 days; lumbar drain if needed. Gelfoam alone is not enough for a symptomatic leak. Outcome same if treated well |
| Iatrogenic segmental instability | From aggressive medial facetectomy or pars removal |
| Epidural fibrosis | Back and leg pain about 3 months later; gadolinium MRI separates it from recurrent disc; re-exploration does poorly |

| Wound infection | About 1%; diabetics |
|---|---|
| Vascular injury | Rare: instruments through the ALL; immediate resuscitation and vascular help; mortality up to 50% |
| Untreated / late | Sexual and urinary dysfunction (may need permanent catheterisation), chronic pain, leg weakness |

Exam & Viva Pearls (MCQs discussed)
- NOT a feature of CES: positive Babinski (UMN sign).
- Most common symptom of CES: back pain (94%), not bladder symptoms.
- Young man with unilateral radiculopathy, back pain and saddle anaesthesia: cauda equina syndrome with urinary retention and absent ankle jerk (urinary retention defines CES more than perianal numbness).
- Hemi-CES differs from CES in all of the following EXCEPT: the cause is usually a herniated disc (both).
- Patient with a pacemaker and a cauda/conus picture: CT myelogram before surgery.
- CES in 1–6% of lumbar disc cases; PVR below 200 mL: 98% NPV.
- Bladder in CES: detrusor areflexia, autonomous (sacral, LMN) bladder with overflow.
- Stages: CES-S, CES-I, CES-R; operate within 24 h ideally and certainly within 48 h.

### Take-Home Summary
- Think CES in any back pain with bilateral leg symptoms, perineal change or bladder complaints; examine the perineum, do a PR with squeeze test and a PVR scan.
- Emergency MRI (with whole-spine screening) and dynamic X-ray; CT myelogram if MRI is impossible.
- Decompress urgently (within 24–48 h); add fusion only for instability. Counsel about slow and incomplete bladder and sexual recovery.
