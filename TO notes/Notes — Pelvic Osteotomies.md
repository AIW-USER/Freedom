Pelvic Osteotomies – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Pelvic osteotomies are cornerstone procedures in pediatric and adolescent hip
reconstruction. They are not interchangeable surgeries and must be selected based
on age, pathology, acetabular deficiency pattern, femoral orientation, and presence of
arthritis.
All pediatric hip disorders may require a pelvic osteotomy at some stage, but the type
of osteotomy differs for each condition. The ultimate goal is to provide a stable,
concentric, and congruent hip joint, thereby preventing early osteoarthritis.

Indications for Pelvic Osteotomy
Common conditions requiring pelvic osteotomy: - Developmental Dysplasia of Hip
(DDH) - Residual acetabular dysplasia - Adolescent hip dysplasia - Neuromuscular hip
instability (CP) - Perthes disease - Sequelae of SCFE

Radiological Assessment of Acetabular Dysplasia
### Lateral Center Edge Angle (LCEA)
- <20°: Acetabular dysplasia
- 20–25°: Borderline dysplasia
- 25–40°: Normal
- 40°: Pincer morphology (FAI)
### Acetabular Inclination (AI)
- <3°: Pincer morphology
- 3–13°: Normal
- 13°: Acetabular dysplasia
### Acetabular Index (Age Dependent)
- Decreases with age
- Around 25° at 1 year is normal
- Around 10° by adolescence
Always interpret AI using age-specific normative values.

### Advanced Imaging
- CT scan for:
  - Anterior and posterior acetabular coverage
  - Acetabular version
  - 3D reconstruction for spatial understanding
- Femoral version assessment is mandatory
  - Excessive femoral anteversion affects final outcome
  - Murphy’s CT protocol improves accuracy

### Aims of Pelvic Osteotomy
- Provide stable, concentric, congruent hip
- Improve biomechanics
- Reduce contact pressures
- Prevent progression to hip arthritis

### Factors Guiding Choice of Osteotomy
- Severity and location of dysplasia
- Patient age and skeletal maturity
- Status of triradiate cartilage
- Femoral version and neck-shaft angle
- Presence of arthritis
- Surgeon experience and family preference

### Classification of Pelvic Osteotomies
1. Reconstructive Osteotomies
Aim to preserve and restore normal hip biomechanics.
### A. Reorientation Osteotomies (Incomplete)
- Change orientation by hinging
- Alter acetabular volume and shape
- Inherently stable
- Do not require fixation
Examples: - Pemberton - Dega - San Diego
### B. Redirectional Osteotomies (Complete)
- Change acetabular position
- Volume remains same
- Require fixation

Examples: - Salter - Triple osteotomy - Ganz Periacetabular Osteotomy (PAO)

Reorientation Osteotomies (Incomplete)
### Pemberton Osteotomy
- Indication: DDH in children
- Starts above AIIS
- Hinges at triradiate cartilage and pubic symphysis
- Provides anterior and lateral coverage
### Dega Osteotomy
- Indication: Neuromuscular hip dysplasia
- Cut from acetabular roof to triradiate cartilage
- Hinges only at triradiate cartilage
- Provides posterior coverage
### San Diego Osteotomy
- Most versatile
- Anterior to posterior cut
- Hinges medially
- Graft placement determines direction of coverage
- Useful for global deficiency

Redirectional Osteotomies (Complete)
### Salter Osteotomy
- Complete innominate osteotomy
- Hinges at pubic symphysis
- Limited correction (~20–25°)
- Best for children <8 years
### Triple Osteotomy
- Three cuts: ilium, pubis, ischium
- Greater freedom of acetabular movement
- Used in older children and adolescents
### Periacetabular Osteotomy (PAO – Ganz)
- Single incision
- Preserves posterior column
- Powerful correction
- Preferred for adolescent dysplasia

<!-- page 4 -->

Redirectional osteotomies always require fixation.
Salvage Pelvic Osteotomies
Used when congruent hip cannot be achieved.

Indications
- Perthes disease (late stages)
- Incongruent hip joint
Types
- Shelf acetabuloplasty
- Chiari osteotomy
### Shelf Acetabuloplasty
- Increases lateral coverage
- Converts non weight-bearing bone into weight-bearing surface
- Fibrocartilage formation instead of hyaline cartilage
### Biomechanical Effects
- Increases center-edge angle
- Decreases Sharp angle
- Prevents lateral subluxation
- Improves containment

### Shelf Acetabuloplasty – Key Steps
1. Intraoperative arthrogram to assess reducibility
2. Adductor ± psoas tenotomy if needed
3. Slot creation above acetabulum
4. Iliac crest graft harvested
5. Graft impacted into slot
6. Rectus femoris reattached
Variants
- Slotted acetabular augmentation (Staheli)
- Secured shelf with screw or plate
- Reverse shelf technique

### Role of Femoral Osteotomy
- Excessive femoral anteversion common in:
  - Neuromuscular hips

<!-- page 5 -->

- DDH sequelae
- Femoral derotation or varus osteotomy often required
- Pelvic osteotomy alone may fail if femoral deformity not addressed

### Timing of Surgery
- Residual dysplasia after reduction:
  - Observe till 4–5 years
  - Many hips remodel spontaneously
- Operate only if dysplasia persists beyond 5 years

### Exam Pearls
- Pelvic osteotomy choice depends on age and triradiate cartilage status
- Pemberton for DDH, Dega for neuromuscular hips
- San Diego is the most versatile reorientation osteotomy
- Salter is limited to children <8 years
- PAO is preferred in adolescent dysplasia
- Shelf osteotomy is a salvage procedure
- Always assess femoral version before pelvic surgery
- Not all residual dysplasia requires surgery
