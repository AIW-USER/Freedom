Pelvic Fractures – Dr. Anurag Tiwari
Overview
Pelvic fractures range from low energy stable injuries to life threatening high energy
trauma with massive hemorrhage. Management is guided by mechanism, posterior ring
involvement, hemodynamic status, and stability. Early identification of instability,
associated injuries, and appropriate mechanical stabilization are crucial.
Faculty Remark: “Posterior ring decides everything. If the posterior ring is injured,
assume instability until proven otherwise.”

## Anatomy Relevant to Pelvic Injuries
### Key Bones
- Innominate bone: ilium, ischium, pubis.
- Sacrum: keystone of pelvic ring.

### Major Ligaments
- Acetabulum, pubic symphysis, SI joints.

- Posterior SI ligaments: primary stabilizers.
- Anterior SI ligaments: resist external rotation.
- Sacrotuberous ligament: resists vertical shear.
- Sacrospinous ligament: resists external rotation.
- Symphyseal ligaments: stabilize anterior ring.
Clinical clue: L5 transverse process fracture indicates iliolumbar ligament injury.
## Classification Systems
Young–Burgess (Mechanism Based)
### APC – Anteroposterior Compression
- APC I: diastasis <2.5 cm, ligaments stretched.
- APC II: anterior SI and symphyseal disruption, posterior SI intact.
- APC III: complete SI disruption. Fully unstable.
### LC – Lateral Compression
- LC I: sacral impaction + rami fracture.
- LC II: ipsilateral sacral fracture + iliac wing fracture.
- LC III: “windswept pelvis”, contralateral APC injury.

### Vertical Shear (VS)
- Vertical displacement of hemipelvis.
- Entire posterior ring disrupted.
### Combined Mechanism
- Mix of forces, often high energy polytrauma.

Tile Classification (Stability Based)
### A – Stable
- A1 avulsion injuries.
- A2 pubic rami fractures.
- A3 transverse sacral fractures.
### B – Rotationally Unstable, Vertically Stable
- B1 external rotation.
- B2 lateral compression.
- B3 bilateral.
### C – Rotationally and Vertically Unstable
- C1 unilateral.
- C2 bilateral asymmetrical.
- C3 bilateral complete.

## Clinical Evaluation
### Symptoms and Signs
- Pelvic pain, inability to stand.
- Shock in high energy trauma.
- Leg length discrepancy.

### Associated Injuries
- External rotation deformity.

- Urethral injury (blood at meatus).
- Bladder rupture.
- Vaginal/rectal injuries.
- Abdominal solid organ injuries.
- Chest trauma.
### Neurovascular Check
- Femoral pulses.

<!-- page 3 -->

- Perineal sensation.
- Sciatic nerve exam.
Do not perform pelvic compression tests repeatedly due to risk of clot
dislodgment.

## Imaging
X ray – AP Pelvis
Key findings: - Symphyseal widening. - SI joint widening. - Sacral fractures. - Crescent

Inlet View
fracture.

- AP translation.
- Symphyseal widening.

### Outlet View
- Internal or external rotation deformity.

- Vertical displacement.

### CT Scan
- Sacral foramina visualization.

- Essential for mapping posterior ring injuries.
- Evaluates sacral fractures, SI joint diastasis, crescent fractures.
- Identifies hematomas and active bleeds.

## Hemodynamic Management
### Hemorrhage Sources
- 90 percent venous plexus.
- Arterial injury in 10 percent.

### Pelvic Binder
- Bone surface bleeding.

- Apply over greater trochanters.
- Reduces pelvic volume.
### When to Remove
- After stabilization in OT or once binder is replaced with fixation device.

<!-- page 4 -->

### Preperitoneal Packing
- For persistent hemodynamic instability.

Angioembolization
- Combined with external fixation.

- For arterial bleeding.
- Elderly more likely to need it.

## Mechanical Stabilization
External Fixation
Indications
- Unstable pelvic fractures in unstable patients.
- Temporary stabilization in polytrauma.
Techniques
- Iliac crest pins.

### C Clamp
- Supra acetabular pins: stronger bone, more stable.

- Used for posterior ring disruption.
- Rapid closure of SI diastasis.

## Definitive Management
Non operative
- LC I injuries.
- Stable rami fractures.

### Operative Indications
- APC I with minimal diastasis.

- SI joint diastasis.
- Sacral fractures with displacement.
- Symphysis diastasis >2.5 cm.
- Crescent fracture.
- Unstable Tile B or C fractures.

## Surgical Approaches
### Anterior Pelvic Ring
- Pfannenstiel approach.
- Modified Stoppa approach.

### Posterior Pelvic Ring
- Allows plating of symphysis and superior ramus.

- Posterior SI joint plating.
- Trans iliosacral screws in S1/S2.
- Lumbopelvic fixation for comminuted sacral fractures.

## Complications
- Massive hemorrhage.
- Nerve injury (sciatic, L5 roots).
- Urethral injury.
- Infection.
- Malunion.
- Chronic pelvic pain.

### Exam Pearls
- Posterior ring = key determinant of instability.
- Binder must be placed on trochanters.
- LC I usually stable; APC II/III unstable.
- Tile C = rotational + vertical instability.
- Crescent fracture = iliac wing fracture with SI joint involvement.
- Preperitoneal packing works only after external fixation.
- Iliosacral screws are placed in S1 and S2.

Final Faculty Insight: “Stabilize physiology first, then stabilize the pelvis. Posterior
ring fixation is the foundation of definitive treatment.”
