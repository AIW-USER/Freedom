Pitfalls in Bone Biopsy – Dr. Rajat Gupta (Oct 2025)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Most textbook biopsy principles (oval window, small cortical hole etc.) are written for OPEN biopsy. Today almost all bone tumour biopsies are NEEDLE biopsies – many of those rules do not apply, and other practical rules matter more.
- A "negative" or non-diagnostic biopsy is usually not the pathologist's fault – it is about how and where the biopsy was taken.
- The lecture covers the 10 commonest biopsy mistakes, with real cases, and how to biopsy a cystic lesion.
- Golden rule: never do a definitive surgery (curettage, excision, nailing, arthroscopic excision) until you have a histological diagnosis. Biopsy is done to confirm your differential diagnosis, not to find one.

Opening case – distal radius lesion, 25-year-old man
- Lytic lesion of the distal radius reaching the subchondral bone (= epiphyseal–metaphyseal, not just metaphyseal – be precise when you read the X-ray).
- MRI: fluid–fluid levels throughout, but with SOLID areas. Solid areas inside a so-called ABC mean a secondary ABC – here a giant cell tumour with secondary ABC.
- Needle biopsy taken from the cystic part reported only "cyst wall, haemorrhage and fibrin, no malignancy" – a non-diagnostic sample. Target the solid part.

1. Sending the sample to multiple labs → do not divide the specimen
- Bone and soft-tissue tumours are heterogeneous – one area may be low grade, another high grade. Dividing the sample sends different parts of the tumour to different labs.
- Case: 55-year-old man, swelling in the popliteal fossa, treated as a Baker's cyst/lipoma with arthroscopic decompression and excision. Three labs gave three answers: spindle cell lipoma (benign), low-grade myxofibrosarcoma, and myxoid liposarcoma.
- If the report does not fit the clinical picture, send the SAME slides and blocks to a second lab for review.

2. Sending the sample to "any" pathologist
- Just as an arthroscopy surgeon may not be good at paediatric orthopaedics, a pathologist good at breast or thyroid may not be good at bone tumours.
- Case: 10-year-old boy, sclerotic lesion of the proximal tibia on X-ray and MRI, no periosteal reaction. Needle biopsy reported "fibrous dysplasia".
- But fibrous dysplasia is ground-glass, not ivory-sclerotic. A sclerotic lesion in a young patient is osteosarcoma until proven otherwise, even without periosteal reaction (it just has not lifted the periosteum yet).
- "No malignant osteoid seen" does not exclude osteosarcoma – it may be a sampling error. Always correlate with radiology.
- After 2 months of waiting, periosteal reaction reached the growth plate; review by a musculoskeletal pathologist = osteosarcoma. The joint could no longer be saved – an osteoarticular resection was needed in a 9-year-old.

3. Not doing the biopsy as a separate procedure
- Do not confuse curettage with biopsy. A "curettage + biopsy in one go" or "definitive surgery + send tissue for HPE" is an UNPLANNED surgery.
- Case: 80-year-old woman with knee pain for a year (taken as arthritis), pathological fracture nailed and reamings sent. Reamings are destroyed by the reaming – in a study of 90 cases a diagnosis was made in only 60% (Surgeon 2007). Never rely on reamings for diagnosis. Re- biopsy later: undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma; she ended up with a hindquarter amputation.
- Case: 21-year-old man, well-defined epiphyseal–metaphyseal distal femur lesion assumed to be GCT; curettage + biopsy done together; report "GCT with aneurysmal change". Four months later – large soft-tissue mass. Slide/block review: giant-cell-rich osteosarcoma.

<!-- page 4 -->

- Even in pathological fractures: splint the limb and wait 5–7 days for the biopsy report before definitive fixation.

4. Arthroscopic biopsy – never
- Case: lesion in the femoral notch reported as ACL/PCL degeneration, arthroscopic biopsy done – 6 months later the whole joint was contaminated.
- Intra-synovial lesions biopsied arthroscopically are often reported as (nodular) PVNS when they are actually synovial sarcoma.
- For notch lesions: CT-guided or, better, ultrasound-guided needle biopsy (pure soft-tissue lesions may not be seen well on CT).

5. Piecemeal excision is NOT excision biopsy
- Case: young man, soft-tissue swelling of the distal thigh, removed in bits without MRI – final pathology high-grade pleomorphic sarcoma. The tumour-bed excision then needed a large resection, plastic surgery and multiple procedures. "What was an easy surgery turned out to be a disaster."
- Do not assume a soft-tissue lump is a lipoma – get an MRI.
Excision biopsy is acceptable only when ALL of these are true:
- Superficial tumour (above the fascia) – established on MRI
- Size < 3 cm
- MRI done before surgery
- En bloc removal in one piece with good margins (so no revision is needed if it turns out to be a sarcoma)

6 & 7. Open biopsy technique – flaps, haematoma and hatch marks
- Do not raise flaps as in routine orthopaedic surgery – this causes haematoma, and haematoma spreads tumour (case: proximal femur sarcoma after open biopsy; local recurrence at the open-biopsy site despite margin-negative resection).
- Avoid large hatch marks – close with a 3-0 or 4-0 needle (smaller bites). Everything around the scar has to be excised at definitive surgery.
- Case: open biopsy in Ewing sarcoma of the tibia – a routine intercalary resection then needed an additional hemisoleus flap and skin grafting. Around the knee and leg there is little spare skin.

8. Multiple entry points
- Two needle sites or two open biopsy sites = twice the skin and muscle to remove at definitive surgery.
- Single point of entry: because tumours are heterogeneous, angle the needle in different directions through the SAME entry point to sample several areas.

9. Biopsy scar not in line with the surgical incision
- Common error around the knee: a midline (TKR-type) incision. Anteriorly you contaminate the rectus femoris (the workhorse of the knee); posteriorly the neurovascular bundle.
- Distal femur: go medial or lateral – not anterior, not posterior.
- Proximal tibia: medial or lateral to the patellar tendon – not anterior, not posterior.
- CT-guided biopsy from behind the proximal tibia contaminated the gastrocnemius–soleus and the vascular bundle. An interventional radiologist does not know your incision – mark it for them. If you do not know the incision, refer the patient to an orthopaedic oncologist.
- Reference for biopsy approaches to every bone: Liu PT et al. "Anatomically based guidelines for core needle biopsy of bone tumors" – RadioGraphics 2007;27:189–206 (free online).

10. FNAC for a bone tumour
- FNAC is not reliable for bone tumours – there is no role of FNAC in diagnosing a bone tumour. Always core needle biopsy.

### Where Should the Biopsy Be Done?
- Mankin's "Hazards of the biopsy, revisited" (JBJS Am 1996; Musculoskeletal Tumor Society, 597 patients): diagnostic error 17.8%; errors, complications and change in outcome were 2 to 12 times higher when the biopsy was done in the referring institution rather than a treatment centre; 18 patients had an unnecessary amputation because of the biopsy.
- The same results as the 1982 study – nothing had changed in 15 years.
- Simplest rule: when in doubt, call an orthopaedic oncologist or refer the patient BEFORE the biopsy.

### How to Biopsy

| Lesion | Technique |
|---|---|
| Solid | Needle in, core of tumour out – straightforward |
| Cystic (fluid–fluid levels) | Needle aspirate gives only blood and clot. Heparinise the needle; under C-arm guidance aspirate into a heparinised syringe and empty it into heparinised saline; then SCRAPE the cyst walls under C-arm – this gives the tumour tissue; pour the bowl through a mop (sieve) to collect the tissue |
| Bone + soft tissue component | Sample the soft-tissue part – as representative, no decalcification needed |
| Core biopsy "gun" | The breast/thyroid biopsy gun can be used for bone lesions with a soft component (e.g. GCT distal radius); press afterwards to prevent haematoma |

<!-- page 7 -->

- Cystic lesions: do the biopsy under spinal or general anaesthesia – not sedation + local – scraping the walls is painful and the patient will not allow an adequate sample.
- Tourniquet can be used (for biopsy or definitive surgery) – elevate the limb for 1–2 minutes and inflate; do NOT exsanguinate with an Esmarch.
- If scraping with one hand, aspirate with the other when possible; scrape first, then apply negative pressure and withdraw.

### Reading the Pathology Report (Q&A)
- Always have a differential diagnosis before the biopsy – the biopsy confirms it.
- A very long, descriptive report ("giant-cell-rich lesion, possibility of GCT cannot be ruled out…") usually means the pathologist is not sure. Call the pathologist: if they are confident, proceed; if not, ask where to get a review.
- Round cells → think lymphoma or round cell sarcoma (by age); spindle cells → other sarcomas such as osteosarcoma.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Solid areas within fluid–fluid levels = secondary ABC (e.g. GCT with secondary ABC), not primary ABC.
- Sclerotic lesion in a child = osteosarcoma until proven otherwise; fibrous dysplasia is ground- glass.
- Reamings are not reliable for histology (~60% diagnostic yield).
- Excision biopsy: superficial, < 3 cm, after MRI, en bloc.
- Biopsy tract around the knee: medial or lateral, never anterior (rectus) or posterior (neurovascular bundle).
- No FNAC for bone tumours. No arthroscopic biopsy. No multiple labs.
- Mankin 1996: complications 2–12 times higher when biopsy is done outside a treatment centre.

### Take-Home Summary
- Biopsy is a planned, separate procedure – never combined with curettage, excision or fixation.
- One needle entry point, in line with the future incision, sampling several directions; the solid/soft-tissue part of the tumour.
- Whole specimen to one musculoskeletal pathologist; review slides and blocks if the report does not fit.
- No FNAC, no arthroscopic biopsy, no piecemeal "excision biopsy", no flaps or big stitches.
- If in doubt – refer before the biopsy, not after.
