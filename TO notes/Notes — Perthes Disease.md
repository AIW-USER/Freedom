Perthes Disease – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Perthes disease, also known as Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease (LCPD), is an idiopathic,
self-limiting avascular necrosis of the capital femoral epiphysis in children. It has a
prolonged natural history, often spanning 3–4 years, and the final outcome depends on
age, extent of involvement, containment, and remodeling potential.
A clear understanding of staging, prognostic classifications, and treatment
principles is essential for exams and clinical decision-making.

Definition
Perthes disease is an idiopathic, self-limiting avascular necrosis of the capital femoral
epiphysis.

Important Exclusions
If an underlying cause is identified, it is not Perthes disease: - Sickle cell disease -
Coagulopathies - Hypothyroidism - Skeletal dysplasias

Etiology and Theories
Exact cause is unknown. Proposed theories include: - Genetic predisposition -
Second-hand smoking - ADHD and hyperactivity - Mechanical stress - Vascular
theories: - Temporary loss of arterial supply during transition of blood flow - Venous
congestion

Epidemiology
- Male predominance (≈5:1)
- Common age group: 5–10 years (India)
- Bilateral involvement: 10–15 percent
- Southwestern belt of India shows higher incidence
Extremes of age and symmetrical bilateral disease mandate evaluation for
secondary causes.

### Clinical Features
- Painless limp (most common)
- Hip or referred knee pain
- Restricted hip abduction and internal rotation
- Trendelenburg gait
- Apparent limb shortening in advanced stages
Any child aged 4–10 years with limp and restricted abduction should be suspected of
Perthes disease.

Imaging
X-ray
- Primary diagnostic modality
- AP pelvis and frog-leg lateral views
### MRI
- Not routinely required
- Contrast-enhanced MRI is superior if diagnosis is unclear
- Useful to differentiate from TB hip and other conditions

Classifications Overview
Perthes disease has multiple classifications, divided into three functional groups:
1. Staging (Disease progression)
2. Prognostic classifications (Active stage)
3. Outcome classifications (Healed stage)

Staging Classifications
Used to describe natural history of disease progression.

### Waldenström / Elizabethtown (Most Common)

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| Stage I | Avascular necrosis, increased sclerosis |
| Stage II | Fragmentation |
| Stage III | Revascularization and regeneration |

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| Stage IV | Complete healing |

### Elizabethtown Sub-stages (High Yield)
  - 1a: Sclerosis without loss of height
  - 1b: Sclerosis with loss of height
  - 2a: Early fragmentation
  - 2b: Advanced fragmentation, no new lateral bone
  - 3a: Early new bone, porotic, <1/3 width
  - 3b: Mature new bone, >1/3 width
  - 4: Healed, remodeled head

Prognostic Classifications (Active Stage)
### Catterall Classification (Head Involvement)

| Group | Head Involvement |
|---|---|
| I | Anterior epiphysis only (25%) |
| II | Anterior + central sequestrum (50%) |
| III | Only small portion uninvolved (75%) |
| IV | Total head involvement (100%) |

Head-at-Risk Signs (Catterall)
Mnemonic: C G M L H - Calcification lateral to epiphysis - Gage sign (V-shaped
radiolucency) - Metaphyseal cyst - Lateral subluxation - Horizontal epiphysis

Herring Lateral Pillar Classification (Most Important Prognostic Tool)
Based on height of lateral pillar during fragmentation stage:

| Type | Description | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| A | Height maintained | Good |
| B | <50% height loss | Intermediate |
| B/C | Borderline | Variable |
| C | >50% height loss | Poor |

Outcome Classifications (Healed Stage)
### Stulberg Classification

| Class | Description | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| I | Spherical, congruent | Excellent |
| II | Spherical, minor deformity | Good |
| III | Aspherical, congruent | Fair |
| IV | Aspherical, incongruent | Poor |
| V | Flat head, incongruent | Very poor |

### Aims of Treatment
  - Prevent femoral head deformity
  - Maintain congruency
  - Prevent lateral extrusion
  - Delay or avoid early osteoarthritis

### Principles of Treatment (Salter’s Principles)
  - Maintain hip range of motion
  - Contain femoral head within acetabulum
  - Protect weight-bearing portion of epiphysis

When to Contain the Hip
### Indications for Containment
  - Stage 1a, 1b, 2a
  - Stage 2b with good ROM
  - Age >6 years
  - Extrusion >20 percent
  - Involvement of ≥50 percent of epiphysis
### Do Not Contain
  - Stage 3a, 3b, 4
  - Severe stiffness or hinge abduction
  - Age <6 years with minimal involvement and no extrusion

Extrusion Assessment
Reimer’s Migration Index
MI = (Lateral uncovered head / Total head width) × 100
- MI >20% = Indication for containment

### Range of Motion Considerations
- Loss of abduction and internal rotation is typical
- VDRO only if adequate ROM present
If Hip is Stiff
- Traction for 2–3 weeks
- Adductor tenotomy
- Petrie cast or abduction brace

Surgical Options
### Varus Derotation Osteotomy (VDRO)
- Changes weight-bearing area
- Corrects extrusion
- Varus not >20°
- Final neck-shaft angle ≥105°
- GT epiphysiodesis mandatory

### Hinge Abduction
- Absolute contraindication to VDRO
Management
- Valgus osteotomy
- Shelf acetabuloplasty

Shelf Acetabuloplasty
Indications
- Hinge abduction
- Post-collapse incongruent hips
- Coxa magna

<!-- page 6 -->

Mechanism
- Increases acetabular coverage
- Decreases Sharp angle
- Increases center-edge angle
- Fibrocartilage formation

Management of Sequelae
### Coxa Breva
- Distal GT transfer
- Relative or absolute neck lengthening
### Coxa Magna
- Acetabuloplasty
- Femoral head reduction
- Osteochondroplasty

Faculty Quote
“Perthes disease is not treated to heal the bone. It is treated to prevent deformity and
preserve a lifetime hip.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- Perthes is idiopathic and self-limiting
- X-ray is diagnostic, MRI is adjunct
- Herring classification is most reliable prognostic tool
- Extrusion >20% requires containment
- VDRO contraindicated in hinge abduction
- Best outcomes are Stulberg I and II
- Early diagnosis dramatically improves prognosis
