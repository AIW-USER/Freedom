Kienbock’s Disease – Dr. Amit Mittal
Overview
Kienbock’s disease is avascular necrosis of the lunate leading to progressive collapse,
altered carpal mechanics, and eventual arthritic changes. Early diagnosis is essential
because treatment strategy depends entirely on stage, ulnar variance, lunate
morphology, and vascular supply patterns.
Faculty Remark: “Always remember, uniform MRI signal change suggests
Kienbock, focal signal change suggests something else.”

Lunate Anatomy Essentials
Blood Supply Patterns (Page 3)
Three patterns determine vascular vulnerability: - Y pattern: good supply. - X pattern:
good supply. - I pattern: single vessel supply, highest risk for AVN.
I-pattern lunate is most prone to Kienbock’s disease.

### Lunate Shape Types (Pages 4–5)
- Type I: Apex/crest proximal, coexists with ulnar-negative variance, highest risk.
- Type II: Rectangular.
- Type III: Square.
Type I lunate + I-pattern supply = maximum biomechanical vulnerability.

### Etiology – Key Concepts
- Multifactorial origin.
- No correlation between acute lunate fracture and later Kienbock.
- MRI of acute lunate fracture shows transient focal edema, while Kienbock shows diffuse, progressive involvement.
- Seen more commonly in ulnar-negative variance, though not a statistically proven cause.
- Lunate in negative variance wrists receives unequal load distribution causing radial-side compression.
- Interosseous pressure increases in wrist extension, hence extension must be avoided.
- Lower radial inclination increases risk.

Radiology & Diagnostic Evaluation
### X-Ray Findings (Page 12)
- Earliest sign: Increased lunate density (sclerosis).
- Others: fracture lines, fragmentation, collapse, arthritis.
### MRI (Page 13)
- Most sensitive modality.
- T1 uniform decrease = Kienbock.
- T1 focal decrease = consider:
  - Ulnar impaction.
  - Intraosseous ganglion.
  - Enchondroma.
### Vascular Staging: Lichtman Classification (Pages 14, 42)

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Normal X-ray, diffuse T1 ↓, bone scan + |
| II | Sclerosis, fracture lines, no collapse |
| IIIA | Lunate collapse, carpal height maintained |
| IIIB | Collapse + capitate migration, scaphoid hyperflexion, radioscaphoid angle >60° |
| IV | Radiocarpal and midcarpal arthritis |

### Carpal Height Ratio (Page 15)
- CH/MH (carpal height divided by 3rd metacarpal height).
- Decrease indicates capitate migration → Stage IIIB.

### Biomechanics & Classification Pearls
- Ulnar-negative variance increases load on lunate.
- Wrist extension raises interosseous pressure by ~40 mmHg.
- Radial inclination reduction increases risk.
- Radial shaft should be used to measure ulnar variance (Page 10).
- First classification was by Stahl, modified by Lichtman.

Management Principles
### General Rules
- No role for conservative care after diagnosis.
- Treatment depends on stage + ulnar variance.
- Goals: unload lunate, revascularize lunate, or perform salvage.

Stage-Based Management
### Stage I, II, IIIA with Ulnar-Negative Variance
1. Joint Leveling Procedures
### Radial Shortening Osteotomy (preferred) (Pages 19–21)
- Extraarticular, preserves wrist ROM.
- Shorten radius by 2–3 mm only.
  - First 2 mm reduces 90 percent load.
  - 3 mm provides no additional benefit.
- Oblique cut → 2 mm wafer.
- Transverse cut → 3 mm wafer.
- Diaphyseal osteotomy compresses better than metaphyseal.
### Ulnar Lengthening Osteotomy (Page 23)
- Done at distal one third ulna.
- Release interosseous membrane 1 mm.
- Good results but complications: ulnocarpal impingement, nonunion.
2. Vascularised Bone Grafting (Pages 25–28)
Sources: - 2nd metacarpal base graft. - 3rd metacarpal base graft. - Dorsal distal
radius graft.
Vascular pedicles: - ICSRA 1–2: short pedicle. - ICSRA 2–3: good arc of motion. -
ICSRA 4–5: best choice — large vessel caliber and excellent arc.

### Stage I, II, IIIA with Ulnar-Positive Variance
- Radial shortening contraindicated.
- Ulnar shortening contraindicated.
- Best option:
  - Capitate shortening + capitohamate fusion (Page 31).
  - Do not release 4th extensor compartment.
  - Excise tip of hamate.

Radial Closing Wedge Osteotomy (Pages 32–33)
Used when radial inclination is abnormal. - Redistributes force from radiolunate to
radioscaphoid joint. - Osteotomy must be proximal to DRUJ.

<!-- page 4 -->

Salvage Procedures (Stage IIIB with collapse + migration)
(Page 35–38) - Scaphocapitate fusion (better than STT fusion). - Proximal row
carpectomy. - Silastic or pyrocarbon lunate arthroplasty. - Pyrocarbon preferred. -
Wrist fusion for advanced collapse.
Biomechanics: - SC fusion reduces radiolunate force by 12 percent. - Capitate
shortening + capitohamate fusion reduces force by 60 percent.

### Stage IV (Radiocarpal + Midcarpal Arthritis)
- Interposition arthroplasty.
- Wrist fusion.

### Key Faculty Insights
- “Focal MRI changes are not Kienbock — always think of impaction or ganglion.”
- “Most students forget that radial shortening must be limited to 2–3 mm.”
- “Stage 3B is all about migration. Look at capitate height to differentiate.”

### Exam Pearls
- I-pattern blood supply + Type I lunate → highest risk.
- Earliest X-ray sign: sclerosis of lunate.
- Most sensitive test: MRI T1 uniform low signal.
- Stage I: intermittent pain.
- Stage IIIB:
  - Capitate migration.
  - Scaphoid hyperflexion.
  - Radioscaphoid angle >60°.
- Best vascular graft: 4–5 ICSRA.
- Radial shortening goal: only 2–3 mm.
- Contraindication for joint leveling: ulnar-positive variance.
- Preferred salvage: scaphocapitate fusion.
