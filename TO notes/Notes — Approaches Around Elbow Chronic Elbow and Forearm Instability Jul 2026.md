Instability & Forearm Instability – Dr. Aviral Gupta

### Overview / Key Concepts
- A largely descriptive lecture: the named approaches (who described them and which plane they use), chronic lateral and medial instability (presentation, tests, reconstruction) and longitudinal forearm instability (Essex-Lopresti).
- Many approaches are now mostly historical, but the names and their basic idea are asked in MCQs and in DNB theory.
- Lateral side: every approach lies between two boundaries, the radial nerve anteriorly and the LUCL posteriorly.
- Ligament reconstruction: recreate the native origin and insertion; the humeral attachment must sit at the isometric point.
- Forearm = one functional joint (PRUJ + interosseous membrane + DRUJ/TFCC). The radial head is the primary stabiliser against proximal migration of the radius.

Posterior approaches
Campbell triceps split: 15 cm midline posterior incision. The triceps tendon is split in the
midline down to bone and the periosteum of the distal humerus incised. The lateral half of the
triceps is reflected laterally together with the anconeus; the medial half is reflected from its
olecranon insertion together with the forearm flexor fascia. Exposes the posterior distal humerus.
Campbell aponeurosis tongue: instead of splitting, a tongue-shaped flap of triceps aponeurosis
is reflected proximally and the medial and lateral muscle masses are dissected off. Originally
done in elbows stiff in extension: the elbow is flexed and the aponeurosis repaired in a V-Y
fashion, which lengthens the triceps mechanism and releases the extension contracture.
Wadsworth extensile posterolateral: follows Campbell (triceps aponeurosis reflected) and also
elevates the anconeus from its origin on the lateral epicondyle, giving a much greater view of the
radial head and the posterior distal humerus. Drawback of these triceps approaches: poor view of
the intra-articular distal humerus.
Olecranon osteotomy (MacAusland and Muller): much greater exposure of the articular
surface than the triceps splitting or reflecting approaches. The osteotomy is made about 2 cm
from the olecranon tip. Fixed with TBW, K-wire TBW (most common), TBW over a cancellous
screw, or a plate.

Medial approaches
- Needed less often, since most work (even on the medial side) can be done from laterally. Indications: coronoid fixation when an intact radial head blocks the lateral view, and medial osteophyte excision.
- Molesworth / Campbell: incision centred on the medial epicondyle, 5 cm proximal and distal; isolate and retract the ulnar nerve; medial epicondylectomy with a small osteotome; reflect the common flexor origin with the fragment. Very good view of the medial joint and articular surface, but needs an epicondylectomy.
- Hotchkiss "over the top": skin incision between the posterior midline and the medial midline incisions; key landmarks are the medial epicondyle and medial supracondylar ridge. Identify and protect the MABCN (painful neuroma). The flexor-pronator mass between PT (anterior) and FCU (posterior) is split parallel to its fibres down to bone, staying extracapsular. Brachialis is reflected from medial to lateral, then the capsule is incised and reflected. The coronoid lies just beneath. Can be extended posteromedially by elevating the triceps.

Chronic Elbow Instability
Chronic lateral instability (PLRI)
- Causes: neglected or improperly treated trauma (fracture fixed but the LUCL not addressed), steroid injections for lateral epicondylitis (ligament attenuation), chronic varus load.
- Case shown: rotational machine injury with segmental ulna fractures fixed by plating; LUCL not addressed; presented at 4 months with lateral pain and swelling, a dimple between two bony prominences, a drop sign on the lateral X-ray (about 4 mm ulnohumeral gap) and radiocapitellar gapping on the AP view.

Symptoms
- Apprehension or weakness when reaching out to an object (varus load on the elbow).
- Painful clicking or snapping.
- Apprehension on axial loading in supination and extension, as in rising from a chair using the armrests: the supinated radius and proximal ulna rotate laterally off the humerus (posterolateral rotatory instability).
- Unremitting lateral elbow pain with swelling.

Chronic medial instability
- Secondary to trauma, mostly in overhead-throwing athletes (javelin, gymnasts): repeated valgus load attenuates the MCL.
- Difficulty with throwing (shoulder abduction + external rotation puts valgus stress on the medial elbow).
- May have ulnar neuritis: weak grip and ulnar-sided numbness from traction on the nerve or medial joint overload.
- Milking manoeuvre: shoulder externally rotated, elbow flexed beyond 90°, valgus stress (pull on the thumb); pain over the medial elbow.
- Moving valgus stress test: same valgus load kept constant while the elbow is taken from flexion to extension; pain at about 70–120° is typical of symptomatic MCL insufficiency.
- Case shown: fall on the elbow, presented at 40 days with valgus alignment, increased valgus opening and a painful milking manoeuvre (normal side pain free); a tiny avulsed fragment from the medial epicondyle; complete avulsion confirmed at surgery and reattached.

LUCL reconstruction (chronic lateral instability)
- Position and approach: supine, forearm across the chest or flat on the table; Kocher or EDC split approach.
- Graft: palmaris longus (same or opposite side; convenient but may be too short), half FCR, or gracilis (best diameter and length, but involves the lower limb).
- Ulnar tunnels: one at the level of the proximal margin of the radial head, several millimetres posterior to the joint; the second about 2 cm distal, over the supinator crest (LUCL insertion).
- Humeral tunnel: just anterior and distal to the isometric point (anteroinferior lateral epicondyle) so that the final attachment lies at the isometric point; exits on the posterolateral cortex and the graft is tied over a cortical button.
- Key points: graft stays extracapsular (no contact with the joint surface); tension the graft with the elbow in 45–60° flexion and full pronation to reduce any residual posterolateral subluxation.
- Sling / box loop technique: used when both collateral ligaments need reconstruction at the same time.

Forearm Instability
Anatomy and biomechanics
- The forearm works as one joint for pronation and supination: the ulna is the fixed, load- bearing bone; the radius rotates around it.
- Stabilisers: PRUJ proximally, interosseous membrane (IOM) in between, DRUJ and TFCC distally. PRUJ and DRUJ must stay congruent and the forearm length must be maintained for rotation.
- Radial head = primary stabiliser against proximal migration of the radius; secondary = IOM (central band) and the TFCC/DRUJ structures.
- About 75–80% of the axial load at the wrist passes through the radiocarpal joint; the IOM transfers it to the ulna, and at the elbow most load crosses the ulnohumeral joint.
- IOM has 5 bands; the central band is the most important and runs at about 20–25° (this angle is used when it is reconstructed).

Essex-Lopresti injury
- Definition: longitudinal forearm instability from an axial force: radial head fracture + IOM tear + TFCC/DRUJ disruption.

<!-- page 9 -->

- Recognise early: tenderness over the radial head and along the forearm, DRUJ tenderness and instability; X-ray the elbow AND the wrist; ultrasound can show IOM tears.
- If missed: painful loss of pronation and supination. Rare, but a favourite exam question.

Management (lateral approach)
- Fix the radial head; if not fixable, excise it and do the pull test.
- Pull test: pull the radial shaft proximally with a clamp and watch the ulnar variance at the wrist under IITV. Increase of 3 mm or more: radial head replacement. Less than 3 mm: excision alone is acceptable.
- Then check the DRUJ after repair or replacement. If unstable: above-elbow slab in supination, DRUJ pinning, or TFCC repair.
- Central band reconstruction (PL or gracilis) is rarely done; if asked, the graft is placed at about 25°.

Neglected instability
- Painful loss of pronation and supination. Salvage = single bone forearm: proximal ulna joined to the distal radius (fixed with a DCP).
- Relieves pain (load passes straight from the radiocarpal joint through the united bone to the ulnohumeral joint) but rotation is not regained.
- Position: slightly more pronation than mid-prone (most functional).

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Kaplan = ECRB/EDC; Kocher = anconeus/ECU; Henry (anterior) = mobile wad/biceps tendon.
- Lateral approaches lie between the radial nerve (anterior) and the LUCL (posterior); pronate the forearm to protect the PIN.
- Kocher cannot be extended distally; the annular ligament is the distal limit of deep dissection.
- Bryan-Morrey is the posterior approach that keeps the extensor mechanism in continuity.

<!-- page 10 -->

- Olecranon osteotomy about 2 cm from the tip; chevron gives rotational stability and more healing surface.
- Campbell tongue approach allows V-Y lengthening of the triceps for an extension contracture.
- PLRI: pivot shift test, dimple and clunk at about 40° flexion; drawer test positive at 80–90° = posterolateral capsule laxity too.
- LUCL graft tensioned at 45–60° flexion in full pronation; humeral tunnel at the isometric point; graft extracapsular.
- MCL: sublime tubercle tunnels; Jobe 3-ply figure-of-8 vs docking 2-ply.
- Moving valgus stress test: pain at 70–120°.
- Essex-Lopresti pull test: 3 mm or more = replace the radial head.
- Single bone forearm: slightly more pronated than mid-prone.

### Take-Home Summary
- Know each approach by author and interval; the cross-section map (slide 21) summarises them.
- Chronic lateral instability presents with apprehension on varus load or chair push-up; confirm with PLRI tests, stress views and MRI; reconstruct the LUCL at the isometric point.
- Chronic medial instability is typical of throwers; milking manoeuvre and moving valgus stress test; MCL reconstruction via sublime tubercle tunnels.
- In any radial head fracture with forearm or wrist pain, think Essex-Lopresti: keep the radial head (fix or replace) and address the DRUJ.
