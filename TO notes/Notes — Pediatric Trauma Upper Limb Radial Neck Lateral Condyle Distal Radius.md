Pediatric Trauma Upper Limb – Radial Neck, Lateral
Condyle, Distal Radius – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Upper limb fractures in children show distinct remodeling potential, injury patterns,
and complication profiles compared to adults. This lecture focuses on three
high-yield, exam-favorite injuries: - Radial neck fractures - Lateral condyle humerus
fractures - Distal radius fractures
Correct decision-making depends on age, displacement, stability, and physeal
involvement, not just radiographic appearance.

Radial Neck Fractures in Children
Epidemiology
- Rare injury
- ~5% of pediatric elbow fractures
- Third most common elbow fracture after SCH and lateral condyle

### Pathoanatomy and Biomechanics
- Radial head in children is entirely cartilaginous
- Blood supply primarily from metaphysis
- Malreduction causes:
  - Eccentric radial head
  - Cam-type impingement at radiocapitellar joint
  - Incongruent proximal radioulnar joint

### Indications for Reduction
- Angulation >30°
- Significant translation
- Increasing age (reduced remodeling)
Angulation >30° shifts center of rotation eccentrically.
### Acceptable Angulation
- <30° in children <10 years may be observed

- 30° requires reduction
- If residual angulation <45° after reduction, may accept to avoid open surgery

Closed Reduction Techniques
Commonly described methods: - Patterson method - Jeffery method - Neher–Torch
method - Kaufman method - Monson method - Esmarch bandage method - Orthokids
technique (combined method)
### Patterson Method
- Elbow extension
- Forearm supination
- Varus force
- Thumb pressure on radial head

### Minimally Invasive Techniques
- Percutaneous K-wire leverage (intrafocal)
- Metaizeau technique (elastic nail or bent wire)
### Technical Tips to Protect PIN
- PIN crosses lateral radius ~3 finger breadths distal to neck
- Safe zone: posterolateral surface
- Pronation increases safety
- Avoid blind reinsertion and multiple attempts

### Open Reduction
- Indicated only when all closed methods fail
- Approach: Anconeus–ECU interval
- Preserve periosteum and vascularity
Results inferior to closed reduction
### Complications of Radial Neck Fractures
- Loss of motion (pronation > supination)
- Radial head overgrowth (20–40%)
- Osteonecrosis (10–20%), common after open reduction
- PIN injury
- Physeal arrest → cubitus valgus
- Radioulnar synostosis (most serious)

Lateral Condyle Humerus Fractures
Epidemiology
- 10–20% of pediatric elbow fractures
- Second most common after supracondylar fracture

### Mechanism of Injury
- Pull-off theory: Varus stress with wrist extension
- Push-off theory: Radial head impact on lateral condyle
Most are Salter–Harris Type IV injuries.

Classifications
### Milch Classification
- Type I: Through capitulum
- Type II: Medial to trochlea
Not predictive of treatment or outcome
### Jakob Classification

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| I | <2 mm displacement, intact hinge |
| II | >2 mm, no rotation |
| III | Displaced with rotation |

### Song Classification (Most Useful)
- Incorporates internal oblique view
- Stages 1–5 based on stability and displacement
Key Concept: - Stage 3 fractures are complete and unstable, even if minimally
displaced

Imaging
- AP, lateral, and internal oblique view mandatory
- MRI, ultrasound, or arthrogram for equivocal cases

<!-- page 4 -->

Arthrogram
- Lateral soft spot or posterior olecranon fossa
- Assesses cartilage hinge
- Guides decision for CRPP vs ORIF

### Treatment Principles
- <2 mm displacement: Cast with close weekly follow-up
- 2 mm displacement: CRPP or ORIF
Even minimally displaced fractures can displace later
### Fixation and Pin Removal
- CRPP preferred when feasible
- Large metaphyseal fragment in older child: Screw fixation
- Pin removal after radiological union, often 4–6 weeks

Complications
- Lateral spur (common, benign)
- Nonunion
- Malunion
- Cubitus valgus ± tardy ulnar nerve palsy

Distal Radius Fractures in Children
### Key Concept: Remodeling Depends on Physis, Not Age
- Distal radial physis is:
  - Fastest growing in forearm
  - Second fastest in upper limb
  - Contributes ~75% of forearm growth

### Common Injury Patterns
- Torus fractures (metaphyseal)
- Greenstick fractures (shaft)
- Salter–Harris injuries (Type I and II most common)

<!-- page 5 -->

### Myths and Facts
- Overriding distal radius fractures in children <10 years can remodel fully
- Repeat manipulation after 7 days increases physeal injury risk

### Safety of K-wire Across Physis
- Smooth wires are safe if:
  - Single attempt
  - Wire <2.5 mm
  - Low torque
  - Avoid peripheral physis
Multiple passes increase risk of growth arrest
### Acceptable Alignment Criteria

| Age | Angulation | Displacement |
|---|---|---|
| <8 years | ≤45° | ≤60% |
| 8–12 years | ≤30° | ≤50% |
| Adolescents | ≤15° | ≤40% |

### Indications for Fixation
- Floating elbow
- Median nerve compression symptoms
- Compartment syndrome
- Unacceptable alignment

### Physeal Arrest and Follow-up
- All distal radius fractures need 6–12 months follow-up
- Management options:
  - Ulnar epiphysiodesis
  - Ulnar shortening osteotomy
  - Radial corrective osteotomy

Faculty Quote
“In children, it is not the X-ray that decides treatment. It is the physis.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- Radial neck angulation >30° requires reduction
- Avoid open reduction of radial neck whenever possible
- Internal oblique view is essential for lateral condyle fractures
- Lateral condyle fractures are Salter–Harris Type IV
- Lateral spur after fixation is benign
- Distal radius remodeling depends on proximity to physis
- Do not re-reduce distal radius physeal fractures after 7 days
- Follow all distal radius fractures for at least 6–12 months
