Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Avascular Necrosis (AVN) of the Hip
Faculty: Dr. Mukul Mohindra

### Introduction & Historical Background
- Synonyms: Osteonecrosis, Chandler’s Disease – often called the coronary artery disease of the hip.
- Earliest concept: Hippocrates – recognized bone death in living persons.
- Haenish (1925) & Freund (1926): Coined Idiopathic AVN.
- Jaffe & Pomeranz (1934): Described the pathological changes.
- James Russell (1974): Wrote the first comprehensive book on osteonecrosis.
Definition:
AVN is a pathological process resulting from loss of blood supply to the femoral
head, leading to ischemic bone death and subsequent collapse.

### Types & Etiopathogenesis
1. Traumatic AVN
- Due to disruption of medial femoral circumflex artery (primary blood supply of femoral head).
- Common causes:
  - Fracture neck femur
  - Hip dislocation
  - SCFE (slipped capital femoral epiphysis)

Described by: Femister, who also promoted the concept of bone grafting for AVN.
2. Non-Traumatic (Chandler’s Disease)
- Final common pathway → intravascular coagulation and thrombosis.
### Direct Causes:
- Irradiation, vasculitis, hypercoagulable states
- Hematologic malignancies (leukemia, lymphoma)
- Dysbaric disorders (decompression sickness)
- Sickle cell disease
### Indirect Causes:
- Steroid use, alcoholism → fat accumulation → raised intraosseous pressure → vascular compression
- SLE, viral infections (HIV, CMV, Hepatitis)

- Protease inhibitors (HIV meds)
Idiopathic: Most common overall (no identifiable cause).
### Probable vs Definite Causes (Kulkarni):
- Definite: SLE, irradiation, post-traumatic, steroids, Gaucher’s, hemoglobinopathies, dysbaric, transplant
- Probable: Alcohol abuse, idiopathic, gout

### Epidemiology & Demographics
- Males > Females
- Average age: 30–50 years
- Bilateral involvement: 50–75% cases → Always evaluate opposite hip (MRI preferred even if X-ray normal)
- Multifocal AVN: 3% cases (may involve other joints – shoulder, knee)

### Pathophysiology & Blood Supply
- Main arterial supply: Medial circumflex femoral artery → retinacular vessels → femoral head.
- Secondary: Artery of ligamentum teres (branch of obturator artery).
- Watershed zone: Subchondral area (anterosuperolateral) – first to undergo ischemia.

### Clinical Presentation
- Pain: Insidious onset, groin pain.
- Movement restriction: Internal rotation → Abduction → Flexion.
- Sectoral Sign / Axis Deviation / Caffey’s Sign:
  - Internal rotation reduced in flexion > extension.
  - Seen in both AVN and Perthes’ disease.
- Gait: Antalgic ± Trendelenburg gait.

Imaging & Diagnosis
X-Ray
- Views: AP, frog-leg lateral, and opposite hip.
- Earliest sign: Crescent sign – subchondral fracture under intact cartilage.
- Late signs: Sclerosis, lysis, collapse, loss of sphericity.

<!-- page 3 -->

### Bone Scan
- Early stage: Cold area (decreased uptake)
- Later: Donut sign – peripheral uptake with central photopenic zone.
MRI – Investigation of Choice (99% sensitivity/specificity)
- T1: Hypointense band
- T2: Bright lesion with double line sign
- Bone marrow edema → indicator of poor prognosis.
Functional Bone Marrow Tests (rarely used clinically)
- Bone-seeking isotope studies
- Bone marrow pressure (normal 20–30 mmHg)
- Oxygen saturation & intramedullary venography
- Core biopsy → identifies cause (lipid infiltration, marrow disease, etc.)
### Blood Investigations
- CBC, Lipid profile, Uric acid, LFT/KFT
- Hemoglobin electrophoresis (sickle cell)
- ANA (SLE), viral markers (HIV, Hepatitis)
- Coagulation profile

### Natural History
- Progression: Necrosis → Fragmentation → Collapse → Secondary OA
- Some cases → spontaneous repair via revascularization.
### Bad Prognostic Factors
- Large lesion extent (Kerboul angle > 240°)
- Subchondral collapse
- Anterosuperolateral lesion site
- Marrow edema on MRI
- Age > 50 years Bilateral disease is NOT a bad prognostic factor.

### Prognostic Measurement – Kerboul’s Combined Necrotic Angle
- Sum of necrotic arcs on mid-sagittal + mid-coronal MRI.
  - <190° → Low risk
  - 190–240° → Moderate risk
  - 240° → High risk → Early collapse → THR likely

Classifications
### Ficat & Arlet (Modified)

| Stage | Radiological Features |
|---|---|
| 0 | Preclinical, normal X-ray; positive marrow tests |
| 1 | Normal X-ray, MRI positive (edema) |
| 2A | Osteoporosis, cysts, sclerosis – no collapse |
| 2B | Crescent sign (subchondral fracture), sphericity maintained |
| 3 | Loss of sphericity – head flattening |
| 4 | Arthritic changes on acetabular side |

### Steinberg / Univ. of Pennsylvania (1984)
- 6 stages; each subdivided into A/B/C (extent of collapse <15%, 15–30%, >30%)
- MRI-based, emphasizes extent + collapse → better prognostication.

Management Principles
AVN is irreversible; treatment aims to delay collapse, preserve head, and
relieve pain.
### A. Medical Management (Pre-collapse Stages I–IIA)
- Analgesics / NSAIDs for pain
- Bisphosphonates (esp. Alendronate 70 mg/week) – prevent subchondral collapse.
- Statins – in steroid/alcohol-induced AVN.
- Anticoagulants – in documented hypercoagulable states (e.g. Enoxaparin).
- Adjunctive:
  - Hyperbaric oxygen therapy
  - Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)
Goal: Retard progression and protect contralateral hip.

### B. Surgical Management
1. Core Decompression (CD)
- Indication: Early AVN before collapse (Ficat I–IIA)
- Principle: Reduces intraosseous pressure + stimulates angiogenesis.
- Technique:

<!-- page 5 -->

- Traditionally: single 8–10 mm core.
  - Preferred: multiple (3.2 mm) drill holes to avoid fracture risk.
  - Target: anterosuperolateral necrotic area under fluoroscopy.
2. Core Decompression + Bone Grafting
  - For subchondral collapse (Ficat IIB).
  - Options:
  - Non-vascularized fibula (strut support)
  - Vascularized fibula (technically demanding, less common)
  - Muscle pedicle grafts (TFL, Sartorius)
3. Bone Grafting Techniques
- Lightbulb Technique: Cortical window in femoral neck → remove necrotic bone → fill with graft.
  - Trapdoor Technique: Through articular surface after surgical dislocation.
  - Retrograde Grafting: Via trocar from lateral cortex → curettage + backfill with graft or substitute.
4. Alternatives to Bone Grafts
  - Bone Morphogenetic Proteins (BMP)
  - Bone Marrow Aspirate Concentrate (BMAC)
  - Platelet Rich Plasma (PRP)
  - Porous Tantalum Rods
  - Mesenchymal Stem Cells (10⁶ CFU concentration → ~75% 1-year success per Zhou et al.)
5. Rotational Osteotomies
- Indication: Small lesions (<200° Kerboul; <30% head involvement)
- Age: <40 yrs; full ROM; no acetabular pathology (≤ Stage 3)
- Technique: Intertrochanteric region osteotomy
  - Medial lesions → Varus osteotomy
  - Anterolateral lesions → Valgus-flexion osteotomy (Sugioka’s)
  - Caution: Alters anatomy → makes future THR difficult.
6. Arthroplasty (THR)
  - Indication: Advanced stages (Ficat III–IV), loss of sphericity, arthritis.
  - Total Hip Replacement preferred over resurfacing.
  - Resurfacing (Birmingham technique) – largely abandoned due to poor long-term outcomes.

### Stage-Wise Treatment Summary

| Stage (Ficat–Arlet) | Preferred Treatment |
|---|---|
| 0–I | Medical management ± Bisphosphonates |

| Stage (Ficat–Arlet) | Preferred Treatment |
|---|---|
| IIA | Core decompression ± Osteotomy (<200° Kerboul) |
| IIB | Core decompression + Bone grafting |
| III | Osteotomy (<200° Kerboul) / THR if extensive |

### Surgical Approaches (for Arthroplasty / Dislocation Procedures)
  - Anterior: Somerville (for DDH)
  - Anterolateral: Watson-Jones
  - Lateral: Hardinge
  - Posterolateral: Gibson
  - Posterior: Osborne/Moore/Southern
  - Safe Surgical Dislocation (Ganz approach):
  - Posterior approach with trochanteric flip osteotomy.
  - Preserves medial femoral circumflex artery.
  - Head dislocated anteriorly via posterior route.

### Key MCQ & Exam Pearls
  - Earliest X-ray sign: Crescent sign.
  - Most sensitive investigation: MRI.
  - Commonest site of initial necrosis: Anterosuperolateral femoral head.
  - Kerboul angle >240° → poor prognosis.
  - Definite causes: Steroids, SLE, irradiation, Gaucher’s, trauma.
  - Probable causes: Alcohol, idiopathic, gout.
  - Bisphosphonate of choice: Alendronate 70 mg/week.
  - Core decompression effective till: Stage I–IIA.
  - Bone grafting (fibula): Stage IIB.
  - Best candidate for osteotomy: <40 yrs, Kerboul <200°, full ROM.
  - THR reserved for: Stage III–IV with collapse/arthritis.
