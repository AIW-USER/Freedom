Brachial Plexus Injuries – Dr. Priyanka Sharma
Overview / Key Concepts
Brachial plexus injuries (BPI) are complex nerve injuries involving the C5–T1 nerve
roots, with presentations ranging from transient neuropraxia to complete root avulsions.
Understanding anatomy, injury patterns, timing of intervention, and reconstructive
options is crucial for exam preparation and clinical decision-making.
Broadly classified into: - Neonatal brachial plexus palsy (NBPP / OBPI) - Traumatic
brachial plexus injury (TBPI)

Anatomy of the Brachial Plexus
### Root Contribution
- Classical roots: C5, C6, C7, C8, T1
- Prefixed plexus: C4 contribution
- Postfixed plexus: T2 contribution
### Structural Analogy
- Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Terminal branches
Trunks
- Upper trunk: C5 + C6
- Middle trunk: C7
- Lower trunk: C8 + T1
Cords (Named in relation to axillary artery)
- Lateral cord: anterior divisions of upper + middle trunk
- Medial cord: anterior division of lower trunk
- Posterior cord: posterior divisions of all three trunks

Important Branches by Level
### Root Level
- Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7), winging of scapula
- Dorsal scapular nerve (C5)
### Trunk Level
- Suprascapular nerve
- Nerve to subclavius

### Cord Level
- Lateral and medial pectoral nerves
- Upper and lower subscapular nerves
- Thoracodorsal nerve
### Terminal Branches

| Cord | Major Terminal Nerves |
|---|---|
| Lateral | Musculocutaneous, part of Median |
| Medial | Ulnar, part of Median, medial cutaneous nerves |
| Posterior | Axillary, Radial |

Neonatal Brachial Plexus Palsy (NBPP / OBPI)
Etiology
In utero / during descent - Uterine anomalies (e.g., bicornuate uterus)
During expulsion (most common) - Shoulder dystocia - Humeral or clavicular fracture
- Maternal diabetes - Macrosomia (>4.5 kg) - Breech delivery - Forceps or
vacuum-assisted delivery - Birth asphyxia
Caesarean section reduces but does not eliminate risk.

### When to Intervene in NBPP
- No recovery of biceps function at 3 months
- Failed cookie test at 9 months
### Cookie Test
- Normal: child brings food to mouth with elbow flexion and shoulder adduction
- Failure indicates inadequate recovery and need for surgical evaluation

### Naraka Classification (NBPP)

| Type | Involvement | Clinical Features |
|---|---|---|
| I | C5–C6 | Classic Erb palsy |
| II | C5–C7 | Extended Erb palsy |
| III | C5–T1 | Total palsy, no Horner |
| IV | C5–T1 | Total palsy with Horner syndrome |

<!-- page 3 -->

Types III and IV have poor prognosis and often require early surgery.

Erb Palsy (C5–C6)
### Clinical Deformity
- Waiter’s tip posture
- Arm adducted, internally rotated
- Elbow extended, forearm pronated
### Nerve Deficits
- Axillary nerve, deltoid, teres minor
- Suprascapular nerve, supraspinatus, infraspinatus
- Musculocutaneous nerve, biceps
- Radial nerve contribution via C6

### Klumpke Paralysis (C8–T1)
- Involvement of ulnar and median nerves
- Loss of intrinsic hand muscles
- Claw hand deformity
- Wrist in extreme extension
- Worse prognosis than Erb palsy

### Co-Contraction and Trumpet Sign
- Seen in partially recovered NBPP
- Simultaneous activation of antagonistic muscles
- Trumpet sign: child abducts shoulder and pronates forearm while bringing hand to mouth

Traumatic Brachial Plexus Injury (TBPI)
### Common Causes
- High-speed road traffic accidents
- Motorcycle accidents
### Special Entities
- Burners and stingers (dead arm syndrome)
  - Seen in contact sports
  - Transient C5–C6 weakness
  - Managed conservatively

<!-- page 4 -->

- Parsonage–Turner syndrome (brachial neuritis)
  - Severe shoulder pain followed by patchy paralysis
  - Autoimmune, viral, drug-related

### Mechanisms of Injury
- Traction injury: arm pulled down, neck bent away
- Compression injury: lateral neck flexion
- Direct blow at Erb’s point

Preganglionic vs Postganglionic Injury
Landmark: Dorsal root ganglion (DRG)

### Preganglionic Injury Features
- High-speed trauma
- Horner syndrome
- Winging of scapula
- Severe neuropathic pain
- Intact Lewis triple response
- Elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic nerve)
Prognosis is poor.

### Horner Syndrome
- Ptosis
- Miosis
- Anhidrosis
Indicates C8–T1 sympathetic chain involvement

Diagnostic Evaluation
### CT Myelography
- Gold standard for root avulsion
- Done 3–4 weeks post-injury
- Shows pseudomeningocele
### MRI Brachial Plexus
- Preferred initial imaging

<!-- page 5 -->

- Detects:
  - Pseudomeningocele
  - Empty nerve root sleeves
  - Cord shift

Electrodiagnostic Studies
### EMG
- Detects denervation
- Useful after 10–14 days (proximal), 3–6 weeks (distal)
### Nerve Conduction Studies
- SNAP preserved in preganglionic injuries
- CMAP reduced in denervation
### SMEP
- Highly sensitive
- Performed after 4–6 weeks

Treatment Options
### Nerve Repair
- Direct repair rarely possible
- Regeneration rate: 1 mm/day
### Nerve Grafting
- Reversed cable nerve grafts
- Sural nerve commonly used
### Vascularized Ulnar Nerve Graft (VUNG)
- Used in C8–T1 avulsions
- Pedicled graft from affected side

Nerve Transfers (Neurotization)
### Extraplexal Donors
- Spinal accessory nerve
- Intercostal nerves
- Contralateral C7
- Hypoglossal nerve

<!-- page 6 -->

### Intraplexal Donors
- Phrenic nerve
- Pectoral nerve

Important Named Transfers
### Oberlin Transfer
- Ulnar nerve fascicle to musculocutaneous nerve
- Restores elbow flexion
Mackinnon modification: - Additional median nerve fascicle to brachialis

### Somsak Transfer
- Nerve to long head of triceps to axillary nerve
- Restores shoulder abduction
### Spinal Accessory to Suprascapular Nerve
- Improves shoulder function

### Muscle Transfers
- Pedicled latissimus dorsi, elbow flexion
- Pedicled pectoralis major
- Free functional muscle transfer (gracilis)

### Secondary Procedures in OBPI
- External rotation osteotomy
- Triangular tilt surgery
- Mod Quad procedure (LD, teres major, subscapularis, pectoralis release)
- Botox for co-contractions

Faculty Quote
“Timing is everything in brachial plexus injuries. Miss the window, and function is lost
forever.” – Dr. Priyanka Sharma

### Exam Pearls
- Biceps recovery at 3 months guides NBPP intervention
- Failed cookie test at 9 months mandates surgery

- MRI is preferred early investigation

- Spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve is high-yield
