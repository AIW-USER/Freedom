Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Shin Pain in
Runners
Faculty: Dr. Shikha Dhaundiyal
Department: Sports & Exercise Medicine

OVERVIEW
Focus: Clinical evaluation and management of shin pain in athletes and runners.
Key Causes: - Medial Tibial Stress Fracture
- Anterior Tibial Stress Fracture
- Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome (MTSS)
- Chronic Exertional Compartment Syndrome (CECS)
Dr. Shikha’s Quote:
“Every runner’s shin tells a story — of training errors, biomechanics, and
recovery balance.”

### ANATOMY & FUNCTIONAL OVERVIEW
- The leg (knee to ankle) bears body weight during running.
- Bones: Tibia (major load-bearing) & Fibula (support).

- Compartments:
  - Anterior: Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL, Peroneus tertius.
  - Lateral: Peroneus longus & brevis.
  - Superficial Posterior: Gastrocnemius, Soleus, Plantaris.
  - Deep Posterior: Tibialis posterior, FHL, FDL, Popliteus.

### PATHOGENESIS OF SHIN PAIN

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| Bone Stress | Repeated microtrauma → stress reaction → possible stress fracture |
| Inflammation | Chronic traction at muscle insertion → periosteal irritation |
| Vascular Insufficiency | Arterial entrapment or venous congestion ↑ compartment pressure |
| Compartment Pressure | Fascia fibrosis → reduced compliance → ↑ pressure on exercise |

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| Nerve Entrapment | Local or referred neural compression causing pain/numbness |

HISTORY & CLINICAL EVALUATION
### Key History Points:
- Training errors: Sudden change in distance, surface, or intensity.

- Footwear: Worn-out or incorrect shoes.

- Warm-up: Pain improving post warm-up → MTSS or muscle strain.

- Conditioning: Poor diet, sleep, or Vitamin D deficiency → stress injuries.

- Female Athlete Triad / RED-S: Amenorrhea, low bone density, disordered eating.
### Pain Characteristics:

| Type | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Activity-related, relieved by rest | Bone stress injury / MTSS |
| Improves after warm-up | MTSS / Tendinopathy |
| Appears after fixed distance, | CECS |

relieved by rest

| Persistent, night pain | Stress fracture or tumor |
|---|---|
| With swelling/ecchymosis | Impending compartment syndrome |

Ask about paresthesia, weakness, and vascular symptoms to exclude
entrapment or DVT.

CLINICAL EXAMINATION
Observation
- Alignment: Pes planus, genu valgum/varum, tibial torsion, navicular drop.
- Posture during standing and walking.
Palpation

| Site | Suggestive Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Focal tenderness on tibia | Tibial stress fracture |
| Upper fibula tenderness | Syndesmotic (Maisonneuve) injury |
| Posteromedial tibia | MTSS |

<!-- page 3 -->

### Movement Assessment
- Plantarflexion: Loss of pulse → Popliteal artery entrapment.

- Dorsiflexion loss: Calf tightness → MTSS.

- Resisted movements: Pain → Tendinopathy/muscle strain.

- Hopping/Running tests: Increase pain in stress fractures or CECS.

### LAB & IMAGING INVESTIGATIONS

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| CBC, ESR | Rule out infection/inflammation |
| Urine myoglobin, CPK | Rhabdomyolysis detection |
| Serum Ca, Vit D, Mg | Bone metabolism assessment |
| D-dimer, Coagulation | Exclude DVT |
| Thyroid Profile | Hypothyroidism → tendinopathy |
| EMG/NCV | Rule out neuropathic cause |
| Compartment pressure test | Diagnostic for CECS (>25 mmHg post-exercise) |
| MRI / CT | Identify edema, fracture line, periosteal changes |

X-ray changes appear 2–3 weeks post-injury; MRI is most sensitive early
indicator.

MEDIAL TIBIAL STRESS FRACTURE
Features: - Gradual onset pain ↑ with activity, may persist at rest/night.
- Tenderness at posteromedial tibia, calf pain common.
- Risk factors: High-arched (cavus) or over-pronated foot.
Treatment: - Rest & reduced load (crutches if needed).
- Aircast brace for 2–6 weeks.
- Cross-training: Swimming, cycling.
- Soft tissue massage (distal → proximal).
- Return when pain-free & no tenderness.
“If tenderness persists, the bone hasn’t healed — don’t rush the run.”
Prevention: Correct training errors, nutrition, biomechanics, and warm-up.

### ANTERIOR TIBIAL STRESS FRACTURE
- Dull aching anterior tibial pain; tenderness at anterior cortex.
- X-ray: “Dreaded black line” due to cortical tension.

- Poor blood supply → delayed healing.
Management: - Offload limb: Pneumatic Aircast or KT taping.
- Stop NSAIDs & Smoking.
- LIPUS therapy for bone healing.
- Surgery: IM nailing ± bone grafting if non-union.

MEDIAL TIBIAL STRESS SYNDROME (MTSS / Shin Splints)
Definition: Diffuse posteromedial tibial tenderness (upper 2/3–lower 1/3 junction).
Pathogenesis: Repetitive traction of soleus (main culprit) and tibialis posterior →
fascia inflammation.
Risk Factors: - Training errors, pronated foot, poor shoes.
- Calf tightness (gastrosoleus).
- Weak hip abductors.
- Female gender, low Vit D/calcium, RED-S.
- Prior stress fracture.
Treatment: - Activity restriction: Avoid running/jumping.
- Cross-training: Swimming/cycling.
- Stretching: Soleus-focused & foam rolling.
- Analgesics + Muscle relaxants.
- Supplements: Ca + Vit D3 + Mg.
- Severe cases: Aircast or surgical fascia release.
Prevention: - Correct training, biomechanics (flat foot, weak glutes).
- Maintain nutrition/hydration.
- Use medial arch insoles/taping.
- Avoid anti-pronated shoes (can worsen imbalance).
“The real culprit isn’t the calf — it’s the overworked soleus.”
CHRONIC EXERTIONAL COMPARTMENT SYNDROME
(CECS)
Mechanism: Non-compliant fascia → ↑ compartment pressure during activity →
ischemia & pain.
Pathophysiology:
Inflammation → fibrosis → ↓ elasticity → ↓ perfusion → hypoxia → pain & paresthesia.

<!-- page 5 -->

Diagnosis: - Exercise-induced pain, resolves with rest.
- Compartment pressure >25 mmHg post-exercise or ↑>10 mmHg from baseline.
- Confirm with ICP testing or Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS).
Deep Posterior CECS:
- Pain/bursting sensation at posteromedial tibia during exertion.
- May include tingling or numbness.
- Pressure >25 mmHg diagnostic.
Treatment: - Conservative: Rest, massage, dry needling, biomechanical correction.
- Surgical: Fasciotomy/Fasciectomy (preferred), Periosteal stripping (revision only).
Surgical Pearls: - Target affected compartment only.
- Prevent hematoma/cellulitis.
- Early ROM + cryotherapy.
- No clear evidence favoring fasciectomy over fasciotomy.
Rehab: - Limited WB × 3–5 days → full WB as tolerated.
- Start swimming/cycling post-suture removal (~2 weeks).
- Light jogging at 4–6 weeks; full RTP at 8–12 weeks.
“Every fascia released must be followed by early motion — stiffness kills
recovery.”

ANTERIOR & LATERAL COMPARTMENT SYNDROMES
Muscles Involved: - Anterior: Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL, Peroneus tertius.
- Lateral: Peroneus longus & brevis.
Symptoms: - Anterior: Pain lateral to shin + paresthesia in 1st web space (Deep
peroneal nerve).
- Lateral: Pain anterior to fibula + paresthesia over dorsum of foot (Superficial peroneal
nerve).
- Tightness in gastrosoleus common.
Management: - Activity modification: Avoid running/jumping.
- Manual therapy: Myofascial release, dry needling.
- Footwear: Reduce heel height.
- Running biomechanics: Midfoot/forefoot strike, shorter stride, lower vertical tibial
angle.
- Refractory cases: Botox or surgical fasciotomy.

### SURGICAL OUTCOMES
- Best results: Nerve entrapment syndromes.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Poor outcomes: Deep posterior CECS due to incomplete release.

- Complications: Hematoma, cellulitis, nerve injury.
Success Predictors: - Measure compartment pressures at multiple intervals.
- High pressures pre/post = poorer prognosis.

### TAKE-HOME SUMMARY
- Shin pain = multifactorial. Always assess training, biomechanics, and nutrition.

- MTSS ≠ Stress Fracture: Diffuse vs focal tenderness.

- CECS: Pain on exertion, relieved by rest, diagnostic ICP >25 mmHg.

- Soleus muscle = key culprit in MTSS.

- Avoid anti-pronated shoes; focus on natural gait correction.

- Rehab progression: Rest → Cross-train → Gradual load → Return to play. “Pain-free stride isn’t luck — it’s alignment, awareness, and adaptation.”
