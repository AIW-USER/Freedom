Fractures of Shaft and Distal Tibia – Dr. Anurag
Tiwari
Overview
Fractures of the tibial shaft and distal tibia (pilon fractures) are common lower limb
injuries. Shaft fractures are typically high energy injuries, while distal tibia fractures often
involve the articular surface and soft tissue damage. Both require careful assessment of
soft tissues, alignment, and stability.
Faculty Remark: “Soft tissue decides the timing and implant. Bone decides the
technique.” – Dr. Anurag Tiwari

## Tibial Shaft Fractures
Epidemiology
- Commonest long bone fracture.
- High energy trauma in young, low energy in elderly.
- Associated with:
  - Fibula fracture.
  - Compartment syndrome.
  - Open injuries.

### Anatomy Essentials
- Tibia is subcutaneous medially, making it vulnerable to open fractures.
- Blood supply:
  - Nutrient artery (posterior tibial) enters posterolaterally.
  - Periosteal branches from anterior tibial artery.
- Fibula assists in load transfer (up to 16 percent).
Deforming forces: - Proximal fragment → flexed and valgus (by knee flexors). - Distal
fragment → varus and extension.

Classification
### AO Classification
- 42-A: Simple.
- 42-B: Wedge.

<!-- page 2 -->

- 42-C: Complex.
### Winquist and Hansen (Comminution)
- Grade 0 to 4.
Gustilo–Anderson (for open fractures)
- Grade 1 to 3C based on soft tissue.

### Clinical Evaluation
- Look for open wounds.
- Check distal pulses.
- Evaluate compartments.
- Assess knee and ankle joint involvement.

Imaging
- AP and Lateral radiographs including knee and ankle.
- Check rotational alignment.
- CT if extension into joints.

Management
### Non Operative (rare)
- Undisplaced fractures.
- Long leg cast 6 to 8 weeks.
- High rates of malunion.
### Operative Options

| Implant | Indication |
|---|---|
| IM nailing (gold standard) | Most fractures |
| Plate fixation | Metaphyseal, very proximal/distal, polytrauma |
| External fixation | Open fractures, damage control |

Intramedullary Nailing (IMN)
Advantages
- Biological, load sharing, minimal soft tissue disruption.

<!-- page 3 -->

### Entry Points
- Patellar tendon split (most used).
- Parapatellar.
- Suprapatellar (SPN) – better alignment, less pain, easier reduction.
### Reduction Aids
- Poller screws (blocking screws).
- Temporary unicortical plating.
- Reduction forceps.
- K wires.
### Common Problems
- Malalignment (most common) – varus or valgus due to inadequate reduction.
- Anterior knee pain.
- Delayed union.

Open Fractures
Management follows BOA–BAPRAS and Gustilo principles.

Protocol
1. IV antibiotics within 3 hours.
2. Tetanus prophylaxis.
3. Primary debridement.
4. Temporary stabilization (external fixator).
5. Early soft tissue cover (within 72 hours ideally).
### Flap Options
- Medial gastrocnemius for proximal third.
- Soleus for middle third.
- Free flap for distal third.

### Complications – Shaft Tibia
- Malunion (varus most common).
- Delayed union.
- Non union.
- Infection.
- Knee pain (IMN).
- Compartment syndrome.

## Distal Tibia (Pilon) Fractures
Overview
Pilon fractures involve the articular surface of the tibial plafond. These are high
energy injuries with severe soft tissue trauma.
Dr. Anurag: “In pilon fractures, you treat the soft tissues first and the bone later.”
Mechanism
### High Energy
- Axial loading (fall from height, RTA).
- Leads to comminution, articular impaction.
### Low Energy
- Rotational injuries.

Anatomy
- Tibial plafond covered by thin cartilage.
- Medial malleolus supports deltoid ligament.
- Fibula provides 20 to 25 percent load transfer.
### Deforming Forces
- Talus drives tibial plafond into comminution.
- Distal fragment: external rotation.
- Proximal fragment: internal rotation.

Imaging
- AP, Lateral, Mortise.
- CT required for mapping:
  - Anterolateral fragment (Chaput).
  - Medial fragment.
  - Posterolateral fragment (Volkmann).
  - Central impaction.

<!-- page 5 -->

Classification
Ruedi–Allgower
1. Type 1 – Non displaced.
2. Type 2 – Simple displacement.
3. Type 3 – Comminuted.
### AO/OTA
- 43-A: Extra articular.
- 43-B: Partial articular.
- 43-C: Complete articular.

### Soft Tissue First Principle
- Wait until wrinkle sign appears.
- Avoid early ORIF in swollen skin → wound breakdown.
- Stage 1: Spanning external fixator.
- Stage 2: CT scan.
- Stage 3: Delayed fixation.

Management Approach
### Stage 1 – Temporary Stabilization
- Delta frame external fixator.
- Restore length and alignment.
- Avoid over reduction.
### Stage 2 – CT Based Planning
- Identify key fragments.
- Plan approach based on column.
Stage 3 – Definitive Fixation
Goals
- Restore articular congruity.
- Restore alignment.
- Strong metaphyseal support.
Approaches

| Approach | Indication |
|---|---|
| Anterolateral | Chaput fragment, anterolateral plafond |
| Medial | Medial malleolar fragment |

<!-- page 6 -->

| Approach | Indication |
|---|---|
| Posterolateral | Volkmann fragment |

### Fixation Strategies
- Buttress plating.
- Raft screws beneath articular surface.
- Bone grafting for central defect.
- Fibular fixation often helps restore tibial length.

### Complications – Distal Tibia
- Wound breakdown.
- Infection.
- Malalignment.
- Varus collapse.
- Post traumatic arthritis.
- Non union.

### Viva and Exam Pearls
- Tibia is most common long bone fracture.
- Varus malalignment most common after IM nailing.
- Suprapatellar nailing improves alignment.
- Pilon fractures always staged.
- Wrinkle sign indicates readiness for surgery.
- Fibular fixation helps restore length.
- Ruedi–Allgower: 3 types.
- AO 43 for distal tibia.

Final Faculty Insight: “Right timing and right alignment are the two pillars of
successful tibial fracture management.”
