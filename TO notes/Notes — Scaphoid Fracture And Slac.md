Scaphoid Fracture and SLAC – Dr. Amit Mittal
Overview
Scaphoid fractures are the most common carpal fractures, and their complications,
particularly nonunion and avascular necrosis, can lead to Scaphoid Nonunion
Advanced Collapse (SNAC) or Scapholunate Advanced Collapse (SLAC).
Understanding the vascularity, fracture patterns, investigation protocols, and treatment
algorithms is essential for preventing long term wrist dysfunction.
Faculty Remark: “If a scaphoid fracture goes into nonunion, it is because someone
did not diagnose or treat it well in the first place.”

Anatomy Essentials
### Scaphoid Features
- Boat shaped bone spanning proximal and distal carpal rows.
- Covered 80 to 90 percent by articular cartilage.
- No muscle or tendon insertions.
- Acts as a dynamic link between the carpal rows.
- Blood supply is retrograde from distal to proximal pole, making proximal pole fractures prone to AVN.
Vascularity
- Distal pole: good supply.
- Waist: moderate.
- Proximal pole: worst supplied → highest nonunion risk.

### Mechanism of Injury
- Fall on outstretched hand.
- Forced dorsiflexion with radial deviation.
- Car accidents, sports injuries.

Clinical Evaluation
### Primary Signs
- Anatomical snuffbox tenderness.
- Tubercle tenderness (more sensitive if examined within hours).
- Scaphoid compression test.

<!-- page 2 -->

- Pain with axial load.
### Secondary Indicators
- Swelling over radial wrist.
- Pain with resisted pronation.
- Pain on radial deviation.
Absence of X ray findings does not rule out fracture.
Radiological Assessment
### Standard Views
- PA neutral.
- Lateral.
- PA in ulnar deviation.
- Stecher’s view (20° ulnar deviation, wrist pronated) improves visualization.
### CT Scan
- Best for assessing fracture line, displacement, sclerosis, and union.
- Critical for preoperative planning.
### MRI
- Gold standard for detecting occult fractures.
- Best tool for diagnosing AVN.

Classification
### Herbert Classification

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| A1 | Tubercle fracture |
| A2 | Stable incomplete fracture |
| B1 | Distal oblique |
| B2 | Waist unstable |
| B3 | Proximal pole |
| B4 | Trans scaphoid perilunate |
| B5 | Comminuted |
| C | Delayed union |
| D | Established nonunion |

### Other Useful Categories
- Stable vs unstable.
- Acute vs chronic.
- Displaced vs undisplaced.

Treatment of Scaphoid Fractures
### Non Operative
- Indicated for stable, nondisplaced fractures.
- Immobilization: Short arm thumb spica.
- Duration:
  - Distal pole: 6 to 8 weeks.
  - Waist: 10 to 12 weeks.
  - Proximal pole: 4 to 6 months (worst vascularity).
Operative
Indications: - Displacement >1 mm. - Humpback deformity. - Comminution. - Proximal
pole fractures. - SNAC wrist. - High demand patient.

### Surgical Options
- Percutaneous screw fixation (retrograde or antegrade).
- Open reduction + internal fixation (ORIF).
- Bone grafting for nonunion:
  - Nonvascularized graft (Russe, wedge graft).
  - Vascularized graft (1,2 ICSRA based graft etc).

Complications
- Nonunion.
- Malunion (humpback deformity).
- AVN of proximal pole.
- SNAC wrist.
- Midcarpal instability.

## Understanding SLAC and SNAC Wrist
SLAC Wrist – Scapholunate Advanced Collapse
Caused by chronic scapholunate ligament injury.

<!-- page 4 -->

### Radiographic Progression
1. Radioscaphoid arthritis.
2. Capitatescaphoid arthritis.
3. Lunatecapitate arthritis.
4. Preserved radiolunate joint (until late stages).
### Clinical Features
- Radial wrist pain.
- Weak grip.
- Painful ROM.

SNAC Wrist – Scaphoid Nonunion Advanced Collapse
Caused by neglected scaphoid fracture → nonunion.

Staging
- Stage 1: Arthritis at radial styloid.
- Stage 2: Radioscaphoid arthritis.
- Stage 3: Capitolunate arthritis.

Management of SLAC/SNAC Wrist
### Stage Based
- Early stage: Styloidectomy + radial sided procedures.
- Mid stage: Proximal row carpectomy (PRC) or scaphoid excision + 4-corner fusion.
- Advanced: Wrist arthrodesis.
### PRC vs 4-Corner Fusion

| Procedure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| PRC | Faster recovery, simpler, good results | Not suitable if capitolunate arthritis present |
| 4-Corner fusion | Strong, stable, preserves some motion | More technically demanding, nonunion risk |

## Perilunate Injuries (Brief Integration)
Often associated with scaphoid fractures.
- Lesser arc injuries: Pure ligamentous.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Greater arc injuries: Trans scaphoid, transcapitate, transradial styloid etc.
Perilunate dislocations must be identified early to avoid chronic instability and arthritis.

### Surgical Pearls (Dr. Mittal)
- Always check for associated SL injury in scaphoid fractures.
- Pain out of proportion → suspect occult fracture.
- Thumb spica early reduces nonunion risk.
- Do not rely solely on X ray; CT or MRI early prevents missed diagnoses.
- Correct screw orientation is critical for union.

### Exam Pearls
- Snuffbox tenderness + normal X ray → suspect scaphoid fracture → immobilize.
- Proximal pole fracture → highest AVN risk.
- SLAC = ligament injury; SNAC = scaphoid nonunion.
- PRC contraindicated in capitolunate arthritis.
- 4-Corner fusion indicated in mid stage collapse.
- Herbert classification commonly used.
- Retrograde screw fixation preferred in most scaphoid fractures.

Final Faculty Insight: “Think of scaphoid injuries as time sensitive. Early diagnosis
prevents SNAC, and understanding kinematics helps prevent SLAC.”
