Cumulative Trauma and Soft Tissue Disorder –
Dr. Anurag Tiwari
Overview
Cumulative trauma disorders are overuse injuries caused by repetitive micro trauma
exceeding the tissue’s capacity to heal. These conditions involve tendons, sheaths,
entheses, bursae, and soft tissue interfaces, and are common in both athletes and
occupational settings.
Faculty Remark: “Soft tissue injuries are all about load — too much, too soon, too
often.”

Basic Concepts
What is Cumulative Trauma?
- Repeated micro injuries without adequate rest.
- Breakdown in tissue homeostasis.
- Causes chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and pain.
### Structures Commonly Affected
- Tendons (tendinitis, tendinosis, paratenonitis).
- Enthesis (enthesopathy).
- Myotendinous junction.
- Bursae.
- Fascia.
- Nerves (compression neuropathies).
- Stress fractures.
### Risk Factors
- Poor ergonomics.
- Repetition.
- Forceful grip.
- Vibration tools.
- Awkward postures.
- Poor conditioning.

## Classification of Soft Tissue Disorders
1. Tendinopathy
- Tendinitis: inflammatory.
- Tendinosis: degenerative, no inflammation.
- Paratenonitis.
- Tenosynovitis.
2. Enthesopathy
- Inflammation at tendon–bone junction.
- Example: lateral epicondylitis.
3. Myotendinous Injuries
- Strains, partial tears.
- Occur during eccentric loading.
4. Bursitis
- Olecranon, prepatellar, trochanteric bursae.
5. Fasciopathy
- Plantar fasciitis.
- Iliotibial band friction.
6. Nerve Compression Disorders
- Carpal tunnel.
- Cubital tunnel.
- Radial tunnel.
7. Stress Injuries
- Stress reaction.
- Stress fractures.

## Mechanisms of Soft Tissue Injury
### Micro Trauma Cycle
1. Repetitive load → micro tears.
2. Inadequate recovery → inflammation.
3. Collagen disorganization → weaker tendon.
4. Degeneration → tendinosis.
5. Persistent overload → partial or complete rupture.

<!-- page 3 -->

Pathophysiology
- Increased fibroblast activity.
- Neovascularization (painful nerves accompany vessels).
- Collagen type III replaces type I → weaker structure.

## Clinical Features
- Pain with activity, relieved by rest.
- Tenderness at tendon, enthesis, or muscle belly.
- Swelling or crepitus.
- Morning stiffness.
- Weakness in high grade tears.

## Investigations
X-Ray
- Usually normal.
- May show calcification in chronic enthesopathy.
### USG
- Best for real time evaluation.
- Shows tendon tears, neovascularity.
### MRI
- Gold standard.
- Differentiates tendinosis, tears, bursitis, and bone edema.

## Management Principles
1. Rest and Activity Modification
- Reduce load.
- Correct biomechanics.
- Ergonomic correction.
2. Medication
- NSAIDs (short term only).
- Avoid long term NSAIDs in tendinosis.

<!-- page 4 -->

3. Physiotherapy
- Eccentric loading exercises.
- Stretching programs.
- Proprioception training.
- Strength correction of kinetic chain.
4. Bracing and Splints
- Useful in epicondylitis, plantar fasciitis, patellar tendinopathy.
5. Injections
- Steroid injections: good for acute inflammation but avoid repeated use.
- PRP: beneficial in chronic tendinosis.
- Local anesthetic for diagnostic blocks.
6. Shockwave Therapy
- Useful in chronic tendinopathies.
7. Surgical Options (rare)
- For partial tears failing conservative treatment.
- Debridement of degenerative tendon tissue.
- Release of tight structures.

## Common Cumulative Trauma Disorders (Clinical
Approach)
1. Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow)
- Overuse of ECRB.
- Pain over lateral epicondyle.
- Pain with resisted wrist extension.
- Treatment: eccentric ECRB training, counterforce bracing.
2. Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer’s Elbow)
- Overuse of wrist flexors.
- Pain worse with resisted wrist flexion.
3. De Quervain’s Tenosynovitis
- First dorsal compartment.
- Positive Finkelstein.
4. Plantar Fasciitis
- Heel pain, worse in morning.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Treat with stretching, heel cups, shockwave therapy.
5. IT Band Syndrome
- Lateral knee pain.
- Worse with downhill running.
6. Patellar Tendinopathy
- “Jumper’s knee”.
- Pain at inferior pole of patella.
7. Trochanteric Bursitis
- Pain over greater trochanter.
- Often associated with gluteus medius tendinosis.
8. Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy
- Impingement signs: painful arc, Hawkins, Neer.
9. Achilles Tendinopathy
- Pain 2–6 cm above calcaneus.
- Nodularity.

## Red Flags (To Avoid Misdiagnosis)
- Night pain.
- Rest pain.
- Fever.
- Significant swelling.
- Neurological deficits.
- Sudden inability to bear weight.
- Signs of infection.
- Suspected neoplasm.
These require further imaging and evaluation.

### Exam Pearls
- Tendinosis = degeneration, not inflammation.
- Eccentric loading is cornerstone of treatment.
- Steroid good for acute inflammation, not chronic tendinosis.
- PRP useful for chronic tendinosis.
- IT band syndrome affects lateral knee.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Plantar fasciitis worst in morning.
- Stress injury early changes seen best on MRI.

Final Faculty Insight: “Correct the load, correct the biomechanics, and most
cumulative trauma disorders resolve without surgery.”
