Pediatric Trauma Lower Limb – Dr. Sanjay Arora
Overview / Key Concepts
Pediatric lower limb trauma differs fundamentally from adult injuries due to open
physes, elastic bones, thick periosteum, and ongoing growth. The primary goals
are to prevent growth arrest, angular deformity, limb length discrepancy, and early
degenerative changes, rather than merely achieving union.
This lecture systematically covers injuries from pelvis to foot, with emphasis on
mechanisms, growth-related risks, and exam-relevant management principles.

Pelvic Injuries in Children
### Epidemiology and Characteristics
- Result from high-energy trauma
- Often associated with polytrauma
- Visceral injuries may occur without obvious pelvic fractures
- Pediatric pelvis is elastic and largely cartilaginous
Key Differences from Adults
- More single bone fractures than ring disruptions
- Higher incidence of avulsion injuries
- Less hemorrhage due to vasoconstriction and vessel mobility

Pelvic Fracture Classifications
### Tile Classification
- Type A: Rotationally and vertically stable
- Type B: Rotationally unstable, vertically stable
- Type C: Rotationally and vertically unstable
### Torode and Zieg Classification (Pediatric Specific)
- Type I: Avulsion injuries
- Type II: Iliac wing fracture, stable ring
- Type III: Pelvic ring fracture without segmental instability
- Type IV: Pelvic ring fracture with segmental instability

### Pelvic Avulsion Injuries (High Yield)

| Site | Muscle Attached | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Ischial tuberosity | Hamstrings | Most common |
| AIIS | Rectus femoris | Common |
| ASIS | Sartorius | Common |
| Pubic symphysis | Abdominal muscles | Rare |
| Iliac crest | Abdominal muscles | Rare |
| Lesser trochanter | Iliopsoas | Least common |

Management
- Minimally displaced: Conservative
- Significantly displaced: ORIF

### Pelvic Injury Complications
- Premature triradiate cartilage closure
- Limb length discrepancy
- Growth disturbance and acetabular dysplasia
- Chronic pain
- Rarely, death due to hemorrhagic shock

Fracture Neck of Femur in Children
### Key Facts
- Rare (<1% of pediatric fractures)
- High-energy trauma
- Considered a vascular emergency
### Why Important?
- High rates of:
  - Avascular necrosis
  - Nonunion
  - Coxa vara

### Delbet Classification

| Type | Description | AVN Risk |
|---|---|---|
| I | Transepiphyseal | Very high |
| II | Transcervical (most common) | High |

<!-- page 3 -->

| Type | Description | AVN Risk |
|---|---|---|
| III | Cervicotrochanteric | Moderate |
| IV | Intertrochanteric | Low |

Pathophysiology
- After age 3, physis blocks metaphyseal blood supply
- Main supply via lateral epiphyseal artery (MFCA branch)
- Age 3–12 years is most vulnerable period

### Management Principles
- Treat as emergency
- Early reduction and fixation
- Capsular decompression if delayed
- Internal fixation + spica immobilization
Fixation
- <8 years: Smooth pins preferred
- 8 years: Cannulated screws acceptable

Complications
- AVN (risk decreases from Delbet I to IV)
- Nonunion (up to 10%)
- Coxa vara
- Chondrolysis

Femoral Shaft Fractures
### Important Points
- Before walking age → suspect non-accidental injury
- Most common type: Closed, transverse, non-comminuted
- Adolescents resemble adult injury patterns
### Treatment Determinants
- Age
- Weight
- Fracture stability

<!-- page 4 -->

### Age-Based Management (Femur Shaft)

| Age / Weight | Preferred Treatment |
|---|---|
| <6 months | Pavlik harness or spica |
| 6 months–5 years | Spica cast |
| 5–11 years (<40–50 kg) | Flexible nails |
| >11 years or >50 kg | Rigid trochanteric IM nail or plate |

Complications
- Limb length discrepancy (overgrowth or shortening)
- Malrotation
- Infection
- Cast-related complications

Distal Femur Physeal Injuries
### Key Facts
- Contribute ~70% of femoral growth
- Very high risk of growth arrest
Management
- Undisplaced: Long leg cast
- Displaced: Gentle reduction + fixation
Anatomic reduction is critical
Proximal Tibial Injuries
### Tibial Tubercle Fractures
- Avulsion injury
- Risk of compartment syndrome
- Often need fixation and protection
### Proximal Tibial Metaphyseal Fracture
- Seen in 3–6 years
- May cause Cozen’s phenomenon (late valgus)

<!-- page 5 -->

Tibial Spine Fractures
Mechanism
- ACL avulsion equivalent
### Meyers–McKeever Classification
- Type I: Undisplaced
- Type II: Hinged
- Type III: Completely displaced
Management
- Type I: Cast
- Type II: Cast or fixation
- Type III: Arthroscopic fixation

Patellar Sleeve Fractures
### Key Features
- Age: 8–12 years
- Cartilaginous sleeve avulsion
- Inability to perform straight leg raise
Management
- Undisplaced: Cast
- Displaced: ORIF with tension band technique

### Tibial Shaft Fractures
- ~15% of pediatric fractures
- 39% occur in mid-diaphysis
Management
- Conservative: Majority
- Operative: TENS or plating in unstable or older children

Foot Injuries
### Lisfranc Injuries
- Rare but frequently missed
- Weight-bearing radiographs essential
- Isolated base of 2nd metatarsal fracture is a red flag

<!-- page 6 -->

### Hardcastle Classification
- Type A: Total incongruity
- Type B: Partial incongruity (most common in children)
- Type C: Divergent displacement

### Compartment Syndrome
- Risk increases with:
  - Tibial shaft fractures
  - Tibial tubercle fractures
- Diagnosis often delayed in children
- High index of suspicion required

Ankle Physeal Injuries
Epidemiology
- 5% of all fractures
- 15–20% of physeal injuries
- Most common physeal injury in lower limb
### Salter–Harris Distribution

| Type | Frequency |
|---|---|
| II | Most common |
| III | ~25% |
| IV | ~25% |
| I | Uncommon |
| V | Rare |

Transitional Fractures
### Tillaux Fracture
- SH Type III
- Anterolateral epiphyseal avulsion
### Triplane Fracture
- SH Type IV equivalent
- Fracture in sagittal, coronal, and axial planes
CT scan mandatory for assessment

<!-- page 7 -->

### Principles of Ankle Fracture Management
- Articular step-off <1–2 mm
- Gentle reduction (75% traction, 25% manipulation)
- Avoid repeated manipulations
- Prioritize joint congruity over physeal preservation in adolescents

Complications
- Premature physeal arrest
- Angular deformity
- Limb length discrepancy
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis

Faculty Quote
“In pediatric lower limb trauma, our enemy is not non-union. It is growth arrest.” –
Dr. Sanjay Arora

### Exam Pearls
- Pediatric pelvic fractures often have severe visceral injury
- Avulsion injuries are common around pelvis
- Fracture neck femur in children is a vascular emergency
- Delbet classification predicts AVN risk
- Femoral shaft fracture before walking age suggests child abuse
- Distal femur and ankle physes have highest growth arrest risk
- CT scan is mandatory for Tillaux and triplane fractures
- Articular congruity is paramount in ankle injuries
