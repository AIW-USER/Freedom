Pediatric Femoroacetabular Impingement –
Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Pediatric and adolescent femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is not a disease but a
process resulting from abnormal hip morphology subjected to repetitive mechanical
loading. Unlike adults, children and adolescents rarely have established osteoarthritis,
and the focus is on hip preservation.
FAI should only be diagnosed when all three components are present: - Hip pain -
Restricted range of motion - Radiological evidence of impingement morphology
Isolated radiological abnormalities without symptoms do not constitute FAI.

Definition of FAI Syndrome
FAI is an abnormal mechanical abutment between the femoral head–neck junction and
the acetabular rim, leading to: - Labral tears - Chondral delamination - Progressive
restriction of motion - Early secondary osteoarthritis if untreated
Key Concept FAI represents the interaction between abnormal anatomy, activity
level, and time.

Why Pediatric FAI is Different
- No primary osteoarthritis at presentation
- Higher activity levels
- Growth plate involvement
- Remodeling potential exists
- Etiology often linked to childhood hip disorders

### Etiological Conditions Associated with Pediatric FAI
- Developmental dysplasia of hip (DDH) and its sequelae
- Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)
- Perthes disease and Perthes sequelae
- Post-traumatic deformities (AIIS avulsion malunion)
- Skeletal dysplasias
- Acetabular retroversion

Pathomechanics
- Repetitive collision during hip flexion and internal rotation
- Progressive labral injury
- Chondral delamination (especially in cam lesions)
- Reduced extremes of motion

Types of FAI Morphology
### Cam Impingement (Most Common in Pediatrics)
- Aspherical femoral head
- Reduced head–neck offset
- Secondary to physeal growth disturbance
- Develops during physeal maturation (11–14 years)
- Common in young athletic males
### Pincer Impingement
- Acetabular overcoverage
- Coxa profunda
- Acetabular retroversion
- Less common in pediatric population
### Mixed Type
- Most common overall
- Combination of cam and pincer morphology

### Sports Commonly Associated with FAI
- Hockey
- Football
- High-level gymnastics
- Jumping sports
- Running
- Tennis
High pivoting and repetitive hip loading increase risk.

Clinical Presentation
Symptoms
- Groin pain (may refer to knee)

<!-- page 3 -->

- Activity-related pain
- Reduced sports performance
- Mechanical symptoms (catching, locking)
### Red Flag ROM Findings
- Hip flexion <90–100°
- Internal rotation <10° or absent
- Obligatory external rotation on flexion

### Gait Examination
- Antalgic gait
- Positive foot progression angle (SCFE)
- Trendelenburg gait (Perthes, dysplasia)

### Muscle Tightness Evaluation (Essential)
- Ober test: Iliotibial band tightness
- Ely test: Rectus femoris tightness
- 90–90 test: Hamstring tightness
Muscle tightness alone can mimic FAI symptoms.

### Special Tests
- FADIR test (Flexion, Adduction, Internal Rotation): Most sensitive for FAI
- FABER test: Hip and SI joint evaluation
- Stinchfield test: Intra-articular pathology

Radiological Evaluation
### Ideal AP Pelvis X-ray Criteria
- Symmetric obturator foramina
- Coccyx 1–3 cm above pubic symphysis
- Visible acetabular teardrops
- Neutral rotation (lesser trochanters minimally visible)

### Essential Views for FAI
- AP pelvis (standing preferred)
- Dunn view (45° flexion, 20° abduction)

<!-- page 4 -->

- False profile view
Dunn view is most sensitive for cam lesions.

Key Radiological Parameters
### Alpha Angle
- Normal: 50–55°
- Increased in cam impingement
### Head–Neck Offset Ratio
- Normal: >0.17
- Reduced or absent in cam lesions
### Pincer Indicators
- Crossover sign
- Posterior wall sign
- Ischial spine sign
- Coxa profunda

### CT Scan
- Used for:
  - Assessing acetabular version
  - 3D preoperative planning
- Not routine due to radiation exposure

### MRI and MR Arthrogram
- MRI evaluates:
  - Labral tears
  - Chondral injury
  - Synovitis
- MR arthrogram:
  - Better labral delineation
  - Increasingly replaced by high-quality 3T MRI
Labral cyst indicates chronic labral tear.

### Diagnostic Injection
- Intra-articular local anesthetic ± steroid

<!-- page 5 -->

- Symptom relief confirms intra-articular source
- Important before surgical decision-making

Management Algorithm
### Non Operative (First Line)
- Activity modification
- Physiotherapy (muscle stretching)
- NSAIDs
Up to 50 percent improve without surgery.

### Operative Options
- Hip arthroscopy (preferred)
- Open surgical dislocation
- Periacetabular osteotomy (for dysplasia or severe pincer)

Hip Arthroscopy in Pediatrics
Indications
- Symptomatic FAI
- Labral tears
- Loose bodies
- Post-traumatic impingement
Advantages
- Minimally invasive
- Lower complication rate
- Excellent visualization

### Arthroscopic Principles
- Use of traction table
- Fluoroscopic guidance
- 70° arthroscope
- Interportal capsulotomy
- Treat cause first (osteochondroplasty)
- Then repair labrum

<!-- page 6 -->

Osteochondroplasty
- Reshaping femoral head–neck junction
- In children: osteochondroplasty, not osteoplasty
- Avoid physeal injury
- Aim to restore head–neck offset

### Labral Management
- Labral repair preferred over debridement
- Anchor-based fixation
- Restoration of suction seal

### Outcomes and Hip Preservation
- Best outcomes in young, active patients
- Early intervention prevents arthritis
- Labral repair plus morphology correction is key

Faculty Quote
“FAI is not what you see on X-ray. It is what happens when abnormal anatomy is
overloaded over time.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- FAI is a clinical syndrome, not a radiological diagnosis
- Cam impingement is most common in adolescents
- Dunn view best demonstrates cam morphology
- Alpha angle >55° suggests cam impingement
- FADIR is the most sensitive clinical test
- Muscle tightness can mimic FAI
- Labral cyst indicates chronic labral tear
- Hip arthroscopy aims at hip preservation, not arthritis treatment
