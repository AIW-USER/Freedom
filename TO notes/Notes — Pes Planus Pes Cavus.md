Pes Planus and Pes Cavus – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay
Overview
Pes planus and pes cavus represent opposite ends of foot arch abnormalities.
Understanding angles, clinical patterns, biomechanics, and management
algorithms is essential for exam preparation.
Faculty Remark: “These four angles must stay in your mind for every foot
case—Hibb’s, Meary’s, calcaneal pitch, and tibio-talar angle. They guide flat foot and
cavus foot interpretations.”

### Key Radiological Angles
- Hibb’s angle
- Meary’s angle (long axis talus to first metatarsal)
- Calcaneal pitch (increased in cavus, decreased in planus)
- Tibio-talar angle
These angles form the foundational assessment for pes planus and pes cavus.

## PES PLANUS (FLAT FOOT)
Types of Pes Planus
Congenital
- Inherited
- Generalized hyperlaxity
- Congenital vertical talus (CVT)
- Physiological flat foot
Adolescent
- Inherited
- Tarsal coalition (common)
### Adult Acquired
- Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) – most common

<!-- page 2 -->

Congenital Rigid Flat Foot / CVT
### Key Features
- Detected at birth
- Rounded medial and plantar prominence
- Abnormal talar head position
- Forefoot dorsiflexed at midtarsal joint
- Convex sole with deep dorsolateral crease
### With Growth
- Talus becomes “hourglass-shaped”
- Callosities under anterior calcaneus
- Medial border callosities
- Severe forefoot abduction
- Heel off the ground during stance
Radiology
- Long axis of talus deviates significantly (vertical talus)
- Meary’s angle markedly increased

## Flexible Pes Planus
Features
- Physiological or hyperlaxity related
- Mild to no deformity at rest
- Heel valgus and forefoot abduction during weight bearing
- Deformity corrects passively
- Symptoms appear later

Clinical Examination (Structured Approach)
Standing
- Observe alignment up to knee
- Fullness over medial ankle
- Forefoot abduction
- Too-many-toes sign
- Heel valgus that corrects on heel raise (flexible deformity)
Sitting
- Tenderness: tibialis posterior, subfibular region
- Talar head callosity

<!-- page 3 -->

- Assess subtalar movement
Supine
- Silfverskiöld test (gastrocnemius tightness)
- Forefoot varus assessment
Prone
- Subtalar joint movement and rigidity
### Tibialis Posterior Function Tests
- Single and double leg heel raise
- Resistance inversion testing
- Hopping-sign for tendon integrity
### Johnson and Strom Classification (PTTD)
- Stage 1: mild pain
- Stage 2: tendon involvement
  - 2A: heel raise present
  - 2B: heel raise absent
- Stage 3: rigid deformity
- Stage 4 (Myerson): ankle involvement

### Radiology for Flat Foot
- Weight-bearing AP and lateral foot & ankle views
- Hindfoot alignment (Salzman view)
- MRI for PTT evaluation
- CT for rigid foot / suspected tarsal coalition
### Radiographic Findings
- Talonavicular sag
- Naviculocuneiform sag
- Forefoot abduction
- Uncoverage of talus
- Meary’s angle increased

Management of Pes Planus
### General Principles
- Only symptomatic patients require surgery
- Avoid cosmetic surgery demands

<!-- page 4 -->

### Age Based
- 0–3 years: supervised neglect
- 3–12 years: orthotics + physiotherapy
- Adolescents/adults: clinical and radiological guided management
### Conservative Management
- Tibialis posterior strengthening
- Intrinsic foot muscle strengthening
- Gastrocnemius stretching
- Medial arch support / UCBL orthosis
### Management in PTTD Stage 2
- Stage 2A: Tibialis posterior debridement (open or tendoscopic)
- Stage 2B: Combined reconstructive procedures:
  - Gastrocnemius recession
  - Medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy
  - Lateral column lengthening
  - FDL transfer to navicular
  - (Rare) cuneiform plantarflexion osteotomy
### Extra-Osseous Talotarsal Stabilization (EOTTS)
- Small sinus tarsi stent
- Corrects talar plantarflexion and inversion
- Useful in select adolescent flexible flat feet
Rigid Flat Foot (Often due to tarsal coalition)
- If symptomatic: coalition resection + fat/fascial interposition
- If bony coalition or arthritis: double or triple arthrodesis

### Exam Pearls – Pes Planus
- Talonavicular uncoverage = classic radiographic sign
- Too-many-toes sign = hindfoot valgus + forefoot abduction
- Meary’s angle increases in flat foot
- Stage 2B PTTD → cannot do single leg heel raise
- Sinus tarsi stents are for selected adolescent flexible planus

PES CAVUS
Overview
Pes cavus is a high arch foot that does not flatten during weight bearing. Most cases
are neurologic and progressive.
Faculty Remark: “Half of cavus feet are due to CMT. Treat cavus like a
neuromuscular disorder unless proven otherwise.”

### Key Features
- High arch maintained on stance
- Increased calcaneal pitch (>25–30 degrees)
- Plantarflexed medial forefoot
- Forefoot adduction
- Hindfoot varus

Etiology
Neuromuscular (Most common)
- Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT)
- Muscular dystrophy
- Poliomyelitis
- Spinal dysraphism
- Syringomyelia
Congenital
- Idiopathic cavus
- Residual clubfoot
- Arthrogryposis
Traumatic
- Residual compartment syndrome
- Crush injuries
- Malunited midfoot fractures

### Biomechanics of Cavus Foot
- Talar head moves superiorly
- Subtalar joint becomes vertical and rigid
- Hindfoot locked in inversion

<!-- page 6 -->

- Load shifts to lateral border and forefoot
- Toe clawing due to intrinsic weakness + extrinsic overpowering

Clinical Examination
### Key Signs
- Lateral column pain
- Hindfoot varus
- Peek-a-boo sign (heel pad visible from front)
- Claw toes
- Gait abnormalities (toe-first strike, instability)
- Assess subtalar and midfoot flexibility
Coleman Block Test
Determines the driver of deformity: - Positive test: hindfoot varus corrects →
deformity is forefoot-driven - Negative test: hindfoot varus persists → hindfoot-driven
(rigid)
This test guides surgical planning.

Radiology
- AP and lateral foot views
- Overcoverage of talus by navicular
- High calcaneal pitch
- Meary’s angle positive
- Double-dome sign (sinus tarsi)

Management of Pes Cavus
### Stepwise Algorithm
1. Flexible Cavus (Coleman Positive)
### Address Forefoot First
- Dorsiflexion osteotomy of 1st metatarsal ± lesser rays
- Peroneus longus to brevis transfer
- Plantar fascia release if needed
### Then Address Hindfoot (If Needed)
- Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy

<!-- page 7 -->

### Address Equinus (If Present)
- Gastrocnemius recession or Achilles lengthening
### Address Toe Deformities
- Jones procedure
- EHL transfer

2. Rigid Cavus (Coleman Negative)
### If Severe Arthritis
- Subtalar, double or triple arthrodesis
### If No Arthritis
- Lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy
- Tendon balancing procedures
- Plantar fascia release
- Midfoot osteotomies if needed

### Exam Pearls – Pes Cavus
- CMT is the most common cause
- Peek-a-boo sign indicates hindfoot varus
- Coleman test decides whether to start with forefoot or hindfoot
- Calcaneal pitch >25–30 degrees
- Toe clawing from intrinsic weakness

Final Faculty Insight: “Pes planus and cavus look opposite, but both demand a
structured evaluation. Get the angles right, the staging right, and management falls into
place.”
