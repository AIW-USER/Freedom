MRI of the Wrist – Dr. Nafisa Shakir Batta (Jun 2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- How a wrist MRI is done and planned, the normal anatomy in each plane, then common cases: scaphoid fracture and AVN, DISI, SNAC and SLAC wrist, scapholunate tear, TFCC tear, Kienbock disease, intersection syndromes, de Quervain tenosynovitis and carpal tunnel syndrome.
- The wrist needs high-resolution, small field-of-view imaging in a dedicated coil; small TFCC or ligament tears are invisible on a large field of view.
- The coronal fat-suppressed (PDFS) image is the single most useful sequence.

Technique
- Positioning: the wrist must be at the magnet isocentre, so the patient lies prone in the "superman" position with the hand overhead in a dedicated wrist coil (a head coil can substitute; never a body coil). Images are flipped for display. Mildly claustrophobic patients usually manage.
- Flexible coils allow the distal forearm (e.g. proximal intersection syndrome); request a specific finger or thumb MRI for digit problems.
- Planning: axials perpendicular to the forearm (parallel to the radiocarpal joint); coronals along the axis of the RADIUS (the ulna can sit dorsally); sagittals perpendicular to the coronals. Sagittals show the most pulsation artefact (in line with the radial and ulnar arteries).
- Slices 3 mm with a 0.3–0.5 mm gap (2.5 mm on 3 T).

| Sequence | Use |
|---|---|
| Coronal PDFS (first, most important) | Acute pathology: oedema, fluid, TFCC, intrinsic ligaments, alignment |
| Coronal GRE | Trabeculae, bone edges, cartilage |
| Coronal T1 | Marrow fat (AVN, sclerosis), small chip fractures hidden by oedema, lipoma, accessory muscles |
| Axial T1/T2 + axial fat-suppressed | Tendons, median and ulnar nerves, carpal tunnel |
| Sagittal fat-suppressed | Alignment and axes (radius, lunate, capitate, 3rd metacarpal collinear) |
| T2 cartilage mapping (3 T) | Early cartilage dehydration, e.g. early lunatomalacia |

Normal Anatomy
Coronal
- Distal radius, sigmoid notch, distal ulna (subosseous cysts at the fovea are common); scaphoid (distal pole, waist, proximal pole), lunate (may have a hamate facet), triquetrum.
- Mid-section shows six carpal bones; pisiform and trapezium appear only in volar sections (pisiform with the hook of hamate at the carpal tunnel).
- Gilula arcs, cartilage thickness (slight radial thinning is normal), no fissures or lunate cysts.
- Ulnar variance: negative variance predisposes to radiolunate impaction (and Kienbock); positive variance to ulnolunate impaction, TFCC tear and lunate chondromalacia. Measure on X-ray, not MRI (it changes from slice to slice).
- Radial styloid: high inclination or elongation (e.g. after a Colles fracture) causes radioscaphoid arthritis. Check the ulnar styloid and the prestyloid recess.
- Intrinsic ligaments: scapholunate (dorsal, interosseous and volar parts; may look striated) and lunotriquetral; also check the STT joint.
- Extrinsic ligaments wrap the dorsal and volar surfaces; mucoid degeneration and ganglia are common (computer users, gymnasts with radiocarpal impaction).

TFCC components
- Articular disc; dorsal and volar radioulnar ligaments; foveal and styloid slips with the ligamentum subcruentum between them (a little heterogeneous synovial signal here is NORMAL, not a sprain).
- Slips to the lunate and triquetrum (ulnolunate, ulnotriquetral, ulnocapitate); prestyloid recess; ulnar meniscus homologue; ulnar collateral ligament; ECU in its subsheath.
- Thin radial cartilage beside the disc is not a tear.

- Scapholunate and lunotriquetral ligaments are also well seen on axials.

Sagittal
- Radius, lunate, capitate and 3rd metacarpal collinear in a well-positioned wrist.
- The scapholunate angle is easier on a lateral X-ray (bones superimposed) than on MRI.
- Carpal boss: bony hypertrophy at the 3rd CMC (base of 3rd metacarpal and capitate), e.g. in a boxer.

Cases
Scaphoid fracture and AVN
- Subtle on AP and oblique views; the ulnar deviation (scaphoid) view shows the cortical breach.
- A fracture a few weeks old with ongoing pain: CT for union, MRI for viability.
- Report: Herbert classification, level (distal, mid or proximal waist), distraction, step, comminution, lunate subluxation.
- Blood supply is retrograde (branches of the radial artery enter distally), so the proximal pole is at risk of AVN.
- Signs of AVN: proximal pole denser than distal on X-ray or CT; complete loss of marrow fat (dark) on T1. Oedema in the distal fragment can look dark on T1 too, so compare with fat- suppressed images.
- Perfusion (dynamic contrast) MRI: measures blood flow in regions of interest; zero enhancement in the proximal pole = necrosis. Matters because a dead proximal pole should not simply be fixed; a viable one can be freshened and fixed with a compression screw.

Carpal instability and collapse
- DISI (dorsal intercalated segment instability): the scaphoid flexes and the lunate tilts dorsally, either after a scaphoid fracture (proximal pole still tied to the lunate) or after a scapholunate ligament tear with a normal scaphoid. DISI is more common than VISI.
- Scapholunate angle: normal 30–60°.
- Scapholunate ligament tear: frayed or absent dorsal, interosseous and volar parts on coronal and axial images; widened gap (Terry Thomas sign on X-ray).
- SNAC wrist: long-standing scaphoid nonunion (often with proximal pole AVN), DISI, proximal migration of the capitate, radiocarpal and intercarpal arthritis.
- SLAC wrist: scapholunate advanced collapse: the same collapse and arthritis after a scapholunate ligament tear.

TFCC tear
- Clues: DRUJ effusion, fluid in the prestyloid recess, a "floppy" disc; look on coronal images (axials are poor for TFCC).
- Case shown: perforation of the articular disc, a traumatic Palmer 1A tear; foveal and styloid slips intact.
- Small tears are missed on low-resolution 1.5 T scans; 3 T or MR arthrography helps when there is little fluid.

Kienbock disease
- Lunate AVN; associated with negative ulnar variance (radiolunate abutment) in active patients.
- Small, sclerotic lunate; T1 shows loss of marrow fat. In children (incompletely ossified carpus looks unusual normally) it may revascularise.

Carpal tunnel syndrome
- Median nerve thickened with loss of the fascicular pattern proximal to the retinaculum, narrowed under it, normal distally; causes include a thick or scarred retinaculum and fatty hypertrophy in the tunnel.
- Compare the nerve area proximally and at the compression; no absolute cut-off.
- Use ULTRASOUND, not MRI, for carpal tunnel syndrome: higher resolution, real time; MRI may be normal in early cases. Ultrasound also shows thenar wasting.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Wrist MRI: prone superman position, dedicated coil, coronals along the radius.
- Coronal PDFS shows TFCC, SL and LT ligaments in one view.
- Normal: heterogeneous signal at the ligamentum subcruentum; multiple APL slips; thin radial cartilage next to the TFCC.
- Negative ulnar variance: radiolunate impaction, Kienbock; positive: ulnolunate impaction, TFCC tear.
- Proximal pole scaphoid AVN: dark on T1; perfusion MRI confirms.
- SNAC = scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse; SLAC = scapholunate advanced collapse.
- Ultrasound, not MRI, for carpal tunnel syndrome.

### Take-Home Summary
- Read coronal first (bones, alignment, TFCC, intrinsic ligaments, marrow), then axial (tendons, nerves, tunnel), then sagittal (axes).

- Insist on high-resolution dedicated wrist imaging; give the clinical question (e.g. viability of the proximal pole) so the right sequences are done.
