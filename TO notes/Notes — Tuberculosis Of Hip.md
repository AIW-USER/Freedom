Tuberculosis of Hip – Dr. Daivik T. Shetty
Overview / Key Concepts
Tuberculosis of the hip is the second most common osteoarticular TB after spinal TB. It
constitutes about 15 percent of all osteoarticular TB cases. Most patients are in the first
three decades of life. TB hip is considered a diagnostic dilemma because its various
stages mimic multiple hip pathologies.
Key points: - Always secondary to pulmonary or lymph node TB. - Spread occurs
through hematogenous route or direct extension. - Early diagnosis is difficult because
initial symptoms overlap with common hip disorders.

Etiopathogenesis
### Primary Focus and Spread
- Primary focus: lungs, lymph nodes or viscera.
- Spread to bone via hematogenous route.
- Osteoarticular TB occurs when bacilli reach bone or synovium.
Initial Focus Areas (based on page 7 diagram)
1. Acetabular roof: most common. Joint involvement is delayed.
2. Epiphysis: rapid spread to joint as it is intracapsular.
3. Metaphysis (Babcock’s triangle): common in children.
4. Greater trochanter: spreads through trochanteric bursa.

### Pathogenesis (page 8)
- Synovial hypertrophy and congestion.
- Necrosis of subchondral bone, sequestrum formation.
- Kissing lesions from opposing articular surfaces.
- Cold abscess forms and may perforate the capsule.

Clinical Features
### Systemic Symptoms
- Fever, evening rise of temperature.
- Loss of weight and appetite.
- Cough.
- Night sweats.

<!-- page 2 -->

### Early Local Features (page 10)
- Insidious onset hip pain, often referred to medial knee or thigh.
- Tractioning behavior of affected limb for pain relief.
- Stiffness progressing to limping.
- Night cries: child wakes up due to pain when muscle spasm relaxes in sleep.
- Fullness around hip due to cold abscess.
### Limping Across Stages (page 12)
- Early stage: stiffness, flexion deformity.
- Progression: pain due to grating of exposed bone.
- Advanced stage: true shortening from destruction.
### Late Features (page 13)
- Limb length discrepancy.
- Deformity, stiffness.
- Pathological dislocation.
- Cold abscess or sinus.
### Wandering Acetabulum (page 14)
- Advanced destruction of head and acetabulum.
- Constant muscle pull displaces head proximally.
### Healing (page 15)
- If untreated, fibrosis results in fibrous ankylosis in deformed position.

Cold Abscess Sites (page 17)
Pus collects in joint and exits through weak inferior capsule area. Possible locations: -
Intrapelvic abscess. - Femoral triangle. - Medial, lateral or posterior thigh. - Ischiorectal
fossa.

### Stages of TB Hip
1. Stage of Synovitis (page 19)
- Increased joint fluid.
- Hip assumes FABER position (flexion, abduction, external rotation).
- Apparent lengthening.
- Terminal movement restriction.
2. Stage of Early Arthritis (page 21)
- Articular cartilage involvement.

<!-- page 3 -->

- Spasm of strong muscles: flexors and adductors.
- FADIR position (flexion, adduction, internal rotation).
- Apparent shortening.
3. Stage of Advanced Arthritis (page 22)
- Cartilage destroyed.
- True shortening.
- FADIR position, exaggerated deformity.
- Reduced joint space.
4. Advanced Arthritis with Subluxation / Dislocation (page 23)
- Gross shortening.
- Wandering acetabulum.
- Jog of movements if fibrous ankylosis or exaggerated movement in dislocation.

Diagnostic Evaluation
### X-ray Findings (page 27)
- Early: haziness, soft tissue swelling.
- Later: osteopenia, peripheral erosions.
- Phemister triad:
  - Juxta-articular osteoporosis.
  - Peripheral erosion.
  - Reduced joint space.
### MRI (page 28)
- Highly sensitive for early diagnosis.
- Shows bone marrow edema and effusion.
### Tissue Biopsy (page 29)
- Histopathology.
- AFB staining.
- PCR and culture.
### Mantoux Test (page 30)
- High false positives in endemic areas.
### AFB Positivity (page 31)
- Synovial fluid <10 percent.

<!-- page 4 -->

- Synovial tissue <20 percent.
Important Principle (page 33)
Failure of noninvasive diagnosis mandates tissue diagnosis.

Radiological Classification (Shanmugasundaram)
### Types (pages 34 to 41)
1. Normal type – only synovial disease.
2. Wandering acetabulum – proximal migration of femoral head.
3. Dislocating type – partial dislocation with muscle spasm.
4. Perthes type – resembles Perthes disease, head destruction.
5. Protrusio acetabuli – medial wall weakness.
6. Atrophic type – minimal infection, small head.
7. Mortar and pestle – saucer shaped acetabulum and small head.
Prognosis: - Normal: 92 percent recovery. - Perthes: 80 percent. - Dislocating: 50
percent. - Wandering acetabulum: 29 percent.

Management
Traditional Treatment (page 45)
Hip spica for 1.5 years led to frame knee, now obsolete.

### Modern Management Options (page 46)
- Anti-TB therapy.
- Traction.
- Gradual mobilization.
- Surgical intervention based on stage.
Traction (page 47)
Benefits: - Relieves muscle spasm. - Corrects deformity. - Maintains joint space. -
Separates joint surfaces.

Stage wise Treatment
### Stage 1: Synovitis (page 48)
- ATT.
- Traction and rest.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Mobilization after pain reduces.
### Stage 2: Early Arthritis (page 49 and 50)
- ATT and traction.
- Synovectomy and joint debridement.
- Enhances delivery of ATT due to improved vascularity.
Complications: - AVN of femoral head. - Slipped capital femoral epiphysis. - Iatrogenic
fractures.

### Stage 3: Advanced Arthritis (page 52)
- Arthrolysis to improve ROM.
- Subtotal synovectomy preserving posterior capsule.
- Postoperative traction and rehabilitation.
Stage 4: Advanced Arthritis with Subluxation / Dislocation (page 53)
Options: - Excision arthroplasty. - Arthrodesis. - Hip replacement.
Sandhu et al: ATT and traction can still achieve healing in children.

Surgical Procedures
Excision Arthroplasty (Girdlestone) (page 54)
Pros: - Allows squatting and cross leg sitting. - One time surgery. Cons: - Trendelenburg
gait persists. - 2 to 3 cm shortening. - Walking stick needed outdoors.

Arthrodesis (pages 56 to 58)
Indicated when a stable painless hip is required.
Ideal fusion position: - Flexion: 30 degrees in adults, 1 degree per year in children. -
External rotation: 5 to 10 degrees. - Neutral abduction adduction.
Pros: - Stable hip, good sitting ability. - One time operation.
Cons: - Secondary arthritis of spine, opposite hip, ipsilateral knee. - No floor activities
possible.

Extra articular Fusions (pages 59 to 63)
- Ischiofemoral osteotomy (Brittain).
- Abbott Lucas two stage fusion.
### Total Hip Arthroplasty (page 65 and 66)
- Safe in healed TB.
- Growing evidence supports THA in active TB with ATT cover.

<!-- page 6 -->

Perioperative ATT examples: - 3 months pre op and 15 months post op. - 2 weeks pre
op and 12 months post op.

Faculty Quote
“Traction and timely ATT heal most cases of TB hip, but the key is recognizing the stage
and intervening before deformity becomes irreversible.” — Dr. Daivik T. Shetty

### Exam Pearls
- Acetabular roof is the most common initial focus.
- Night cries occur due to relaxation of muscle spasm during sleep.
- FABER position is seen in synovitis stage.
- FADIR position is characteristic of early and advanced arthritis.
- Phemister triad is classical for osteoarticular TB.
- Wandering acetabulum indicates advanced destruction.
- First movement lost in TB hip: extension.
- Excision arthroplasty allows floor sitting; arthrodesis offers stability but restricts activities.
- AFB positivity is low, making biopsy and PCR important.
- THA in active TB is now accepted with appropriate ATT cover.
