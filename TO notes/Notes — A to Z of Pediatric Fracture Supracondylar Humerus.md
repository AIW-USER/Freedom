A to Z of Pediatric Supracondylar Humerus Fracture
– Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Supracondylar humerus fracture (SCH) is the most common pediatric elbow fracture,
accounting for nearly 60% of elbow injuries in children. It typically occurs between
4–10 years and carries significant risk of neurovascular compromise and
compartment syndrome.
Understanding fracture pattern, reduction principles, pin configuration, and complication
prevention is critical for exams and emergency management.

### Epidemiology & Etiology
- Age group: 4–10 years
- Mechanism: FOOSH (fall on outstretched hand)
- Types:
  - Extension type: ≈97%
  - Flexion type: 3–4%

### Anatomy and Biomechanics
- Fracture occurs just proximal to olecranon fossa (thinnest part)
- Direction of displacement determines nerve and vascular injury

| Displacement | Structure at Risk |
|---|---|
| Posterolateral | Median nerve, AIN, brachial artery |
| Posteromedial | Radial nerve |
| Flexion type | Ulnar nerve |

Clinical Examination
### General Examination
- Look for associated injuries
- Distal radius fracture + SCH = Floating elbow (high compartment risk)
### Local Examination
- Swelling, tenderness, deformity
- Skin puckering (brachialis sign)

Neurovascular Examination
### Nerve Assessment
- Median nerve / AIN: OK sign
- Radial nerve: Thumbs up, finger extension
- Ulnar nerve: Finger abduction, fist
### Vascular Assessment
- Palpable pulse
- SpO₂ monitoring
- Best indicator: Hand perfusion
Pale, pulseless hand = absolute surgical emergency
Radiological Evaluation
### Mandatory Views
- AP and lateral elbow
- Entire humerus and forearm
### Fat Pad Sign
- Posterior fat pad = occult fracture

Key Radiological Lines and Angles
### AP View
- Baumann Angle: 64–82°
  - Every 5° change = 2° change in carrying angle
- Metadiaphyseal angle
- Humeroulnar (carrying) angle
### Lateral View
- Anterior humeral line
  - Passes through middle third of capitellum (>4 yrs)
- Shaft-condylar angle (≈40°)
- Teardrop sign
- Crescent sign (cubitus varus)

<!-- page 3 -->

Classification
### Gartland Classification

| Type | Description | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| I | Undisplaced | Cast |
| II A | Posterior hinge intact, no rotation | Cast / CRPP |
| II B | Posterior hinge intact with rotation | CRPP |
| III | Completely displaced | CRPP |

### Leitch Type IV
  - Unstable in flexion and extension
  - Global instability
### De Boeck
  - Medial column comminution
  - High risk of cubitus varus

Reduction Principles
### Closed Reduction Steps
1. Longitudinal traction
2. Correct varus–valgus
3. Flexion maneuver with olecranon pressure
4. Pronation or supination based on periosteal hinge

| Displacement | Reduction Maneuver |
|---|---|
| Posteromedial | Pronation |
| Posterolateral | Supination |

Special Reduction Techniques
### Brachialis Sign Positive Fracture
  - Brachialis milking technique
  - Tansen joystick technique (Nepal)

<!-- page 4 -->

Pin Configuration
Lateral Pinning (Preferred)
Rules for stable lateral pinning: - At least 2 cortices purchase - Pin separation ≥
one-third humeral width - Two 2 mm pins better than three thin pins

### Indications for Medial Pin
- Medial oblique fractures
- Intercondylar extension
- High supracondylar fractures
- Internal rotation instability
Medial pin risks ulnar nerve injury
Open Reduction
Indications
- Failed closed reduction
- Open fracture
- Vascular compromise not improving
### Preferred Approaches
- Anterior approach (most recommended)
- Lateral approach
Posterior approach avoided
Pink Pulseless Hand
### Management Algorithm
- Emergency CRPP
- Assess perfusion, Doppler, nerve status

| Hand Status | Management |
|---|---|
| Pale, non-perfused | Immediate exploration |
| Pink, perfused, no deficit | Close observation |
| Pink, poor Doppler or nerve palsy | Exploration |

Prophylactic fasciotomy if prolonged ischemia

⏱ Timing of Surgery
### Emergency Surgery
- Open fracture
- Skin tenting
- Neurovascular compromise
- Floating elbow
- Compartment syndrome
Otherwise
- Splint and operate next morning

Complications
Early
- Pin tract infection (≈4%)
- Pin migration
- Ulnar nerve palsy
- Compartment syndrome
Intermediate
- Elbow stiffness
- Myositis ossificans
Late
- Cubitus varus
- Cubitus valgus
- Volkmann ischemic contracture

Cubitus Varus (Gunstock Deformity)
Nature
- Three-plane deformity:
  - Coronal: Varus
  - Sagittal: Extension
  - Axial: Internal rotation

Assessment of Cubitus Varus
Clinical
- Carrying angle comparison

<!-- page 6 -->

### Rotational Tests
- Yamamoto test
- Bhende test
Radiology
- Increased Baumann angle
- Crescent sign
- Fishtail deformity (progressive)

### Does Cubitus Varus Remodel?
- Sagittal deformity may remodel
- Varus does NOT remodel

### Indications for Surgery (Cubitus Varus)
- Cosmetic deformity
- Functional issues
- PLRI
- Ulnar nerve instability
- Recurrent lateral condyle fractures

### Surgical Options for Cubitus Varus
- Simple closing wedge osteotomy
- Step cut osteotomy
- Dome osteotomy
- Oblique lateral closing wedge osteotomy (current gold standard)

Oblique Lateral Closing Wedge Osteotomy
Key Features: - Apex near original fracture site - Equal limb osteotomy - No lateral
prominence - Fixed with K-wires or plate

TRASH Lesions
The Radiographic Appearance Seemed Harmless
Includes: - Unossified medial condyle fracture - Transphyseal distal humerus fracture -
Entrapped medial epicondyle - Osteochondral fracture dislocation - Radial head
compression fracture - Monteggia variants

Faculty Quote
“The commonest mistake in supracondylar fractures is not the reduction, but missing
instability and accepting varus.” – Dr. Shalin Shah

### Exam Pearls
- Extension type SCH is most common
- Posterior fat pad = occult fracture
- Baumann angle is key intraoperative guide
- Posterolateral displacement injures median nerve
- Pink pulseless hand needs urgent reduction
- Cubitus varus is not just cosmetic
- Oblique lateral closing wedge osteotomy is current standard
- Always suspect TRASH lesions in innocent-looking X-rays
