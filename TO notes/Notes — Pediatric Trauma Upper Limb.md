Pediatric Trauma Upper Limb – Dr. Sanjay Arora
Overview / Key Concepts
Pediatric upper limb trauma differs significantly from adult injuries due to the presence
of growth plates, thick periosteum, and high remodeling potential. Correct
understanding of injury patterns, acceptable deformity, and indications for
intervention is essential to prevent long-term deformity and functional loss.
This lecture covers: - Principles of pediatric fractures - Forearm injuries - Elbow
fractures - Proximal humerus and clavicle fractures

### Anatomy of the Growing Bone
- Epiphysis: End of bone, forms joint
- Physis (Growth plate): Weakest area
- Metaphysis: Flared region near physis
- Diaphysis: Shaft
- Periosteum: Thick, biologically active in children
Key Concept: - Ligaments and tendons are stronger than bone in children - Bone fails
before soft tissue

### Injury Patterns in Growing Bone
- Bones tend to bow rather than break
- Unique fracture patterns:
  - Torus (Buckle) fracture: Compression injury
  - Greenstick fracture: One cortex breaks, other bends
  - Plastic deformation: Microscopic osteon realignment without cortical break
Periosteum often remains intact, providing: - Intrinsic stability - Faster healing

### Physeal Injuries
- ~20% of pediatric skeletal injuries
- May cause growth disturbance
- Injury near physis may stimulate overgrowth

### Salter–Harris Classification

| Type | Description | Growth Risk |
|---|---|---|
| I | Through physis | Low |
| II | Physis + metaphysis (Thurston–Holland) | Moderate |
| III | Physis + epiphysis | High |
| IV | Epiphysis + physis + metaphysis | High |

V Crush injury to physis Very high
- Most common: Type II, followed by I, III, IV, V
- Growth disturbance can occur with any type

### Principles of Pediatric Fracture Healing
- Faster healing than adults
- Shorter immobilization needed
- Malalignment consolidates early
- Remodeling depends on:
  - Younger age
  - Proximity to physis
  - Angulation in plane of joint movement
Rotational deformities do not remodel
Forearm Fractures (Most Common Site)
Patterns
- Distal radius physeal injuries (SH I, II)
- Metaphyseal fractures
- Both-bone forearm fractures
- Midshaft fractures

### Distal Radius Physeal Injuries
- Usually SH I or II
- Commonly dorsally displaced (Colles type)
- Normal volar tilt: 0–11°
### Reduction Indications
- Inability to correct dorsal angulation

<!-- page 3 -->

- 10% displacement
- Poor cast molding

Metaphyseal Forearm Fractures
### Buckle Fractures
- Opposite cortex intact
- Stable injuries
- Treatment:
  - Splint or cast
  - Immobilization: 2–3 weeks
### Greenstick Fractures
- One cortex broken, other bent
- Unstable, tend to recur
- Require reduction and proper molding

### Acceptable Angulation – Distal Forearm

| Age Group | Acceptable Angulation |
|---|---|
| Infants | <30° |
| Children | <15° |
| Peripubertal | Needs 2–3 years growth to remodel |

Midshaft Radius–Ulna Fractures
### Reduction Indications
- Any rotational deformity
- Radial or ulnar angulation
### Acceptable Angulation

| Age | Angulation |
|---|---|
| Infants | <25° |
| Children | <10° |

- Acceptable displacement in young children: up to 90%

### Casting Principles
- Above-elbow cast for all forearm reductions

<!-- page 4 -->

- Three-point molding essential
- Thick periosteum aids stability
Bayonet apposition may be accepted in young children with adequate growth
remaining

### Plastic Deformation
- Does not remodel
- Must be reduced if:
  - Visible deformity
  - Restricted pronation–supination

Monteggia Fracture–Dislocation
Definition
- Proximal ulna fracture with radial head dislocation
### Bado Classification

| Type | Radial Head Displacement |
|---|---|
| I | Anterior |
| II | Posterior |
| III | Lateral |
| IV | Both bone fractures + radial head dislocation |

### Key Principle
- Restore ulnar length to reduce radial head

Elbow Fractures
### Ossification Centers (CRITOE)
- Capitellum
- Radial head
- Internal (medial) epicondyle
- Trochlea
- Olecranon
- External (lateral) epicondyle

<!-- page 5 -->

### Radiological Lines
- Anterior humeral line: Should pass through middle third of capitellum
- Radiocapitellar line: Should bisect capitellum in all views

Supracondylar Humerus Fractures
### Gartland Classification
- Type I: Undisplaced
- Type II: Angulated
- Type III: Completely displaced
- Type IV: Multidirectional instability (intraoperative diagnosis)
Complications
- Nerve injury (AIN most common)
- Brachial artery injury
- Compartment syndrome
- Cubitus varus
- Volkmann ischemic contracture

### Lateral Condyle Fractures
- 15% of pediatric elbow fractures
- Usually SH Type IV
- High risk of nonunion and AVN
Management
- <2 mm displacement: Cast
- 2–4 mm: CRPP
- 4 mm or rotated fragment: ORIF
Complications
- Nonunion
- Cubitus valgus
- Tardy ulnar nerve palsy
- Fish-tail deformity

### Medial Epicondyle Fractures
- Common in adolescents
- Often associated with elbow dislocation

<!-- page 6 -->

Management
- <4 mm displacement: Immobilization
- 5 mm displacement: Fixation
### Absolute Indications for Surgery
- Intra-articular incarceration
- Ulnar nerve palsy
- Articular involvement

Proximal Radius Fractures
### Management by Angulation

| Angulation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| <15° | Posterior slab |
| 15–30° | Early orthopedic referral |
| >30° | Reduction required |

Preferred fixation: Elastic intramedullary nail (Metaizeau technique)

### Proximal Humerus Fractures
- Very high remodeling potential
### Acceptable Deformity

| Age | Angulation / Displacement |
|---|---|
| 1–5 yrs | 70°, 100% |
| 5–12 yrs | 50°, 50% |
| >12 yrs | 30° |

Management
- Sling or sugar-tong splint

### Clavicle Fractures
- 10–15% of pediatric fractures
- 90% midshaft
Management
- Simple sling preferred
- Figure-of-eight brace offers no advantage

<!-- page 7 -->

Posteriorly displaced proximal clavicle fractures risk tracheoesophageal
compression

Faculty Quote
“In children, bone injury patterns are unique. Understanding the physis prevents lifelong
deformity.” – Dr. Sanjay Arora

### Exam Pearls
- Buckle fractures are stable, greenstick fractures are unstable
- Rotational deformities never remodel
- SH Type II is the most common physeal injury
- Always assess radial head alignment in forearm fractures
- Anterior humeral line is key for supracondylar fractures
- Lateral condyle fractures have high nonunion risk
- Proximal humerus fractures remodel remarkably well
- Simple sling is sufficient for most clavicle fractures
