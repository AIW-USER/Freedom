Tibial Bowing Part 1 (Congenital Pseudoarthrosis
and Related Conditions) – Dr. Sanjay Arora
Overview / Key Concepts
Bowing of the leg in children may be congenital or developmental. Among these,
anterolateral tibial bowing is the most important because it is associated with
congenital pseudoarthrosis of tibia (CPT), a limb threatening condition with an
unpredictable natural history. In contrast, posteromedial tibial bowing is usually
benign, while anteromedial bowing is often linked to fibular deficiency.
This lecture focuses primarily on anterolateral bowing and congenital
pseudoarthrosis of tibia, with an overview of posteromedial bowing.

### Types of Tibial Bowing
1. Anterolateral Bowing
- Most common pathological bowing
- Associated with congenital pseudoarthrosis
- Prognosis depends on:
  - Presence or absence of fracture
  - Age at first fracture
- Early onset fracture (<4 years) has poorer prognosis
- Common associations:
  - Neurofibromatosis type 1
  - Fibrous dysplasia
2. Posteromedial Bowing
- Usually benign
- Obvious at birth
- Gradually remodels with growth
- Main long term issue is limb length discrepancy
3. Anterior or Anteromedial Bowing
- Usually associated with congenital fibular deficiency

Pathology of Congenital Pseudoarthrosis of Tibia
Etiopathogenesis
- Exact cause unclear

<!-- page 2 -->

- Disease is primarily periosteal in origin
- Hyperplasia of fibroblasts forms dense fibrous tissue
- Fibromatosis lies between bone ends causing:
  - Compression
  - Osteolysis
  - Persistence of pseudoarthrosis
### Pathobiological Considerations
- Fibrous hamartoma replaces normal periosteum
- Leads to osteolysis and vascular constriction
- Bone remains viable despite atrophy
- Medullary canal often obliterated by sclerosis
- Osteocytes produce low levels of BMP
- Increased osteoclast activity
### Pathomechanical Factors
- Diaphyseal anterolateral angular deformity
- Proximal migration of fibula
- Small cross sectional area at CPT site increases fracture risk

Classification Systems
Multiple classifications exist, but Boyd and Crawford are most commonly used.

### Boyd Classification
- Type I
  - Pseudoarthrosis with anterior bowing
  - Defect present at birth
  - Other congenital deformities may coexist
- Type II (Most common)
  - Anterior bowing with hourglass constriction
  - Spontaneous fracture or after minor trauma
  - Known as high risk tibia
  - Usually presents after 2 years
  - Strongly associated with NF1
- Type III
  - Pseudoarthrosis within a congenital cyst
  - Fracture recurrence less common
- Type IV
  - Originates in sclerotic bone segment
  - Medullary canal partially or completely obliterated
  - Stress fracture develops gradually

<!-- page 3 -->

- Prognosis relatively good
- Type V
  - Associated with dysplastic fibula
- Type VI
  - Intraosseous neurofibroma or schwannoma
  - Extremely rare
### Crawford Classification
- Type I: Non dysplastic anterolateral bowing with medullary sclerosis
- Type IIa: Failure of tubularization
- Type IIb: Cystic changes
- Type IIc: Frank pseudoarthrosis
### Paley Classification
- Type I: Atrophic bone ends, mobile pseudoarthrosis, no prior surgery
- Type II: Atrophic bone ends, mobile pseudoarthrosis, previous surgery
- Type III: Broad bone ends, stiff pseudoarthrosis, with or without prior surgery

### Clinical Features
- Anterolateral bowing at junction of middle and distal third of tibia
- Usually unilateral
- Skin dimple over apex of bow
- Associated findings:
  - Fibular dysplasia
  - Ankle valgus
  - Limb length discrepancy

### Aims of Treatment in CPT
- Achieve bony union
- Prevent refracture
- Correct limb length inequality
- Correct associated growth abnormalities
- Prevent ankle valgus and secondary arthritis

### Principles and Lessons Learnt in CPT Management
- Complete excision of fibrous hamartoma back to normal fat planes
- Recanalization of medullary canal is essential
- All tissues at CPT site must be viable
- Autogenous cancellous bone graft preferred due to higher stem cell content

<!-- page 4 -->

- Rigid fixation is critical
- Intramedullary fixation reduces refracture risk
- Angular correction at CPT site is mandatory
- Periosteal grafting helps restore healthy periosteum
- Larger cross sectional area of union lowers refracture risk
- Accidental tibiofibular cross union prevents refracture

### Strategies to Achieve Union
- Liberal excision of pseudoarthrosis
- Bone grafting with intramedullary fixation
- Ilizarov external fixator
- Vascularized fibular graft or ipsilateral fibula transfer
### Intramedullary Fixation
- First line procedure
- Can be performed at any age
- Union rates around 85 percent
### Vascularized Fibular Graft
- Union rate 90–95 percent
- Refracture rate up to one third
- Lack of permanent IM fixation increases risk

### Prevention of Refracture
- Long term orthotic protection till skeletal maturity
- Retain intramedullary rod till maturity

Limb Length Discrepancy and Ankle Valgus
### Limb Length Inequality
- Minimize by achieving early union
- Limb equalization procedures when required
### Ankle Valgus Prevention
- Ensure fibular union
- IM rod crossing ankle joint reduces valgus progression

### Prophylaxis in Anterolateral Bowing
- Non resolving bowing requires fracture prevention

<!-- page 5 -->

- Clamshell orthosis providing circumferential support
- In infants before walking age, education and protection is sufficient
- Orthotic protection continued till skeletal maturity

### Cross Union Concept
- Described by Choi and later popularized by Paley
- Also called four in one osteosynthesis
### Technique Summary
- Tibia and fibula prepared proximally and distally
- Three layer grafting:
  - Periosteum
  - Cancellous bone graft
  - BMP2
- Telescoping rod fixation
- Ilizarov frame for compression and rotational stability

### Complications of CPT Treatment
- Refracture (14–60 percent)
- Progressive tibial malalignment
- Limb length discrepancy
- Ankle valgus
- Ankle stiffness and degenerative arthritis

Posteromedial Tibial Bowing
### Clinical Features
- Congenital posterior and medial bowing of tibia and fibula
- Calcaneovalgus foot at birth
- Foot may abut concave tibia
- Skin dimple over apex
- Underdeveloped calf muscles
- Decreased ankle plantarflexion
### Natural History
- Rapid correction in first year of life
- Remodeling occurs mainly via physeal reorientation
- Growth inhibition persists leading to predictable limb length discrepancy
- Average discrepancy around 3.5 cm

<!-- page 6 -->

Management
- Stretching exercises in infancy
- Serial casting for severe contractures
- Later management focuses on limb length discrepancy:
  - Contralateral epiphysiodesis
  - Limb lengthening using external fixator

Faculty Quote
“Congenital pseudoarthrosis is not a bone disease, it is a periosteal disease. Unless you
remove the pathology completely, union will not occur.” – Dr. Sanjay Arora

### Exam Pearls
- Anterolateral tibial bowing is associated with congenital pseudoarthrosis
- Prognosis depends on age of first fracture
- Boyd Type II is the most common and is called high risk tibia
- Disease pathology is periosteal, not intramedullary
- Complete excision of fibrous hamartoma is mandatory
- IM fixation is first line treatment
- Cross union prevents refracture
- Posteromedial bowing remodels but causes limb length discrepancy
