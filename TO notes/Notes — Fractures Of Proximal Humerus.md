Fractures of Proximal Humerus – Dr. Daivik T Shetty

Overview
This lecture covers anatomy, classification, mechanisms, treatment principles, and
complications of proximal humerus fractures, with a strong focus on concepts of
reduction, implants, and surgical decision-making.
Dr. Daivik’s Remark:
“Always aim to restore anatomy and balance, not just alignment. Varus is unforgiving,
valgus is well tolerated.”

Anatomy Review
### Key Bony Landmarks

| Structure | Description |
|---|---|
| Humeral Head | Covered by articular cartilage (160° arc). Radius of curvature ≈ 25 mm (range 23–29 mm). |
| Anatomical Neck | Junction between articular cartilage and normal bone. Fractures here are rare but have high risk (90–100%) of AVN. |
| Greater Tuberosity (GT) | Lateral prominence for supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor attachment. HTD (Head-to-Tuberosity Distance) = 8–10 mm. |
| Lesser Tuberosity (LT) | Medial prominence, attachment of subscapularis. |
| Intertubercular Sulcus | Between GT and LT, houses the long |
| (Bicipital Groove) | head of biceps. Acts as a landmark for retroversion (7 mm anterior to medullary axis) and a reference point for reduction. |
| Surgical Neck | Commonest fracture site (weak metaphyseal zone). |
| Deltoid Tuberosity | Lateral shaft prominence. |

### Epidemiology & Mechanism
- Bimodal distribution: Young males (high-velocity), older females (osteoporotic).

- 3rd most common non-vertebral osteoporotic fracture.
Mechanisms
1. Compressive load of glenoid on humeral head.

2. Bending forces at surgical neck.

3. Tensile forces by rotator cuff → tuberosity avulsion.

Common Associated Injuries
Distal radius, proximal femur, humeral shaft comminution, glenoid rim, or coracoid
avulsion.

Classification Systems
Codman (Original Concept)
Four main fragments: 1. Humeral Head
2. Greater Tuberosity
3. Lesser Tuberosity
4. Shaft (proximal to pectoralis major insertion)

### Neer’s Classification

| Type | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Displacement Definition | >1 cm translation or >45° angulation |
| One-part | Undisplaced (single unit) |
| Two-part | GT, LT, surgical neck, or anatomical neck fractures |
| Three-part | Surgical neck + GT or LT |
| Four-part | All four fragments displaced |
| Variants | Head-split fracture, fracture-dislocation |

### AO/OTA Classification
- 11-A (extra-articular unifocal)

- 11-B (extra-articular bifocal)

- 11-C (intra-articular)

### Clinical Presentation
- Severe shoulder pain, immobility, swelling, and ecchymosis.
- “Shoulder Terrible Triad Injury”: combination of abductor injury, brachial plexus injury, and anterior glenoid injury.
- Nerve injury: Axillary nerve (motor – deltoid, teres minor; sensory – regimental badge area).

- Vascular injury: Rare (Axillary artery kinking or circumflex artery avulsion).

Management Principles
### Non-operative Indications
- Stable, undisplaced fractures (<1 cm, <45° angulation).

- Elderly, low-demand, or medically unfit patients.

- Poor bone quality.
Non-operative Exceptions
Greater Tuberosity Fractures: | Patient | Surgical Indication | |———-|———————|
| Overhead athlete | >3 mm displacement | | Adult | >5 mm displacement |

### NEER Rehabilitation Protocol

| Phase | Duration | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | 0–3 weeks | Pendulum exercises; avoid external rotation. |
| Phase 2 | 3–9 weeks | Active-assisted elevation after radiographic healing. |
| Phase 3 | >9 weeks | Active motion + strengthening + manual therapy. |

PROFHER Trial (JAMA 2015):
No significant long-term difference between surgical and conservative management for
select patients.

### Operative Management Options
1. Closed reduction & percutaneous fixation (2- or 3-part fractures)

<!-- page 6 -->

- Contraindicated in calcar deficiency, LT fracture, metaphyseal comminution.

  - Modified Resch Technique: 2 lateral-to-medial and 1 anterior-to-posterior K-wires.
2. Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF) with PHILOS plate
  - Indicated in 2-, 3-, 4-part fractures in younger patients.

  - Position: Beach chair (beware BP misreading on dependent leg).

  - Approaches:
    - Deltopectoral (workhorse) – extensile, visualizes joint.

    - Deltoid splitting – lateral plate placement, risk of axillary nerve injury (~5–7 cm from acromion).

Torus Concept
Humeral head supported by a “three-legged stool.” Reduction must restore this triad for
mechanical balance.

### Levels of PHILOS Plate Holes

| Level | Screw Direction / Function |
|---|---|
| A | Parallel, slightly upward |
| B | Staggered, convergent into head |
| C | Upward, engaging humeral head |
| D | Combination hole (can act as calcar screw) |
| E | Divergent upward screws, critical for calcar support |
| F | First shaft screw (adjusts plate vertically) |
| G–H | LCP combination shaft screws |

### Common Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Screw penetration | Most common with PHILOS plating |
| Varus collapse | Lack of medial support or calcar screws |
| AVN | Poor reduction, anatomical neck fractures |
| Impingement / stiffness | Tuberosity malunion or improper reduction |
| Non-union | Seen in smokers, alcoholics, osteoporosis |
| Infection | Postoperative risk |

### Viva / Exam Pearls
- Neck–shaft angle = 135°, valgus tolerated better.
- Head–Tuberosity distance (HTD) = 8–10 mm.
- Grashey view = true AP (30–40° tilt).
- Hertel’s criteria – <8 mm calcar, >2 mm hinge disruption.
- PHCA supplies 64% of humeral head.
- Deltopectoral approach – extensile, visualizes head-split fractures.
- Varus collapse – prevented by medial calcar screws or fibular strut.
Final Takeaway from Dr. Daivik:
“Respect the anatomy, restore the geometry, and your fixation will stand the test of
time.”
