Talus Fracture – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay
Overview
Talus fractures are high stake injuries due to the bone’s unique anatomy, extensive
cartilage coverage, and delicate blood supply. Almost all talar fractures are
intraarticular, making anatomical reduction and stable fixation essential to prevent
AVN and arthritis.
Faculty Remark: “Every talus fracture deserves respect. Use dual approaches,
avoid compressing the medial comminution, and protect the deltoid artery at all costs.”

Pertinent Anatomy
### Key Points
- 70 percent of the talus is covered with articular cartilage.
- No muscular attachments, but multiple ligament attachments (ATFL, deltoid, spring ligament components).
- Three major arterial contributors:
  - Posterior tibial artery → deltoid artery (most important), artery of tarsal canal.
  - Anterior tibial artery → artery of tarsal sinus.
  - Peroneal artery.

### Why AVN Occurs
- The deltoid artery supplies nearly 70 percent of the talar body.
- Displaced neck or body fractures disrupt this supply.
### Ossification and Variants
- Primary center: prenatal.
- Secondary center: 8 to 11 years (posterior process).
- Variants:
  - Stieda’s process (elongated lateral tubercle).
  - Os trigonum (unfused secondary ossification center).
  - Os supratalare.

Classification
### Central Fractures
- Neck fractures.

- Body fractures.
### Peripheral Fractures
- Head.
- Lateral process.
- Posterior process.
Differentiating Neck vs Body
Depends on where the inferior fracture line exits relative to the lateral process. -
Anterior to lateral process → neck fracture. - Posterior to lateral process → body
fracture.

### Hawkins Classification (Neck Fractures)
- Type 1: undisplaced.
- Type 2: subtalar joint subluxation.
- Type 3: subtalar plus ankle dislocation.
- Type 4: pan talar dislocation (added by Canale and Kelly).

Pathomechanics
### Talus Neck Fracture (Aviator’s Fracture)
- Forced dorsiflexion causing the talar neck to impact against the anterior tibia.
### Talus Body Fracture
- Axial loading mechanism.
### Lateral Process Fractures (Snowboarder’s Fracture)
- Sudden inversion or eversion.
- Large fragments → fix.
- Small fragments → excise.
### Posterior Process Fractures
- Forced inversion in slight dorsiflexion or plantarflexion.
- Commonly misdiagnosed as ankle sprain.
- Large fragments → fix, small → excise.

Radiology and Workup
### Essential Views
- AP.
- Lateral.
- Canale and Kelly view (critical for neck visualization).

<!-- page 3 -->

- CT scan for all suspected talar fractures.
### CT Utility
- Detects medial comminution.
- Assesses subtalar and tibiotalar joint involvement.
- Guides fixation strategy.
Key Insight
Nearly all talar neck fractures show medial comminution, predisposing to varus
malunion if compression screws are applied incorrectly.

Management Principles
Timing
- Urgent reduction for dislocated talus fractures.
- Definitive fixation can wait until swelling subsides.
- Timing does not correlate with AVN development if no dislocation is present.
Surgical Approach
Dual approach is the standard of care. - Anteromedial approach: tip of medial
malleolus to first ray, protects saphenous vein. - Anterolateral approach: tip of fibula to
4th metatarsal base, splitting EDB. - Both approaches improve visualization for
alignment and avoid rotational errors.

### Medial Malleolus Osteotomy
- Needed only for talar body fractures.
- Short oblique osteotomy preferred.
- Posterior cortex should be cracked rather than completely cut.

Fixation Strategy
### Neck Fractures
- Non comminuted: two compression screws perpendicular to fracture line.
- Comminuted (usually medial):
  - Lateral screw → compression.
  - Medial screw → positional, not compressive.
  - Consider small medial buttress plate.
  - Bone graft if comminution is significant.
### Body Fractures
- Combination of screws, plates, and wires as required.

<!-- page 4 -->

- Avoid overhandling of fragments attached to deltoid ligament.
### Do Not
- Do not remove small medial neck fragments attached to deltoid ligament.
- Do not rely on single approach.

Complications
Avascular Necrosis (AVN)
Risk increases with Hawkins grade. - Type 1: 0 to 16 percent - Type 2: 20 to 50 percent
- Type 3: 70 to 100 percent - Type 4: up to 100 percent

### Hawkins Sign
- Subchondral lucency at 6 to 8 weeks.
- Indicates intact vascularity.
- Absence after 12 weeks → consider MRI.
### Varus Malunion
- Most common complication.
- Results from medial comminution + overcompression.
- Causes varus hindfoot and gait difficulty.
- Treatment: osteotomy, subtalar fusion if advanced.
Arthritis
- Subtalar arthritis: up to 81 percent in long term.
- Tibiotalar arthritis: nearly 18 percent.
Infection
- Particularly in open fractures (up to 25 percent).

### Exam Pearls
- Always differentiate neck vs body fracture by inferior fracture line.
- Dual approach is mandatory for accurate reduction.
- Medial comminution → medial positional screw, not compressive.
- Hawkins sign predicts vascularity.
- Canal view is crucial intraoperatively.
- Never perform medial malleolus osteotomy for neck fractures.

Final Faculty Insight: “Your job in talus surgery is simple. Restore anatomy, protect
the blood supply, and prevent varus collapse. Everything else follows.”
