Pilon Fracture – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay
Overview
Pilon fractures are axial compression injuries of the distal tibial plafond, often high
energy and associated with severe soft tissue trauma. These fractures require careful
staging, CT based planning and fragment specific fixation.
Faculty Remark: “Span, scan and then plan. A pilon without a CT scan is a surgery
planned blindly.”

### Anatomy Essentials
- Distal tibia is irregular rectangular in shape and articulates with the dome of the talus.
- Wider anteriorly, concave in coronal and sagittal planes.
- Medial malleolus has smaller articular involvement.
- Articulates with medial aspect of fibula.
- Understanding the plafond geometry is essential to identify anterior, posterior and medial shear patterns.

Mechanism of Injury
### Primary Mechanism: Axial Compression
- Fall from height or high velocity RTA.
- Talus drives into tibial plafond → impaction of articular surface.
- If fibula intact → medial impaction pattern more likely.
### Other Contributing Forces
- Bending
- Rotational
- Shearing
### Forced Position Patterns
- Forced plantarflexion → posterior plafond injury.
- Forced dorsiflexion → anterior plafond injury.

### Clinical Presentation
- Non ambulatory, severe pain, swelling.
- Visible deformity in high energy injuries.

- Must evaluate for open fractures, soft tissue coverage and neurovascular status.
- Use Ganga scoring system for open injuries to decide limb salvage feasibility.

Radiology
X Rays
Look for: - Fibula fracture or intact (guides severity and impaction pattern). - Articular
comminution. - Impaction fragments: size, location. - Subluxation or dislocation at ankle
or talofibular joint. - Syndesmotic widening. - Associated hindfoot injuries: talus,
calcaneus.

### CT Scan (Mandatory)
- Must be obtained after limb is spanned and length restored.
- Defines primary and secondary fracture lines.
- Identifies coronal/sagittal splits, comminution, talar dome injury.
- Helps classify fracture pattern and plan approaches.
- CT influences surgical plan in over 60 percent of cases.

Classification Systems
### Rüedi and Allgöwer Classification
- Type 1: cleavage fractures without displacement.
- Type 2: displaced but with large osteochondral fragments.
- Type 3: comminuted and impacted fractures.
### OTA (43) Classification
- 43-A: Extraarticular
- 43-B: Partial articular
- 43-C: Complete articular (C1 simple, C2 simple articular with metaphyseal comminution, C3 multifragmentary articular)

Approach to the Patient
### General Evaluation
- Pilon fractures are high energy injuries → always rule out other life threatening trauma.
- Assess for spine, head, abdominal injuries.
### Local Evaluation
- Open vs closed.

<!-- page 3 -->

- Soft tissue envelope.
- Neurovascular assessment.
- Associated injuries: talar body/neck fractures, tibial shaft, plateau, calcaneal fractures.
### Immediate Priorities
- Urgent reduction if fracture dislocation.
- Temporary stabilization: slab or external fixator.

### Span, Scan and Plan
1. Span the ankle with external fixator (or slab if fibula intact and alignment acceptable).
2. Scan only after restoring length and alignment.
3. Plan fragment specific reconstruction.

### Goals of Treatment
- Biological exposure.
- Anatomical articular reconstruction.
- Elevate impacted fragments and fill metaphyseal voids (bone grafting).
- Stable internal fixation.
- Atraumatic closure.
- Early ROM; weight bearing after 8–12 weeks.

### Surgical Management Options
1. Single Stage Internal Fixation
- Rare. Only when swelling minimal and soft tissue envelope excellent.
2. Staged Internal Fixation (Most Common)
Stage 1: - Fibula fixation if needed (helps regain length and ligamentotaxis). -
Temporary external fixator application.
Stage 2: - Definitive tibial plafond reconstruction.

3. External Fixation with Limited Internal Fixation
- When soft tissue condition poor.
- Useful in open fractures.
4. Primary Arthrodesis
- For non reconstructible C3 injuries.

### Surgical Approaches
- Anterolateral approach: excellent visualization of anterior plafond.
- Anteromedial approach: used for medial impaction fragments.
- Posterolateral approach: for posterior malleolus and fibula.
- Direct lateral approach: fibular fixation.
### Soft Tissue Principles
- Use curvilinear incisions when crossing ankle joint.
- Maintain minimum 5–7 cm between incisions.
- Prefer longer incisions with thick flaps to protect vascularity.

Fixation Principles
### Fragment Specific Fixation
- Identify anterior, medial, posterior and Chaput fragments.
- Reduce posterior malleolus early → converts C → B pattern.
- Use low profile buttress plates (one third tubular or recon plates) for medial and anterior shear.
### Key Technical Points
- Fix fibula first to restore length.
- Avoid compressing impacted fragments before elevating them.
- Bone graft metaphyseal defects.
- Check syndesmotic stability intraoperatively.
- Avoid bulky anatomical plates in medial column due to soft tissue risks.

Complications
- Wound breakdown.
- Infection.
- Malunion (especially medial collapse if impaction not addressed).
- Post traumatic arthritis.
- Missed syndesmotic injury.

### Exam Pearls
- Medial impaction is common when fibula intact.
- Always span before CT to get a meaningful reconstruction plan.
- Posterior malleolus reduction changes overall plafond geometry.
- Low profile plates preferred in comminuted pilon fractures.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Delayed surgery 10–14 days improves soft tissue outcomes.

Final Faculty Insight: “Anatomical reconstruction of articular surface and respect
for soft tissues decide success in pilon fractures.”
