Deformity Correction in Common Pediatric
Deformities – Dr. Sanjay Arora
Overview
Pediatric deformity correction follows the same principles as adults, but techniques
differ because of the growing physis, thick periosteum, and elastic bones.
Understanding natural history, CORA principles, guided growth, and osteotomy
selection is essential.
Faculty Remark: “Techniques change, but principles are forever. Respect the
physis and understand the deformity before correcting it.”

## Principles of Pediatric Deformity Correction
Principle 1 – Differentiate Deformity vs Malformation
Deformity
- Congenital or acquired angular or rotational malalignment.
- Examples:
  - Developmental hip dysplasia.
  - Coxa vara.
  - Genu valgum.
  - Tibia vara.
  - Ankle valgus.
  - Clubfoot.
  - Cavovarus foot.
  - Congenital hallux varus.
Malformation
- “Made wrong” structurally, not just malaligned.
- Categories:
  1. Too large (macrodactyly)
  2. Too small (brachydactyly)
  3. Too many (polydactyly)
  4. Too few (hypoplasia)
  5. Failure of separation (syndactyly, synostosis)
- Examples:
  - Proximal femoral focal deficiency.
  - Cruciate ligament deficiency.

<!-- page 2 -->

- Fibular/tibial hemimelia.
  - Subtalar synostosis.
  - Cleft foot.
  - First metatarsal longitudinal epiphyseal bracket.
Key Difference:
Deformities can be reconstructed.
Malformations must be constructed (e.g., centralization for radial club hand,
pollicization).

Principle 2 – Distinguish Deformity from Physiological
Variants
Normal age-related variations: - Flatfoot: 90 percent infants, 25 percent adults. -
Metatarsus adductus: common in babies, rare in adults. - Natural alignment
changes: - Infant: genu varum. - 2–4 years: physiological valgus (14–16 degrees). - 6
years: adult alignment (6–7 degrees valgus). - Femoral anteversion: 25–35 degrees in
children, decreases to slight retroversion in adults.
Most physiological deformities resolve spontaneously. Avoid premature surgery.
Principle 3 – Know the Natural History Before Treating
Ask: - Does the deformity merely look different, or will it cause pain, disability, or
progression?
Examples: - Fibrous cortical defects: varus up to 30 degrees resolves. - Cubitus
varus: once thought cosmetic; now known to cause: - Posterolateral rotatory instability
(PLRI). - Triceps snapping. - Lateral condyle fractures. - Tardy ulnar palsy.

Principle 4 – Correct Deformity at the Site of Deformity
(CORA Method)
### CORA (Center of Rotation of Angulation)
- Draw anatomical/mechanical axes.
- Intersection = CORA.
- Correct here using:
  - Opening wedge.
  - Closing wedge.
  - Dome osteotomy.

<!-- page 3 -->

### Away-from-CORA Correction
- Necessary if anatomy limits direct correction.
- Requires translation of fragment.
### Foot CORA
- Hindfoot deformity correction site may not always be the bone containing CORA.

## Cubitus Varus
### Key Points
- Triplanar deformity: varus + extension + internal rotation.
- Causes functional issues and cosmetic deformity.

### Indications for Surgery
- Varus >5–10 degrees.
- Age >5 years.
- One year after initial trauma.
- Flexion/extension deformity present.

### Surgical Options
- Medial open wedge.
- Lateral closing wedge (most common).
- Step-cut osteotomy.
- Dome osteotomy.
- French osteotomy (corrects rotation).
- Guided growth in selected young cases.
PLRI may require LUCL reconstruction + osteotomy.

## Deformities Around the Knee
Genu Varum
### When Pathological?
- Present after 2 years.
- Unilateral.
- Associated with shortening.
- Severe.
- Child with obesity.

<!-- page 4 -->

Causes
- Tibial hemimelia.
- Blount disease.
- Rickets.
- Focal fibrocartilaginous dysplasia.
- HME, achondroplasia.

Tibia Vara (Blount Disease)
Infantile (<3 years), Juvenile, Adolescent
### Radiology Characteristics
- Abrupt varus at proximal tibia.
- Medial metaphyseal beaking.
- Irregular medial physis.
- Lateral thrust.
### Drennan’s Angle (MDA)
- 11 degrees → high risk of progression.
- <11 degrees → likely to resolve.
### Treatment Options
- Osteotomy:
  - Closing wedge.
  - Opening wedge.
  - Rab’s osteotomy (biplanar + derotation).
  - Chevron osteotomy.
  - Dome osteotomy.
- Adolescent tibia vara:
  - Osteotomy.
  - Realignment with fixator.
  - Lateral epiphyseodesis / guided growth.
### Focal Fibrocartilaginous Dysplasia
- <30 degrees → observation.
- 40–50 degrees → osteotomy.

Genu Valgum
Causes
- Idiopathic.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Previous proximal tibia fracture (Cozen phenomenon).
- Hypophosphatemic rickets.
- Obesity.
- Multiple hereditary exostosis.
Assessment
- Intermalleolar distance.
- Full-length radiographs.
- Identify deformity site (DFO vs PTO).
  - Disappears in flexion → distal femur.
  - Persists → proximal tibia.
Management
- Observation.
- Guided growth (8-plate, staples, screws).
- Corrective osteotomy:
  - Lateral opening wedge.
  - Medial closing wedge.
  - Dome osteotomy.
- Combined osteotomy + guided growth in severe cases.
- Peroneal nerve decompression if correction >15–20 degrees.

### Exam Pearls
- Malformations must be constructed, not reconstructed.
- Physiological genu varum → genu valgum → adult alignment sequence.
- Drennan’s angle >11 degrees predicts Blount progression.
- Guided growth requires healthy physis + growth remaining.
- Correct at CORA to avoid translation.
- Cubitus varus is triplanar with risk of PLRI.
- Lateral thrust is classic for Blount disease.
- Genu valgum disappears in flexion if deformity is distal femoral.

Final Faculty Insight: “Deformity correction in children is about timing,
understanding natural history, and respecting the physis. Correct the cause, not just the
angle.”
