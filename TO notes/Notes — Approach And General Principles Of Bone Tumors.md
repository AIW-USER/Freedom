Approach and General Principles of Bone Tumors –
Dr. Rajat Gupta
Overview
Approaching a patient with a suspected bone tumor requires a structured evaluation
combining clinical assessment, radiological interpretation, and histopathological
confirmation. All three must correlate to reach a correct diagnosis.
Faculty Remark: “Clinical, radiology, and pathology must match. If one does not fit,
rethink the diagnosis.”

## Clinical Assessment
Age-Based Diagnostic Clues
Age is one of the strongest predictors for likely tumor types.

### Children and Adolescents
- Simple bone cyst (SBC)
- Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC)
- Fibrous dysplasia (FD)
- Non ossifying fibroma (NOF)
- Osteochondroma
- Chondroblastoma
- Osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma
- Malignancies: Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, leukemia
- Langerhans cell histiocytosis
### Young Adults and Middle Age
- Giant cell tumor (GCT)
- Fibrous dysplasia
- Enchondroma
- Chondroblastoma (up to 25 years)
- Adamantinoma
- Chondrosarcoma (from middle age onward)
Elderly
- FD, enchondroma
- Metastasis (most common)
- Multiple myeloma

- Lymphoma
- Chondrosarcoma
- Secondary osteosarcoma

### Pain Characteristics
- Night pain: Osteoid osteoma
- Intermittent pain: Fibrous dysplasia
- No pain: Enchondroma, osteochondroma

### Location-Specific Patterns
- Knee: GCT, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma
- Scapula & pelvis: Ewing sarcoma (youth), chondrosarcoma (adults)
- Wrist: GCT
- Hand: Enchondroma

## Radiological Evaluation

1. Age
A systematic approach with seven checkpoints.

2. Tumor Location
Differentiate skeletally mature vs immature.

### Longitudinal Axis
- Epiphyseal: GCT, chondroblastoma, clear cell chondrosarcoma
- Metaphyseal: Osteochondroma, enchondroma, UBC, ABC, NOF, FD, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, Ewing (50 percent)
- Diaphyseal: NOF, FD, OFD, adamantinoma, Ewing, myeloma, metastasis, occasional osteosarcoma
### Transverse Axis
- Cortical: NOF, adamantinoma, OFD, osteoid osteoma, infection, rare metastasis
- Medullary: Enchondroma, chondrosarcoma, myeloma, lymphoma, metastasis

### 3. Type of Lesion
- Geographical: Well defined (UBC, ABC, GCT, NOF)
- Moth eaten: Myeloma

<!-- page 3 -->

- Permeative: Osteosarcoma, Ewing, leukemia, metastasis

### 4. Matrix Characteristics
- Fibrous (ground glass): Fibrous dysplasia
- Chondroid (popcorn calcification): Enchondroma, chondrosarcoma
- Osteoid (ivory/ossified matrix): Osteosarcoma
- No matrix: GCT, ABC, Ewing

### 5. Zone of Transition
- Narrow (sharp): Benign lesions
- Wide (ill defined): Malignant tumors

### 6. Periosteal Reaction
- Solid: NOF, osteoid osteoma, infection
- Lamellated (onion peel): Ewing
- Spiculated (sunburst): Osteosarcoma
- Codman triangle: Aggressive lesions

### 7. Soft Tissue Component
- Seen in GCT and malignant bone tumors

## MRI Evaluation
MRI is essential for: - Determining extent of disease - Assessing relation to
neurovascular bundle - Detecting skip metastasis (always order full length MRI) -
Locating ideal biopsy site

### Key MRI Signs
- Fluid fluid levels: ABC or telangiectatic osteosarcoma
- T2 hyperintense lobulated lesion: Chondroid tumors

## Biopsy Principles
Biopsy confirms the diagnosis and guides further management.

Why Biopsy?
biopsy a tumor you might operate.”

- Confirm diagnosis
- Plan definitive treatment

### When to Biopsy?
- Determine prognosis

- Only after complete imaging

### Who Should Biopsy?
- MRI helps target the solid, enhancing portion

- Orthopedic oncologist
- Interventional radiologist trained in oncologic protocols
Poorly done biopsies cause:
- Contamination
- Need for extensive resection
- Increased risk of recurrence

### How to Biopsy – Core Principles
- Shortest route
- In line with planned surgical incision
- Avoid joints and arthroscopy
- Avoid neurovascular bundle
- Single entry point
- Biopsy periphery, not necrotic center
- Avoid transverse incisions
- Do not raise flaps
- Achieve good hemostasis
- Place drain in line with incision
- Do not exsanguinate if using tourniquet
- Never divide specimen
### Acceptable Techniques
- Core needle biopsy: Preferred, cost effective, minimal contamination
- Open biopsy: When more tissue is required or pathology expertise is limited

## Staging of Bone Tumors
### Enneking System (Benign)
- Latent: NOF
- Active: ABC

Enneking (Malignant)
- Aggressive: GCT

- Stage I: Low grade (IA intracompartmental, IB extracompartmental)
- Stage II: High grade (IIA intracompartmental, IIB extracompartmental)

### AJCC System
- Stage III: Metastatic

- Based on grade, size (8 cm cutoff), metastasis
- Pulmonary metastasis: Stage IVA
- Extrapulmonary metastasis: Stage IVB

## Staging Investigations
- Bone scan + CT chest (2 mm cuts)
- PET CT (whole body, head to toe)
- Ewing sarcoma requires bone marrow biopsy

## Principles of Oncologic Surgery
### Margin Concepts
- Intralesional: Curettage (GCT), debulking (metastatic disease)
- Marginal: Excision through reactive zone (e.g., pedunculated osteochondroma)
- Wide: En bloc resection including cuff of normal tissue
- Radical: Removal of entire compartment (e.g., BKA for foot sarcoma)
Faculty Remark: “Amputation is not always radical. A compartment defines
radicality, not the level.”

## Reconstruction Options
Biological
- Extracorporeal radiotherapy (ECRT)

- Cryotherapy

### Non Biological
- Megaprosthesis

### Exam Pearls
- Age is the strongest diagnostic clue
- Fluid fluid levels, always differentiate ABC from telangiectatic osteosarcoma
- Popcorn calcification = chondroid matrix
- Always order full length MRI/X ray to detect skip lesions
- Biopsy should follow surgical approach
- Never take transverse incisions
- Curettage = intralesional; wide resection preserves local control

Final Faculty Insight: “Respect oncologic principles. A wrong biopsy can ruin an
otherwise curable limb salvage surgery.”
