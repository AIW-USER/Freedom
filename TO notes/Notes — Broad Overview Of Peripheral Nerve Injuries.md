Broad Overview of Peripheral Nerve Injuries –
Dr. Mukul Mohindra
Overview / Key Concepts
Peripheral nerve injuries are a high-yield, clinically oriented topic that integrates
anatomy, mechanisms of injury, clinical examination, and decision-making regarding
repair or reconstruction. A clear understanding of nerve anatomy, muscular supply,
levels of palsy, and compression sites allows accurate localization and management.
This lecture provides a conceptual framework to approach all peripheral nerve injuries
in a structured and exam-oriented manner.

Classification of Peripheral Nerve Injuries
### Based on Pathophysiology (Seddon)
  - Neuropraxia: Conduction block, no axonal disruption
  - Axonotmesis: Axonal disruption, intact endoneurium
  - Neurotmesis: Complete nerve discontinuity

### Sunderland Classification

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Neuropraxia |
| II | Axon disruption, endoneurium intact |
| III | Endoneurium disrupted |
| IV | Perineurium disrupted |
| V | Complete nerve transection |

Higher grades have poorer prognosis and usually require surgery.

Anatomy-Oriented Approach
### Macroscopic Anatomy
  - Peripheral nerves are organized into:
  - Epineurium
  - Perineurium
  - Endoneurium

- Blood supply via vasa nervorum
- Nerves tolerate stretch up to ~15% before ischemia

### General Anatomical Rules (Exam Mnemonics)
- Every nerve changes compartment at coracobrachialis insertion
- Every nerve enters the forearm between two heads of a muscle
These rules help in rapid anatomical recall during exams.

Muscular Compartments of Upper Limb
Arm
- Anterior compartment: Flexors
- Posterior compartment: Extensors
Forearm
- Anterior: Flexors and pronators
- Posterior: Extensors and supinators

### Functional Nerve Identity
- Musician’s nerve: Ulnar nerve (fine movements)
- Labourer’s nerve: Median nerve (power grip)
- Extensor nerve: Radial nerve

Muscular Supply Concepts (High Yield)
### Forearm Flexors
- Median nerve supplies all flexors of forearm
- Except:
  - Flexor carpi ulnaris
  - Medial half of FDP
These two are supplied by ulnar nerve

### Hand Muscles
- Median nerve supplies:
  - All thenar muscles
  - Except adductor pollicis

<!-- page 3 -->

- Ulnar nerve supplies:
  - All interossei
  - Medial two lumbricals
  - Adductor pollicis

High vs Low Nerve Palsy Concept
### High Palsy
- Lesion proximal to major motor branches
- More muscle paralysis
- Deformity may be less obvious due to loss of antagonists
### Low Palsy
- Lesion distal to major branches
- Fewer muscles affected
- Deformity often more pronounced
Example: - Ulnar paradox: High ulnar palsy causes less clawing than low palsy

### Claw Hand – Pathophysiology
- Loss of intrinsic muscles
- MCP hyperextension due to EDC
- IP flexion due to FDS and FDP
Seen in: - Ulnar nerve palsy - Median nerve palsy - Combined palsies - Volkmann
ischemic contracture - Leprosy

### Sensory Loss Principles
- Sensory loss helps localize nerve injury
- Autonomous zones are most reliable

| Nerve | Autonomous Zone |
|---|---|
| Median | Tip of index finger |
| Ulnar | Tip of little finger |
| Radial | First dorsal web space |

Sites of Compression (Conceptual)
### Median Nerve
- Pronator teres
- FDS arch
- Carpal tunnel
### Ulnar Nerve
- Cubital tunnel
- Guyon’s canal
### Radial Nerve
- Spiral groove
- Arcade of Frohse
- Wartenberg syndrome

### Clinical Examination Strategy
1. Observe posture and deformity
2. Test key muscles, not all muscles
3. Check autonomous sensory zones
4. Assess joint suppleness
5. Look for trophic changes

Principles of Nerve Repair
Timing
- Primary repair: Within 1 week
- Delayed primary: 2–4 weeks
- Secondary repair: >1 month
Techniques
- Epineural repair (gold standard)
- Fascicular repair (select cases)
- Nerve grafting if gap >2–3 cm

### Nerve Regeneration
- Rate: ~1 mm/day
- Slower in proximal injuries
- Motor end plates degenerate after prolonged denervation

### Role of Tendon Transfers
- Indicated when:
  - Nerve recovery unlikely
  - Late presentation
  - Failed nerve repair
- Principles:
  - Supple joints
  - Adequate sensation
  - Expendable donor

### Prognostic Factors
- Age (younger better)
- Level of injury (distal better)
- Time to repair
- Associated vascular injury
- Type of injury (sharp vs crush)

Faculty Quote

### Exam Pearls
- Neuropraxia recovers without surgery
- Sunderland Grade IV and V usually need repair
- Median nerve supplies all flexors except FCU and medial FDP
- Ulnar nerve supplies all interossei
- Radial nerve has maximum motor loss but minimal sensory disability
- Autonomous zones are crucial for localization
- High palsy may look less deformed than low palsy
- Tendon transfer is a salvage, not primary treatment
