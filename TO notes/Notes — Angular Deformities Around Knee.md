Overview / Key Concepts
Angular deformities around the knee are common pediatric and adolescent problems
and form an important part of clinical examination and exam-oriented orthopedics. The
major deformities include genu varum, genu valgum, and genu recurvatum. Correct
identification of the deformity, quantification, localization of the bone involved,
understanding physiological alignment, and age-appropriate management are critical.

Definitions
### Genu Varum
- Leg lies medial to the knee
- Increased distance between medial joint lines of knee (> 6 cm)
### Genu Valgum
- Leg lies lateral to the knee
- Increased intermalleolar distance (> 8 cm)
### Genu Recurvatum
- Knee hyperextension > 5°

### Normal Physiological Alignment by Age

| Age | Alignment |
|---|---|
| Birth – 18 months | Physiological genu varum |
| 18 – 24 months | Neutral alignment |
| 2 – 5 years | Physiological genu valgum (can be up to 15–20°) |
| 5 – 6 years | Adult alignment (5–6° valgus) |

Any deviation beyond this pattern is considered pathological.

Clinical Quantification of Deformity
### OPD Measurements
- Genu Valgum: Intermalleolar distance > 8 cm

<!-- page 2 -->

- Genu Varum: Medial knee joint line distance > 6 cm
Radiological Assessment
### Mechanical Axis
- Drawn from center of femoral head to center of ankle
- Normally passes slightly lateral to knee center

| Axis Position | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Medial to knee | Genu varum |
| Lateral to knee | Genu valgum |

Quantification on Scanogram
### Femoro-tibial Angle (FTA)
- Angle between long axis of femur and tibia
- Normal: 5–6° valgus

| Angle | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| > 6° valgus | Genu valgum |
| < 4° valgus / varus | Genu varum |

Identifying the Bone Involved
### Clinical Method
- Patient supine, knees flexed
- View from foot end
- Parallel tibiae suggest femoral deformity
- Divergent tibiae suggest tibial deformity
### Radiological Angles

| Angle | Normal | Decreased Indicates |
|---|---|---|
| LDFA (Lateral Distal | ~87° | Femoral valgus |

### Femoral Angle)
MPTA (Medial ~87° Tibial varus
Proximal Tibial Angle)

Exam rule: Valgum is usually femoral, varum is usually tibial.

Causes
### Genu Varum
- Physiological (≤ 2 years)
- Rickets
- Blount’s disease (most important)
- Congenital familial tibia vara
### Genu Valgum
- Idiopathic (most common)
- Cozen’s phenomenon (proximal tibial fracture)
- Skeletal dysplasias (rare)

### Indications for Correction
- Femoro-tibial angle > 15°
- Intermalleolar distance > 10–12 cm
- Mechanical axis in Zone 3
- Mechanical axis in Zone 2 with symptoms (knee pain, recurrent patellar subluxation)

Management Principles
### Step 1: Assess Growth Remaining
- Boys: till 16 years
- Girls: till 14 years
### Step 2: Choose Modality

| Growth Remaining | Treatment |
|---|---|
| 1–2 years | Permanent hemi-epiphysiodesis |
| > 2 years | Reversible hemi-epiphysiodesis (staples or 8-plate) |
| Growth plates fused | Corrective osteotomy |

Overcorrection is advised due to rebound phenomenon.

Osteotomy Options Around Knee
Distal Femoral Osteotomy (for valgum)
- Closed wedge osteotomy (preferred)
- Open wedge osteotomy (needs graft, risk of collapse)

<!-- page 4 -->

- Dome osteotomy (no length loss, risk of quadriceps fibrosis)
- Supracondylar V osteotomy (coronal plane, good contact)
- Step-cut translational osteotomy
Closed wedge preferred due to better union and less loss of correction.
Blount’s Disease (Tibia Vara)
Pathology
- Developmental disorder of proximal medial tibial physis
- Seen in early walkers and obese children (>95th percentile)
### Clinical Features
- Usually bilateral, can be unilateral
- Acute varus below knee
- Genu recurvatum
- Limb length discrepancy
Gait
- Siffert’s Katz sign (varus thrust during walking)

### Radiological Features of Blount’s Disease
- Metaphyseal flaring
- Fibular physis near knee level
- Physeal bar
- Metaphyseal–Diaphyseal Angle (Drennan)

| Angle | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| < 11° | Normal |
| 11–16° | Doubtful |
| > 16° | Blount’s disease |

### Langenskiöld Classification

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Medial metaphyseal beak |
| II | Depression |
| III | Step formation |
| IV | Ossification of step |
| V | Separate medial fragment |

<!-- page 5 -->

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| VI | Medial physeal bar |

### Treatment of Blount’s Disease

| Type | Management |
|---|---|
| I–II | Bracing (Mermaid brace) for 2 years |
| III–IV | Surgery (guided growth or osteotomy) |
| V–VI | Osteotomy + medial tibial plateau elevation |

Screen all patients for SCFE.
### Rickets with Angular Deformity
- Seen after 2 years of age
- Genu varum more common than valgum
### Healing Assessment
- Best marker: X-ray (white line of Frankel)
### Timing of Osteotomy
- Only after serum alkaline phosphatase normalizes

### Windswept Deformity
- Valgus on one side, varus on the other
- Children: Rickets
- Adults: Rheumatoid arthritis
Order of Surgery in RA
Lower Limb: Foot → Hip → Knee
Upper Limb: Hand → Shoulder → Elbow

Genu Recurvatum
Causes
- Ligament laxity
- Congenital knee dislocation

<!-- page 6 -->

- Quadriceps weakness
- Physeal injury
Associations
- Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita
- Absent cruciates
- Hypoplastic patella
Management
- Serial manipulation and casting

Ligament Laxity Assessment
### Beighton Score
- Adults: ≥ 5
- Children: ≥ 6

Faculty Quote
“Correct identification of the deformity, the bone involved, and growth remaining makes
treatment straightforward.” – Dr. Mukul Mohindra

### Exam Pearls
- Mechanical axis is the most reliable indication for surgery
- Valgum is usually femoral, varum usually tibial
- Closed wedge osteotomy is preferred
- Blount’s disease must be differentiated from physiological varum
- X-ray is best to assess rickets healing
- Never perform osteotomy in active rickets
- Screen Blount’s disease patients for SCFE
