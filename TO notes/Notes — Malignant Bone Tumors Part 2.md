Malignant Bone Tumors – Part 2 – Dr. Rajat Gupta
Overview
Part 2 covers Ewing’s Sarcoma, Chondrosarcoma, Adamantinoma, and Chordoma,
focusing on clinical features, radiology, pathology, staging, treatment, and key decision
making principles.
Faculty Remark: “Every malignant bone tumor must be approached systematically.
Age, location and radiology already give you half the diagnosis.”

## Ewing’s Sarcoma
Epidemiology
- Second most common primary bone cancer in children and adolescents.
- Most common primary bone malignancy <10 years.
- Peak age: 5–25 years.
- Common in flat bones, metaphysis or diaphysis of long bones.

### Clinical Features
- Pain and swelling.
- Fever, raised ESR/CRP.
- Raised WBC.
- Can mimic infection.

Radiology
### X Ray Checklist
- Age: skeletally immature.
- Location: metaphyseal or diaphyseal.
- Type: permeative lytic.
- Matrix: none.
- Zone of transition: wide.
- Periosteal reaction: onion peel most common; Codman triangle may occur.
- Soft tissue component: frequently present.
- Flat bones: nonspecific destructive pattern.

<!-- page 2 -->

Histopathology
- Small round blue cells.
- CD99 and MIC2 positive.
- PAS positive, reticulin negative.
- NKX2 also positive (highly specific).
- Translocation t(11,22) most common.
Differential: Lymphoma (PAS negative, reticulin positive).
### Evaluation Protocol
1. X Ray
- Defines extent of destruction and fractures.
2. MRI (Full length)
- Extent of marrow and soft tissue involvement.
- Relation to neurovascular structures.
- Mandatory to assess skip lesions.
3. PET CT
- Staging and treatment response assessment.
- SUV reduction >50 percent suggests good response.
4. If Bone Scan + CT Chest Used
- Must add bone marrow aspiration for marrow metastasis.

### Prognostic Factors

| Factor | Poor Prognosis |
|---|---|
| Stage | Metastatic disease, bone mets worse than lung |
| Size | >200 ml volume |
| Location | Axial skeleton |
| Pathological fracture | Present |
| Chemotherapy response | Necrosis <90 percent |
| Margins | Positive margins |
| Lab parameters | Raised ESR, LDH, WBC |
| Grade | Not applicable (Ewing’s is always high grade) |

<!-- page 3 -->

Treatment
Chemotherapy
WAC + IE alternating cycles - WAC = Vincristine, Adriamycin, Cyclophosphamide. - IE
= Ifosfamide + Etoposide.

Radiation Therapy
Indications: - Positive margins. - Poor chemo response (<90 percent necrosis). -
Surgically unresectable disease. - When surgery results in major morbidity (e.g.,
sacrum).

Surgery
- Mainstay for local control.
- Limb salvage preferred if good response.
- Pathological fracture not an absolute contraindication.

## Chondrosarcoma
### Key Facts
- Second most common non hematologic primary bone malignancy.
- Most common malignancy of hand bones.
- Presents >40 years.
- Slow growing, symptoms may be present for years.

### Radiological Clues
- Skeletally mature.
- Metaphyseal extending into epiphysis/diaphysis.
- Permeative lytic lesion.
- Popcorn calcification (chondroid matrix).
- Wide zone of transition.
- No periosteal reaction.
- Soft tissue calcified mass.

### MRI Signature
- T2 hyperintense, lobulated cartilage tumor.
- Plain T2 images are essential.

<!-- page 4 -->

### Malignant Transformation Clues
- Expansion.
- Cortical erosion.
- Endosteal scalloping.
- 5 cm size.
- Marrow edema.
- Soft tissue component.

### PET CT Grading
- SUV <2 → benign.
- SUV >3 → malignant.
- Borderline 2–3 → correlate clinically and radiologically.

Variants
### Clear Cell Chondrosarcoma
- Low grade.
- Epiphyseal.
- Mimics chondroblastoma/GCT.
- Radiographs may look benign.
### Mesenchymal Chondrosarcoma
- High grade.
- Small round blue cells.
- Requires chemotherapy.
### Dedifferentiated Chondrosarcoma
- High grade.
- Poor prognosis.
- Often arises in neglected cases.
- May require chemotherapy depending on subtype.

Treatment
- Surgery is mainstay.
- No role of radiation, no chemotherapy except in mesenchymal or dedifferentiated types.
### Low Grade (Grade 1)
- Extended curettage + cementing possible.

<!-- page 5 -->

- PET CT used to avoid under grading.
### High Grade
- Wide local resection.

## Adamantinoma
### Key Points
- Arises from aberrant epithelial nests.
- Most common in tibia, third decade.
- Males.
Radiology
- Diaphyseal.
- Multiple sharply demarcated lesions.
- Separated by dense sclerotic bone.
- Cortical based lesion.
Histology
- Cytokeratin positive.
- Low grade malignancy.
Differential
- OFD in younger patients.
Treatment
- Wide resection.
- No chemo or radiotherapy.
### Reconstruction Options
- Intercalary resection + vascularized fibula transfer.
- Biological reconstruction preferred.

## Chordoma
### Key Facts
- Most common primary sacral malignancy.
- Second most common primary spinal malignancy.
- Age 40–60 years.
- Sacrococcygeal > spheno occipital > spinal.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Low grade malignancy.
Clinical
- Long standing pain.
- Neural compression symptoms.
- Arises from midline (unlike sacral GCT which arises from ala).
Radiology
- May show calcification.
Treatment
- Surgical resection is primary treatment.
- Radioresistant and chemoresistant.
- Proton beam therapy is an option when surgery causes severe morbidity.

### Exam Pearls
- Onion peel periosteal reaction → Ewing’s.
- CD99/MIC2 positive small round blue cells → Ewing’s.
- Grade is not a prognostic factor in Ewing’s.
- SUV >3 strongly suggests malignant chondroid tumor.
- Clear cell chondrosarcoma is epiphyseal and can mimic GCT.
- Adamantinoma is cortical and cytokeratin positive.
- Chordoma arises from midline, not sacral ala.
- Pathological fractures in Ewing’s are not absolute contraindications for limb salvage.

Final Faculty Insight: “Malignant tumors are predictable once you master patterns.
Age, location and matrix are your strongest allies.”
