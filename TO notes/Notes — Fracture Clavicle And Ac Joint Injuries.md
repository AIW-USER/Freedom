Fracture Clavicle & AC Joint Injuries – Dr. Anurag Tiwari

Overview
- Clavicle fractures and acromioclavicular (AC) joint injuries are common shoulder girdle injuries seen in trauma and sports cases.
- Clavicle fracture is one of the most common fractures in the human body, both at birth and in adulthood.
- Understanding relevant anatomy, mechanisms, and fixation techniques is crucial for effective management.
Dr. Anurag’s Remark:
“Clavicle injuries may look simple, but every non-union teaches us where mechanics or
biology was disrespected.”

Relevant Anatomy
Clavicle
- S-shaped bone: Medial two-thirds convex anteriorly; lateral one-third concave anteriorly.
- Medial end: Quadrangular, articulates with the manubrium forming the sternoclavicular (SC) joint.
- Lateral end: Flat, articulates with the acromion forming the acromioclavicular (AC) joint.
- Surface landmarks:
  - Superior surface: Subcutaneous, minimal soft tissue cover.
  - Inferior surface: Subclavian groove, costoclavicular ligament impression.

### AC Joint
- Synovial type (atypical) – fibrocartilaginous intra-articular disc.
- Stabilizers:
  - Static: Capsule, AC ligament, CC ligaments (conoid and trapezoid).
  - Dynamic: Deltoid and trapezius.

| Ligament | Function |
|---|---|
| AC Ligament | Horizontal stability |
| Coracoclavicular (CC) Ligament | Vertical stability |
| Conoid Ligament | Medial and conical, provides central (medial) stability |
| Trapezoid Ligament | Lateral, quadrangular, provides lateral stability |

Key Measurements: * Trapezoid insertion: 3 cm from lateral end. * Conoid
insertion: 4.5 cm from lateral end. * Distance between them: 1.5 cm. * Normal
Coracoclavicular (CC) Distance: 11–13 mm (1.1–1.3 cm). Increase = AC joint
dislocation.

Important Facts about Clavicle
- Only long bone lying horizontally.
- First bone to start ossification and last to complete.
- Only long bone that ossifies by intramembranous ossification.

### Mechanism of Injury

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| Direct trauma | Fall directly on shoulder (most common) |
| Indirect trauma | Fall on outstretched hand transmitting force to clavicle |

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|

| Type | Description |
|---|---|

### Clinical Assessment
  - Local pain, deformity, swelling, and tenderness.
  - Crepitus and abnormal mobility.
  - Assess for impending skin breach (spike fragment under skin).
  - Examine neurovascular status – subclavian vessels & brachial plexus.

Management of Clavicle Fractures
Non-Operative
- Indicated for most cases (especially middle-third, undisplaced).
- Sling preferred over figure-of-eight brace (less discomfort and skin issues).
- No attempt at reduction — high muscular forces prevent maintenance.
- Begin pendulum exercises early.
### Operative Indications

| Indication | Example |
|---|---|
| Displacement > 2 cm | Shortening or overlap |
| Comminution | Unstable fragments |
| Floating shoulder | Associated glenoid neck fracture |
| Open fracture / skin threat | Spike under skin |
| Neurovascular injury | Subclavian vessel or brachial plexus |
| Bilateral clavicle fracture | High morbidity |

Surgical Approaches & Implants
Approaches

| Approach | Notes |
|---|---|
| Superior (Anterosuperior) | Workhorse; risk to neurovascular structures below |
| Anteroinferior | Safer, avoids neurovascular injury but technically demanding |

Precaution: Identify and protect supraclavicular nerves (three branches) to prevent
neuroma or numbness.

AC Joint Injuries
Mechanism
- Direct fall or blow to shoulder → force transmitted to acromion → AC/CC ligament rupture.
### Rockwood Classification

| Type | Ligament Injury | Description |
|---|---|---|
| I | Sprain (AC partial tear) | No displacement |
| II | AC torn, CC intact | Subluxation |
| III | Both AC & CC torn | Dislocation (clavicle up) |

### Management of AC Joint Injuries

| Type | Management |
|---|---|
| I & II | Non-operative (sling, physiotherapy) |
| IV–VI | Operative fixation |
| III | Controversial — Non-op for general, op for young/athletes/manual workers |

Non-Operative
- Sling for 3–4 weeks → early motion.
- Persistent pain post 2–3 months → consider distal clavicle excision (1–2.5 cm resection).

### Operative Techniques

| Technique | Concept |
|---|---|
| Phemister (K-wire) | AC joint fixation with K-wires (now obsolete due to migration risk) |
| Hook Plate | Maintains reduction under acromion (remove after 8–10 weeks) |
| Bosworth Screw | Coracoclavicular screw fixation (remove after 8–10 weeks) |
| TightRope / Endobutton System | FiberWire between CC tunnels maintaining 1.2 cm CC distance |
| Suture Anchor | Coracoid + clavicle tunnels, anatomical site (3 cm trapezoid, 4.5 cm conoid) |
| Weaver–Dunn Procedure | Coracoacromial ligament transfer to clavicle + distal clavicle excision |
| Hamstring Autograft | Anatomic conoid + trapezoid |
| Reconstruction (Mazzocca) | ligament reconstruction using tendon graft |

Dr. Anurag’s Insight:
“For chronic AC dislocations, anatomical tendon reconstruction remains the gold
standard. Tendon-to-bone healing outlasts any implant.”

### Exam / Viva Pearls
- Commonest site: Middle third of clavicle.
- Best view: 10–15° cephalic tilt (Zanca View).
- CC distance: 11–13 mm; >25% increase = disruption.
- Type II Neer: High non-union risk.
- Rockwood III: Controversial; surgical in active individuals.
- Avoid hook plate anterior to acromion → impingement.
- Weaver–Dunn: Coracoacromial ligament transfer for chronic cases.
- Remove implants (hook/Bosworth) after 8–10 weeks.
Final Faculty Remark:
“Don’t underestimate clavicle or AC joint injuries — they’re the gateway to mastering
shoulder surgery.”
