Pediatric Bone Infections – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Pediatric musculoskeletal infections include acute and chronic osteomyelitis and septic
arthritis. Early diagnosis prevents complications such as physeal damage, pathological
fractures and chronic sinus formation. Most infections arise via hematogenous spread
and require coordinated care involving orthopedics, infectious disease specialists,
plastic surgery and nutrition support.
Key points: - Osteomyelitis accounts for 5 percent of pediatric orthopedic operative
cases. - Seventy five percent occur as part of systemic sepsis. - Neonates and infants
have unique vascular anatomy which predisposes them to rapid spread. - Management
depends on early recognition, appropriate imaging and stage specific intervention.

Etiology and Pathophysiology
### Classification by Duration
- Acute: less than 2 weeks.
- Subacute: 2 to 6 weeks.
- Chronic: beyond 6 weeks with established sequestrum.
### Classification by Source
- Hematogenous: most common in children.
- Exogenous: secondary to open trauma or wounds.
### Neonatal Osteomyelitis (pages 9 to 14)
- Sluggish metaphyseal blood flow and fewer phagocytic cells.
- Hairpin bends in nutrient vessels predispose to bacterial seeding.
- In children more than 2 years, physis blocks spread, leading to subperiosteal abscess.
- In children less than 2 years, transphyseal spread may involve physis and joint.
### Circumferential Abscess (pages 13 and 18)
- Devitalizes periosteum.
- Causes sequestrum formation.
- Increases pathological fracture risk.

Microbiology
### Common Organisms (pages 19 to 20)
- Staphylococcus aureus: most common.
- MRSA: increasing prevalence, including community acquired.
- Pseudomonas: IV drug users, hospital acquired.
- Fungal infections: NICU graduates, chronically ill.
- Salmonella: sickle cell disease.
- Group B streptococcus, gram negative coliforms, H influenzae: infants.
- Pasteurella: animal bites.
- Eikenella: human bites.
### K. Kingae Infection
- Often culture negative.
- Requires PCR for detection.
- Does not significantly elevate CRP.

Clinical Features
### History Indicators (pages 21 to 24)
- NICU admission.
- Long term IV antibiotics.
- Irritability, pseudoparalysis.
- Fever, failure to thrive.
- Local warmth, tenderness, active inflammation.
- Sinus discharge in chronic disease.
### Neonatal Infection Table (page 17)
- Preterm infants: nosocomial, gram negative and fungal organisms.
- Healthy term infants: community acquired MRSA.
- Diagnosis more prompt in term infants.

Diagnostic Evaluation
### Laboratory (page 25)
- ANC more than 12000: significant.
- CRP more than 20 mg per L: highly sensitive, good negative predictive value.
- ESR more than 20 mm per hour: sensitive but slow to normalize.
- Blood culture: mandatory before antibiotics.
- X ray:
  - Soft tissue swelling by 48 hours.

<!-- page 3 -->

- Periosteal reaction by 7 days.
- MRI: gold standard for early diagnosis, marrow edema and abscess.
- CT: useful in chronic cases for sequestrum mapping.
- PCR: for rare pathogens.
- Whole genome sequencing: detects MRSA variants.

Imaging in Chronic Osteomyelitis (pages 33 to 37)
### Key Radiological Findings
- Sequestrum: dead bone.
- Involucrum: reactive new bone.
- Cloacae: drainage openings.
- Rim sign: hyperintense rim on MRI surrounding active disease.
### Nuclear Studies
- Useful when MRI limited by hardware or fibrosis.
- FDG PET scan has highest diagnostic accuracy.

Management Principles
### Nade’s Principles for Acute Osteomyelitis (page 26)
1. Antibiotics effective before abscess formation.
2. Abscess requires surgical drainage.
3. Antibiotics prevent recurrence.
4. Surgery should not damage normal bone.
5. Antibiotics must continue post surgery.
### Acute Osteomyelitis (pages 26 to 29)
- IV antibiotics for 4 to 6 weeks.
- Subperiosteal abscess: drainage and bone window creation.
- Use oval bone window to reduce fracture risk.
- Curettage and medullary decompression via infant feeding tube.
### Subacute Osteomyelitis (page 30)
- Indolent course.
- Requires debridement and curettage.
Gledhill Classification (page 31)
Outlines radiologic patterns of subacute OM including metaphyseal lucencies, cortical
hyperostosis and onion skin appearance.

### Epiphyseal Osteomyelitis (page 32)
- Primary epiphyseal infection.
- Common in knee region.
- Tuberculosis most common in India.
- Debridement and appropriate antimicrobial therapy.
- Minimal risk of angular deformity.

Chronic Osteomyelitis
### Hallmark Features (page 33)
- Discharging sinus.
- Granulation tissue.
- Dead infected bone in compromised soft tissue.
Imaging and Classification (pages 33 to 45)
### Cierny and Mader Types
- A: Medullary.
- B: Superficial.
- C: Localized.
- D: Diffuse.
### Beit Cure Classification (pages 42 to 45)
- Type A: Brodie abscess.
- Type B1 to B4: sequestrum with varying involucrum quality.
- Type C: sclerosis.
Treatment: - Type A: drilling and curettage. - Type B1 to B3: sequestrectomy and
curettage. - Type B4: requires stabilization. - Type C: drainage and long term antibiotics.

### Surgical Principles (pages 46 to 47)
- Radical debridement to paprika sign.
- Wait for adequate involucrum before sequestrectomy when possible.
- Cortical window should be oval, created with drill holes.
### Dead Space Management (page 48)
- Papineau grafting and VAC therapy.
- Local and free flap coverage.
### Biofilm Management (page 49)
- Requires high dose local antibiotics.
- Adequate debridement essential.

<!-- page 5 -->

### Cement Spacers and Masquelet Technique (page 50)
- Two stage reconstruction.
- Induced membrane creates vascular envelope for grafting.
### Absorbable Calcium Sulphate Biocomposites (page 51)
- Deliver antibiotics without systemic toxicity.
- Avoid second surgery.
- Useful with heat sensitive antibiotics.

### Complications (page 52)
- Non union.
- Re infection.
- Pathological fracture.
- Osteonecrosis.
- Septic arthritis.
- Physeal damage leading to deformity.

Important Differentials Discussed in Cases
### Caffey’s Disease (pages 70 to 73)
- Infantile cortical hyperostosis.
- Benign self limiting.
- Presents with irritability and bone pain.
CRMO – Chronic Recurrent Multifocal Osteomyelitis (pages 79 to 80)
- Auto inflammatory disorder.
- Bilateral or multifocal lesions.
- Normal inflammatory markers.
- Treated with NSAIDs.
### CIPA (pages 92 to 93)
- Congenital insensitivity to pain and anhidrosis.
- Leads to hypertrophic callus.
### Scurvy (pages 87 to 93)
- Irritable child, metaphyseal white line.
- Responds dramatically to Vitamin C.

Surgical Techniques Highlighted
### Intramedullary Decompression Using Gigli Saw (page 18)
- Effective for pan medullary abscess.
- Safe and widely available.
- Allows removal of intramedullary necrotic tissue.

Faculty Quote
“Always culture all biopsies and biopsy all cultures because musculoskeletal infections
can mimic tumors and metabolic disorders.”

### Exam Pearls
- Staph aureus is most common, but MRSA is rising including community acquired MRSA.
- Abscess requires surgical drainage; antibiotics alone are ineffective.
- Transphyseal spread occurs in children under 2 years.
- Epiphyseal osteomyelitis in India is often tubercular.
- Presence of sequestrum is hallmark of chronic OM.
- CRP is best marker for treatment response.
- Type B4 chronic OM requires stabilization after sequestrectomy.
- Caffey disease and scurvy are key differentials in infants.
- CRMO presents with multifocal lesions and minimal systemic symptoms.
