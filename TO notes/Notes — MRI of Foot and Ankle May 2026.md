### Overview / Key Concepts
- A systematic way to read ankle and foot MRI (normal anatomy first), then ligament injuries, peroneal and Achilles pathology, osteochondral lesions, coalitions and other cases.
- Ankle MRI vs foot MRI: an ankle MRI covers the hindfoot and part of the midfoot. For forefoot or toe problems (metatarsalgia, turf toe, Morton neuroma, Lisfranc) ask for a FOOT MRI.
- Most images shown are from a 1.5 T magnet (what is seen in everyday practice); 3 T images are clearer.

### How to Read an Ankle MRI

| Sequence/plane | What to look at |
|---|---|
| Sagittal fat-suppressed (PD FS) | Bones: marrow oedema, cortical depression, elongated Stieda process (or os trigonum), retrocalcaneal fluid |
| Sagittal T1 (non fat-suppressed) | Muscles, Kager fat pad, scarring after surgery, Stieda process / os trigonum |
| Coronal ("money image" for the medial side) | Deltoid ligament complex first, then the tibiotalar joint for osteochondral injury, calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) laterally, coalitions |
| Axial | Most ligaments: ATFL, PTFL, CFL (deep to the peroneal tendons) and the syndesmotic ligaments; tendons |

- Do NOT assess the ATFL on coronal images: always use the axial images (at the level where the talus looks egg-shaped / "8").
- Syndesmotic ligaments are assessed on axial slices where tibia and fibula are seen without the talus; their normal striated (stippled, lamellated) look is not a tear.

Deltoid ligament anatomy

| Layer | Parts | MRI note |
|---|---|---|
| Deep | Anterior and posterior tibiotalar | Normal fibrofatty striations with high signal; do not call it torn |
| Superficial | Tibiocalcaneal (posterior), tibiospring (to the spring ligament), tibionavicular (anterior) | Does not attach to the talus; continuous sling with the spring ligament complex |

### Foot MRI
- Plantar muscles (flexor hallucis brevis medial and lateral heads, abductor, adductor with transverse and oblique heads), sesamoids.
- Lisfranc ligament complex: from the medial cuneiform to the base of the 2nd metatarsal; any widened space with an oedematous ligament or fluid cleft = injury.
- MTP joints are best seen on coronal images (flexor tendons, collateral ligaments, plantar plate).
- Morton neuroma (figure-of-eight intermetatarsal mass) vs intermetatarsal bursitis: hard to separate on MRI, easier clinically.

### Ankle Sprains and Instability
Low ankle sprain High ankle sprain

| Mechanism | Inversion (ankle rolls inward) | External rotation of foot/ankle |
|---|---|---|
| Structures | Lateral ligament complex (ATFL, CFL, PTFL) | Syndesmosis (tibia and fibula held together) |
| Course | Most common; single sprain needs no MRI (ultrasound can show the ATFL) | More pain, longer rehabilitation; NEVER miss it |

- About 20% of ankle sprains develop chronic instability: pain, repeated sprains, apprehension ("giving out"), reduced performance. This is when MRI is indicated.
- Functional instability: proprioceptive; the ATFL may be attenuated but present.
- Mechanical (anatomical) instability: lateral joint opening on varus stress.

Lateral ligament complex
- Sequence of failure: ATFL first, then CFL. A CFL tear without an ATFL tear does not occur. PTFL almost never tears (only in high-energy injury; may show mucoid degeneration or posterior impingement).
- ATFL tears: the most common ligament injury of the ankle; partial (low or high grade; high grade behaves like full thickness) or full thickness; talar- or fibular-sided.

<!-- page 3 -->

- A thick, grey ATFL = scar remodelling (weeks to months after injury); it may mature into a good scar or re-tear. A dark, thick scar can look intact on MRI but still be insufficient (MRI is not dynamic).
- A thickened, nodular CFL (as thick as the peroneal tendons) = old tear with scarring; trace the CFL over several slices (it runs obliquely backwards).

Stress radiographs
- Always request BILATERAL varus stress views (equal stress both sides), not one ankle.
- Lateral joint opening suggests ATFL injury; talar tilt (angle between the tibial plafond and talar dome) over about 4° (or a side-to-side difference) suggests ATFL + CFL injury. Example shown: 1° normal side vs 13° injured side.
- Helpful when MRI shows a scarred "intact" ATFL.

### Syndesmotic (High Ankle) Injury
- Ligaments: anterior inferior tibiofibular (AITFL), posterior inferior tibiofibular (PITFL), interosseous ligament, transverse tibiofibular ligament. Bony attachments: Chaput (anterior tibial), Wagstaffe (anterior fibular), Volkmann (posterior tibial) tubercles.
- X-ray: on the mortise view, a tibiofibular clear space over 5 mm, measured 1 cm above the joint, means the syndesmosis is open; loss of the normal tibiofibular overlap on AP is also abnormal.
- Missed syndesmotic injuries are more serious than low sprains: the talus displaces on weight bearing.
- MRI clue: posterior malleolar marrow oedema without a fracture suggests a syndesmotic injury.
- In adolescents the equivalent may be a Tillaux (Chaput tubercle) avulsion fracture.
- Associated injuries: osteochondral defects (15–25%), peroneal tendon injuries (up to 25%), deltoid injury, fractures (Weber B/C, 5th metatarsal base, anterior process of the calcaneum, lateral/posterior process of the talus).

### Medial (Deltoid) Injury
- Look on coronal images: loss of the normal striated pattern, fluid into the deep layer, medial marrow contusions.
- Sleeve avulsion (fascial sleeve of the medial malleolus): superficial deltoid origin, medial malleolar periosteum and flexor retinaculum strip off the bone together; tibialis posterior may herniate into the gap.
- A small lateral malleolar fleck fracture raises the likelihood of peroneal tendon instability.

### Peroneal Tendons and Retinacula
- Boomerang sign: split tear of peroneus brevis, which wraps around peroneus longus (longus may pass through the split).
- Superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) tear: tendons sublux or dislocate anteriorly, causing lateral snapping (often dynamic, best shown on ultrasound). SPR injuries are graded 1–4.
- Inferior peroneal retinaculum thickening, peroneal tenosynovitis; accessory peroneus quartus is a normal variant.

### Osteochondral Lesions of the Talus
- Common after sprains (high or low); grade 1 injuries (marrow oedema, cartilage delamination) are invisible on X-ray.
- Location is described with a 9-zone grid; zones 4 (medial) and 6 (lateral) lesions often need repair (as taught).

| Berndt and Harty stage | MRI finding |
|---|---|
| I | Subchondral compression (marrow oedema) |
| IIa | Subchondral cyst |
| IIb | Incomplete separation of the fragment |
| III | Complete separation, not displaced |
| IV | Displaced (or inverted) fragment |

### Other Conditions and Cases

| Condition | Key imaging points |
|---|---|
| Achilles tendinosis / insertional disease | Thick Achilles, calcaneal spur, undersurface tear, retrocalcaneal effusion, calcific tendinopathy; ultrasound is enough for most Achilles problems |
| Plantar fasciosis | Thickened plantar fascia |
| Runner with forefoot pain | Stress reaction/fracture of the metatarsals, sesamoid fracture; Freiberg infraction (flattened metatarsal head, sclerosis) |
| Soft-tissue tumours | Synovial sarcoma likes the foot and ankle near small joints; fibromatosis; giant cell tumour of tendon sheath shows dark (haemosiderin) blooming on GRE |
| Talocalcaneal coalition | C-sign on the lateral X-ray (continuous posterior talus and sustentaculum) is diagnostic; cysts, hypertrophy, stress oedema; CT classification types 1–5 |
| Calcaneonavicular coalition | Elongated anterior process of calcaneum ("anteater") or of the navicular; dorsal talar beaking; may be fibrous or osseous; pes planus |
| Anterolateral process fracture of the calcaneum | Look for an underlying calcaneonavicular coalition |
| Plantar fascia avulsion (pole vaulter falling on the heel) | Marrow oedema, avulsion of the lateral cord, oedema of abductor digiti minimi and flexor digitorum brevis |
| Tingling in the medial sole after ATFL repair | Accessory flexor digitorum accessorius longus in |
| (modified Broström) | the tarsal tunnel |
| Posterior ankle impingement | Stieda process / os trigonum; after excision, scarring and posterior talocalcaneal chondral changes; ultrasound-guided fenestration and steroid into the scar can help |
| Failed OATS for a talar OCL | Inadequate bony "shouldering" around the graft |

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- ATFL on axial images; deltoid and CFL on coronal images.
- ATFL tears first, then CFL; PTFL rarely.
- Talar tilt over about 4° on bilateral varus stress views = lateral ligament insufficiency.
- Syndesmosis: clear space over 5 mm, 1 cm above the joint; posterior malleolar oedema is a clue.
- Boomerang sign = peroneus brevis split tear.
- C-sign = talocalcaneal coalition.
- GRE blooming = giant cell tumour of tendon sheath (haemosiderin).

### Take-Home Summary
- Read the ankle in a fixed order (sagittal bones, coronal medial side and talar dome, axial lateral and syndesmotic ligaments and tendons), never miss a high ankle sprain, and correlate scarred-looking ligaments with stress views and the clinical picture.
