Giant Cell Tumor of Bone – Dr. Rajat Gupta
Overview
Giant cell tumor (GCT) is a benign aggressive bone tumor commonly affecting young
adults. Despite being benign, it behaves locally aggressively and may recur. A
systematic clinical, radiological and pathological approach is essential for diagnosis and
management.
Faculty Remark: “Not every giant cell rich lesion is a giant cell tumor. Always
correlate with imaging and clinical context.”

## Epidemiology & Key Characteristics
### General Features
- Age: 20–40 years most common.
- Female predominance.
- Accounts for 5 percent of all bone neoplasms.
- More common in Indian and Chinese populations.
- Rarely multicentric (2 percent) either:
  - Synchronous or
  - Metachronous
- Lung metastasis in 2–3 percent, usually in recurrent or neglected cases.
### Common Locations
- Around the knee (distal femur, proximal tibia)
- Distal radius
- Proximal humerus

## Radiology
### Typical Radiographic Features
- Epiphyseal in skeletally mature.
- Metaphyseal in skeletally immature.
- Geographic, eccentric lytic lesion.
- No matrix mineralization.
- Narrow zone of transition.
- No periosteal reaction.
- May have soft tissue extension.

<!-- page 2 -->

- Soap bubble appearance classic.
- Characteristically no sclerotic rim.
### Campanacci Grading

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| 1 | Well marginated borders |
| 2 | Cortical expansion, cortex intact |
| 3 | Cortical breach with soft tissue extension |

### Important Differentials
- ABC (look for fluid–fluid levels) – primary vs secondary.
- Chondroblastoma.
- Clear cell chondrosarcoma.
- Brown tumor (check serum calcium, PTH).
- Giant cell rich osteosarcoma.

## MRI Characteristics
- Epiphyseal–metaphyseal, eccentric.
- T1: hypointense.
- T2: hyperintense or mixed.
- Solid component usually present.
- May have secondary ABC (fluid levels).
- Assess soft tissue involvement.

## Work Up
### Blood Tests
- Serum calcium is mandatory.
- Alkaline phosphatase, PTH when needed.
### Biopsy Principles
- GCT must be confirmed histologically.
- “Giant cell rich lesion” is not equivalent to GCT.
- Must distinguish from giant cell rich osteosarcoma, ABC, brown tumor.
Faculty Remark: “Always insist on a definite diagnosis. A vague ‘giant cell rich’
report is not acceptable.”

## Treatment Modalities

### Surgical
Extended Curettage
Steps
1. Large cortical window.
2. Thorough curettage.
3. High speed burr – critical step.
4. Pulse lavage.
5. Use of adjuvants (makes it extended curettage).

Adjuvants

| Adjuvant | Penetration | Advantages | Problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquid nitrogen | 14 mm | Maximal penetration | Fractures, nerve injury |
| Phenol | 1 mm | Less harmful | Soft tissue irritation |
| Argon beam | 4 mm | Coagulation effect | Limited availability |
| Hydrogen peroxide | Direct effect | Safe, commonly used | Limited penetration |

Reconstruction After Curettage
### Bone Graft
- Biological.
- Donor site morbidity.
- Delayed weight bearing.
- Hard to detect recurrence.
Cement (PMMA)
- Immediate availability.
- Allows early weight bearing.
- Easier detection of recurrence.
- Not proven to cause arthritis. Subchondral bone loss is the main risk factor.

### Sandwich Technique
- Graft under subchondral bone, cement filling below.
- Combines benefits of both.
- Risk: Subchondral collapse.

<!-- page 4 -->

### Resection – Indications
- Pathological fracture with deformity.
- Recurrent tumor with joint destruction.
- Insufficient residual bone.
- Expendable bones: distal ulna, proximal fibula.
### Reconstruction Options
- Megaprosthesis.
- Biological reconstruction (fibular arthroplasty, ulnar translocation).
### Distal Radius Reconstruction

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Fibular arthroplasty | Good wrist motion, cosmetically good | Unstable joint, donor morbidity, incongruent joint |
| Ulnar translocation | Stable joint, vascular graft, no donor morbidity | Cosmetic deformity, hour glass wrist |

## Non Surgical Modalities
Angioembolization
- Reduces tumor blood supply.
- Used preoperatively to reduce blood loss.
- Definitive treatment for inoperable GCT (pelvis, sacrum, spine).
- Often combined with bisphosphonates.

### Zoledronic Acid
- 4 mg monthly.
- Causes apoptosis of osteoclasts.
- Helpful in reducing recurrence risk.
- Role in inoperable tumors.

Denosumab
- Fully human monoclonal antibody against RANKL.
- Loading dose Day 1, 8, 15, then monthly (varies).
Effects
- Reduces pain.
- Decreases vascularity.
- Produces hard shell that eases dissection.

<!-- page 5 -->

Concerns
- May increase recurrence after curettage.
- Not useful as adjuvant post curettage.
- Stromal cells remain viable.
- Rare reports of sarcomatous transformation.
- Histology becomes misleading – pathologist must be informed.

## Special Situations
### Pathological Fracture
- Not an absolute indication for resection.
- Many cases can undergo curettage + cementing.
- Resection only when deformity or joint destruction present.
### Recurrent GCT
- Curettage still possible in many cases.
- Resection when joint is destroyed.
### Pelvic GCT
- Often requires embolization + limited resection + curettage.

### Exam Pearls
- GCT is epiphyseal in mature skeletons.
- No sclerotic rim, soap bubble appearance.
- ABC vs GCT: look for solid component.
- Always check serum calcium to rule out brown tumor.
- Curettage must include high speed burr.
- Cement does not cause arthritis; loss of subchondral bone does.
- Pathological fracture is not absolute indication for resection.
- Denosumab improves handling but may increase recurrence after curettage.

Final Faculty Insight: “GCT management is all about choosing the right surgery for
the right patient. Curettage done well is better than resection done unnecessarily.”
