Overtraining Syndrome: Recognition, Prevention and
Management – Dr. Shikha Dhaundiyal (Sep 2025)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Continues from RED-S (relative energy deficiency in sport): athletes of either sex may present not with an injury but with vague, "bizarre" symptoms of failed physiological adaptation.
- Training: a process of OVERLOAD that disturbs homeostasis and causes acute fatigue; with adequate recovery it leads to improved performance (adaptation, supercompensation).
- Overreaching and overtraining form a continuum: functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, overtraining syndrome (OTS).
- There is NO universal diagnostic test or criterion for OTS: it is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion based on history.

The Training Continuum (Meeusen et al. 2006)

| State | Process | Performance | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training (overload) | Overload | Acute fatigue, then increase | Days |
| Functional overreaching (FOR) | Intensified training, short term | Temporary decrement, then SUPERCOMPENSATI ON (increase) | Days to about 2 weeks |

### Overtraining Syndrome
- Definition: maladapted response to excessive exercise without adequate rest, disturbing multiple systems (neurological, endocrine, immune) with mood changes.
- Hallmark: INABILITY TO SUSTAIN INTENSE EXERCISE and decreased SPORT- SPECIFIC performance even when the training load is maintained or increased ("unexplained underperformance syndrome").
- Prevalence: highest in high-volume endurance sports (swimming, triathlon, road cycling, rowing): e.g. 4–6 h a day, 6 days a week for months without scheduled recovery days; also gym-goers doing strength training 6 days a week.

### Risk Factors
- Training error: imbalance between load and recovery (biggest factor); too many competitions; monotonous training (also causes overuse injuries); personal, emotional and psychological problems; emotional demands of work/occupation; altitude and heat exposure; illness; low energy availability.

### Physiology and Markers
- Changes involve hormonal, cardiovascular, metabolic, muscle and immune domains (e.g. reduced maximal HR and VO2max time to exhaustion, altered resting HR).
- Heart rate variability: reflects autonomic balance (higher HRV = more vagal tone); may rise, fall or not change, so it is NOT a diagnostic marker, but useful as part of exclusion and monitoring.
- Biochemistry: muscle glycogen often normal, blood glucose usually unchanged (glucose:insulin ratio for insulin resistance), lactate, CK, urea, glutamine, CRP, ferritin, electrolytes: supportive for EXCLUDING other causes, not diagnostic of OTS.

Hypotheses (Multifactorial)

| Hypothesis | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Glycogen | Prolonged intense training depletes muscle glycogen: fatigue, reduced time to exhaustion, catabolic state (reduced glucose transport, hepatic dysfunction, low IGF-1, reduced anabolic signalling). Carbohydrate repletion speeds recovery (24–48 h) and reduces DOMS; raises RPE (Borg scale) |
| Central fatigue (serotonin) | Low glycogen raises free fatty acids, which displace tryptophan from albumin; free tryptophan competes with branched-chain amino acids to enter the brain, raising 5-HT (increased receptor sensitivity). BCAA and carbohydrate supplementation may reduce central fatigue |
| Glutamine | Glutamine (most abundant free amino acid; immune cell fuel) falls after prolonged (over 2 h) or repeated intense exercise: immunosuppression, infections |
| Oxidative stress | Reactive oxygen species: muscle damage, reduced citrate synthase, mitochondrial dysfunction, early fatigue; need antioxidants and periodised training |

### Clinical Diagnosis
- OTS is diagnosed mainly from history:
- 1. Decreased performance persisting for weeks to months DESPITE recovery.
- 2. Disturbances in MOOD.
- 3. Absence of other medical conditions explaining underperformance (anaemia, thyroid disease, infection, asthma, diabetes, malnutrition, etc.).
- Note: if the athlete has not had a period of rest or recovery, OTS cannot be diagnosed.
- Complaints: inability to meet prior performance standards, prolonged recovery, weight loss, raised resting HR, frequent injuries, sleep disorder, emotional instability, apathy.
- Always take an extensive nutritional history (low energy availability, macro- and micronutrients).

### Monitoring: Training Diary
- Daily record: session type, duration and intensity, sleep hours, fatigue (1–10), mood (good/neutral/low), soreness, resting HR and RPE (Borg 6–20 scale).
- Team practice (e.g. with an IPL team): wellness questionnaire 6–8 h before the match, total quality recovery (TQR) 1 h before, GPS during, RPE 30 minutes after.

Prevention
- Periodisation of training (preparation, conditioning, competition and recovery phases) and tapering before competition.
- Adjust volume and intensity according to performance and mood; avoid monotony.
- Adequate calories, carbohydrate, hydration and sleep; manage life stress; mental toughness and resilience.
- Rest period of over 6 hours between exercise bouts; at least 1 rest day a week; record training diaries; re-screen after illness before returning to load.

Management
- No proven pharmacological treatment; treat according to symptoms with a team (physician, psychologist, nutritionist, coach).
- Initial assessment: rule out organic disease; decide FOR vs NFOR vs OTS.
- FOR: restore the balance between load and recovery.
- NFOR/OTS: rest (at least 2 weeks), then reassess; if symptoms improve, gradual re- introduction: start with very low volume (e.g. 5–10 min/day), increase volume before intensity as tolerated, monitor with the training diary; seek psychological support.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- FOR recovers within about 2 weeks with supercompensation; NFOR weeks to months; OTS months or more.
- Parasympathetic OTS in endurance sports, sympathetic OTS in anaerobic sports.
- No biomarker is diagnostic; OTS is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Central fatigue: tryptophan, serotonin, BCAA competition.

### Take-Home Summary
- Suspect overtraining when sport-specific performance falls despite rest and is accompanied by mood change; exclude medical causes, rest the athlete, rebuild load gradually with monitoring, and prevent it with periodisation, nutrition and recovery.
