Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – First-Time Shoulder Dislocation
Faculty: Dr. Anurag Tiwari

### OVERVIEW
- First-time shoulder dislocation = acute traumatic anterior dislocation of the glenohumeral joint occurring after initial trauma.
- Commonly affects young, active males (especially <25 years, contact sports).
- The shoulder sacrifices stability for mobility → most unstable joint of the body.
- Important to understand anatomy, mechanism, imaging, and principles of reduction to manage effectively and prevent recurrence. “The first dislocation teaches us anatomy; the recurrent one teaches us biomechanics.” – Dr. Anurag Tiwari

ANATOMY & STABILIZERS OF THE SHOULDER
### 1⃣ Labrum
- Fibrocartilaginous rim attached to glenoid margin.
- Functions:
  - Deepens the glenoid socket (~50% increase in depth).
  - Enhances joint stability (concavity–compression effect).
- Injury: Detachment of anteroinferior labrum = Bankart lesion, hallmark of recurrent instability.

- Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres Minor, Subscapularis.

- Space between Supraspinatus & Subscapularis tendons.

- Stabilizes long head of biceps and resists inferior translation.

### RISK FACTORS FOR RECURRENT DISLOCATION
- Age <20 years: Highest recurrence risk (~70–90%).
- Generalized laxity: Beighton score ≥5/9.
- Previous shoulder injury.
- Contact/overhead sports.
- High-demand occupation. Prognostic pearl: Younger the patient → greater the recurrence risk.

### CLINICAL FEATURES
- Pain, swelling, and deformity after trauma.
- Loss of shoulder contour (flattened deltoid).
- Typical limb attitudes:
  - Anterior dislocation: Abduction + ER.
  - Posterior dislocation: Adduction + IR (light bulb sign).
  - Inferior dislocation: Hyperabduction (arm locked overhead).
- Neurological: Test axillary nerve (sensation over deltoid patch).
- Vascular: Check brachial/axillary artery pulses.

Commonest nerve injured: Axillary nerve.

### ASSOCIATED INJURIES
- # Greater tuberosity (GT): → Anterior dislocation (posterolateral avulsion).
- # Lesser tuberosity (LT): → Posterior dislocation.
- Axillary nerve or artery injury.
- Bony Bankart / Hill–Sachs lesions.

RADIOGRAPHIC EVALUATION
### Standard Views

| View | Utility |
|---|---|
| Grashey (True AP) | True GH alignment view |
| Axillary Lateral | Best for confirming direction of dislocation |
| Scapular Y View | Defines anterior vs posterior dislocation |
| Stryker Notch View | Detects Hill–Sachs lesion |
| West Point View | Detects Bony Bankart lesion |

Mnemonic: Stryker → Humeral head; West Point → Glenoid rim.
### CT / MRI Indications
1. Diagnostic uncertainty on X-ray.
2. Associated fracture (GT/LT or glenoid rim).
3. Quantify glenoid bone loss.
4. Evaluate Hill–Sachs or Bankart lesion.
5. Young athlete (<25 yrs) → consider early CT/MRI to plan preventive repair. Critical bone loss: 20–25% → failure risk for soft-tissue repair (may need Latarjet).

### 2⃣ Immobilization
- Traditional: 3 weeks in adduction–IR.
- Current concept: 1 week in external rotation – promotes anterior capsulolabral healing & reduces stiffness.
- Prolonged immobilization (>3 weeks) → stiffness, especially in elderly. Practical approach: 1 week ER brace for young; tailor based on age & compliance.

3⃣ Operative Indications (for first episode)
- Associated fracture (#GT, #LT, glenoid rim).
- Bony Bankart lesion.
- Rotator cuff tear (esp. >40 yrs).
- Failure of closed reduction.
- High-demand young athlete (<25 yrs) with Bankart on MRI → consider early arthroscopic Bankart repair. Deltopectoral approach used if open reduction required.

### REHABILITATION

| Phase | Duration | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | 0–1 week | Immobilization in ER brace |
| Phase 2 | 1–3 weeks | Gentle pendulum & assisted ROM |
| Phase 3 | 3–6 weeks | Active ROM, avoid extreme ER |
| Phase 4 | >6 weeks | Strengthening & proprioception |

Gradual return to sport ~3 months if stable.

### EXAM & VIVA PEARLS
- Most common dislocation: Anterior (95%).
- Most common nerve: Axillary nerve.
- Typical deformity: Arm abducted & externally rotated.
- Dugas’ sign: Cannot touch opposite shoulder.
- Hill–Sachs: Posterior–superior humeral head indentation.
- Bony Bankart: Glenoid rim fracture.
- Critical bone loss: >20–25%.
- Preferred immobilization: 1 week ER brace.
- Kocher’s sequence: Traction–ER–Abduction–IR.
- TUBS vs AMBRI: Traumatic vs Atraumatic.
- CT scan: Quantifies bone loss; MRI – soft tissue.
