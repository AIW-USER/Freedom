Hip Dislocation and Femoral Head Fractures –
Dr. Anurag Tiwari
Overview
Hip dislocation is a true orthopedic emergency and must be reduced immediately to
prevent avascular necrosis. Femoral head fractures often accompany dislocations,
especially in high energy trauma. Accurate assessment of limb position, associated
injuries, proper imaging, and timely reduction define outcomes.
Faculty Remark: “Hip dislocation reduction is a race against time. Every minute
counts.”

## Anatomy Essentials
### Osseous and Soft Tissue Anatomy
- Hip joint is a ball and socket joint, highly stable due to bony congruence and ligamentous support.
- Femoral head covered by acetabular socket by about 40 percent; labrum adds another 10 percent.
Ligaments
- Iliofemoral ligament: strongest in the body, inverted Y shape, attached from AIIS to intertrochanteric line.
- Pubofemoral ligament: anterior and medial support.
- Ischiofemoral ligament: posterior support, attaches 1 to 1.5 cm proximal to intertrochanteric crest.
### Capsule Attachments
- Anterior: directly on intertrochanteric line.
- Posterior: proximal to intertrochanteric crest.
### Sciatic Nerve and Piriformis Variation
- In 80 to 90 percent, sciatic nerve exits inferior to piriformis.
- In 10 to 20 percent, it pierces or passes above piriformis.
- Important for approach planning and understanding nerve palsy risk.

## Spectrum of Hip Dislocations
Types
- Posterior (most common, 90 percent).
- Anterior (10 percent).
- Central fracture dislocation (acetabular floor disruption).
### Pure vs Fracture Dislocations
- Pure dislocation: Hip in flexion, adduction, internal rotation.
- Fracture dislocation: Less flexion, less adduction, leads to impact on posterior wall causing fracture.

## Mechanism of Injury
### Posterior Dislocation
- Classic dashboard injury.
- Flexion, adduction, internal rotation.
### Anterior Dislocation
- Hyperabduction and external rotation.
- Superior (pubic) vs Inferior (obturator) type depending on flexion angle.

## Clinical Features
### Posterior Dislocation
- Limb in flexion, adduction, internal rotation.
- Shortening.
- Palpable femoral head in gluteal region.
- Vascular sign of Narath: Feeble or absent femoral pulse due to lack of counter surface.
- Sciatic nerve injury causing foot drop.
### Why Foot Drop?
1. Common peroneal component is superficial.
2. Fibers are phylogenetically newer and more sensitive to stretch and ischemia.
### Anterior Dislocation
- Limb in abduction, external rotation.
- Femoral nerve or vessel compression possible.

## Imaging
X ray
### Pre Reduction
- Single AP pelvis with both hips sufficient.
- Key findings:
  - Loss of congruency.
  - Femoral head size: smaller in posterior, larger in anterior dislocation.
  - Shenton line broken.
  - LT less visible in internal rotation (posterior).
  - Look for fragments in joint.
Post Reduction

CT Scan
Five essential views: AP, Inlet, Outlet, Iliac oblique, Obturator oblique.

- Mandatory post reduction.
- Checks congruency, intra articular fragments, femoral head fracture, acetabular

MRI
involvement.

- Not routinely needed acutely.
- AVN seen at 6 to 8 weeks.
- Early prediction possible if obturator externus injury noted.

## Reduction Techniques
Closed Reduction Methods
Mnemonic: SABER - Stimson - Allis - Bigelow - East Baltimore - Rochester

Principles
- Continuous traction, no jerky movements.
Techniques
- Allis: Most common, traction in flexion.
- Walker modification: For anterior dislocations.
- Bigelow: Traction in deformity then abduct and externally rotate.
- Rochester: Needs both hips flexed, not suitable in bilateral injuries.
- Stimson: Prone, gravity assisted.

## Open Reduction
Indications
- Failure of two closed reduction attempts.
- Intra articular fragments.
- Instability post reduction.
- Femoral head fracture requiring fixation.
- Iatrogenic sciatic nerve palsy.
Approaches
- Anterior dislocation: Smith Peterson.
- Posterior dislocation: Surgical safe dislocation (Ganz).
- Posterior wall fracture: Kocher Langenbeck.

## Femoral Head Fracture Management
### Pipkin Type 1
- Small fragments can sometimes be left.
- Large weight bearing fragments require fixation.
### Pipkin Type 2
- Requires anatomic fixation due to weight bearing area involvement.
### Pipkin Type 3
- High risk of AVN.
- Often managed by arthroplasty in older adults.
### Pipkin Type 4
- Address acetabular fracture in addition to head.

## Complications
Early
- Sciatic nerve injury (common peroneal component).
- Femoral nerve injury.

Late
- Femoral vessel injury.

- AVN.
- Post traumatic arthritis.
- Heterotopic ossification (more common with anterior OR).
- Instability.

### Viva and Exam Pearls
- Posterior dislocation is most common.
- Foot drop due to peroneal component injury.
- Pre reduction X ray: single AP view enough.
- Post reduction: five views essential.
- Pipkin classification is for femoral head fractures.
- Early reduction (within 6 hours) reduces AVN risk.
- Obturator externus injury on MRI predicts AVN.

Final Faculty Insight: “Respect the soft tissues, reduce early, assess stability, and
you will get predictable results.”
