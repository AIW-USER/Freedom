### Overview / Key Concepts
- Second lecture of the scoliosis series (after clinical assessment; treatment comes next). Modalities: X-ray (basic), CT and MRI.
- Scoliosis is a THREE-dimensional deformity: coronal curve, sagittal deformity (kyphosis) and vertebral rotation (maximum at the apex).
- Alarm sign: the commonest curve in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is RIGHT thoracic. A LEFT-sided (thoracic) curve should make you suspect a neural anomaly (e.g. Arnold-Chiari malformation with syrinx) and get an MRI.

### X-ray Views

| View | Details |
|---|---|
| Whole spine standing AP | From the skull/CV junction to the pelvis and hip joints on a long cassette (or thoracic and lumbar films stitched by software). Never only a lumbar or dorsal film. |
| Whole spine standing lateral | Sagittal profile (kyphosis, lordosis). |
| Why STANDING | The deformity shows its true size only under gravity: a 30 degree standing curve may measure 24 to 25 degrees lying down. |
| Side-bending views (supine) | Taken lying down, bending to each side; show flexibility (structural vs non-structural). Mainly before surgery. |
| Traction view | For large curves (about 100 degrees or more) where costo-iliac impingement (ribs against the iliac crest) blocks a true bending film. Patient lifted gently or pulled supine (one assistant at the head, one at the legs). Example: 100 degrees corrected to 60 degrees = flexible. |
| Fulcrum bending view | Patient lies over a bolster/pillow; used for kyphosis (e.g. Scheuermann) flexibility. |
| Stagnara view | For severely rotated curves where the AP film hides the curve: the X-ray BEAM (not the patient) is rotated to face the true plane of the curve. |

- On the standing AP film assess: plane and side of deformity, severity, primary and secondary curves, apex, end vertebrae, neutral and stable vertebrae, curve magnitude (Cobb angle), coronal balance.

### Curves and Key Vertebrae

| Term | Definition / how to find it |
|---|---|
| Primary curve | First to appear, largest Cobb angle, stiffer; leaves the patient imbalanced. |
| Secondary (compensatory) curves | Develop above and below to balance the primary curve; smaller and more flexible. |
| Curve regions | Proximal thoracic, main thoracic, thoracolumbar/lumbar; a curve is named by the level of its apex. |
| Apical vertebra (apex) | Vertebra (or disc) with the greatest deviation from the centre line, like the peak of a mountain; also the most rotated. |
| End vertebrae | Upper and lower vertebrae with MAXIMUM TILT toward the concavity (endplate lines; use the pedicles if endplates are not seen). Example in the lecture: T7 to L2. |
| Neutral vertebra | Part of the curve but NOT rotated: pedicles equidistant from the spinous process. Fusion should end at a neutral vertebra. |
| Stable vertebra | Most proximal vertebra bisected (or nearly) by the CSVL (central sacral vertical line, drawn perpendicular to the line across the iliac crests through the centre of the sacrum). Example: L4. |

Cobb angle
- Draw lines along the SUPERIOR endplate of the upper end vertebra and the INFERIOR endplate of the lower end vertebra (pedicle borders if endplates are not seen); the Cobb angle

is the angle between these lines or their perpendiculars. Software can calculate it once the end

- Scoliosis (Scoliosis Research Society): lateral curvature over 10 degrees measured by the Cobb method on a standing X-ray.
- Example: proximal thoracic 21, main thoracic 54 (primary), lumbar 30 degrees.

Structural vs non-structural curve
- Structural: side-bending Cobb angle stays 25 degrees or more. Non-structural: side-bending Cobb under 25 degrees.
- Example: main thoracic 59 to 43 degrees on bending (structural); proximal thoracic 29 to 19 degrees and lumbar curve correcting (non-structural).
- Why it matters: only structural curves are fused; non-structural curves fall in line after the structural curve is corrected.

Vertebral rotation
- Judged by the pedicles: in a neutral vertebra both pedicles are equidistant from the spinous process. With rotation the convex-side pedicle moves toward the centre and the concave-side pedicle disappears (grades neutral, +, ++, +++, ++++). Maximum rotation at the apex.

### Lenke Classification (Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis Only)
- Six curve types based on which curves are structural (side-bending 25 degrees or more); not meant for neuromuscular or congenital scoliosis. Lumbar modifier A, B, C and sagittal modifiers are added.

| Type | Name | Structural curves |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Main thoracic | Main thoracic only |
| 2 | Double thoracic | Proximal thoracic + main thoracic |
| 3 | Double major | Main thoracic (primary) + thoracolumbar/lumbar |
| 4 | Triple major | Proximal thoracic + main thoracic + thoracolumbar/lumbar |
| 5 | Thoracolumbar/lumbar | Thoracolumbar/lumbar only |
| 6 | Thoracolumbar/lumbar with main thoracic | Thoracolumbar/lumbar is the PRIMARY (larger) curve + structural main thoracic |

- Type 3 vs type 6: both have structural thoracic and lumbar curves; in type 3 the main thoracic is primary, in type 6 the thoracolumbar/lumbar curve is primary.

### Assessing the Chance of Progression
- Progression depends on how much growth is left. Markers on X-ray: closure of the triradiate cartilage, fusion of the greater trochanter apophysis, Risser grade (elbow closure needs a separate film; usually not used).
- Risser grade (iliac apophysis): 0 none; 1 up to 25%; 2 up to 50%; 3 up to 75%; 4 complete; 5 fused to the ilium. Low Risser = much growth left = high risk of progression.
- Open triradiate cartilage, unfused greater trochanter and no iliac apophysis = patient has not yet entered/passed the growth spurt: high risk.
- Case 1: about 100 degree curve, triradiate cartilage still fusing, trochanter unfused, Risser 0: large growth potential, intervene.
- Case 2: 33 degree main thoracic curve (21 and 16 degrees above and below), triradiate cartilage and trochanter fused, Risser 3: near skeletal maturity, low risk; reassure and observe.
- Large curves can keep progressing even after skeletal maturity (example in the lecture: a 60 degree thoracolumbar curve in a mature woman).

### CT and MRI

| Investigation | Indications / findings |
|---|---|
| CT (with 3D) | Complex/congenital anomalies (shows the hemivertebra and exact apex for planning); pedicle size before screws (thin pedicles in neurofibromatosis); bony spur/bar of diastematomyelia. |
| MRI | Before every deformity surgery; neurological deficit or progressing deficit; absent (abdominal) reflexes; neurocutaneous markers (cafe-au-lait spots, dimple, tuft of hair); rapid progression; severe pain; foot deformity; age under 10 years; atypical curves (left thoracic, short-segment), wide canal or thin pedicles. |

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Always whole spine STANDING AP and lateral; bending films supine.
- Structural curve = side-bending Cobb 25 degrees or more; only structural curves are fused.

<!-- page 9 -->

- Traction view for curves around 100 degrees or more; Stagnara view = rotate the beam, not the patient.
- End vertebra = maximal tilt; neutral vertebra = no rotation; stable vertebra = bisected by CSVL.
- Lenke: 1 MT, 2 double thoracic, 3 double major, 4 triple major, 5 TL/L, 6 TL/L with MT.
- Risser 0 to 5; low Risser + open triradiate cartilage = high risk of progression.
- Worst congenital pattern: unilateral unsegmented bar with contralateral hemivertebra.
- Left thoracic curve = MRI (Chiari/syrinx, tethered cord).

### Take-Home Summary
- Read the standing whole-spine films systematically (curves, apex, end, neutral and stable vertebrae, Cobb angles, rotation), test flexibility with bending or traction films, judge growth left with Risser and the triradiate cartilage, classify AIS with Lenke, and use CT for bony anatomy and MRI for the cord before any surgery.
