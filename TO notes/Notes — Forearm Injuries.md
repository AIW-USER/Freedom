Forearm Injuries – Dr. Anurag Tiwari

Overview
- The forearm extends from the elbow to the wrist and involves several potential fracture sites: olecranon, coronoid, radial head/neck, diaphyseal (both bones), Monteggia, Galeazzi, distal radius, and TFCC (Triangular Fibrocartilage Complex).
- In this lecture, Dr. Anurag focuses on Olecranon fractures, Both bone forearm fractures, Monteggia, and Galeazzi injuries.
Dr. Anurag’s Remark:
“The forearm acts as a joint. You can fix the bone perfectly, but if rotation is lost, you’ve
lost the function.”

Anatomy & Biomechanics
### Axis of Forearm Rotation
- From: Center of radial head → Ulnar fovea.
- The ulna remains static, and the radius rotates around it during supination and pronation.
### Interosseous Membrane
- Direction: From proximal radius to distal ulna.
- Central band is strong, helps in load transfer and forearm stability.
- Ruptured in Essex–Lopresti injuries.
### Radial Bow

| Parameter | Description |
|---|---|
| Maximum Bow | At 60% of distance from biceps tuberosity to distal articular surface |
| Normal Value | 15 mm lateral bow |
| Importance | Restoring the bow is essential for full pronation-supination |

Treatment of Olecranon Fractures
Non-Operative
- For undisplaced fractures with intact extensor mechanism.
- Slab or cast in slight extension.
### Operative Indications
- Displacement >2 mm
- Extensor mechanism disruption
- Articular incongruity
- Elbow instability
- Open fracture

### Operative Techniques
1. Tension Band Wiring (TBW)

| Indication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Simple transverse fracture (Mayo 2A) | Most common fixation |
| Contraindications | Comminuted fracture, unstable elbow, distal to sigmoid notch |
| K-wire direction | Toward anteromedial cortex (not intramedullary) |

<!-- page 4 -->

| Indication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Tip | Engage opposite cortex for stability; bury wires under triceps to prevent prominence |

2. Intramedullary Screw Fixation
- 6.5 mm cancellous screw with washer ± TBW.
- Starting point: Center in AP plane, slightly lateral in ML plane.
- Hardware buried under triceps to prevent irritation.
3. Plate Fixation

| Plate Type | Indication |
|---|---|
| Pre-contoured locking plate | Gold standard (unstable/comminuted fractures) |
| 1/3rd tubular or recon plate | Alternative if locking plate unavailable |

Home Run Screw: Long screw from olecranon tip to anterior cortex for maximal
stability.
Avoid angle screw — causes prominence and soft-tissue irritation.

4. Fragment Excision + Triceps Advancement (Salvage Procedure)

| Indication | Condition |
|---|---|
| Comminuted, non-reconstructible | Elderly, osteoporotic bone |

fractures

| Failed ORIF | Low-demand patients |
|---|---|
| Coronoid must be intact | Fragment <75% of olecranon |

Tip: Attach triceps posteriorly to maximize lever arm and extension power.
Complications
- Prominent hardware (bury K-wires, avoid corner screw)
- Non-union / Malunion
- Heterotopic ossification
- Elbow stiffness

Both Bone Forearm Fractures
Mechanism

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Direct | Nightstick fracture (ulna) — defensive injury |
| Indirect | Bending → same level fracture; Rotational → different levels (spiral) |

### Displacement Pattern

| Fracture Level | Cast Position |
|---|---|
| Proximal third | Supination |
| Middle third | Neutral |
| Distal third | Pronation |

### Clinical Examination
- Always examine elbow (PRUJ) and wrist (DRUJ).
- Neurovascular check: Fist–Paper–Scissor–OK sign.
- Compartment syndrome: Most common site – Leg, 2nd most – Forearm.

Radiology
- Get entire forearm X-ray (one film: wrist to elbow).
- True AP & lateral views.
- Evaluate PRUJ (McLaughlin’s line) and DRUJ alignment.
- Ulnar variance >5 mm or styloid base fracture → suspect DRUJ injury.

### Treatment Principles

| Goal | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Length | Prevent shortening |
| Alignment | Maintain axis |
| Rotation | Most critical for function |

Dr. Anurag: “Forearm behaves like a joint. Absolute reduction and rigid fixation are
a must.”

Non-Operative
- Stable, undisplaced, isolated ulnar fractures (<50% displacement, <10° angulation).
- E.g., Nightstick fracture.

<!-- page 6 -->

Operative
- All other displaced or unstable fractures.
- Preferred method: ORIF with plate fixation.

### Fixation Choices

| Implant | Notes |
|---|---|
| 3.5 mm DCP / LCDCP plate | Gold standard (absolute stability) |
| Nail | For open, segmental, osteoporotic, or polytrauma cases |

Nail Length Calculation: * Olecranon to ulnar styloid = X →
Ulnar nail = X – 1 cm; Radial nail = X – 3 cm
Nail Entry Points: * Radius: Just lateral to Lister’s tubercle. * Ulna: Slightly lateral to
olecranon tip.
Hybrid Fixation: One bone plate + one bone nail in selected cases.

Monteggia Fracture
Definition | Proximal ulna fracture + PRUJ (proximal radioulnar joint) dislocation |
Classification | Bado Types I–IV (direction of radial head displacement = apex direction)
|
Most common type | Type II (posterior) in adults, Type I (anterior) in children |

### Jupiter Modification (of Bado Type II)
- 2A – Proximal (olecranon/coronoid)
- 2B – Metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction
- 2C – Diaphyseal
- 2D – Metadiaphyseal with extension
### Treatment Algorithm
1. ORIF of ulna with plate.
2. Check spontaneous reduction of radial head.
3. If persistent dislocation → correct PUDA (15°).
4. If still unreduced → open reduction (annular ligament interposition).

Galeazzi Fracture
Definition | Distal radius shaft fracture + DRUJ disruption |
Synonyms | Fracture of Necessity, Piedmont Fracture, Reverse Monteggia |
DRUJ Injury Signs | Ulnar styloid base fracture, DRUJ widening, ulnar variance >5 mm |

Classifications

| System | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Walsh / Rettig & | I | Within 7.5 cm of distal articular |
| Raskin | II | surface → High DRUJ instability >7.5 cm from articular surface |
| By Angulation | I II | Apex volar (volar dislocation of ulna) Apex dorsal (dorsal dislocation of ulna) |

<!-- page 8 -->

### Treatment Algorithm
1. ORIF of radius (plate).
2. Check DRUJ reduction:
  - Reduced + stable → No fixation needed.
  - Reduced but unstable → 2 K-wires parallel, 1 cm proximal to joint.
  - Still dislocated → Open reduction (check ECU/EDM interposition).
3. Immobilization:
  - Dorsal Galeazzi → Supination.
  - Volar Galeazzi → Pronation.

Complications

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Early | Compartment syndrome, nerve injury |
| Late | Non-union, malunion, synostosis |
| Synostosis risk factors | Head injury, same-level fractures, comminution, single incision, graft at interosseous border |

Pediatric Forearm Fractures
### Unique Features

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Plastic deformation | Bending without cortical break |
| Greenstick fracture | Incomplete fracture with one cortex intact |
| Torus (Buckle) fracture | Incomplete compression fracture, usually distal radius |

### Cast Index
- Formula: Sagittal width / Coronal width = 0.7 (ideal).
- Maintains interosseous molding and rotation.
Acceptable Angulation (≤10 years)

| Level | Acceptable Angulation |
|---|---|
| Distal third | ≤20° |
| Middle third | ≤15° |
| Proximal third | ≤10° |

|---|---|

### Key Exam Pearls
- Axis: Radial head to ulnar fovea.
- Forearm acts as a joint → Absolute fixation.
- TBW for simple olecranon #, plate for unstable/comminuted.
- PUDA – 15°, maintain for proper reduction in Monteggia.
- Type II Monteggia – Most common in adults.
- Galeazzi = Fracture of necessity.
- Cast index 0.7 for proper pediatric immobilization.
Final Faculty Remark:
“In forearm surgery, alignment unites bone, but restoring rotation unites function.”
