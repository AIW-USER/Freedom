Flexor Tendon Injuries and Reconstruction – Dr.
Priyanka Sharma (Jun 2026)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Anatomy of the long finger flexors, pulleys, Camper chiasma, blood supply and healing; zones of injury; jersey finger; repair techniques; two-stage (Hunter) reconstruction; anaesthesia for hand surgery; complications; rehabilitation protocols.
- Adhesions are the commonest complication, especially in zone II; every technical step (clean tendon ends, buried knots, sheath repair, early mobilisation) aims to prevent them.
- A2 and A4 pulleys must always be preserved or reconstructed to prevent bowstringing.

Anatomy
FDS FDP

| Number | 4 (index, middle, ring, little) | 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Nerve | Median nerve (all four) | Index and middle: anterior interosseous nerve (median); ring and little: ulnar nerve |
| Muscle bellies | Separate belly for each finger (independent) | One common belly dividing into four slips |
| Action | PIP flexion | DIP flexion |

- FPL: separate muscle belly to the thumb; flexes the IP joint.
- MP flexion comes from the intrinsics: the lumbricals arise from the radial side of each FDP tendon and insert into the dorsal extensor expansion: MP flexion with IP extension (intrinsic- plus position: MP 90° flexion, IPs straight).

Pulleys
- Fingers: five annular pulleys (A1–A5; rigid rings) and three cruciate pulleys (C1–C3; collapsible, flexible, allow motion).
- A2 (proximal phalanx) and A4 (middle phalanx) are the most important: they prevent bowstringing and must be repaired or reconstructed.
- Thumb: A1, oblique pulley (over the proximal phalanx, most important for preventing bowstringing) and A2.
- Congenital trigger thumb: tight A1 pulley; the child cannot extend the thumb (often noticed late because babies keep a fist). Release the A1 completely under sedation/local anaesthesia without damaging the next pulley.

Camper chiasma
- At the proximal phalanx the FDS splits; its two halves decussate (Camper chiasma) and insert on the middle phalanx near the PIP joint. FDP passes through the split and becomes SUPERFICIAL distal to it, inserting at the distal phalanx.
- Landmark in injuries at the proximal phalanx: the superficial tendon distal to the chiasma is the FDP.

Blood supply and healing
- Digital arteries give transverse branches to the vincula (longa and brevia) supplying each FDS and FDP; synovial fluid also nourishes the tendon. Repair the digital artery under magnification when injured.

| Intrinsic healing | Extrinsic healing |
|---|---|
| Tenocytes within the tendon and sheath | Inflammatory cells from outside (via vincula and synovial fluid) |
| Supple, vascular environment; preserves gliding | More adhesions |

The healing to promote: repair the sheath too

### Zones of Flexor Injury

| Zone | Extent | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| I | FDP insertion to FDS insertion | FDP only: single tendon, good results |
| II ("no man's land", Bunnell) | FDS insertion to the proximal edge of the A1 pulley | FDS and FDP in the same tight sheath: adhesions, hardest recovery. Repair BOTH tendons in clean lacerations |
| III | A1 pulley to the distal edge of the carpal tunnel | Lumbrical origins: find and repair them |
| IV | Carpal tunnel | 9 tendons (4 FDS, 4 FDP, FPL) + median nerve |
| V | Forearm proximal to the carpal tunnel | Deep cuts can divide all tendons, median and ulnar nerves and both arteries: "spaghetti wrist" |

### Jersey Finger
- Closed avulsion of FDP from the distal phalanx when a flexed finger grabbing a jersey is forcibly extended (rugby, football): loss of active DIP flexion; the opposite of mallet finger.
- Unlike mallet finger, splinting does not work because the tendon retracts: explore and reinsert.
- Reinsertion options: pull-out suture over a button on the nail plate (or at the fingertip) for 6– 8 weeks, K-wire, or mini suture anchors through a zig-zag exposure.

### Exploration Incisions
- Never a straight longitudinal incision across creases (flexion contracture, poor exposure).
- Use crease-based incisions with Z (proximal and distal) extensions, Bruner zig-zag incisions for the whole finger, or a mid-lateral (neutral line) incision, which also serves neurovascular repair; follow palmar and wrist creases with Z-extensions proximally.

### Primary Repair Technique
- Flexor tendons are cylindrical: use a core suture (not a simple or mattress stitch) plus a peripheral (epitendinous) running suture.
- Freshen both ends with a No. 11 blade; align the tendon orientation.
- Modified Kessler-Mason: 2- or 4-strand core stitch, 3-0 nylon (or Prolene), purchase 5–7 mm from the cut end; 5-0 nylon running coaptation stitch.
- Knots buried between the tendon ends, never on the surface; non-absorbable monofilament (no braided or absorbable sutures).
- Other techniques to know by name: Bunnell, cruciate, Savage, Tang (multi-strand), modified Becker, 4-, 6- and 8-strand repairs. Any works if the principles are followed.

### Two-Stage Flexor Reconstruction (Hunter-Salisbury)
- Indications: delayed presentation, tendon loss, repeated adhesions, collapsed sheath (no bed for a primary graft).
- Prerequisites: supple skin (provide skin cover first), at least protective sensation, adequate vascularity, full passive range of motion of the joints (physiotherapy first if stiff).
- Stage 1: silicone (Hunter) rod sutured distally to the FDP stump and passed to the forearm/palm to create a pseudosheath; reconstruct A2/A4 pulleys with tendon graft; full passive range on the table; physiotherapy.
- Stage 2 (after 3–4 months): retrieve the rod while railroading a tendon graft through the mesothelium-lined pseudosheath; check free gliding through the full range of fingers and wrist.
- Pulvertaft weave: the graft is woven through the native tendon for the junction (not a Kessler core suture).

### Anaesthesia for Hand Surgery

| Technique | Key points |
|---|---|
| WALANT (wide-awake local anaesthesia, no tourniquet) | Lignocaine 2% with adrenaline + sodium bicarbonate (bupivacaine for longer cases); the patient can move the finger to test the repair |
| Bier block (intravenous regional anaesthesia) | Exsanguinate with an Esmarch bandage; double- cuff tourniquet inflated to SBP + 100 mmHg (or 2.5 x SBP); 0.5% plain lidocaine about 1 mL/kg through a distal IV cannula; wait 6–8 min; release the distal cuff and keep the proximal one. Never release early (hypotension, convulsions, arrhythmia, death); bupivacaine contraindicated |
| Axillary block | Supine, arm abducted and externally rotated; inject above and below the axillary artery; does not block the intercostobrachial nerve |
| Supraclavicular block | At the brachial plexus in the scalene region; anaesthetises the whole upper limb; ultrasound guidance preferred |

Complications

| Complication | Points |
|---|---|
| Adhesions (commonest) | Mostly zone II; physiotherapy first, tenolysis if it fails (more handling can cause more adhesions) |
| Rupture | Weak repair or too vigorous early motion |
| Joint contracture | Prolonged flexed splinting with delayed therapy |
| Triggering | Over-tight pulley repair or a nodular repair site |
| Swan neck | Intrinsic imbalance: PIP hyperextension, DIP flexion |
| Lumbrical-plus finger | FDP divided distal to the lumbrical origin: FDP pull goes through the lumbrical: paradoxical IP |

Rehabilitation
- Dorsal blocking splint for 4–6 weeks (POP, then thermoplastic): MP 80–90° flexion, IPs straight.
- Supervised early active-assisted motion (EAM) within the splint, especially after zone II repairs; a hand therapist is invaluable.

| Protocol | Splint | Motion | Force / excursion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duran | Static dorsal blocking: wrist slight flexion, MP about 45°, IPs near extension | Controlled passive motion: isolated DIP, isolated PIP, then combined | Low force, low excursion |
| Kleinert | Dorsal splint (wrist about 30° flexion, MP 30–40°) with rubber- band traction from the nails to the wrist (palmar pulley added) | Active extension against the band, passive flexion by the band; bands off at night with fingers strapped straight | Low force, low excursion |
| Strickland (place and hold) | Hinged wrist splint (wrist 20° flexion, MP 50°) | Tenodesis: passive flexion, then the patient holds the fingers flexed about 5 s with minimal effort | Moderate force, higher excursion |
| Mayo synergistic | Hinged | Active wrist motion with finger motion | Low force, high excursion |

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- FDS: median nerve, separate bellies, PIP flexion; FDP: dual nerve, common belly, DIP flexion.
- A2 and A4 (fingers), oblique pulley (thumb) prevent bowstringing.
- Distal to Camper chiasma the FDP is superficial.
- Intrinsic healing preserves gliding.
- Zone II = Bunnell's no man's land; repair both tendons.
- Jersey finger: FDP avulsion; needs reinsertion, not a splint.
- Core 3-0 + epitendinous 5-0; purchase 5–7 mm; buried knots.
- Two-stage Hunter rod: stage 2 at 3–4 months; prerequisites: supple skin, sensation, vascularity, full passive ROM.
- Lumbrical-plus: paradoxical IP extension; quadriga: flexion lag of neighbours.

- Bier block: SBP + 100 mmHg or 2.5 x SBP, no bupivacaine.

### Take-Home Summary
- Know the zone to predict the structures and outcome; repair with strong core sutures, preserve pulleys and sheath, and start protected early motion with an appropriate protocol to avoid adhesions.
