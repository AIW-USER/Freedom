### Overview / Key Concepts
- The spinal column exists to protect the spinal cord; stability depends on bone AND ligaments, especially the posterior ligamentous complex (PLC).
- Bangle analogy: a ring broken at one place stays in position (stable); broken at two places it separates (unstable). Same idea as the Denis three-column concept.
- Assessment runs in four steps: accident site response, emergency room management, imaging, treatment strategy.
- TLICS (morphology + PLC + neurology) guides the decision to operate; the AO type guides HOW to operate (short or long segment, need for fusion).
- Exam favourites: neurogenic vs spinal shock, ASIA grading, TLICS scoring, levels of fixation, ligamentotaxis.

Biomechanics

| Region | Features | Typical injury |
|---|---|---|
| T1–T8 | Relatively rigid (rib cage), kyphotic | Flexion injuries predominate |
| T9–L2 (thoracolumbar junction) | Transition from rigid to mobile and from kyphosis to lordosis; high stress | Most injuries occur here |
| L3–sacrum | Mobile, lordotic | Axial load injuries predominate |

- Crane analogy: the centre of gravity lies in front of the spine, so the anterior column is under compression and the posterior elements act like the crane cable under tension. If the posterior tension band fails, the whole system collapses. Always look at the posterior elements on the X-ray (spinous process fracture, widened interspinous gap).
- Instability (White and Panjabi): loss of the ability of the spine under physiological loads to keep the relationship between vertebrae so that there is no damage or irritation to the cord or roots and no incapacitating deformity or pain. In simple words: on standing, walking or bending there should be no abnormal movement, subluxation or dislocation.

### Accident Site and Emergency Room
- At the site: never make a patient with back pain or weakness sit up or drink water; log-roll, wait for the ambulance and move on a spine board. Log-roll in hospital too (bed, X-ray, MRI). Immobilise the cervical spine (collar) if there is neck pain, upper limb weakness, facial injury or an unconscious patient.
- Airway and breathing: high cervical injury (C3, C4, C5) paralyses the phrenic nerve and diaphragm (paradoxical breathing). More than half of thoracic (T1–T8/T9) fractures have rib or chest injury; look for haemothorax, pneumothorax and diaphragm rupture (stomach shadow in the chest).
- Circulation: a cord injury patient can be hypotensive from neurogenic shock (below).

- Bulbocavernosus reflex: squeezing the glans or pulling the catheter causes anal sphincter contraction (local reflex arc through the conus). Absent = still in spinal shock, so the neurological examination is not final; reassess (for example every 6 hours).
- Deficit that persists after spinal shock has ended = complete injury, poor prognosis.
- The bulbocavernosus reflex will not return in conus and cauda equina lesions.

Neurological examination and ASIA
- Motor power, sensation and reflexes. The key question: complete or incomplete injury (incomplete has a much better prognosis).
- Sacral sparing = perianal sensation, voluntary anal contraction, great toe flexion. Present = incomplete injury.

Imaging
- X-ray: never hesitate to X-ray a painful spinal segment; whole spine in the unconscious patient and in facial injury; detects about 80%.
- What to look at: vertebral body height (compare with the levels above and below), interpedicular distance (widening = burst), transverse process fractures, spinous process fracture or widening, and alignment: the anterior and posterior vertebral body lines and the spinous process line should be smooth. A break suggests subluxation or dislocation (unstable).

<!-- page 4 -->

- MRI and CT: for all patients with a neurological deficit, and for neurologically normal patients whose X-ray suggests an unstable fracture.
Radiographic signs of instability: kyphosis more than 20°; loss of more than 50% of anterior
vertebral body height; multiple adjacent compression fractures with significant deformity;
dislocation or malalignment; posterior ligament involvement.

Classification
Denis three-column classification

| Column | Contents |
|---|---|
| Anterior | Anterior longitudinal ligament, anterior annulus, anterior 2/3 of the vertebral body |
| Middle | Posterior 1/3 of the vertebral body, posterior annulus, posterior longitudinal ligament |
| Posterior | Pedicles, facets, lamina, spinous process and the posterior ligaments |

Rule as taught: one column involved = stable; two or more columns = unstable.

- Compression = one end plate (anterior part); burst = both end plates.
- Distraction scores more (4) than dislocation (3).
- Incomplete cord scores more (3) than complete cord (2) because it can recover and is a surgical emergency.
- TLICS is the only one of the three that includes neurology.
- PLC injured (3): palpable gap between spinous processes, splaying of spinous processes (widened interspinous space), facet diastasis, perched facets or subluxation, translation. In flexion-distraction and fracture-dislocation the PLC is taken as injured (3) without checking MRI.
- PLC suspected (2): in compression or burst fractures, look for high signal in the posterior ligaments on MRI; none = 0.
- PLC components: ligamentum flavum, interspinous ligament, facet capsules, supraspinous ligament.

Treatment Strategy
Conservative treatment
- For compression fractures and burst fractures with less than 50% height loss and less than 20° kyphosis (TLICS 0–3).
- No bed rest. Use a Taylor brace, not an LS belt (an LS belt is not enough even at L1–L2). Brace when up (walking to the bathroom, sitting to eat); not needed when lying down.
- Calcium and supportive measures; usually settles in 6–8 weeks.
- Standing X-rays every 2–3 weeks: if the collapse or kyphosis increases significantly, operate.

Surgical indications
- Unstable burst fractures (A3, A4).
- All distraction injuries (B1, B2, B3).
- Fracture-dislocations (C).

Rod contouring
- Follow the normal curve: lordosis in the lumbar spine, mild kyphosis in the thoracic spine, a straight rod at the thoracolumbar junction.

Reduction and realignment
- Burst fracture with kyphosis: leave the index screw slightly proud; seating a straight rod pushes the fractured vertebra forward and corrects the kyphosis (index screw or "lordosing" screw technique).
- Flexion-distraction: screws two levels above and below, then cantilever a straight rod into the screw heads so the opened posterior elements close.
- Fracture-dislocation: if facets are still intact, nibble half of the facet to ease reduction. Options: towel clips on the spinous processes (often fractured, so less useful), or a temporary rod held with a rod holder to manipulate and reduce (more powerful), then fix with the rod on the other side.

<!-- page 8 -->

Decompression
- Needed when there is a neurological deficit with canal compromise.
- Fracture-dislocation and flexion-distraction: reduction and realignment usually decompress the canal.
- Burst fracture with normal neurology: no decompression needed even with large retropulsion (60% shown); the canal remodels within about a year.

| Method | How | When |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect (ligamentotaxis) | Gentle, simultaneous distraction of the rods on both sides tightens the PLL, which pushes fragments back into the body | Only if the PLL is intact: on CT the cortical side of the retropulsed fragment faces the canal |
| Direct: laminectomy + transpedicular | Through one pedicle, push loose fragments from the canal back into the vertebral body | Fragment rotated (cortex not facing the canal) = PLL torn; ligamentotaxis will not work |
| Anterior decompression | Corpectomy from the front | Popular in the 1980s and early 1990s; posterior decompression is now considered sufficient in nearly all cases |

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Most fractures occur at T9–L2, the junction of rigid and mobile spine.
- Hypotension + bradycardia in cord injury = neurogenic shock (above T5): treat with vasopressors, not fluids.

<!-- page 9 -->

- Spinal shock ends with return of the bulbocavernosus reflex (24–48 h); only then call an injury complete.
- Sacral sparing = incomplete injury, better prognosis.
- ASIA C vs D: key muscles below grade 3 vs grade 3 or more.
- TLICS: compression 1, burst 2, translation 3, distraction 4; PLC 0/2/3; neurology 0/2/3; 0–3 conservative, 4 either, 5 or more surgery.
- Taylor brace, no bed rest, for stable fractures.
- Short segment + index screw for burst and bony Chance; long segment (2 above, 2 below) for B2 and C; 3 above and 3 below in ankylosing spondylitis.
- Fuse ligamentous injuries (B2, B3, C); bony injuries (burst, bony Chance) need stabilisation only.
- Ligamentotaxis needs an intact PLL.

### Take-Home Summary
- Protect the cord from the roadside onwards: log-roll, collar, ABC with an eye on the chest and neurogenic shock.
- Examine fully (ASIA), recheck after spinal shock, image with X-ray then CT and MRI when unstable or neurologically affected.
- Score TLICS to decide surgery; use the AO type to plan levels, reduction, decompression and fusion.
