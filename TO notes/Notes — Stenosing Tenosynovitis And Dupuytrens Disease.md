Stenosing Tenosynovitis and Dupuytren’s Disease –
Dr. Amit Mittal
Overview
This lecture covers two common yet clinically significant hand conditions: stenosing
tenosynovitis (trigger finger, De Quervain’s) and Dupuytren’s disease.
Understanding tendon biomechanics, pulley system function, fascial anatomy, and
surgical principles is essential for effective management.
Faculty Remark: “Trigger finger is never because the tendon is too big, it is
because the pulley is too tight.”

## Part 1: Stenosing Tenosynovitis

### Anatomy & Pathophysiology
- Stenosing tenosynovitis results from mismatch between tendon volume and pulley capacity.
- Primary pathology: thickening of A1 pulley, not tendon.
- Leads to pain, catching, locking.
### Pulley System
- A1 pulley: Primary site for trigger finger.
- A2 and A4: Critical pulleys, must be preserved.
### Tendon Anatomy
- FDS and FDP surrounded by synovial sheath, designed for smooth gliding.
- Inflammation → granulation tissue (“rice bodies”) causing further stenosis.

### Clinical Features
- Pain over A1 pulley.
- Tender nodule, catching/locking.
- Finger stuck in flexion, released with snap.
- Worse in morning.
Associations
- Diabetes.
- Rheumatoid arthritis.

<!-- page 2 -->

- Hypothyroidism.
- Pregnancy.

Diagnosis
- Clinical, no radiology needed.
- USG only if atypical presentation.

### Management of Trigger Finger
1. Conservative Treatment
- NSAIDs.
- Activity modification.
- Finger splinting.
### Steroid Injection
- First-line in non diabetic patients.
- 70–80 percent success in non diabetics.
- Less effective in diabetics.
- Complications: fat atrophy, skin depigmentation, rare tendon rupture.

2. Percutaneous Release
- Done with 16 or 18G needle.
- Release only A1 pulley.
- Test by asking patient to flex and extend.
- Avoid in thumb (risk to radial digital nerve).

3. Open Release
- Gold standard.
- Longitudinal incision over A1.
- Release A1 pulley; preserve A2.
- Ensures complete decompression.

## De Quervain’s Tenosynovitis
Stenosing tenosynovitis of first dorsal compartment. - APL and EPB tendons
involved. - Caused by repetitive thumb abduction, wrist radial deviation.

### Clinical Features
- Pain over radial styloid.
- Positive Finkelstein test.
- May show thickening at wrist.
### Anatomy Pearl
- First dorsal compartment often divided by subsheath, EPB in separate compartment.
- Injections fail if subsheath not addressed.
Management
- Thumb spica splint.
- NSAIDs.
- Steroid injection (high success).
- Surgery: Release first dorsal compartment, ensure complete release of subsheath.

## Intersection Syndrome
- Friction at crossing of ECRL/ECRB with APL/EPB.
- Pain 4–6 cm proximal to radial styloid.
- Crepitus common.
- Managed conservatively; surgery rarely required.

## Part 2: Dupuytren’s Disease

### Anatomy and Pathogenesis
- Progressive fibroproliferative disorder of palmar fascia.
- Not a tendon disease.
- Caused by myofibroblast-mediated contraction.
- Strong genetic predisposition (Northern European).
### Key Fascial Structures
- Pretendinous bands.

<!-- page 4 -->

- Spiral band.
- Natatory ligaments.
- Grayson and Cleland ligaments.
Pathology
- Nodules → cords → contractures.
- Ring and little fingers most commonly affected.
### Risk Factors
- Diabetes.
- Alcoholism.
- Smoking.
- Epilepsy (phenytoin).
- Trauma.

### Clinical Features
- Palmar nodules.
- Pitting.
- Skin tethering.
- MCP and PIP flexion contractures.
- Tabletop test positive (cannot place hand flat).

Management
1. Non Operative
- Observation in early nodular stage.
- Steroid injection for painful nodules.
- Collagenase injections (CCH) can be used for cords.

2. Surgical Management
### Percutaneous Needle Fasciotomy (PNF)
- Minimally invasive.
- Higher recurrence.
- Ideal for elderly or low demand patients.
### Limited Fasciectomy
- Standard procedure.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Removes pathologic fascia.
- Lower recurrence than PNF.
Dermofasciectomy
- For recurrent, aggressive disease.
- Requires skin grafting.
### Intraoperative Pearls
- Protect digital nerves, especially near spiral band.
- Cords spiral around neurovascular bundle, increasing risk of injury.
- PIP contracture correction may require capsulotomy.

### Contracture Correction Principles
- MCP flexion contractures correct more easily than PIP.
- PIP contractures <30° fare better.
- Persistent PIP tightness indicates joint contracture, not just cord.

### Exam Pearls
- Trigger finger pathology lies in A1 pulley, not tendon.
- Steroid injections are highly effective; less in diabetics.
- De Quervain requires complete release of subsheath.
- Intersection syndrome pain is proximal to radial styloid.
- Dupuytren’s is a fascial disease, not tendon disease.
- Positive tabletop test indicates surgical need.
- Needle fasciotomy has high recurrence.
- PIP joint correction often requires capsulotomy.

Final Faculty Insight: “Hand surgery succeeds on fine details. Whether it’s
releasing a pulley or excising a cord, precision decides the outcome.”
