Fracture Dislocations of Foot – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay
Overview
Fracture dislocations of the foot involve midfoot, hindfoot, and tarsometatarsal
(Lisfranc) joints. These are high energy injuries and often missed without a structured
approach. Key goals include early identification, anatomical reduction, restoration
of column length, and stable fixation.
Faculty Remark: “Never miss a foot dislocation. Every missed injury becomes a
deformity that is almost impossible to correct later.”

Fundamental Anatomy and Biomechanics
### Hindfoot and Midfoot Joints
- Talonaviular (TN) joint and calcaneocuboid (CC) joint form the transverse tarsal (Chopart) joint.
- These joints work with the subtalar joint to perform inversion and eversion.
- Lateral column is rigid at the Chopart level, medial column is mobile.
- At the Lisfranc joint, biomechanics reverse: medial side becomes rigid, lateral becomes flexible.
### Importance of Column Length
- Loss of column length leads to deformities like planovalgus or cavovarus foot.

Mechanisms of Midfoot Injuries
Midfoot injuries are classified based on direction of force:

1. Longitudinal Stress (Axial Load)
- High energy.
- Axial load on plantarflexed forefoot.
- Commonly associated with Lisfranc injuries.
2. Medial Stress Injury
- Inversion of plantarflexed foot.
- Causes navicular nutcracker fractures, TN dislocations, CC disruption.
3. Lateral Stress Injury
- Forceful abduction.
- Entire forefoot shifts laterally.

- Tibialis posterior tendon avulsion possible.
4. Plantar Stress Injury
- Direct plantar blow.
- TN and CC joint disruption.
5. Crush Injury
- High energy.
- Severe soft tissue damage.
- Compartment syndrome risk.

### Clinical Evaluation
- Assess soft tissue swelling, blisters, open injury.
- Check compartments (medial, lateral, superficial central, deep central).
- Look for deformity, forefoot displacement, inability to bear weight.
- Pain with inversion-eversion.
- Never force reduction in presence of severe swelling.
### Red Flags
- Plantar ecchymosis → think midfoot or Lisfranc injury.
- Severe swelling + pain out of proportion → compartment syndrome.

Radiological Assessment
### Essential X Rays
- AP, oblique, and lateral views of foot.
- Mortise ankle view.
- Axial heel view.
- Always evaluate spine and pelvis for associated injuries.
### CT Scan
- Mandatory for midfoot fracture dislocations.
- Identifies column shortening, facet depression, comminution.
- Assesses TN and CC involvement.

### Missed Injuries
- Missed midfoot dislocations lead to:
  - Chronic pain.
  - Rigid deformity.
  - Plantigrade loss.

- Collapse requiring arthrodesis.
Important remark: “Every midfoot injury must be approached with suspicion. Normal
X rays do not rule out Lisfranc injury.”

Midtarsal (Chopart) Joint Injuries
### Functional Anatomy
- TN joint is the mobile component.
- CC joint is rigid.

### Classification Based on Mechanism
- Together with subtalar joint, they determine hindfoot inversion and eversion.

- Longitudinal stress → axial load on MT heads.
- Medial stress → navicular fracture + CC dislocation.
- Lateral stress → lateral shift of entire midfoot.
- Plantar stress → plantar blow, TN dislocation.

### Management Principles
- Crush injuries → severe comminution.

- Achieve reduction of TN and CC joints.
- Restore column length.
- Temporary spanning external fixator if severe swelling.
- ORIF for displaced injuries.
- Fusion for severely comminuted or delayed presentations.

Lisfranc (Tarsometatarsal) Joint Injuries
Importance
- Wide spectrum, frequently missed.
- Stability depends on:
  - Bony configuration.
  - Lisfranc ligament (plantar ligament between 2nd metatarsal and medial cuneiform).

### Roman Arch Configuration
- Second metatarsal acts as the keystone.
- Provides vertical and transverse stability.

<!-- page 4 -->

Pathogenesis
- Direct trauma: crush, heavy object.
- Indirect trauma: twisting on a plantarflexed foot.

Radiology – How Not to Miss Lisfranc
### On AP View
- Medial border of 1st MT should align with medial cuneiform.
- Medial cortex of 2nd MT should align with medial cortex of middle cuneiform.
- Diastasis ≥2 mm → Lisfranc injury.
### On Oblique View
- Medial border of 4th MT aligns with medial cuboid.
- Evaluate intercuneiform gaps.
### On Lateral View
- Dorsal displacement of metatarsal base.
### Weight Bearing Views
- Show subtle instabilities.
- Fleck sign indicates avulsion of Lisfranc ligament.

### Clinical Signs
- Plantar ecchymosis (pathognomonic).
- Pain on pronation-abduction stress.
- Painful sagittal motion.
- Piano key sign intraoperatively → Lisfranc ligament rupture.

Management of Lisfranc Injuries
Principles
- Anatomic reduction.
- Rigid fixation for medial column.
- Semirigid fixation (K wires) for lateral column.
### Surgical Approach
- Dorsomedial incision.
- Protect dorsalis pedis artery and deep peroneal nerve.

<!-- page 5 -->

### Fixation Strategy
- Medial column (rigid):
  - Screws or plates across 1st TMT and intercuneiform joints.
- Lateral column (flexible):
  - K wires.
### Severe Cases
- Primary arthrodesis for comminuted or chronic injuries.

### Isolated Foot Dislocations
- Rare (calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiform).
- Require urgent reduction.
- Immobilization after reduction.
- Warn patients about risk of AVN of talus.

### Exam Pearls
- Midfoot injuries require high suspicion.
- Lisfranc ligament is plantar, not dorsal.
- Fleck sign = Lisfranc injury.
- Second metatarsal is keystone of the arch.
- Column length dictates deformity.
- Use CT in all suspected midfoot injuries.

Final Faculty Insight: “Treat every midfoot injury like a potential Lisfranc. Missing it
costs the patient their foot biomechanics for life.”
