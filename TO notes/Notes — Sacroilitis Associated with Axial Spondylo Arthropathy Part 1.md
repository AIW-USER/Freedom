Overview / Key Concepts
Sacroiliitis is inflammation of the sacroiliac (SI) joint and is a hallmark of axial
spondyloarthropathy. It may occur in ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive
arthritis and enteropathic arthropathy. Diagnosis requires understanding anatomy,

Anatomy of the Sacroiliac Joint
biomechanics, pathophysiology, clinical signs and provocative test interpretation.

### Joint Structure
- Synovial diarthrodial joint between sacrum (convex) and ilium (concave).
- Joint space width 1 to 2 mm, overall surface area about 17.6 cm².
- Motion is minimal, usually less than 4 degrees rotation and less than 2 mm translation.
- Females have greater translation than males which increases susceptibility to dysfunction.
### Articular Surfaces
- Sacral surface: thicker hyaline cartilage.
- Iliac surface: thinner fibrocartilage which undergoes earlier degenerative changes.
- At birth, the SI joint resembles a flat zygapophyseal joint. With age, ridges and depressions form for stability.
### Age Related Changes
- Joint becomes stiffer, more irregular.
- Narrowing is often due to accumulation of intra-articular debris rather than true ankylosis.

### Ligaments and Muscles
- Lower bone mineral density in females predisposes to insufficiency fractures.

### Key Ligaments
1. Interosseous sacroiliac ligament – strongest, prevents forward and downward movement.
2. Anterior sacroiliac ligament – thin, blends with joint capsule, nociceptive and pain sensitive.
3. Posterior sacroiliac ligament – strong, includes long and short components connecting PSIS and iliac crest to sacrum.

4. Sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments – form boundaries of greater sciatic foramen through which sciatic nerve passes.
5. Iliolumbar ligament.

Innervation
- Complex and variable.
- Posterior rami: L4 to S3 (posterior joint).
- Anterior supply: L2 to S2.
- L5 dorsal ramus plays a significant role. Neurotomy of L5 ramus may relieve SI pain.

Epidemiology
- Nerve fibers include both myelinated and unmyelinated axons, many nociceptive.

- SI joint is source of 25 percent of lower back pain.
- Prevalence increases to 32 to 37 percent in post lumbosacral fusion patients.

### Clinical Features
- More common in women due to higher joint mobility and load variation.

### Pain Characteristics
- Deep seated posterior pelvic or gluteal pain.
- May radiate to posterior thigh, knee, or entire lower limb.
- Often non midline and below L5 dermatome.
- Pain aggravated by sitting, stair climbing, prolonged walking or lying on affected side.
- May report numbness, popping, or clicking.
- Unilateral involvement more common than bilateral.
### Patient Localisation – Fortin Finger Sign
- Patient points below and medial to PSIS.

Pathophysiology
- Pain above L5 suggests SI origin, below L5 suggests lumbar spine pathology.

### Molecular and Genetic Aspects
- Strong familial recurrence. First degree relatives have 52 times higher AS risk.
- Bilateral sacroiliitis reported with isotretinoin use in acne fulminans.
- IL 1α, IL 1β and IL 1 receptor antagonist genes implicated.
### TNF Pathway
- TNF exists as transmembrane and soluble forms released after cleavage by TACE (ADAM17).
- Both forms bind TNFR I and II.
- TNFRSF1A and TRADD associated with susceptibility.

### Microscopic Pathology
1. Cartilage degeneration – chondrocyte hyperplasia, mucoid change.
2. Endochondral ossification – bone deposition on cartilage remnants.
3. Pannus formation – vascular tissue with CD4+, CD8+ cells and macrophages.
4. Subchondral bone erosion.
5. Osteoclast activation at bone cartilage interface.
6. Sequestrum formation – necrotic bone fragment.
7. Pathologic new bone formation.
8. Marrow inflammatory infiltration.
9. Synovitis.

Imaging Overview
10.Enthesitis – hallmark feature.

Patterns of Sacroiliitis
### Bilateral Symmetrical
- Enteropathic arthritis (Crohn, ulcerative colitis).
- Ankylosing spondylitis.
- Rheumatoid arthritis.
- Hyperparathyroidism.
- Osteitis condensans ilii.
- Multicentric reticulohistiocytosis.
- Whipple disease.
### Bilateral Asymmetrical
- Gout.
- Psoriatic arthritis.
- Reactive arthritis.
- Osteoarthritis.
- Behçet disease.
- Sacroiliitis circumscripta.
Unilateral
- Infection: pyogenic, TB, brucella.
- Neoplastic destructive lesions.
- SAPHO syndrome.
- Paraplegia associated changes.

Examination and Provocative Tests
### Gait and Functional Assessment
- Asymmetric gait due to gluteus maximus underactivation.
- Leg length discrepancy, either structural or functional, may exacerbate SI pain.
### Provocative Tests
1. Thigh Thrust Test – posterior shear through femur. Sensitivity 88 percent, specificity 69 percent.
2. Sacral Thrust Test – pressure over sacrum in prone position. Sensitivity 63 percent.
3. Distraction Test – outward pressure on ASIS. High specificity 81 percent.
4. FABER / Patrick Test – flexion, abduction, external rotation. Causes pain or restricted motion.
5. Compression Test – downward pressure over iliac crest in lateral decubitus.
6. Gaenslen Test – provokes pelvic torque via hip extension and flexion.
7. Palpation Test – deep pressure over SI joint, low reliability.

### Confirmatory Test
- Fluoroscopic guided SI joint block.
- Pain relief more than 75 percent indicates SI origin.

### Spondyloarthropathy Association
- Pain relief 50 to 75 percent suggests partial contribution.

### Inflammatory Back Pain Features
- Age less than 35 to 40 years.
- Insidious onset.
- Morning stiffness more than 30 minutes.
- Improvement with exercise, no improvement with rest.
- Alternating buttock pain.
- Night pain in second half of night.
### Key Spondyloarthropathies
- Ankylosing spondylitis.
- Psoriatic arthritis.
- Reactive arthritis.
- Juvenile AS.
- Enteropathic arthritis.
- Undifferentiated spondyloarthropathy.
### Shared Features
- HLA B27 positivity.

- Enteropathic arthritis: symmetric sacroiliitis, delicate marginal syndesmophytes,

European Spondyloarthropathy Study Group (ESSG)
Requires inflammatory back pain or synovitis plus at least one of: - Family history. -
Psoriasis. - IBD. - Urethritis, cervicitis or acute diarrhea. - Alternating buttock pain. -
Enthesitis. - Sacroiliitis.

### New York Radiographic Criteria (Page 42)
- Grade 0: normal.
- Grade 1: suspicious change.
- Grade 2: definite erosion or sclerosis without joint space change.
- Grade 3: sclerosis, erosions, joint space narrowing or widening, partial ankylosis.
- Grade 4: complete ankylosis.
Positive radiographic sacroiliitis: - Grade 2 bilaterally or grade 3 unilaterally.

ASAS Criteria for Axial SpA (Page 43)

sacroiliitis on X-ray or MRI plus one SpA feature. - HLA B27 arm: HLA B27 positive
