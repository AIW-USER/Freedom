Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Tendo-Achilles (TA) Rupture
Faculty: Dr. Rahul Upadhyay

### OVERVIEW
- Tendo-Achilles (TA) rupture is one of the most frequent soft-tissue injuries of the ankle, often missed or neglected, especially in middle-aged or diabetic patients.
- Common in males aged 30–50 years, typically following sudden eccentric loading of a plantar-flexed foot.
- Mechanism: Sudden dorsiflexion force on a plantar-flexed ankle or push-off injury.
- Can be acute, chronic, or neglected (>4–6 weeks).
- Accurate diagnosis is clinical, supported by imaging for planning. “Every missed Achilles rupture walks into arthritis — not because of bone, but because of imbalance.” – Dr. Rahul Upadhyay

CLINICAL CASE HIGHLIGHT
Patient: 40-year-old diabetic male, carpenter by occupation.
History: 45-day-old neglected TA rupture following a snapping sound and sudden pain
at the back of the ankle.
Findings: Palpable gap, tenderness, inability to walk on toes.
### Special Tests:
- Thompson Test: Positive (no plantar flexion on calf squeeze).
- Matles Test: Positive (foot remains dorsiflexed on knee flexion).
- O’Brien’s Needle Test: Positive (needle fails to move with passive motion – invasive, hence avoided in exams).
Diagnosis (exam format):
“A 40-year-old diabetic male with post-traumatic chronic Tendo-Achilles rupture
with palpable defect.”

### FUNCTIONAL ANATOMY
- Formed by the gastrocnemius, soleus, and plantaris tendons.
- Longest and strongest tendon in the human body (~15 cm).
- Insertion: Posterior superior calcaneal tuberosity.
- Blood supply: Poor in mid-portion (2–6 cm above insertion) → common rupture zone (watershed area).

- Nerve supply: Tibial nerve (S1).
- Two bursae:
  - Retrocalcaneal bursa – between tendon & calcaneum.
  - Subcutaneous Achilles bursa – between tendon & skin.

### RISK FACTORS
- Diabetes Mellitus.
- Corticosteroid / fluoroquinolone use.
- Obesity, sedentary lifestyle.
- High-impact sports (football, basketball, handball).
- Postural malalignment (pes cavus, flatfoot).
- Inadequate warm-up or eccentric overload.

### MECHANISM OF INJURY

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|

| Force | push-off (jump, sprint). |

| 3⃣ Dorsiflexion on | Landing on plantar-flexed foot (basketball, |
| Plantar-Flexed Ankle | fall). |

Most ruptures occur during eccentric contraction of the calf muscle.

### CLINICAL EXAMINATION SUMMARY
- Inspection: Swelling, loss of posterior contour, possible Haglund deformity.
- Palpation: Gap ~2–6 cm above insertion.
- Tests:
  - Thompson Test (Gold Standard): No plantar flexion with calf squeeze.
  - Matles Test: Foot rests dorsiflexed when knee flexed.
  - Sphygmomanometer Test: Drop in pressure <10 mmHg during contraction → rupture.
  - O’Brien’s Needle Test: (Avoided; invasive.)
- Functional Test: Inability to stand on toes, weak plantar flexion.

Dynamic USG – preferred to assess actual apposition during plantar flexion.

CLASSIFICATION (Gap-Based)

| Gap (cm) | Preferred Management |
|---|---|
| <3 cm | Direct end-to-end repair |
| 3–6 cm | V–Y plasty or Turn-Down Flap |
| >6 cm | Tendon Transfer (FHL/FDL) |

### MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES
- Never say “no role” for conservative treatment – even chronic diabetics may benefit.
- Choice depends on: age, demand, chronicity, gap, comorbidities.
### 1⃣ Conservative (Low-Demand / Diabetic / Partial Rupture)
- Functional bracing (walker boot with heel lift 3–4 inches).
- Immobilization in plantar flexion for 4–6 weeks → gradual dorsiflexion.
- NSAIDs, cryotherapy, eccentric calf exercises, contrast baths.
- Excellent results in elderly or high-risk patients.

### 2⃣ Operative (High-Demand / Complete Rupture)

| Gap | Surgical Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| <3 cm | End-to-end repair (Krackow/Bunnell sutures) | Standard repair, strongest biomechanically. |
| 3–6 cm | V–Y lengthening / Turn-Down flap | Moderate gap bridging. |
| >6 cm | FHL/FDL Transfer | Preferred in chronic, large gap, or degenerative tendon. |

SURGICAL TECHNIQUES OVERVIEW
### End-to-End Repair
- Suture pattern: Krackow, Bunnell, or SpeedBridge (FiberWire).
- Foot position: 5° plantar flexion.
- Reinforce with epitendinous suture.
### Tendon Splitting / Detachment (for Haglund or Insertional Tear)
- Detachment: Remove degenerative bone (Haglund bump).
- Fixation:
  - Suture Anchors or
  - Pull-Through Technique (through calcaneal tunnel with button fixation).
- Position: 5° plantar flexion during fixation.
### Turn-Down Flap (Bosworth / Lindholm)
- For chronic gaps up to 10–12 cm.
- Two parallel incisions create a tendon strip from proximal tendon → turned distally to bridge gap.
- Fix to calcaneum or remaining distal stump.
- Advantage: Large gap bridging.
- Limitation: Long incision, skin issues.
### V–Y Plasty
- For smaller gaps (3–5 cm).
- Create V incision proximally → pull down → convert to Y for lengthening.
- Less invasive, good power restoration.

### POSTOPERATIVE CARE & REHABILITATION

| Phase | Duration | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | 0–4 weeks | Immobilization in 20° PF, NWB. |
| Phase 2 | 4–6 weeks | Gradual neutral → partial WB. |
| Phase 3 | 6–10 weeks | Active ROM, gentle stretching. |
| Phase 4 | 10–12 weeks | Strengthening & gait training. |
| Phase 5 | >3 months | Sports-specific rehab, full WB. |

Total rehab: 3–6 months depending on chronicity.

### EXAM & VIVA PEARLS
- Watershed zone: 2–6 cm above insertion.
- Most specific test: Thompson test.
- Dynamic USG: Preferred to assess apposition.
- MRI: Static; may overestimate gap.
- Chronic gap: Turn-down or tendon transfer.
- FHL transfer: Best for chronic & diabetic patients.
- End-to-end repair: For <3 cm gap.
- Position for repair: 5° plantar flexion.

<!-- page 6 -->

- Suture anchor vs Pull-through: Anchor = simple; Pull-through = for poor bone stock.
- Turn-down (Bosworth/Lindholm): For up to 10–12 cm gaps.
- V–Y plasty: Ideal for 3–5 cm gap.
- Conservative: Always consider in low-demand or high-risk cases.
