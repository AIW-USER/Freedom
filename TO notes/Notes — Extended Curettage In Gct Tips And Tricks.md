Extended Curettage in GCT – Tips and Tricks –
Dr. Rajat Gupta
Overview
Extended curettage is the gold standard joint‑preserving surgery for Giant Cell
Tumor (GCT) when the disease extent and residual bone permit adequate clearance.
The success of curettage depends on case selection, meticulous technique, correct
use of adjuvants, and disciplined reconstruction workflow.
Faculty Remark: “If you do not have a burr, you should not be performing curettage.
Burring is not an adjuvant, it is a part of curettage.”

## When to Perform Extended Curettage
Indications
- Adequate disease clearance is possible.
- Sufficient residual bone stock can be preserved.
- Joint preservation is feasible.
### Contraindications (Require Resection)
- Extensive cortical breach with minimal remaining bone.
- Massive soft tissue extension.
- Pathological fracture with deformity.
- Scenarios where adequate margins cannot be achieved.
“Aim for realistic expectations. Some tumors are simply not amenable to curettage.”
## Principles of Extended Curettage
Extended curettage includes three mandatory components, followed by
reconstruction.
1. Adequate exposure
2. Curetting and high‑speed burring
3. Adjuvants
4. Reconstruction

## Adequate Exposure
### Soft Tissue Exposure
- Use atraumatic, muscle‑preserving approaches.
- Lift muscle through natural planes (e.g., vastus lateralis elevation from intramuscular septum).
- Isolate the cavity with hydrogen peroxide–soaked mops to kill spilled tumor cells.
- Remove soft tissue components en bloc, not piecemeal.
- Use cautery for added peripheral thermal kill.
(Image examples on pages 9–10 illustrate en bloc excision and isolation mops.)

### Bony Exposure
- Final goal is a large window, but start with a small window to avoid initial tumor spillage.
- Enlarge gradually after debulking the cavity.
- Use headlight and magnification loupe for better visualization (slide on page 12).

## Curetting and High‑Speed Burring
Steps
- Debulk gross tumor with curettes.
- Identify ridges and inter-osseous pockets containing tumor.
- Use high‑speed burr to remove 1–2 mm of peripheral bone.
- Burr all surfaces systematically. Burring is mandatory.
“Never perform curettage without a burr.”
### Pulsed Lavage
- Use high‑pressure disposable pulsed lavage.
- Flush all debris from the cavity.
- Lavage before and after adjuvants.
(Image on pages 14–16 shows sequence of curetting, burring, lavage.)

## Visualization Under C‑Arm
- Always use fluoroscopy before curettage, after gross curettage, and after burring.
- Ensures completeness of clearance.
- Clarifies disease extent in cases where denosumab may obscure margins.
(Sequence shown on page 16: before → after curetting → after burring.)

## Adjuvants
Options

| Adjuvant | Penetration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid nitrogen | 14 mm | Max penetration, risk of fracture and nerve injury |
| Phenol | <1 mm | Needs alcohol wash; superficial effect |
| Argon beam | 4 mm | Thermal coagulation; expensive |
| Hydrogen peroxide | Direct contact | Universally available, kills superficial cells |

Evidence: (Page 18) Adjuvants do not significantly improve control rates when
curettage + burring is done well.
“Adjuvants are not alternatives to good burring. They only complement it.”
## Workflow Discipline Before Reconstruction
To prevent contamination: - Change gloves, drapes, and instrument sets. - Use a
separate reconstruction trolley. - Harvest bone graft before curettage to avoid donor site
contamination. - Reviews show real cases of iatrogenic implantation at donor sites
when instruments were not changed (page 21).

## Reconstruction (Brief Overview)
Options
- PMMA cement.
- Autograft or allograft.
- Sandwich technique (subchondral graft + cement).
### Key Considerations
- Cement helps early detection of recurrence.
- Graft preferred for subchondral protection.
- Decide preoperatively based on MRI‑based volume assessment.

## Denosumab – Important Notes
Problems
- Converts tumor into woven bone, making margins unclear.
- Increases recurrence after curettage.
- Not an adjuvant after surgery.
### Limited Role
- A single preoperative dose may help reduce vascularity in locations where tourniquet cannot be used (pelvis, proximal humerus).
“Denosumab does not kill stromal cells. It only masks the disease.”

## Identifying Recurrence
- Lytic areas around cement or graft.
- Progressive radiolucency on follow‑up X rays.
- Soft tissue shadows in previously clean areas.
(Examples of recurrence shown across pages 1–3 and page 9.)

### Exam Pearls
- Adequate exposure + burring + lavage = foundation of extended curettage.
- Hydrogen peroxide mops reduce soft tissue seeding.
- Start with a small window, enlarge gradually.
- Burring is mandatory, not optional.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Adjuvants have similar outcomes; none replace good surgical technique.
- Change gloves, drapes, and instruments before reconstruction.
- Cement helps detect recurrence but does not inherently cause arthritis.
- Denosumab increases recurrence after curettage.
- Recurrence is seen as lysis around cement or graft.

Final Faculty Insight: “Recurrence after curettage is not fate. It is almost always
due to poor exposure, poor burring, or poor workflow. Get the technique right and GCT
behaves predictably.”
