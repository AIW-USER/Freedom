Radial Nerve Palsy & Management – Dr. Priyanka
Sharma (Oct 2025)
### Overview / Key Concepts
- Always try to identify the LEVEL of injury in any peripheral nerve injury – it decides which muscles are lost and which repair or transfer is possible.
- Treatment ladder: primary nerve repair → nerve graft → nerve transfer (early, before denervation) → tendon transfers → free functional muscle transfer.
- Exams commonly ask: compression syndromes by name, the level of each branch, the internal splint concept, and the named tendon transfer sets (Modified Robert Jones, FCU, Brand FCR, Boyes superficialis).

Anatomy
- Origin: purely from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus (C5–T1), formed by the posterior divisions.
- Axilla: posterior to the axillary artery; exits inferiorly through the triangular interval → branches to the long and lateral heads of triceps.
- Arm: radial (spiral) groove of the humerus → branch to the medial head of triceps; close to the deep (profunda) branch of the brachial artery. Commonly injured in humeral shaft fractures – in a badly displaced open fracture always look for it; the best time to repair is at the acute trauma.
- Elbow: passes anterior to the lateral epicondyle into the cubital fossa, then divides.
- Termination: deep branch (motor – posterior compartment of the forearm) and superficial branch (sensory – dorsum of hand and fingers).
- Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN): the deep branch pierces the supinator (tent-like cover) and continues as the PIN, close to the posterior interosseous artery (relevant for PIN/supinator syndromes and the posterior interosseous artery flap).

Branches

| Level | Motor supply |
|---|---|
| Radial nerve (main trunk) | Triceps (long, lateral, medial heads), anconeus, brachioradialis, ECRL |
| Deep branch | ECRB, supinator |
| Posterior interosseous nerve (purely motor) | APL, EPB, EPL, ECU, EIP, EDC, EDM |

Sensory: lower lateral cutaneous nerve of arm, posterior cutaneous nerve of arm, posterior
cutaneous nerve of forearm, and the superficial (terminal) branch to the dorsum of the radial 3½
digits.

### Aetiology and Clinical Features

| Cause | Site / mechanism |
|---|---|
| Saturday night palsy / crutch paralysis | Compression in the axilla or upper arm – arm over a chair back or bed edge all night, or a crutch |
| Fracture shaft of humerus | Injury in the spiral groove |
| Wartenberg syndrome | Compression of the superficial branch by the brachioradialis |
| PIN palsy | Distal, purely motor injury of the posterior interosseous nerve |
| Supinator syndrome | Compression under the arcade of Frohse (fibrous edge of the supinator) |

Deficits: loss of elbow extension (high lesions – triceps), weak supination, wrist drop and finger
drop.

Diagnosis
- Clinical examination – knowing which muscles each level supplies localises the lesion.
- Nerve conduction studies (motor and sensory).
- Electromyography – to pinpoint muscle denervation, especially in long-standing injuries.
- MRI – useful in long-standing or closed compression injuries, after fracture fixation, compartment syndrome or tight casts; identifies the level as in brachial plexus imaging.

Nerve Repair and Nerve Transfers
Nerve repair
- Primary epineural repair is best – acute, sharp injuries. Nerve regenerates about 1 mm a day; delay means denervation.
- Cable nerve graft when the gap is more than 2–2.5 cm (a tense repair will not regenerate well). Sural nerve is the usual donor (dispensable, long, good diameter); use 4–6 cables depending on nerve thickness. Local expendable nerves can also be used.

Motor nerve transfer (early cases, before denervation sets in)
- Median → radial nerve: spare fascicles to FDS or FCR are transferred to the PIN and to the branch to ECRB (wrist stabilisation).
- The branches to pronator teres and the anterior interosseous nerve must be preserved – never sacrifice median nerve function.
- Use an intra-operative nerve stimulator to identify which fascicles can be borrowed; for short distal transfers, wide-awake surgery lets the patient move the hand to confirm.

Sensory transfer
- Web-space transfers: fascicles from the ulnar and median nerves to radial sensory fascicles (at digital nerve level).
- Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABC) → denervated superficial radial nerve: does not fully restore sensation but reduces painful neuroma / phantom pain.
- The superficial radial nerve forms very painful neuromas (e.g. after radial forearm flaps) – protect it.

Tendon Transfers
Principles of tendon transfer

| Principle | Why |
|---|---|
| Supple joints before transfer | Stiff joints will not move – active and passive physiotherapy first |
| Soft-tissue equilibrium | Good soft-tissue/flap cover – a skin graft directly over tendons causes adhesions |
| Donor of adequate excursion | Must have enough range to move the recipient |
| Donor of adequate strength | At least MRC grade 3 – a transferred muscle loses one grade |
| Expendable donor | "Borrow from Peter to pay Paul" – do not take away an essential function |
| Straight line of pull | Fewer turns = better function (a small pulley/fulcrum is acceptable) |
| Synergy / tenodesis | Use synergistic action and the tenodesis effect |
| Single function per transfer | One tendon, one function gives the most predictable result |

<!-- page 5 -->

Goals in radial palsy: wrist extension, finger extension, thumb extension with radial abduction,
and stabilisation of the wrist.

Internal splinting (common exam question)
- An early pronator teres → ECRB transfer done at the time of radial nerve repair when recovery is expected to be slow.
- Purpose: restores power grip and wrist stabilisation and prevents stretching of the extensors while the nerve recovers.
- Difference from a formal transfer: it is END-TO-SIDE (PT into the side of ECRB) because ECRB re-innervation is still expected.
- External alternative: static or dynamic cock-up splint.

Named tendon transfer sets

| Procedure | Wrist extension | Finger extension | Thumb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modified Robert Jones | PT → ECRL + ECRB | FCU → EDC of 3rd, 4th, 5th fingers | FCR → EDC of 2nd finger, EIP, EPL |
| Standard FCU transfer | PT → ECRB | FCU → EDC | PL → EPL (single FCU split to EDC and EPL if no PL) |
| Brand (FCR) transfer | PT → ECRB | FCR → EDC | PL → EPL |
| Boyes superficialis transfer | PT → ECRB | FDS (ring) → EDC 3, 4, 5; FDS (middle) → EIP, EPL | FCR → APL, EPB |

- PT → ECRB is the constant, most dependable transfer in all sets (ECRB is the more central, stable wrist extensor; some give PT to ECRB only).
- Debate: is the FCU expendable? Taking both FCU and FCR weakens the wrist – so most sets sacrifice only one wrist flexor.
- Boyes uses FDS of ring and middle fingers (only if FDP is intact) – gives better finger function and keeps FCR for thumb abduction/stability.
- The aim is mass (common) finger extension with power, not individual finger extension.

Technical points
- Usually 3–4 small incisions rather than long incisions (single-incision techniques exist).
- PT → ECRB: take a strip of periosteum in continuity with the PT insertion – PT is more muscular than tendinous, and the strip gives length and anchorage.
- FCU is detached, rerouted around the ulnar border of the forearm and woven into EDC just proximal to the extensor retinaculum.
- Tendon junctions use the Pulvertaft weave (not end-to-end like nerves).
- PL to EPL: the EPL (recipient) is rerouted out of the third dorsal compartment towards the PL – gives combined abduction + extension and PL is too short to reach the dorsum.

- Tension: not so tight that it blocks movement, not so loose that function is lost – check excursion on table.

Post-operative management

| Joint | Position in the long-arm splint |
|---|---|
| Forearm | 15–30° pronation |
| Wrist | About 45° extension |
| MP joints | Slight (10–15°) flexion |
| Thumb | Maximum extension and abduction (prevents first web-space contracture) |
| PIP joints | Left free (passive mobilisation) |

- Cast/splint removed at about 4 (–6) weeks; then removable short-arm splints holding wrist, fingers and thumb in extension for another 2 weeks, removed only for exercise.
- In centres with a dedicated hand therapist, early guided movement reduces adhesions and keeps joints supple.

### Free Functional Muscle Transfer
- When nerve repair, nerve transfer and tendon transfers are not possible because the extensor muscles themselves are lost (e.g. Volkmann's ischaemic contracture, crush, burns).
- Gracilis transferred to the finger/wrist extensors as a microvascular free flap; PIN used as the motor nerve; posterior interosseous artery (or radial recurrent branch) with venae comitantes as vessels.

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- Radial nerve = nerve of the posterior compartment; purely from the posterior cord.
- PIN pierces the supinator; supinator syndrome = compression under the arcade of Frohse; Wartenberg syndrome = superficial radial nerve under brachioradialis.
- PIN is purely motor – no sensory loss in PIN palsy.
- Internal splint = PT to ECRB end-to-side at the time of nerve repair.
- Donor muscle must be at least grade 3 (loses one grade after transfer).
- Modified Robert Jones: PT→ECRL+ECRB, FCU→EDC 3–5, FCR→EDC 2 + EIP + EPL.
- Boyes: PT→ECRB, FDS ring→EDC, FDS middle→EIP+EPL, FCR→APL+EPB.
- Splint position after transfer: forearm 15–30° pronation, wrist 45° extension, MP 10–15° flexion, thumb extended and abducted, PIPs free.

### Take-Home Summary
- Localise the level from the muscles lost; confirm with NCS/EMG/MRI.
- Repair early (epineural); graft gaps > 2–2.5 cm (sural, 4–6 cables); median-to-radial fascicle transfer before denervation sets in.
- Late cases: tendon transfers with PT→ECRB as the constant core plus an FCU, FCR or FDS set for the fingers and thumb.
- Protect the superficial radial nerve – painful neuromas.
