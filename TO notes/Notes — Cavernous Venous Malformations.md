Target Ortho Quick Revision Notes – Cavernous and
Other Venous Malformations (2025)
Faculty: Dr. Himanshu Champaneri

OVERVIEW
Definition:
Cavernous and other venous malformations are benign vascular hamartomas
characterized by abnormal venous channels without intervening brain parenchyma.
They are low-flow lesions with a variable natural course.
Dr. Himanshu’s Quote:
“A cavernoma is not a ticking bomb; it’s a silent clot collector that only
occasionally wakes up.”
Synonyms: Cavernous angioma, Cavernoma, Cavernous hemangioma, Hemangioma.
Nature: Benign, well-circumscribed vascular hamartoma made of sinusoidal vessels.
Structure: - Irregular, thick- or thin-walled vessels. - No intervening brain parenchyma. -
No large feeders or draining veins. - Caverns filled with stagnant blood at various stages
of thrombus formation. - Grossly resembles a “mulberry” → also called “hemorrhoid of
the brain.”
Size: Usually 1–5 cm.
Evolution: May arise de novo; can grow, shrink, or remain unchanged.

### EPIDEMIOLOGY

| Parameter | Findings |
|---|---|
| Proportion of CVMs | 5–13% of all cerebrovascular malformations |
| Common Sites | Supratentorial (48–86%), Brainstem (4–35%), Basal ganglia (5–10%) |
| Multiplicity | 23–50% have multiple cavernomas |
| Spinal vs. Cerebral | 42% of spinal CMs have ≥1 intracranial lesion |
| Risk Factor | Craniospinal radiotherapy |
| Hereditary Form | Autosomal dominant; linked to 3 genes |

Special Note: Patients with spinal cavernomas should undergo brain MRI screening
due to high co-occurrence.

### CLINICAL PRESENTATION

| Presentation | Frequency | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Seizures | 50% | Cortical irritation from hemosiderin deposition |
| Hemorrhage | 25% | Bleeding from cavern walls |
| Focal neurological deficit | 25% | Lesion in motor/sensory cortex |
| Hydrocephalus / | 20–50% | Obstruction of CSF |

Incidental pathways

Key Point:
Unlike AVMs, cavernous malformations are low-flow, hence hemorrhage is not the
most common presentation — seizures are.

### SEIZURE RISK
- Symptomatic CM: 5-year risk of first seizure ≈ 6%.
- Incidental CM: 5-year risk ≈ 4%.
- Mechanism: Iron deposition (hemosiderin ring) around lesion → epileptogenic. Hemosiderin ring = epileptogenic zone.
### HEMORRHAGE RISK

| Parameter | Findings |
|---|---|
| Definition | Symptomatic presence of blood outside the hemosiderin ring |
| First hemorrhage risk | 0.08% per year (very low) |
| Rebleed risk | 5–6× higher after first bleed |
| Brainstem CM | 4.4% per year recurrent risk |
| Familial CM | 4–6% annual risk |
| Spinal CM | 2.1% per year |

No Increased Risk With: Gender, size, multiplicity, pregnancy, anticoagulants, or
physical activity.

<!-- page 3 -->

Trend: Risk of rebleeding declines over time.

### IMAGING FEATURES

| Modality | Findings |
|---|---|
| MRI (GRE/SWI) | Diagnostic modality of choice; shows mixed intensity core with a hypointense hemosiderin rim (“popcorn appearance”). |
| CT Scan | May show calcification or hyperdense lesion (less sensitive). |
| DSA | Usually negative (no shunting); done only to rule out AVM. |
| Follow-up | Serial MRI every 2–3 years or earlier if new symptoms develop. |

Key MRI Finding: “Popcorn” or “mulberry” appearance with a dark hemosiderin rim.

MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES
### 1⃣ Observation
- For asymptomatic or incidentally discovered lesions.
- MRI every 2–3 years.
### 2⃣ Surgical Resection

| Indication | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Accessible, symptomatic lesions | With seizures, hemorrhage, or focal deficit |
| Brainstem lesions | After ≥2 hemorrhages or progressive deficits |
| Temporal lobe cavernoma | For seizure control after correlation with EEG |

Surgical Principles: - Approach via least eloquent cortex.
- Resection includes lesion + surrounding hemosiderin ring.
- Early surgery (<6 weeks post-bleed) for better outcomes.
No surgery for deep-seated, asymptomatic cavernomas.

<!-- page 4 -->

### 3⃣ Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)

| Indication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Deep-seated or eloquent-area cavernomas | Used when surgery risky |
| Results | Comparable to natural history up to 2 years; ↓ hemorrhage risk after 2 years |
| Risk | May induce de novo cavernomas (radiation effect) |

Mechanism: Focused radiation → endothelial damage → thrombosis → size reduction.

OTHER VENOUS MALFORMATIONS
### 1⃣ Vein of Galen Malformation
- Type: Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) involving the vein of Galen.

- Presentation: Neonatal congestive heart failure, hydrocephalus, or seizures.

- Imaging: Midline, tubular, echoic structure (keyhole / comet-tail sign on prenatal USG).

- Treatment: Endovascular embolization (type-dependent).
  - Type I–III: Trans-arterial approach.

<!-- page 5 -->

- Type IV: Trans-venous approach.
- Surgery: Only after 6 months age (high neonatal mortality).
### 2⃣ Venous Angioma / Developmental Venous Anomaly (DVA)

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Structure | Tuft of medullary veins draining into a central vein; normal brain parenchyma in between |
| Flow | Low pressure, no AV shunt |
| Imaging | “Caput Medusae” appearance on MRI/DSA (seen in venous phase only) |
| Treatment | Observation; surgery contraindicated unless associated cavernoma or bleed |

### 3⃣ Angiographically Occult Vascular Malformation (AOVM)
- Not visible on DSA (low flow or small caliber vessels).

- Seen in younger age group.

- Treatment: Symptom-based (antiepileptics or hematoma evacuation).
### 4⃣ Capillary Telangiectasia

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Structure | Dilated capillaries with normal brain in between |
| Association | May occur in HHT (Osler–Weber–Rendu) or Ataxia Telangiectasia |
| Imaging | MRI shows faint enhancement or hemosiderin rim (if prior bleed) |
| Treatment | Observation; evacuate hematoma if symptomatic |

### EXAM & VIVA PEARLS
- Cavernoma = “Mulberry” / “Hemorrhoid of brain.”

- Most common presentation: Seizures (50%).

- First hemorrhage risk: Only 0.08%.

- Epileptogenic factor: Hemosiderin ring.

<!-- page 6 -->

- MRI GRE/SWI: Diagnostic tool of choice.

- Brainstem CM: Surgery after ≥2 bleeds.

- DSA: Usually negative.

- Venous Angioma: “Caput Medusae” appearance; no surgery.

- Vein of Galen malformation: Type III = most common; treat with embolization.

### TAKE-HOME SUMMARY
- Cavernomas are benign, low-flow vascular hamartomas that can cause seizures or focal deficits.

- Hemorrhage risk is minimal, and many are found incidentally.

- MRI (GRE/SWI) is diagnostic; DSA is usually negative.

- Management = Observe unless symptomatic or recurrently hemorrhagic.

- Other venous malformations (DVA, capillary telangiectasia) are also conservative lesions — rarely require intervention.

- Goal: Prevent rebleed, control seizures, and preserve neurological function.
