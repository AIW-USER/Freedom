Growth Modulation & Osteotomies Around Knee in
Children – Dr. Shalin Shah
Overview / Key Concepts
Coronal plane deformities around the knee in children mainly include genu varum and
genu valgum. Unlike adults, these deformities may be physiological, metabolic, or
pathological in children. Correct identification of etiology, origin of deformity, remaining
growth potential, and timing of intervention is essential to avoid unnecessary surgery
and to choose between observation, growth modulation, or osteotomy.

Physiological Alignment in Children
Salenius Curve
Normal age-related variation of tibiofemoral alignment: - Birth to 1 year: Physiological
genu varum - Around 2 years: Neutral alignment - 2 to 6 years: Physiological genu
valgum (up to 15°) - By 7 to 8 years: Adult alignment achieved
Key Point - Varus, valgus, or neutral alignment can all be normal depending on age.

Screening and Clinical Evaluation
Cover-Up Test (1–3 years)
- Cover leg and foot, observe knee alignment
- Varus or neutral alignment at proximal tibia: Positive test, needs imaging
- Valgus alignment: Negative test, observation sufficient
### Clinical Clues to Origin of Deformity
- Valgus deformity disappearing on knee flexion → femoral origin
- Varus deformity persisting on knee flexion → tibial origin
General rule: - Varus usually arises from tibia - Valgus usually arises from femur

Radiological Assessment
### Standing Alignment X-ray (Scanogram)
- Must be weight bearing with patella facing forward
- Foot position is irrelevant

### Mechanical Axis Zones
- Zone 1: Through tibial spine (normal)
- Zone 2: Through tibial condyle (observe)
- Zone 3: Within one knee joint width from center (pathological)
- Zone 4: Beyond one knee joint width (severe deformity)
Mechanical axis deviation into Zone 3 or 4 requires intervention.

When is Genu Varum Pathological?
- Persists after 2 years
- Unilateral deformity
- Associated limb length discrepancy
- Severe deformity
- Associated obesity
### Common Causes
- Rickets
- Blount disease
- Skeletal dysplasias
- Trauma or infection
- Hemimelia

Metabolic and Pathological Conditions
Rickets
- Clinical: Rachitic rosary, widened metaphysis, double malleoli, bowing
- X-ray: Cupping, fraying, metaphyseal widening
- Management: Vitamin D and calcium supplementation

- Persistent deformity may need surgical correction
### Blount Disease
- Infantile and adolescent types
- Associated with high BMI
- Intra-articular deformity
Levine and Drennan Metaphyseal–Diaphyseal Angle - >10°: Suspicious - >16°: High
likelihood of progression

### Focal Fibrocartilaginous Dysplasia
- Localized tibial defect
- Self-limiting
- No surgical intervention needed

Genu Valgum
Causes
- Vitamin D deficiency
- Idiopathic
- Post-traumatic (Cozen phenomenon)
- Limb hypoplasia
- Skeletal dysplasias
### Cozen Phenomenon
- Post proximal tibial metaphyseal fracture
- Valgus develops after injury
- Usually self-correcting within 18 months

Skeletal Dysplasia Overview
Definition
Disorders of cartilage and bone growth leading to disproportionate short stature and
deformities.

### Key Patterns
- Normal trunk, short limbs: Achondroplasia
- Short trunk, normal limbs: Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia
- Rhizomelic, mesomelic, or acromelic limb shortening patterns
### Diagnostic Steps
1. Identify short stature

<!-- page 4 -->

2. Determine disproportion
3. Identify limb segment involved
4. Look for dysmorphism
5. Perform skeletal survey
6. Family pedigree and genetics if needed

### Treatment Options Overview
- Observation
- Bracing
- Growth modulation
- Osteotomy
Choice depends on age, etiology, deformity magnitude, and remaining growth.

Growth Modulation (Guided Growth)
Principle
Based on Hueter–Volkmann law: - Increased compression slows growth - Reduced
compression accelerates growth
Temporary hemiepiphysiodesis allows gradual correction using remaining growth.

Indications
- Open physis
- Adequate growth remaining
- Coronal plane deformity

Techniques of Epiphysiodesis
Permanent
- Phemister technique (physeal destruction)
Temporary (Reversible)
- Staples
- PETS (Percutaneous Epiphysiodesis using Transphyseal Screws)
- Tension band plates (8-plate)
### Advantages of 8-Plate
- Minimal incision
- Reversible

<!-- page 5 -->

- Early mobilization
- Faster and predictable correction

### Complications of Growth Modulation
- Undercorrection
- Overcorrection
- Rebound phenomenon
- Implant failure
- Failure of growth resumption
Rule of Thumb - 1 month of growth ≈ 1° correction

Assessment of Remaining Growth
### Sauvegrain Method
- Elbow radiographs
- Scores epiphyses to estimate remaining growth
### Key Indicators
- Open olecranon and phalangeal physes indicate growth potential
- Fused physes suggest need for osteotomy

Osteotomies Around Knee
Indications
- Physis closed or minimal growth remaining
- Severe deformity
- Failed growth modulation
### Common Osteotomies
- Medial closing wedge distal femoral osteotomy
- Lateral opening wedge osteotomy
- Oblique wedgeless osteotomy (Herzenberg)
- Dome osteotomy
### Planning Principles
- Identify CORA
- Correct mechanical axis to neutral
- Avoid joint line obliquity

<!-- page 6 -->

### Important Surgical Considerations
- Correct both femur and tibia if deformity arises from both
- If correction >25°, consider common peroneal nerve decompression
- Standing scanogram is mandatory, CT scanogram is not useful

“In children, the biggest mistake is treating a physiological deformity surgically and
missing a pathological one. Age and growth potential decide everything.” – Dr. Shalin

### Exam Pearls
- Salenius curve explains normal varus and valgus in children
- Standing scanogram with neutral patella is essential
- Valgus usually arises from femur, varus from tibia
- Mechanical axis should pass through Zone 1
- Growth modulation works only with open physes
- 8-plate hemiepiphysiodesis is reversible and preferred
- Cozen phenomenon usually resolves without surgery
- Skeletal dysplasia must be suspected in disproportionate short stature
