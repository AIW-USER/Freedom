Overview
Part 2 focuses on Knee Dislocation, a limb threatening emergency that frequently
presents reduced at arrival and is often underdiagnosed. The lecture emphasizes
anatomy, mechanisms of injury, classification, vascular and nerve risks, reduction
principles, and reconstruction strategies.
Faculty Remark: “Never forget, knee dislocation means polytrauma until proven
otherwise. Vascular examination is mandatory before and after reduction.”

## Anatomy Relevant to Knee Dislocation
### Static Stabilizers
- Capsule
- Collateral ligaments
  - MCL (superficial and deep)
  - LCL / Fibular collateral ligament
- Cruciate ligaments
  - ACL
  - PCL
- Menisci
  - Medial meniscus

### Dynamic Stabilizers
  - Lateral meniscus

- Quadriceps
- Hamstrings (ST, SM, BF)
- Gastrocnemius (medial and lateral heads)
- Popliteus and popliteofibular ligament

### Corner Complexes
- Iliotibial band

### Posteromedial Corner (PMC)
- Posterior oblique ligament
- Oblique popliteal ligament
- Semimembranosus expansions

- Deep MCL parts
- Posterior horn medial meniscus
### Posterolateral Corner (PLC)
- LCL
- Popliteus tendon
- Popliteofibular ligament
- IT band and biceps femoris support
Importance: PLC injuries commonly cause peroneal nerve palsy.

Mechanism and Associated Injuries
Knee dislocation is a high energy trauma, often part of polytrauma.

### Common Associated Injuries
- Distal femur fractures
- Proximal tibia fractures
- Patella fractures
- Extensor mechanism ruptures
- Meniscal tears
- Cruciate and collateral ligament injuries
- Popliteal artery injury (limb threatening)
- Common peroneal nerve palsy (poor prognosis)

Why Vascular Injury Is Common
Popliteal artery is fixed at: 1. Adductor hiatus 2. Soleal arch
During dislocation, traction between fixed points leads to intimal tear, the most
common vascular lesion.

### Major MCQs
- Most common vascular injury: Intimal tear
- Amputation risk increases drastically after 6 hours of ischemia

Clinical Presentation
### Key Features
- Pain, swelling, deformity
- May present spontaneously reduced
- Watch for:

<!-- page 3 -->

- Dimple sign (posterolateral dislocation, irreducible, open reduction needed)
  - Pre patellar abrasion (dashboard injury, check hip)
### Mandatory Examinations
1. Vascularity: Dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial pulses
2. Neurology: Foot drop, EHL, TA, sensation in first webspace
3. Compartment syndrome: Passive stretch pain

Imaging
### X Ray
- AP and lateral views
- Look for:
  - Dislocation or subluxation
  - Arcuate sign (avulsion of fibular head, indicates PLC injury)
  - Tibial spine avulsion
### Stress Views
- Varus stress → LCL injury
- Valgus stress → MCL injury
### CT Scan
- For associated fractures and avulsions
### MRI
- Essential after reduction
- Assesses ligament tears, meniscus injury, tendons, chondral lesions, stenor like lesions

Stenor Like Lesion (Knee)
Equivalent to Stener lesion of thumb. - Occurs in distal MCL avulsion - Pes anserinus
tendons interpose between tibia and torn MCL - Ligament will not heal without
surgical repair

Classification
### Kennedy Classification (By Direction)
- Anterior
- Posterior

<!-- page 4 -->

- Medial
- Lateral
- Rotary
Not useful for guiding treatment.

### Schenck Anatomical Classification (Most Accepted)

| Type | Structures Injured |
|---|---|
| KD1 | One cruciate torn |
| KD2 | Bicruciate (ACL + PCL) |
| KD3M | ACL + PCL + MCL |
| KD3L | ACL + PCL + LCL |
| KD4 | All four ligaments |
| KD5 | Fracture dislocation |

### Important MCQs
- Most common type: KD3M
- Most common with peroneal nerve palsy: KD3L
### Modified Schenck (Rocher)
- C: Vascular injury
- N: Nerve injury
Example: KD3MC → ACL + PCL + MCL + popliteal artery injury

Management Principles
Step 1 – Immediate Reduction
Knee dislocation is an orthopedic emergency.
### Technique Tips
- Gentle traction may fail in severe deformity
- Exaggerate the deformity first, then reverse it
- Posterolateral dislocation with dimple sign → open reduction only
### Step 2 – Vascular Assessment Algorithm
1. Absent pulses → immediate exploration (or CTA if no delay)
2. Feeble pulses → ABI
  - ABI < 0.9 → Doppler → CTA or exploration
  - ABI > 0.9 → observe 48 hours for thrombosis

<!-- page 5 -->

Step 3 – Immobilization
Options
- External fixator (preferred when instability, poor skin, open dislocation, vascular repair)
  - Femur pins: anterolateral
  - Tibia pins: anteromedial
  - Pins must stay away from future ligament tunnel sites
- Cast (stable and reduced cases)
- Transarticular Steinmann pin (obsolete, high risk of breakage and infection)

Ligament Repair and Reconstruction
Timing
- Ideal: 10 to 21 days post injury once swelling decreases
### Bony Avulsion
- Fix with screws or suture anchors
### Soft Tissue Avulsion
- Repair with anchors or spike washers
### Mid Substance Tears
- Reconstruct with autograft or allograft
### Graft Choices
- Hamstrings, BPTB, quadriceps, peroneus longus
- Avoid hamstrings if MCL is injured (medial side instability)
PLC Reconstruction (LaPrade Technique)
Structures reconstructed: - FCL - Popliteus tendon - Popliteofibular ligament
Tunnels and fixation performed anatomically at: - Lateral epicondyle region - Fibular
head (anterior to posterior) - Posterior tibia

### Exam and Viva Pearls
- Most common vascular injury: intimal tear
- Most common knee dislocation type: KD3M
- Most common with nerve palsy: KD3L
- Dimple sign → irreducible, go for open reduction
- Arcuate sign → PLC or LCL injury

<!-- page 6 -->

- Posterior dislocation → extensor mechanism injury
- Anterior dislocation → ACL rupture
- Always examine pulses before and after reduction
- Knee dislocation = emergency, vascular injury risk high

Final Faculty Insight: “Remember, the knee may look reduced, but the vessel may
still be torn. Always protect the limb first, ligaments later.”
