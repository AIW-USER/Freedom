### Overview / Key Concepts
- Two parts: a scroll-through of normal pelvis and hip MRI anatomy, then common cases: AVN, femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), stress fracture, SCFE, osteoid osteoma, iliac crest apophyseal injury, subchondral insufficiency fracture.
- Knowing normal anatomy is what lets you spot pathology; when you know where the patient hurts, go straight to that area.
- Ask for a "pelvis with right (or left) hip" so the radiologist adds dedicated oblique hip sequences.

Normal Anatomy
Coronal
- Posterior: sacrum, erector spinae and multifidus, piriformis, glutei, ischial tuberosity (hamstring origin).
- Anterior: pubic symphysis region (sports groin: prepubic aponeurotic plate, pyramidalis, adductor longus; look for osteitis pubis), adductor brevis and longus, pectineus with the femoral vessels and nerve lateral to it.
- Iliacus and psoas join (psoas becomes tendinous) and insert on the lesser trochanter.
- ASIS: sartorius origin; tensor fascia lata; rectus femoris (two heads).
- Adductor compartment: obturator externus and internus, adductors brevis, longus, magnus, gracilis. Anterior: rectus femoris and the vasti.

- Hip checklist: spinous process avulsions, sacroiliitis, physes symmetrical, femoral head heights, joint space, acetabular over-coverage, head depression, marrow signal (dark or serpiginous = AVN, even before collapse); compressive and tensile trabeculae.

Axial
- Psoas tendon insinuates into the iliacus: oedema without a tear in a dancer or athlete suggests snapping psoas.
- Piriformis: the muscle just below where the sacrum ends; sciatic nerve passes between piriformis and obturator internus. Piriformis syndrome: oedema, accessory head, sciatic impingement.
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve: in the small fat triangle just below the ASIS (between sartorius and TFL); entrapment = meralgia paraesthetica (injection site).
- Rectus femoris: direct head from the AIIS, indirect (reflected) head from the supra-acetabular ridge.
- Acetabulum: roof, quadrilateral plate (medial wall), anterior and posterior walls. A column fracture always crosses the quadrilateral plate and usually the inferior pubic ramus; anterior or posterior direction decides the column (Judet views help).
- Fovea with ligamentum teres; iliofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments; a little hip fluid is normal; greater trochanter facets.
- Sports groin (athletic pubalgia): rectus abdominis heads, conjoint tendon, linea alba and semilunaris, inguinal ligament. Not a hernia (no protrusion), unless tears reach the superficial ring.
- Quadratus femoris: between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity; narrow space with oedema = ischiofemoral impingement.
- Glutei: gluteus maximus ends in the iliotibial band with TFL and never inserts directly on bone. Gluteus medius has two parts (anterolateral and posterior); one becomes tendinous earlier, so a fat plane between them is NORMAL, not a tear. Gluteus minimus inserts anteriorly.
- Hamstrings: semimembranosus and, behind it, the conjoint tendon (long head of biceps + semitendinosus, which is fleshy early). For a suspected hamstring or rectus femoris tear, request hip AND thigh MRI, since a pelvis scan stops at the lesser trochanter.
- Iliopsoas runs in front of the hip joint: iliopsoas bursitis.
- On GRE axials the posterior acetabular wall should lie lateral to a line from the anterior wall; if they are in line, think pincer impingement.

Oblique axial and oblique sagittal hip sequences
- Orthogonal axials never show the femoral neck in continuity. Dedicated hip studies use OBLIQUE AXIALS along the femoral neck axis (and oblique sagittals).
- They show acetabular and femoral cartilage, the physeal scar, the normal lateral femoral tubercle (not FAI), ligamentum teres, a small normal plica, and the labrum.
- Labral tear: a cleft (sulcus) through the labrum, sometimes with a paralabral cyst.
- Lateral pain: subgluteus minimus or medius bursitis, greater trochanteric pain syndrome, ITB friction.

Cases
Avascular necrosis (AVN)
- Necrosis of the subchondral bone; sclerosis and variable flattening of the head.
- MRI: a serpiginous line in the femoral head marrow is classic, with or without collapse and effusion.
- Without collapse = early; collapse = stage 3; collapse with osteoarthritis = stage 4 (as taught).

<!-- page 4 -->

- Early AVN (e.g. 30-year-old with right hip pain) may show only faint sclerosis on the AP film; the frog-leg view shows the sclerosis and heterogeneity better. Tell the radiologist which side hurts.

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI)
- Types: CAM (aspherical femoral head-neck bump), pincer (acetabular over-coverage), combined (most common).
- A small bump is easily missed on the AP pelvis. Better views: false profile view (standing, pelvis rotated about 60°) and Dunn (or modified Dunn, 45°) view.
- Alpha angle: circle the femoral head; one line from the head centre along the neck axis, another from the centre to the point where the head leaves the circle. Abnormal above about 55° (some use 60° or 65°; the decision depends on activity). Measure on Dunn, false profile, or MRI oblique axials, NOT on an AP view or orthogonal MRI axials.
- MRI oblique axials show the bump, labral tear and paralabral cyst; coronals often miss FAI.
- Young athletes with FAI often also have sports groin (pubic symphysis irregularity, sclerosis, instability).

Other cases

| Case | Key findings / lesson |
|---|---|
| Stress fracture (27-year-old runner) | Sclerotic line at the intertrochanteric region misread as calcific tendinitis (unlikely at 27). CT subtle; T1 MRI shows the fracture line with oedema. Get MRI first |
| Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (11-year-old obese child) | Hip pain without sport at 11–12 years: think SCFE (7–8 years: Perthes). Wide, lucent physis; frog-leg view shows the irregular physis; MRI: physeal widening and fluid, effusion, head slipped backwards; Southwick angle measured |
| Osteoid osteoma (21-year-old basketball player) | Marrow oedema and effusion but NO fracture line, thickened femoral neck cortex: get CT, which shows the nidus. Can occur in the 20s. Intracapsular location explains the large effusion |
| Iliac crest apophyseal avulsion (16-year-old cricketer) | Sudden pain at the crest; separation of the unfused apophysis by the core muscles (type I physeal injury). Often missed |
| Subchondral insufficiency fracture (47-year-old woman) | Sudden hip pain, widespread femoral head and neck marrow oedema, thin curvilinear subchondral line on T1, no serpiginous AVN line. Protected weight bearing and calcium; settles. Do not call it infection or inflammation |

### Exam & Viva Pearls
- T1: only fat bright; T2: fat and fluid; fat-suppressed: acute disease.
- Piriformis lies just below the end of the sacrum; LFCN below the ASIS (meralgia paraesthetica).
- Gluteus medius has two parts: a fat plane between them is normal.
- Column fractures cross the quadrilateral plate.
- Oblique axials along the neck are needed for the alpha angle (abnormal above about 55°).
- Serpiginous line = AVN; frog-leg view for early AVN and SCFE.
- Oedema with no fracture line in a young athlete: CT for an osteoid osteoma nidus.

### Take-Home Summary
- Learn the muscle anatomy and the scan reads quickly; go to where the patient hurts.
- Request dedicated hip sequences (oblique axial and sagittal) for labrum and FAI, and hip plus thigh for hamstring or rectus femoris injuries.
- Match the pattern to the age: SCFE at 11–12, apophyseal avulsions in adolescents, stress fractures and osteoid osteoma in young athletes, insufficiency fractures in middle age.
