The distal radius fracture (DRF) has for the last decade been the focus of many orthopedic and hand surgery meetings. Being the most common of the upper extremity fractures with an incidence of 16 in 10000 persons, the fracture rightfully earns its attraction (Fig. 15.1). In particular, in a population rapidly growing older, and with osteoporosis increasing with age, the number of patients with a DRF will increase substantially. Already today, one-fifth of the fractures in Sweden are in patients over 80 years, and the number of patients in this age span are expected to grow by 40% in 2027.

![[Fig.15.1.png]]
> **Fig. 15.1** The typical bayonet malunion in a Colles’ fracture with radial and axial shortening and dorsal tilt.

The treatment goals were set 200 years ago, by the modest prediction of Abraham Colles in 1814, that the limb, once the fracture has healed, at some remote period again will enjoy perfect freedom in all its motions and be completely exempt from pain. This outcome may not yet be reached for all patients, but with an improved understanding of kinematics, bone quality, and muscle forces acting across the wrist, we have increasingly become able to judge a fracture’s relative stability, and innovative devices have been developed to counteract these forces and restore anatomy. The well-trained surgeon has all the options to fix the majority of fractures providing anatomic position, not only at surgery but also after healing, and with a minimum of complications. What remains unclear is which fractures require surgical intervention, and the trend of an increasing proportion of DRFs being operated on is hardly sustainable. Future research will continue in this direction, and with registers, automated radiographic analyses, and artificial intelligence, we will have tools to identify which fractures need to be fixed.

The goals of this chapter are to provide the surgeon with a detailed understanding of fracture types, better knowledge of wrist and fracture mechanics, improved recognition of individual fracture fragments, and an understanding of the expectations of fracture management.

## THE HISTORY AND RATIONALE FOR MODERN TREATMENT

Beginning with Pouteau (1783), Colles (1814), and Dupuytren (1847), early reports of fractures of the distal radius considered the group of injuries to have a relatively good prognosis, irrespective of treatment. Until the mid-1900s, nearly all fractures of the distal radius were treated in a closed fashion, with or without reduction of alignment. The patients’ expectations were in line with the treatment tools available at the time, and certainly other public health issues surpassed the wrist in importance. Since then, tens of thousands of clinical and basic science publications have contributed to an improved understanding of fracture complexity, stability, and prognosis and have, consequently, driven the development of modern techniques and technology to optimize patient outcomes.

A brief summary of the evolution of modern fracture fixation is shown in Table 15.1, with the sentinel publications and techniques that fundamentally changed treatment of these challenging injuries. Summarizing the evolution of wrist fracture treatment, it appears that (1) there is both general consensus and scientific evidence that restoration of the anatomy of the distal radius is linked to restoration of function, but also (2) no single technique or method will yield results superior to those of other treatment methods given the wide divergence in patient factors like age and activity level but also fracture subtypes, energy absorbed at injury, and related injuries. The indiscriminate use of one method to treat all fractures of the distal radius thus may lead to inability to achieve the outcomes predicted by Abraham Colles.

## FRACTURE EVALUATION

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

The hand can be perceived as a delicate calibrated precision instrument for the upper extremity. Using the whole limb for positioning, the hand can be placed in a perfect position for every task, the thumb-index for precision and the full hand grip for power. However, for the hand to function optimally, a pain-free, stable yet mobile wrist is required.

The distal radius has three concave articular surfaces—the scaphoid fossa, the lunate fossa, and the sigmoid notch—for articulation with the scaphoid, lunate, and ulnar head, respectively (Fig. 15.2). The sigmoid notch is concave, with a poorly defined proximal margin and welldefined dorsal, palmar, and distal margins. The dorsal aspect of the distal radius is convex and acts as a fulcrum for extensor tendon function. The radial styloid area may have a groove for the tendon of the first dorsal compartment, and ulnar to this is a dorsal longitudinal prominence—the Lister tubercle, which acts as a fulcrum for the extensor pollicis longus (EPL) tendon. The distal radius functions as the articular plateau on which the carpus rests and from which supporting ligaments of the wrist arise. The sigmoid notch is the equivalent articular plateau for the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ), and the hand and the radius rotate as a unit around the ulnar head with ulnar-based supporting ligaments as the primary stabilizers (Fig. 15.3). The DRUJ is a trochlear joint with nearly 270 degrees of range of motion in rotation. The joint socket, the sigmoid notch, is small and flat and located at the ulnar side of the distal radius. The condyle, the ulnar head, has a more rounded shape. The joint surfaces have a capacity to slide both sideways and in distal-proximal direction. The stability therefore is relative and limited and provided by the shape of the joint surfaces secured by the ligaments. Forearm rotation takes place both in the distal joint (DRUJ) and the proximal extension ending in the proximal radioulnar joint (PRUJ).

The triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) constitutes the main stabilizing structure of the joint (Fig. 15.4), but the ligament-cartilage complex is delicate and prone to injury. The TFCC contains a dorsal and a volar radioulnar ligament. The ligaments are joined by a centrally located fibrocartilaginous disk/meniscus and extend from the ulnar side of the radius and converge toward the fovea on the ulna, where deep fibers attach to bone. The DRUJ is further stabilized by the ulnocarpal ligaments, extending from TFCC to the lunate and triquetrum. The floor of the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon joins the joint capsule and also contributes as a primary static stabilizer of the DRUJ. In the last decade, the distal border of the interosseous ligament, the distal oblique bundle (DOB), has been recognized as an additional primary joint stabilizer. Classic secondary dynamic stabilizers are the pronator quadratus muscle as well as the flexor and extensor muscles of the wrist.

The distal end of the interosseous membrane forms the distal oblique bundle (DOB), which adds to the static stability. The eight bones of the carpus are arranged in two transverse rows and stabilized by interosseous intrinsic ligaments that run between the respective carpal bones (Fig. 15.5A). In the proximal carpal bone row, the ligaments between the scaphoid and the lunate (SLiL) allow a complex torsion motion permitting the scaphoid to rotate relative to the lunate, a prerequisite for the flexion/extension of the scaphoid during ulnar/radial deviation. The SL is the joint in the carpus with the largest range of motion and the SLiL is prone to injury. The interosseous ligaments in the distal carpal bone row, in contrast, are taut and stable, allowing only limited mobility. The proximal carpal bone row moves as a unit; 50% of the wrist mobility in flexion/extension occurs in the joint between the radius and the proximal carpal bones (radiocarpal joint) and 50% between the carpal bones. Ulnar and radial deviation of the wrist is accompanied by translation/rotation of the lunate and the distal row, combined with flexion/extension of the scaphoid. Forearm rotation takes place mainly in the DRUJ but also to some extent in the carpal bones. There are no tendons or muscles attaching to the carpal bones, and thus the joint motion is controlled by the skeletal geometry and the stabilizing ligaments and only indirectly by the muscles.

From the radius and ulna, strong extrinsic ligaments run longitudinally into the wrist joint capsule (see Fig. 15.5B and C), partly securing stability of the carpal bone rows to each other and partly securing the carpal bones to the forearm bones. Dorsally, the ligaments are organized in a V shape, converging toward the dorsal side of the triquetrum. Volarly, the ligaments also extend in a V shape but converge toward the waist of the capitate, which forms the center of rotation for radial and ulnar deviation.

If stability is provided by the ligaments, wrist motion is mainly controlled by the forearm muscles. The radial nerve innervates virtually all extensor muscles, while the median nerve and the ulnar nerve control the flexor muscles. The ulnar nerve innervates the flexor carpi ulnaris, the strongest of the wrist flexors, and the median nerve the pronator quadratus, the main dynamic stabilizer of the DRUJ.

### RADIOGRAPHIC PATHOANATOMY

Three radiographic measures are used to describe the changed position of the distal articular surface of the fractured radius, the dorsal/volar compression (tilt), the radial compression (radial inclination), and the axial compression (ulnar variance) (Fig. 15.6). The dorsal/volar tilt is measured by the angle created by a line drawn between the dorsal and palmar lips of the lunate facet and the longitudinal axis of the radius. This angle is best seen on a *facet lateral* radiograph, performed with the beam inclined approximately 10 to 30 degrees distal-to-proximal to profile the articular surface of the lunate facet and eliminate the bony overlap of the radial styloid (Fig. 15.7). The radial inclination is average 23 degrees and measured by the angle formed by a line drawn tangential to the distal radial articular surface on a posteroanterior (PA) radiograph and one perpendicular to the shaft of the radius. Ulnar variance averages just under 1 mm negative but ranges widely, and a comparison to the nonfractured contralateral side is often helpful. The variance is the axial difference between the subchondral bone of the midpoint between the volar and dorsal rim (central reference point) of the lunate facet at the distal margin of the sigmoid notch and the most distal articular surface of the ulna, which is measured along the forearm’s longitudinal axis.

Dorsal/volar compression (A) (tilt), radial compression (B) (radial inclination), and axial compression (C) (ulnar variance). The latter is best measured using the central reference point (D), in particular when the fracture is volarly or dorsally tilted.

The identification of the radiographic landmarks is essential, particularly in intraarticular fractures. The classic landmark of the distal radius is the “teardrop,” which represents the volar projection of the *lunate facet* of the distal radius (Fig. 15.8). The lunate facet constitutes an important mechanical buttress preventing subluxation of the lunate and is together, with the short radiolunate ligament, important for the stability in comminuted fractures. In radiographs, the teardrop is identified by the rounded contour of the metaphysis toward the distal 3 to 4 mms, forming the drop-like appearance. In contrast, the distal part of the radius forming the scaphoid facet is flatter. During surgery, the two surfaces are easily identified, and when gently rotating the radius in the lateral view, a double contour with the two volar cortices, the rounded and the flat can be visualized.

![[Fig.15.8.png]]
> **Fig. 15.8** Radiographic landmarks. A, A lateral view of the wrist with (B) the contours of the radial styloid, the lunate facet, and the ulna marked. C, Same radiograms with the radiographic landmarks. The teardrop is the volar extension of the lunate facet and can be distinguished from the volar margin of the scaphoid facet continuing into the contour of the radial styloid.

The teardrop angle may be useful, both preoperatively and during surgery, and represents a line drawn tangential to the subchondral bone of the articular surface through the tip of the teardrop (Fig. 15.9). The teardrop normally forms an angle of 70 degrees with the longitudinal axis of the radius. Even a subtle dorsal rotation of an extraarticular fractured radius alters this relationship and decreases the teardrop angle. Similarly, an impaction injury that splits the lunate facet may drive the lunate into the metaphysis of the radius and rotate the teardrop. The teardrop is narrow and measures just 5 mm at its greatest width, which together with the rounded shape sometimes makes stable fixation difficult with traditional volar implants.

![[Fig.15.9.png]]
> **Fig. 15.9** A, The teardrop angle is measured as a tangent to the articular surface of the volar teardrop with respect to the longitudinal axis of the radius and is normally 70 degrees. B, Increased teardrop angle with a displaced volar marginal fracture. C, Normalized after surgery.

Another useful facet measurement on the lateral film is the anteroposterior (AP) distance, measured between the distal dorsal and volar rims of the lunate facet (Fig. 15.10). The average AP distance is 18 to 20 mm, but if possible, the AP distance on the injured side is compared with that on the uninjured side. A widening raises suspicion of a split of the radius fossa in the coronal plane, which can be difficult to appreciate in the lateral view without computed tomography (CT). Therefore in addition, the position of the lunate relative the lunate fossa can be examined (Fig. 15.11). In a normal wrist, the curvature of the lunate facet of the radius and the curvature of the lunate bone in the lateral projection are virtually concentric. In an uninjured wrist, the AP distance is essentially equal to the diameter of a circle that represents the best fit to the proximal articular surface of the lunate. Nonconcentric articular contours or an altered AP–lunate diameter ratio suggests a coronal split of the lunate facet and/or radiocarpal misalignment. If not corrected, an increased AP distance due to a coronal split and/or misalignment is correlated with increased radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis.

In the frontal view, landmarks are easier to recognize (Fig. 15.12). Ideally, the radiograph is perpendicular to the sagittal plane of the DRUJ, which enables visualization of any fracture entering the DRUJ and determination if there is a gap or step-off. Intraoperatively, the radius can be slightly rotated to open up the joint gap, which facilities greatly both plate positioning and correct placement of the ulnar screws. The radiocarpal joint line, as visualized, corresponds to the part of the curvature perpendicular to the joint line. This is not the actual distal border of the volar or dorsal rim. In the normal unfractured radiograph, which is centered over the radiocarpal joint line, the dorsal rim is seen slightly distal to the joint line, given the anatomic 10- to 15-degree volar tilt. Paradoxically, in dorsally dislocated fractures, the volar rim is seen as the most distal part of radius, which may be confusing during surgery regarding plate orientation and screw placement in a suboptimally reduced fracture. Step-offs and gaps in the joint line in intraarticular fractures are readily visualized in the frontal view, but an evaluation of the volar and dorsal rim separately adds information of a rotation between the radial and ulnar fragments along the coronal axis or an impaction of one of the fragments relative the other.

*The coronal shift* is a rather new entity, describing an ulnar translation of the radius shaft relative the distal fragment (Fig. 15.13), and has been brought to attention in the last few years. A step in the ulnar border of the radius proximal to the DRUJ is easily identified with an apparent too ulnar position of the radius shaft relative the distal fragment in the coronal (PA) plane. Coronal alignment should be carefully assessed as a useful parameter of reduction, alongside the traditional parameters of volar/dorsal tilt, radial inclination, and ulnar variance.

![[Fig.15.13.png]]
> **Fig. 15.13** Coronal shift. The posteroanterior radiograph with a residual ulnar translation of the radius shaft.

While initial plain radiographs help one understand the injury pattern and fragments, special imaging techniques (e.g., traction views after reduction and CT scans) may provide a more accurate diagnosis of the displacement pattern, the number of fragments, and the degree of joint involvement at both the radiocarpal and radioulnar levels. Three-dimensional CT reconstructions allow one to more accurately define the number and the presence of articular fragments and help guide the surgeon in the appropriate incision(s) and approaches. For unique injuries, modern software allows for the subtraction of the carpus with direct visualization of the articular surface of the radius in three dimensions.

#### AO Classification 1986

The AO classification (Fig. 15.16) is the most widely used, derived in 1986 by the Swiss Association for the Study of Internal Fixation (ASIF). It includes both the extent of articular involvement and metaphyseal comminution. The DRFs are divided into three major types: type A (extraarticular), type B (partial articular), and type C (complete articular), further divided into subgroups ending up at a total of 27 subtypes. The AO classification is frequently used in clinical studies, but due to its complexity, the interobserver agreement is low. The three major subgroups are easy and useful to learn and show acceptable agreement. With the type A being extraarticular, the type B fractures have some portion of the articular surface remaining in continuity with the metaphysis. In type C fractures, all fragments of the articular surface are detached from the metaphysis.

![[Fig.15.16.png]]
> **Fig. 15.16** The AO classification. A, Extraarticular fracture affecting neither the articular surface of the radiocarpal nor the radioulnar joints. B, A partial articular fracture affects a portion of the articular surface, but continuity of the metaphysis and epiphysis is intact. C, A complete articular fracture affects the joint surfaces (i.e., radioulnar, radiocarpal, or both) and the metaphyseal area.

#### The Fernandez Classification 1993

A descriptive dynamic fracture classification was developed by Diego Fernández, one of the previous authors of this chapter, and is based on the *mechanism of injury* (see Fig. 15.15). Much can be learned from this classification to *understand* how to best treat a DRF. In addition to the obvious bony injury to the radius, other concomitant injuries such as associated ligamentous lesions, fractures of neighboring carpal bones, and concomitant soft tissue damage are directly related to the direction and the degree of the sustained trauma. An understanding of these injury mechanisms thus facilitates the manual reduction, through the application of a force opposite to the one causing the injury. Fractures of the distal radius can be divided into the following five types, recognizable on standard PA, facet lateral, and oblique radiographs:

![[Fig.15.15.png]]
> **Fig. 15.15** The Fernandez classification of distal radius fractures. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

*Type I* are extraarticular *bending fractures of the metaphysis* in which one cortex fails by the tensile stress and the opposite cortex undergoes a variable degree of comminution (Colles’ or Smith type I fractures; AO types A1–3). *Type II* are *shearing fractures of the joint surface* (the Barton, the reversed Barton, and chauffeur’s fracture; AO types B1–3). *Type III* are *compression fractures of the joint surface* with impaction of subchondral and metaphyseal cancellous bone. These are generally high-energy injuries and usually involve disruption of both the radial and intermediate columns (Mayo type III, medial complex, die punch; AO types C1–2). *Type IV* are *avulsion fractures of ligament attachments,* including volar and dorsal rim as well as radial styloid fractures associated with radiocarpal fracture–dislocations. *Type V fractures* are *high-energy injuries* that involve combinations of bending, compression, shearing, and avulsion mechanisms or bone loss. Typically, there is diaphyseal as well as severe metaphyseal and articular disruption (AO type C3).

#### Melone Classification 1984

In 1984, Melone introduced a classification of intraarticular DRFs and identified four major components: (1) the shaft, (2) the radial styloid area, (3) the dorsal medial facet, and (4) the volar medial facet (see eFig. 15.2). This classification brought attention to the lunate facet of the distal radius equivalent to the radiographic teardrop and described the intermediate column in the later columnar classification.

#### Columnar Classification 1996

Daniel Rikli and Pietro Regazzoni introduced the columnar theory, which has been important for the recent improved treatment regimens of articular fractures by recognizing three columns of the wrist: the radial, the intermediate, and the ulnar columns (Fig. 15.17). The radial column is composed of the radial styloid and the scaphoid facet of the radius, the intermediate column of the lunate facet and the volar-ulnar and dorsal-ulnar corners, and the ulnar column of the ulnar metaphysis. Isolated fractures of the radial column include the chauffeur’s fracture (Fig. 15.18A). A typical fracture of the intermediate column is the classic “die-punch” fracture with articular fragments impacted into the radial shaft due to high axially directed energy (Fig. 15.18B). Fractures of the intermediate column extend into the sigmoid notch and the radioulnar joint. The *ulnar* column contains the structures necessary for forearm rotation and includes the distal ulna, the triangular fibrocartilage, the radioulnar ligaments and nowadays also the distal part of the interosseous membrane, the DOB.

The column classification is *treatment-oriented* and each of the columns are addressed separately. Rikli and Regazzoni recommended orthogonally placed microplates to treat dual-column fractures of the radius and demonstrated the biomechanical and clinical efficacy of the concept.

### Fracture Stability

Treatment of inherently unstable fractures with casting will fail and result in loss of radial length, carpal alignment, or articular congruity. By definition, certain fractures are inherently unstable such as displaced shear fractures (Fernandez type II), comminuted and displaced articular fractures with articular impaction (Fernandez type III), fracture–dislocations (Fernandez type IV), or combined injuries with metaphyseal–diaphyseal comminution (Fernandez type V). Some fractures may require surgical management for other reasons, including open fractures or fractures complicated by nerve compression, compartment syndrome, or multiple injuries.

What is not immediately intuitive is how to classify the rest of the fractures as stable or unstable, preferably early in the acute setting, when an acceptable reduction has been achieved. It is well known that even fractures without primary displacement can develop a secondary collapse under conservative treatment by the time of complete healing, and the opposite, fractures with a considerable primary displacement, may, after reduction and immobilization, heal anatomically in a cast.

The classic predictive model of stability was presented by Lafontaine and colleagues, who defined risk factors associated with redisplacement in 112 patients. Five parameters were defined, and a linear relationship was shown between the number of instability parameters in the prereduction films and ultimate fracture collapse with closed treatment. The following parameters were suggested:
- Dorsal angulation greater than 20 degrees
- Dorsal comminution
- Intraarticular radiocarpal fracture
- Associated ulnar fracture
- Age of more than 60 years

Lafontaine recommended that patients with three or more of the five parameters should be considered for surgical intervention at an early stage of management. Although inclusion of all the factors is the subject of some controversy, subsequent authors have confirmed the idea that stability is a combination of factors, and therefore the term relative stability is best used (Case Studies 15.1 and 15.2).

The first factor is age, which in particular seems to be a strong predictor of later redislocation. Age correlates with osteoporosis, which in turn has been found to correlate with the severity of the fracture.Nesbitt et al., in an attempt to validate the Lafontaine criteria, found age to be the only predictor, and patients over 58 years had a 50% failure rate compared with 17% in a 30-year-old.

The second strong predictor is metaphyseal comminution, dorsalbut also volar and radial and in particular combinations thereof.Radial comminution leads to loss of radial height, dorsal comminution to large initial dorsal angulation, and volar comminution to a high redisplacement rate, not only in volarly but also in dorsally displaced fractures.

A third factor, useful and rather simple to evaluate, is the mechanical axis and carpal malalignment. The *capitate shift* measures the translation of the center of rotation of the capitate from the axis of the radius, proximal to the fracture (Fig. 15.19). In a group of patients who failed closed reduction, McQueen and colleagues demonstrated that failure to restore carpal alignment in the sagittal plane was the single most predictive factor in deterioration of functional outcomes and objective measures of strength. The same author stated that carpal malalignment was the most important indication for correction. The aim when reducing a DRF, therefore, should be to realign the capitate with the axis of the radius, which has recently been shown to correlate strongly to a dorsal tilt of less than 10 degrees.

> **Fig. 15.19** Carpal malalignment is related to volar/dorsal tilt following a distal radius fracture. Reducing the fracture and reducing the volar/dorsal tilt will reduce carpal malalignment. With the hypothesis that combinations of radiographic parameters may be more important than single factors, scoring systems using algorithms have been developed. In the digital age, by speculation, these will be further developed by automated image analysis and artificial intelligence. An early report using computer prediction was presented in 1993. In 2006 MacKenney and colleagues developed a quantitative, weighted formula for determination of fracture instability, based on a prospective study of 3559 patients with extraarticular fractures. Using the formula, a 60% malunion rate was predicted, had all displaced fractures been treated by closed reduction.As previously noted, advanced age was the most important predictive factor of instability, as was the nature of the fracture on the initial films, that is, dorsal comminution and increased ulnar variance (3 mm). Initial dorsal angulation was not predictive but was confounded by the presence of dorsal comminution in the multivariate analysis. An online calculator, the Edinburgh Wrist Calculator (EWC) is available on the web, based on the formula. In a Dutch patient population, the EWC was found to be a poor predictor of fracture redisplacement in displaced DRFs. The predictive value, however, could be improved using less strict criteria of successful reduction. In another study, the EWC was compared with the Lafontaine criteria, and both algorithms were able to predict the final radial height, radial inclination, and final ulnar variance but not final dorsal tilt or carpal malalignment. In addition, the authors investigated the importance of restoring the volar cortical continuity at the initial reduction, which turned out to be the strongest predictor of the final dorsal/volar tilt, the change in tilt, and carpal malalignment at union. The importance of the volar cortex has further been emphasized, also in dorsally displaced fractures, by Wadsten et al., showing 96% radiographic failure with volar cortical comminution. Walenkamp et al. published a comprehensive metaanalysis of 3807 patients in 27 studies predicting secondary displacement. Although many predictors were found in the individual studies, pooling revealed only a significantly increased risk of secondary displacement in fractures with dorsal comminution in women and in patients aged >60 years.

Taken together, there is evidence showing patient age (indirectly gender/osteoporosis) and metaphyseal comminution on the initial trauma films, and to some extent fracture position, are vital predictive tools for assessment and formulation of treatment plans. Guidelines have been published on national levels and, although based on the same studies, paradoxically, come to divergent conclusions.

## TREATMENT OPTIONS AND TECHNIQUES

*[It] is neither the fixation nor the implant [that] dictates the outcome but the ability of the surgeon to meet the goal of satisfactory reduction with the least invasive procedure possible.*

—Kreder et al., 2005

It is no surprise that most randomized trials fail to show convincing evidence of the superiority of one treatment technique over another for the entire spectrum of DRFs. The variety and complexity of fracture patterns, associated injuries to the distal radioulnar and intercarpal ligaments, widely divergent patient factors (e.g., osteoporosis, activity level, systemic illness), and (maybe underrated) the experience and skill of the surgeon, mitigate against a single treatment modality for all types of injuries. Instead, a treatment strategy must be developed to customize the treatment to the injury, with the overarching principle that the least additional surgical disruption of the soft tissue sleeve, necessary to gain fracture stability and articular congruency, will generally impart the best functional outcome.

### Conservative Treatment and Closed Reduction

For all injuries, the patient’s history and physical examination should include age, handedness, occupation, activity level, sports and leisure activities, an extensive medical record, and a list of medications. Particular attention is paid to conditions that might affect bone mass or bone quality, including endocrine disorders, inflammatory diseases, renal disease, steroid use, and other factors. In many centers, a referral to an osteoporosis screening program is offered to the patients. The wrist should be inspected for wounds and tendon and nerve function, paying special attention to the function of the median nerve.

Nondisplaced DRFs in the physiologically young and active cohort can be treated with either a splint or short-arm cast at the surgeon’s discretion while leaving the elbow, fingers, and thumb free. Interval radiographs are not generally necessary for truly nondisplaced fractures. Closer scrutiny should be given to elderly and osteoporotic patients as well as to any fracture with an intraarticular component.

#### Fernandez Type I—Bending Fractures

The prime determinant in dorsally displaced, nonarticular bending (Colles’) fractures is the predicted relative stability of the fracture. In younger patients with minimal dorsal and no comminution of the volar cortex, an adequate closed reduction will be predictably stable. For active patients with a stable fracture type and excellent reduction, a short-arm cast or a Sarmiento slab with three-point support will be worn for 4 to 6 weeks (Fig. 15.20). Some centers use a sugar tong splint for the first 1 to 2 weeks, which is switched to a short-arm cast for another 3 to 4 weeks. Radiographs should be taken at approximately 10 (7–14) days to ensure maintenance of the reduction and thus fracture stability. At many centers an additional radiograph is recommended at 5 to 6 weeks to verify healing in an acceptable position. After cast removal, a simple removable splint is useful for comfort and support between exercises for several weeks until pain-free motion and function have returned.

#### Closed Reduction in the Emergency Room

The greatest challenge of closed treatment of a dorsally angulated fracture is not to reduce the fracture but to maintain the position during healing. If volarly displaced, the fracture is unstable due to the volar translation of the load axis. A volarly displaced fracture is best treated surgically, mandatory when the volar metaphyseal bone is comminuted (Fig. 15.21A). The chances of retaining even a perfect reduction is low. Also, if radially displaced (see Fig. 15.21B), with metaphyseal radial comminution, the tendency for the fracture to return to the original position is high. In dorsally displaced and comminuted fractures (see Fig. 15.21C), the probability that reduction is maintained is higher, even with the load axis transferred dorsally to a certain degree. Regardless of which, all displaced fractures should be reduced and splinted in a position that allows the patient to start immediate antiedema therapy (i.e., in *slight extension* and *slight ulnar deviation* ).

![[Fig.15.21.png]]
> **Fig. 15.21** Comminution of the cortex makes closed reduction less likely to be successful, in particular volarly (A) but also dorsal (B) and radial (C) or combinations thereof. D, Central comminution seen in a die-punch fracture.

The analyses of the postreduction films, together with the prereduction films, aid in determining the appropriate treatment plan. The key to stability is the evaluation of the volar cortex, the calcar of the wrist. The volar cortex takes up the largest load in the uninjured wrist, as seen in the organization of the trabeculae and the thickness of the volar cortex (Fig. 15.22). If the volar cortices are well aligned, the biomechanical axis normal, and a cast with three-point support applied, chances are high to maintain the position during healing (Fig. 15.23). However, initial displacement does impact stability, and all displaced fractures treated conservatively need to be followed closely.

**Technique and follow-up.** Closed reduction is performed under some sort of anesthesia. Options include intravenous (Bier) block and local anesthetics infiltrated into the fracture hematoma. Finger trap traction and/or manual traction seem to be equally effective.The fracture line is identified by palpating the distal fragment, and the hematoma between the fracture fragments is sterilely infiltrated with 1% lidocaine without epinephrine. The anesthetic should be allowed to diffuse around the fracture site for at least 5 to 10 minutes. If using a suspension device, the arm is gently suspended with finger traps attached to the thumb, the index, and the long fingers (Fig. 15.24), and 2 to 5 kg of countertraction applied across the upper part of the arm.

Once the patient is comfortable, the arm is allowed to hang from the finger traps for 5 to 10 minutes. Pressure is applied by the treating physician’s thumb to the distal fracture fragment in a direction that will reduce the displacement (see Fig. 15.24). For a dorsally displaced fracture, the distal fragment is disimpacted by traction and rotated into a slightly flexed position with the physician’s thumb and index finger with a palmarly directed force. A sugar tong splint is applied to maintain the position, and dorsal molds are applied over the metacarpals and the middiaphysis of the radius, with a third palmar countermold placed over the volar apex of the fracture. The recommended position of immobilization for a dorsally angulated metaphyseal fracture is neutral to slight extension, 20 to 30 degrees of ulnar deviation and neutral forearm rotation (see Fig. 15.20). Final radiographs are obtained to confirm reduction.

“Six-pack” digital exercises are begun immediately to reduce edema and prevent contractures (Fig. 15.25). Some patients benefit from supervised therapy, but all patients receive and are instructed to use a home program. Short but frequent exercises are recommended, and after 1 week the patient should be able to make a full fist. If not, intensified supervised physiotherapy is warranted. Passive and active shoulder and elbow motion should be initiated as soon as possible to prevent capsular adhesions and exercised at least three times a day.

Depending on the fracture type and assessed stability, if nonoperative treatment is initially chosen, the patient should be examined after approximately 7 to 10 days with radiographs and an evaluation of the hand and digital motion. *A change of radiographic position is a sign of instability* and should be a signal to reevaluate the treatment plan (Fig. 15.26). One should try to avoid the “slippery slope” of accepting a few degrees of additional dorsal tilt or loss of radial length on consecutive radiographs (Case Study 15.3). Instead, if the fracture collapses during the first 7 to 10 to 14 days, operative intervention must be considered in younger, more active individuals. A second attempt at closed reduction and casting is ineffective.

If closed reduction is successful but the position is impossible to maintain, surgical intervention is again considered. One must take into account the functional demands of the patient and the patient’s general and mental health. Regardless, the patient has to be involved as much as possible in a shared decision and informed about the plans, the expected outcome, the potential complications, and the time frame of returning to an acceptable function and pain-level.

If the assessment of the patient and the fracture ends up in a decision to operate, several options are possible. Which method to use, ideally, should be based on evidence from randomized studies and metaanalyses, but sadly, solid, evidence-based recommendations are weak and the decision most often is based on traditions and consensus-based guidelines. Several metaanalyses have been published. Fu and colleagues concluded from 9 studies and 776 patients that volar angular stable plate provides better Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) scores than external fixation at 3, 6, and 12 months. Better strength and mobility were found at 3 months but not after 6 and 12 months and fewer complications at the 12-month follow-up. Gouk et al. concluded from 4 studies and 228 patients that none of the parameters indicated significant benefits for plate fixation compared with external fixation at follow-up between 24 and 66 months. Xie et al. concluded from 10 studies and 715 patients that volar plate fixation gave better DASH scores and grip strength than external fixation at 3 to 6 months. It thus is difficult to draw solid conclusions regarding the choice of surgical treatment from the scientific literature. In the author’s opinion, it is gradually becoming clearer that volar plate fixation has advantages over other methods

in the early course (6 weeks to 6 months). For the more complex fractures, the differences between the methods appear to be larger and also remain over a longer period. The use of volar plates in volar, comminuted fractures and Barton fracture may be considered uncontroversial.

#### Which Patients to Operate? What Method?

Surgery of a DRF is indicated in active patients when satisfactory reduction cannot be achieved or maintained by closed manipulation and casting. Which surgical treatment to choose when conservative treatments fail requires several considerations. On a macrolevel, one has to consider what resources are at hand, what traditions and guidelines are followed locally, and what methods have been successful at the local hospital or in the country. On a microlevel, the decision should be a shared decision between the surgeon and the patient. The goal is to end up with a pain-free wrist at the functional level the patient requires. Apart from the functional needs, the general health appears to influence the outcome, as well as age, pain reaction patterns, and even socioeconomic factors. These factors influence outcome regardless of conservative or invasive treatment and should be approached by individualized rehabilitation protocols. The skill of the surgeon, or rather the organization behind the surgeon, has been less discussed in this context. With increasing experience, more difficult fractures can be treated successfully, both conservatively and surgically, but even more important, “easy” fractures are dealt with at a low complication rate. Finally, the patient needs to know what escape routes there are. If the wrist continues to be painful, what can be done, what are the risks, and what chances are there for improvement?

### Closed Reduction and Fixation Techniques

#### Percutaneous Pin Fixation

Closed reduction with added percutaneous pins is a relatively simple and effective fixation method ideal for reducible extraarticular fractures and simple articular fractures without metaphyseal comminution and good bone quality. Many techniques have been described; the most commonly used methods are shown in Fig. 15.27. These include pins placed through the radial styloid alone, crossed radial styloid and dorsal–ulnar corner pins, and intrafocal pinning within the fracture. Pins crossing the DRUJ should be avoided if possible.

![[Fig.15.27.png]]
> **Fig. 15.27** Different techniques of percutaneous pinning of unstable bending fractures. A, Pins placed primarily through the radial styloid. B, Crossing pins from the radial and ulnar sides of the distal fragment into the distal shaft. C, The intrafocal technique advocated by Kapandji.

The procedure is performed in the operating room, usually under regional block anesthesia with fluoroscopic guidance and, if preferred, finger traps and traction. Care should be taken to avoid injury to the extensor tendons and the sensory nerves, particularly when inserting the radial pins. The pins are placed at the tip of the styloid by hand through the skin or via a mini-incision to protect the sensory branches. The styloid bone is palpated and the space immediately dorsal to the first dorsal compartment identified as the starting point. A Kirschner wire is inserted using a minidriver, obliquely into the radial styloid with the pin exiting the far cortex proximal to the fracture. The surgeon’s other hand is free to manipulate and stabilize the fracture during pinning. The first pin is used to restore the length and radial inclination of the distal fragment, and the position is checked in the frontal plane. In the lateral projection, the dorsal-to-volar position can be fine-tuned and a second pin inserted in a similar fashion. A third Kirschner wire is placed at the dorsal–ulnar corner of the radius, just radial to the sigmoid notch to secure the volar tilt. The ideal starting position here is just ulnar to the fourth dorsal compartment tendon in the “soft spot” corresponding to the 4 to 5 arthroscopy portal. The wire is gently navigated through the subcutaneous tissues to engage the bone, and its position is confirmed with fluoroscopy. The Kirschner wire is drilled at a 45- to 60-degree angle to the frontal and sagittal planes to exit the volar cortex well *proximal* to the fracture line. For simple articular fractures, a fourth wire can be placed as deemed necessary, between the third and fourth dorsal compartments to stabilize the scaphoid facet fragment, but this is rarely necessary. The extensor tendons are checked by passive flexion of the digits and the wrist. The pins are bent and cut off 1 cm above the skin, or preferably, cut beneath the skin, and the fracture casted. Follow-up is similar to the reduced and nonpinned fracture with a 7-10-14 day radiographic control and 4 to 5 weeks of casting. Pin-tract infection and loss of reduction may occur.

#### Kapandji Pinning

Kapandji introduced the technique of “double intrafocal wire fixation,” which has become popular in Europe, both being a reduction technique, but also theoretically improving the maintenance of the reduction in DRFs compared with the standard percutaneous pinning technique described above (Fig. 15.28A). This technique is also best reserved for simple extraarticular fractures without volar comminution. In dorsally comminuted fractures, some support is added for the distal fragment by wedging the distal fragment. The technique is useful as a reduction technique in general, for example as temporary fixation during plating. The same care has to be taken to avoid iatrogenic nerve and tendon injuries as with standard percutaneous pinning. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a Kirschner wire is inserted *into* the fracture site radially, in a radial-to-ulnar direction, until the ulnar cortex of the radius is reached. The wire driver and wire are tilted distally to “lever” the distal radial fragment, to regain normal radial inclination, and the wire is advanced through the far cortex. The distal fragment should rest upon the wire. Next, a second wire is inserted manually into the fracture, 90 degrees to the first wire in a dorsal-to-palmar direction, and the wire advanced to the palmar cortex. Again, the wire is used as a lever. The distal fragment is rotated into its normal position of volar inclination and the second wire advanced through the palmar cortex of the radius. A third wire, as described by Benoist and Freeland, can be inserted in the medial corner for additional stability (see Fig. 15.28B and C). These pinning techniques are currently being replaced in many places by volar locked plating.

#### External Fixation

The external fixator still remains a useful tool for the practicing wrist surgeon (Fig. 15.29). In less experienced hands, the risks of some complications of the open techniques are avoided but the same complications remain as in the other closed techniques, like sensory branch injury, tendon injuries, and loss of fixation. For the experienced wrist surgeon, the external fixator is a tool to be used in some of the most complicated cases in open fractures or multitrauma, and severely comminuted fractures can be treated, maintaining the length of the radius. In the last few years, the dorsal spanning plate has become popular in these cases, potentially with fewer complications. Just as with the spanning plate, the external fixator can be used as temporary fixation, optimizing the conditions for a definite procedure, but in most cases it constitutes the final surgical treatment. Often method combines closed or miniopen pinning (Fig. 15.30), augmenting fixation with an external fixator to neutralize any forces that might lead to loss of reduction. Arthroscopy is also sometimes combined with external fixation. In experienced hands, arthroscopy allows exact articular congruence and can be combined with bone grafting to buttress the articular fragments.

![[Fig.15.30.png]]
> **Fig. 15.30** Augmented external fixation. A and B, Radiographs of an unstable extraarticular fracture. C and D, Crossed-pin augmentation of external fixation increases the fixation strength. E and F, The fixator tightened with the wrist in a neutral to slightly extended position to allow full flexion and extension of the fingers postoperatively. The conceptual idea of external fixation is to use ligamentotaxis to reduce the fracture. Most fragments are still attached to ligaments and soft tissue (see Fig. 15.5D), and once the original length is restored, these ligaments will pull the fragments into place. Sometimes the most comminuted fractures reduce only by longitudinal traction, thereby proving the concept. For this to happen, the fracture should be fresh and at best not older than a couple of days. In older fractures, the hematoma, which fills out any dead space, starts to develop into callus, blocking the reduction. Two things are important to avoid. First, one must not overstretch the radiocarpal joint. This may cause increased postoperative pain and may severely restrict initiation of the early antiedema therapy and potentially lead to finger stiffness. Check the distance between the capitate and lunate, which should be normal. If widened after the procedure, loosen the frame and allow the joint space to normalize. Second, lock the frame with the wrist in a neutral or slight extension to facilitate the pumping exercises, as long as the reduction is maintained.

**External fixation technique.** Under brachial block or general anesthesia, the anesthetized upper extremity is prepared and draped from the fingertips to the lower part of the arm. Sterile finger traps and a traction device can be used if preferred. Manual reduction is performed to grossly align the fracture fragments and approximate normal length, alignment, and tilt.

When the bridging external fixation technique is used, a 2- to 3-cm-long incision is made over the dorsoradial aspect of the index metacarpal base. Blunt dissection with scissors exposes the metacarpal while carefully preserving and reflecting branches of the dorsoradial sensory nerve. A soft tissue protector is placed on the metacarpal, and 2.5- to 3-mm self-tapping half-pins inserted at a 30- to 45-degree angle, dorsal to the frontal plane of the hand and forearm (Fig. 15.31). Use the fluoroscope to make sure the pins have a central position in the bone to avoid metacarpal bone fracture. Use an awl or Kirschner wire to mark the starting point as the drill has a tendency to slide up on the rounded metacarpal bone.

![[Fig.15.31.png]]
> **Fig. 15.31** External fixation. Two 3-mm half-pins are introduced into the base of the second metacarpal. Another two 3-mm half-pins are then introduced into the distal radius via direct exposure of the radius between the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) and extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) at an angle 30 degrees dorsal to the coronal plane. Next, a 3- to 4-cm dorsoradial skin incision is made, 5 to 8 cm proximal to the wrist joint. Blunt dissection exposes superficial branches of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, the brachioradialis (BR), the two radial wrist extensors, and the radial sensory nerve, which exits in the midforearm between the BR and the extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL). The nerve is frequently found and needs to be protected. Two 2.5- to 3-mm half-pins (1.5 cm apart) are then introduced through a soft tissue protector, between the radial wrist extensors, at an approximately 30-degree dorsoradial angle. The pins should exit the far cortex of the radius and be confirmed fluoroscopically. The frame is mounted according to the instructions, and the bolts are left open during reduction and tightened once an acceptable position is reached. If necessary, augmented fixation with Kirschner wires is performed before or after tightening, whereas bone grafting is preferably performed after (Case Study 15.4).

Any joint incongruity involving the sigmoid notch of the radius needs to be addressed. Usually, percutaneous manipulation of the fragment with a Kirschner wire and fixation of the fragment to the stout volar metaphyseal bone is possible; if not, limited open reduction is required (Fig. 15.32). Through a midline incision, the fourth dorsal compartment is opened, and the tendons are retracted radially. The extensor digiti minimi (EDM) lies directly over the DRUJ and is retracted to the ulnar side. Care should be taken to avoid injury to the dorsoradioulnar ligament when exposing and realigning this fragment. A Kirschner wire is passed at a 45-degree angle to the frontal and sagittal planes to engage the distal and ulnar margin of the dorsal–ulnar fragment and to secure it to the volar cortical bone. Reduction and fixation are confirmed with fluoroscopy.

#### Additional Soft Tissue Injuries

SLiL *:* Concomitant complete tears of the scapholunate or lunotriquetral ligaments in a fresh fracture can be reduced anatomically and pinned or treated by limited open reduction, repair, and pin or screw fixation (see Chapter 13). A minimum of two divergent pins or a temporary compression screw should cross the affected intercarpal articulation, and one or two additional pins should be placed to temporarily stabilize the proximal row to the distal carpal row across the midcarpal joint. Intercarpal pins are left in place for at least 8 weeks and a graduated program of range-of-motion and resistive exercises is begun slowly thereafter. If using a temporary scapholunate screw, the device can be left in place while gentle midcarpal motion is begun with a dart-thrower’s rehabilitation protocol (see Chapter 13). The screw can be removed 4 to 6 months postoperatively.

TFCC: If gross ulnar instability is diagnosed in an active patient, arthroscopy can be performed and open or arthroscopic repair considered (see Chapter 14). However, in contrast to an acute SL-tear, which should be pinned or repaired at the time of fracture repair, a TFCC injury can be repaired at a second stage if symptomatic. In most cases, a perfect reduction and union of the radius fracture in an anatomic position leads to TFCC healing and resolution of ulnar-sided pain and instability. It must be noted, however, that ulnar-sided wrist symptoms may be the last to resolve following these injuries.

### Open Reduction and Fixation: The Basics

#### Open Versus Closed Reduction and Fixation

Open reduction of DRFs may be indicated in active patients when satisfactory reduction cannot be achieved or maintained by closed manipulation, as an alternative to percutaneous fixation or external fixation. The popularity of the volar plate is found among both patients and surgeons. The external fixator is bulky and, given the choice, less preferred by the patients. Just as in pin fixation, wound problems are not uncommon using external fixator, and patients return to the clinic more often for advice and wound treatment. Further, as with any nonrigid fixation, postsurgery follow-up radiograms are commonly advocated since pinning and external fixators are more prone to loss of fixation and malunion. In the metaanalyses or the recent Cochrane report, based on the few available small randomized studies, there is, however, little evidence to support any major measurable clinical difference between the different treatment options. The differences are small, and mostly not exceeding the a priori–determined threshold for clinical importance of approximately 10 DASH scale steps. Larger differences are, however, not to be expected between implants. The median DASH values in the modern studies are less than 10,and a minimally clinically important difference (MCID) larger than 10 between two implant or fixation types, thus, would be impossible to show regardless of sample size. In the British multicenter DRAFFT study, 448 patients between 18 and 89 years were randomized between volar locking plate and pin fixation. Most of the surgeons, 88% or 215 of 244, operated on fewer than three patients. No difference was found in the subjective outcome using patient-rated wrist evaluation (PRWE) and DASH. Although not presented, the DASH values at 12 months appeared to be similar to previous studies with median values less than 10. In the years following the publication of the DRAFFT study, the practice changed dramatically in the United Kingdom, and the proportion of patients having Kirschner wire fixation rose to 42%, with a concurrent reduction of 48% in the group having a plate. The proportion of other procedures stayed the same.

Articular fractures in elderly, inactive patients, and in those with considerable osteoporosis, have traditionally been considered a contraindication to open reduction due to the risk for complications including fixation failure, nonunion, and reflex sympathetic dystrophy. However, since the introduction of fixed-angle internal-fixation devices, both unstable extraarticular and simple articular fractures in the elderly but active osteoporotic patients have had increasingly satisfactory outcomes with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF). Subchondral buttressing with locking pins or screws secured to the plate greatly reduces the incidence of subsidence or secondary articular displacement as well as the need for a supplemental bone graft or bone graft substitute (Fig. 15.34).

![[Fig.15.34.png]]
> **Fig. 15.34** A, In fractures operated in the first few weeks, the subchondral locking pins or screws buttress the joint surface. This reduces subsidence and secondary articular displacement and reduces the need for bone grafting or bone substitute. B, This patient presented late and was operated first 6 weeks after fracture. General factors limiting surgical reconstruction of the articular surface include the number of fragments, their size, the amount of cancellous bone impaction, and associated traumatic lesions of the articular cartilage. Alternatives to open anatomic restoration, including percutaneous fixation and bridge plating, should be considered in cases of extreme articular comminution. In these cases, efforts should be directed at restoring the anatomic relationships of the radius and the ulna, ensuring normal alignment of the hand and carpus with the long axis of the forearm as well as in the DRUJ. If radiocarpal arthritic changes subsequently occur, the restoration of normal length and alignment will greatly facilitate the results of secondary reconstructive procedures.

#### Volar Plate Fixation Technique

The volar locking plate has become the standard for the majority of DRFs and can be used in both articular and nonarticular fractures, regardless of dorsal, volar, or radial displacement of the distal fragment. The plate can span any volar comminution by allowing a unicortical attachment distally, with the locked screws supporting the subchondral plate, using the thick volar cortex and a bicortical purchase for proximal screw fixation. Advantages include the following:

- Anatomic reduction of the volar cortex facilitates restoration of

radial length, inclination, and volar tilt.

- Avoidance of additional dorsal dissection helps preserve the vascu

lar supply of comminuted dorsal fragments.

- Because the volar compartment of the wrist has a larger cross-

sectional space and the implant is separated from the flexor tendons by the pronator quadratus, the incidence of flexor tendon complications can be minimized by a sufficiently proximal plate position.

- When stabilized with a fixed-angle device, the need for bone graft

ing is reduced.

### AUTHOR'S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: VOLAR PLATE BASAL TECHNIQUE

Prepare for a modified Henry incision (Fig. 15.36) by palpating the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon and mark the skin incision. If the area is swollen and the tendon is difficult to identify, try to find the space between the palmaris longus (PL) tendon and the FCR with your index finger. Make a longitudinal incision along the FCR and open the entire length of the subsheath of the tendon. Beware of the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve (Fig. 15.37), which resides between the FCR and PL. Retract the FCR ulnarly. Use a finger to separate the adhesions caused by the hematoma, and retract the flexor tendons and the median nerve until the flat surface of the pronator quadratus (PQ) muscle is reached. Several recurrent radial artery branches may need to be cauterized. Use a retractor and place it radially to the FCR to protect the median nerve from injury. Divide the PQ radially and make a sharp bend ulnarly at the distal end of the muscular tissue. Use an elevator to detach the PQ from the periosteum and raise a fasciomuscular flap, facilitating later closure.

Use an elevator and sharp dissection to visualize the fracture. In dorsally dislocated fractures, the volar cortex of the distal fragment is often hidden under a hematoma. Identify the fracture and continue distally with the elevator, working in a proximal to distal direction, to visualize the volar cortex of the distal fragment. When the soft tissue does not come off anymore, the watershed line (i.e., the origin of the volar joint capsule and the important volar ligaments) has been reached. The dissection should not proceed further distal. Continue ulnarly all the way to the volar ulnar corner, creating space for the plate but also to evaluate the status of the volar ulnar corner.

Use a dental picker to remove any new-formed callus in the fracture gap and reduce the volar/dorsal dislocation by shifting the distal fragment volarly, aligning the volar cortices. A clamp on the radial shaft proximally is helpful during manipulation. One can hold the reduction manually or pin the fracture temporarily with an oblique Kirschner wire through the radial styloid to the ulnar cortex of the radius. Check the position, using the intraoperative fluoroscopy. If the radius shaft is ulnarly displaced (coronal shift), back the temporary pin slightly, use a hook to reduce the coronal shift by pulling the radius shaft radially and drive the pin through the far cortex again. Check the position in the lateral plane, adjust the volar inclination, and secure with a second pin if necessary.

Apply the plate and determine its ideal position using fluoroscopy. To visualize the sigmoid notch, slightly rotate the radius under the fluoroscope to make the x-ray beam go parallel to the dorsal and volar edges of the notch. This facilitates reaching an ideal ulnar position of the plate to support the lunate facet but without the ulnar screws penetrating into the DRUJ. Adjust the plate to a perfect position in the distal ulnar corner. One can use a common oval hole in the proximal aspect of the plate to provide provisional plate fixation. Alternatively, one can pin the plate distally and proximally and check the lateral view to make sure the plate is not protruding volarly to the teardrop. Adjust the plate position as necessary.

Avoid placing the plate distal to the transverse radial ridge, also called the watershed line. Implants positioned too distal on the volar lip of the radius are in direct contact with the flexor tendons, with a high risk of tendon irritation and rupture (Fig. 15.38). The Soong classification is useful as a guide (Fig. 15.39). Hardware placed 2 mm or more above the volar critical line (Soong Grade 1) or within 3 mm of the distal edge of the volar rim (Soong Grade 2) has been demonstrated to have a higher rate of tendon rupture. If fixation is necessary distal to this ridge, one should consider fragment-specific fixation or alternative solutions. If a volar plate is used crossing the border, hardware removal should be scheduled after fracture healing.

Before tightening the last few turns of the first distal screw, make sure the plate is flush to the volar cortex. During distal screw placement, a reduction maneuver, pushing the radial shaft dorsally and flexing the wrist and distal fragment palmarly, is commonly required.

**Technical pearls for the beginner.** *Pegs or screws?* Since the threads are of little use in the metaphyseal bone, pegs might as well be used. If the fracture system collapses and the joint surfaces subside, the pegs will do less harm abrading the cartilage of the carpal bones. Pegs have been reported to be more difficult to extract if necessary.

*One or two rows:* No difference has been shown mechanically between one and two rows of distal screws. In comminuted fractures the second row is used to achieve purchase of more than one screw in each fragment.

*Screw length:* Do not use too long screws in the distal rows! Measure meticulously and subtract at least 2 mm.

*PQ repair:* Although never shown in the studies, flexor tendon injuries appear late, even after several years, and the PQ forms a protecting layer, but the majority of late flexor tendon ruptures are due to a too distal plate.

*Ulnar-radial placement:* To achieve best support for the ulnar volar corner, make sure to place the plate as far ulnarly as possible. Use the subchondral osseous condensation lines as guides and rotate the radius for perpendicular fluoroscope beam direction to avoid DRUJ penetration

*Volar tilt:* If you have a good position of the plate after provisional pinning but volar tilt is not restored, one can lift the proximal part of the plate during drilling close to the subchondral bone. Additional reduction is achieved when the proximal part of the plate is fixed. With this technique, insert all distal screws first.

*Tightening:* Make sure the screws are *tightened into the plate,* or else the implant is not locked and the fracture will collapse. Check *all* screws.

*Fixed or multiangle?* If the reposition and the plate position are perfect, multiangle locking screws are not necessary. Aim for perfect.

*Repair the TFCC?* Always check the DRUJ stability on the other side before surgery and discuss with the patient what to do if a TFCC injury is found. Then check the operated side after fixation. Compare. If anatomic position has been achieved, the DRUJ is often sufficiently stable. A primary TFCC repair after fracture fixation is very uncommon.

*Splint:* Do we need to immobilize after surgery? Maybe not, but a splint keeps the wrist in semiextended position, provides stability and pain control, and is ideal for the antiedema hand therapy.

### Open Reduction and Fixation: Complex Fractures

#### Fernandez Type II—Shear Fractures

The shearing fractures are *inherently unstable* because of the high deforming forces in combination with the loss of bony and ligamentous support. The shearing fractures fare poorly with nonoperative treatment. These are best identified as unstable early on and preferably operated early using open techniques. The basic feature, common to *shearing marginal fractures of the joint surface,* is that a portion of the metaphysis of the distal radius is intact and in continuity with the unaffected area of the joint surface. The ultimate prognosis is good provided the displaced articular fragment or fragments can be precisely reduced and solidly fixed to the intact radius.

*Volarly displaced shear fractures* (AO type B3) are not suited for conservative treatment or percutaneous fixation because of the gross instability and disrupted mechanical axis (Fig. 15.43; see also Fig. 15.11), combined with strong deforming forces of the flexor tendons. Shortening and palmar displacement of the fragment are always associated with volar subluxation of the carpus and misalignment of the DRUJ. This fracture may affect only the most radial or ulnar aspect (Fig. 15.44) of the palmar articular surface, and it may extend ulnarly into the sigmoid notch. Depending on the quality of the bone and the severity of the impact, a variable amount of comminution of the volar fragment may be present. For volarly displaced shear fractures with a larger single fragment, a volar fixed-angle plate is preferable, although a traditional 3.5-mm volar buttress plate or low-profile plate fixation is also appropriate.

#### Fernandez Type III—Joint Surface Compression Fractures

A true die-punch fracture is caused by an axially directed force causing a depression of the joint surface of the intermediate column and a visible step-off. In reality, often shearing forces are involved, and a multitude of fracture patterns are possible (Fig. 15.55), but all variants share comminution of the articular surface of the intermediate column. In young and active individuals these fractures are caused by high-energy accidents, and accurate reduction and maintenance of reduction are essential for an optimal outcome. The severity of the intraarticular engagement may be difficult to judge, and CT scans are preferably added to the plain radiograms to better understand the fracture anatomy and plan the operative approaches.

**Intermediate column single-fragment fractures.** In simple nondisplaced or minimally displaced articular fragment without significant metaphyseal comminution, closed reduction and cast application may be all that is necessary. In many cases, however, the high energy causing this fracture impacts the intermediate column fragment, and operative reduction and stabilization are required. Joint distraction with horizontal finger-trap traction is generally not sufficient to disimpact and realign the small cartilage-bearing fragments, nor accomplish reduction of a rotated volar lip and impacted dorsal–ulnar corner fragments.

Isolated and displaced fractures of the dorsal or volar–ulnar corner are rare (see Fig. 15.44) but potentially serious injuries. Intracarpal ligament injuries may be present, which need to be recognized. An unreduced intermediate column fragment can lead to carpal subluxation, distal radioulnar incongruency, or both. If closed manipulation fails to provide anatomic congruity and stability of the joint surface, open reduction with direct fragment fixation is preferable.

**Three- and four-part injuries.** Further fragmentation of the intermediate column produces more complex diagnostic and treatment challenges. A CT scan is vital and has been demonstrated to alter not only the decision to operate but also the surgical approach in nearly 50% of cases. In general, these fracture types will require surgical skill and combination of (1) longitudinal traction or reduction of the metaphyseal fracture, (2) open reduction for restoration of joint congruity, (3) possible bone grafting of the metaphyseal defect, and (4) often fragment-specific fixation or augmented external fixation. Angular stable plates and miniplate–wireform techniques have enabled stable internal fixation in these complex injuries and are alternatives to combined internal and external fixation (Fig. 15.56; Case Study 15.10). Subchondral placement of pegs to buttress the small articular fragments and simultaneously control shortening and angular displacement are particularly helpful in osteoporotic bones.

#### Type IV—Radiocarpal Fracture–Dislocation

These are high-energy injuries in a young patient cohort that combine bone and soft tissue components and have the potential for significant wrist dysfunction. Dumontier and colleagues describe two distinct variants of radiocarpal fracture–dislocation with important treatment implications. Even though uncommon, a radiocarpal dislocation with only a small fleck of attached bone, by definition, has disrupted each of the volar and/or dorsal extrinsic ligamentous radiocarpal stabilizers and will, unless meticulously repaired, predictably progress to a dorsal or volar translational deformity. In a similar way, an ulnar translational deformity may occur when the RSC and long radiolunate ligaments are injured. A severely comminuted radial styloid fracture may be seen as an analog of the volar rim fracture with the radial screws of the volar plate unable to retain the position of the styloid with the carpus progressing in an ulnar translational deformity.

Treatment consists of anatomic reduction and stable fixation of the styloid and dorsal/volar rim fragments. The volar ligaments are repaired, and suture anchors used if necessary. Check the SLiL. Total ruptures should be repaired and pinned as this injury adds to the instability. Styloid fragments that comprise at least a third of the scaphoid facet of the radius preserve the function of the RSC and long radiolunate ligaments such that anatomic reduction and healing restore stability to the carpus. Use rigid columnar fixation of the styloid with a radial pin plate or a 2.4-mm radial column plate combined with the volar or dorsal rim fixation using a dorsal wire form or a 2.4-mm dorsal plate. Fixation stability is tested in the operating room under fluoroscopy, and postoperative immobilization is customized. Cannulated screw fixation, isolated Kirschner wires, or a tension band can also be used, depending on the size of the styloid fragment and surgeon’s preference. Multiple Kirschner wire or temporary dorsal distraction plating placement is often necessary to stabilize during ligament healing.

#### Type V—Combined/Complex Injury

Finally, for more *complex fracture patterns,* such as type V or C3-3 high-energy fractures (e.g., combined articular, metaphyseal, diaphyseal), no single implant or treatment paradigm is appropriate to solve

all components of the fracture and the soft tissue injuries. A staged reconstruction, using a primary external fixator or spanning plate,with or without later reconstruction, is chosen in many centers. If the fracture has a relatively simple articular component and extensive metaphyseal–diaphyseal comminution with large butterfly fragments, internal fixation with interfragmentary screws and a metadiaphyseal fixed-angle device is the method of choice to simultaneously restore radial alignment and length and to bridge the bony comminution (Fig. 15.57). Initial application of a bridging external fixator to gain length and alignment may be helpful, and it may be left in place at the surgeon’s discretion for additional stability. For more comminuted articular fractures, a combined dorsal–volar exposure with compartment and median nerve release, autologous cancellous bone grafting of the metaphyseal defect, and a combination of external, internal, or fragment-specific fixation may be required. For cases with extensive articular and metaphyseal–diaphyseal disruption, particularly for patients who present with multiple traumas or those who require load bearing on their injured wrist in the immediate postoperative period, bridge (distraction) plating is an effective solution that preserves radial length during the healing process.

#### Fragment-Specific Fixation

The “fragment-specific” classification was introduced in 2005 building on ideas based on the column theory. Robert Medoff developed a classification of intraarticular fractures and recognized five major fragments: the radial styloid, the dorsal wall, the impacted articular, the dorsal–ulnar corner (i.e., “die-punch”), and the volar rim (Fig. 15.58). If the Fernandez classification is excellent for understanding the mechanisms behind a DRF, the fragment-specific classification is extremely useful for how to treat them. Articular fractures are described by identifying the components, either alone or in combination. The fragment-specific classification is treatmentoriented, and fracture fixation and implants were designed to stabilize each major fragment, advancing from single incisions and augmentation by percutaneous pinning, which was popular at the time, to multiple short incisions. The concept was introduced as minimally invasive. To secure the unsupported pins, which tended and still tend to loosen, a hybrid technique was developed, the so-called *pin plate.* A thin miniature 2.0-mm plate is anchored in intact bone proximally, and by letting the wire pass through a hole at the distal free end of the plate, the pin position is secured. The bending stiffness, compared with conventional unsupported Kirschner wire fixation, increases dramatically, and the three-point fixation makes it unlikely for the pin to slide out, thereby achieving a stable fixation for the individual fragment. By using *multiplanar* or orthogonal fixation, pushing the individual fragments from volar, radial, and dorsal toward the ulna head during healing, and with wire forms or *buttress pins* added to support the joint surfaces, stability of even a severely comminuted fracture system can be achieved, not only during surgery but also during healing and consolidation. Mechanical studies using an unstable metaphyseal fracture model have demonstrated that dual 2.0-mm plates, placed at 50- to 90-degree angles to each other in the axial plane, provide fixation superior to either Kirschner wire–augmented external fixation or to a traditional 3.5-mm dorsal “T”-plate.

![[Fig.15.58.png]]
> **Fig. 15.58** The fragment-specific classification by Rob Medoff.

The use of the term “fragment-specific fixation” in this text does not refer to a particular implant type but to the concept of the use of two or more low-profile implants placed strategically along the columns of the distal radius to fix individual fracture fragments.Fragment-specific fixation is not intended to be used for extraarticular or else uncomplicated fractures but rather for unstable high-energy articular fractures of the distal radius. The system is generally contraindicated for fractures with substantial metaphyseal–diaphyseal extension or severe osteoporosis but can be added to a volar plate. Apart from several prospective and retrospective single cohort series, there are randomized studies showing equivalent subjective outcome compared with volar locking plates and superior outcome at 1 year regarding grip strength and forearm rotation compared with external fixator, in unselected cohorts with A and C fractures. The fragment-specific system, however, is not used today as primary system for the bulk of fractures but rather as a powerful tool to be used in complex intraarticular fractures not possible to address by standard techniques. The learning curve is initially steep for these particular implants.

### AUTHOR'S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: FRAGMENT-SPECIFIC FIXATION

**Planning and order.** There are several schools regarding what order the three approaches, volar, radial, and dorsal, should follow. In my hands, I always start out with the volar Henry approach, the PQ split, and expose the volar ulnar corner. The fracture is reduced, and sometimes even the most comminuted fracture falls into place in an anatomic position, sometimes with a little help of the Hagert hook maneuver (see Fig. 15.35F and G). The rest of the operation is then a matter of stabilizing the fragments in whatever order is suitable. The oblique pin is inserted percutaneously into the radial styloid, exiting the radius ulnarly and proximal to the fracture. A second pin may be inserted through the volar rim, distal to the watershed line, through the capsule in an oblique, proximal and dorsal direction (see Fig. 15.35K). A volar locking plate may then be sufficient, provided (1) the lunate facette is not comminuted and is large enough to be covered by the plate, and (2) the radial styloid is not too comminuted, with a risk of the radial screws not being capable of maintaining the reduced position. If deemed too comminuted, a radial pin plate is added to the volar plate securing the stability of the radial column, since both columns always need to be stable after fixation (see Fig. 15.41).

If the dorsoulnar corner has been reduced, it can be reached by the volar screws, and using the two rows of the plate it might be possible to secure at least two locking pegs or screws in the dorsoulnar fragment (Fig. 15.59). In this case the plan should be changed and a dorsal incision added. **Volar rim fracture.** The volar–ulnar or teardrop fragment constitutes the volar half of the lunate facet. Owing both to the strong short radiolunate ligament and the osseous shape with the volar protrusion, the teardrop constitutes the primary restraint to volar subluxation of the carpus. At the same time, the teardrop is a prime stabilizer of the DRUJ, with the volar rim of the sigmoid notch being the origin of the volar radioulnar ligament. Failure either to recognize or adequately stabilize this fragment may result in the dramatic delayed palmar subluxation of the carpus (see Fig. 15.42), but may also lead to articular incongruency, radioulnar instability, and ulnocarpal impaction. As previously described, placing the standard volar locking plate too radial, not covering the ulnar corner, may lead to failure even with a large fragment, but in this chapter we discuss surgical options when the fragment is too small or too comminuted to allow fixation by the volar plate.

Two separate fracture mechanisms can be described ending up with volar rim fragments, the volar shearing fracture (see Fig. 15.43) and dorsal dislocations with avulsion of the volar capsule (see Fig. 15.49). The first type fracture is a volar B.3.3-type shearing fracture caused by a volarly directed shearing force. Most of the time, the scaphoid and lunate portions of the volar articular surface shear off from the distal radius as one unit, known as the Smith Barton fracture, with a volar subluxation and an incongruency between the lunate and the intact *dorsal* lunate facet. Due to the ligamentous constraint, with the short radiolunate ligament still attached to the volar rim, the standard B.3.2 Smith Barton fracture is easily reduced and fixed with a volar plate. In the B.3.3 subtype, however, the volar fragment has fragmented into at least two pieces, a styloid scaphoid facet volar fragment and a separate lunate facet volar fragment. The short radiolunate ligament is still attached to the lunate facet volar fragment, but when the fragment is too small, ending distal to the distal edge of a standard volar plate, the small anterior rim fragment may slide over the distal edge of the plate. Since the small fragment is still attached to the lunate via the short radiolunate ligament, the lunate bone follows, as do the carpus and the whole hand, ending up in a catastrophic volar malposition (see Fig. 15.42). Worse, the fracture may look reduced after volar plate fixation, only to dislocate palmarly during the following weeks. We know that an SL ligament injury is a common feature in DRFs, occurring in up to half of the DRFs. Most of the time these are without consequences but may, as a part of a vicious combination, an unhappy triad, with (1) at least two separate volar rim fragments, (2) a fracture distal to the watershed line, and (3) an SL and/or RSC ligament injury, play a role in allowing the volar dislocation.

If the wrist is dorsally *compressed* but not dislocated, the volar ligaments are intact, in particular the taut and short radiolunate ligament, and the articular surface of the lunate fossa is seen congruent to the lunate. With increasing degree of dorsal dislocation at the time of the trauma, the articular surface continues to get impacted and/or the volar short radiolunate ligament pulls the volar fragment off. A volar rim fracture is seen radiographically, caused by a dorsally directed trauma. If the lunate is dorsally subluxated, with a volar incongruency between the lunate fossa and the lunate bone but no volar fragment, the fracture is fixed dorsally, but a volar approach is mandatory to evaluate the volar ligaments. A tear of the volar capsule is found and sutured. If the volar capsule insertion is bony avulsed, the volar fracture is treated as a volar rim fracture using a volar buttress pin or a volar hook plate.

Technique. The volar–ulnar fracture fragment can be reached through the standard Henry approach, but a more extensile and more midline approach between the flexor tendons and the ulnar neurovascular bundle may be preferred for better exposure if a small size volar ulnar fragment is suspected. The size of the fragment is best measured in preoperative CT scans and should exceed 15 mm, and the axial compression should not exceed 5 mm. During surgery, the fragment can be identified. It is often rotated, best seen on the fluoroscope in the lateral view. Comminution may be difficult to evaluate radiographically, and in particular visually, since the fracture is covered by the volar capsule. It is therefore important to directly visualize and palpate the fragment, using the tip of the forceps distal to the watershed line to diagnose a hidden comminution.

During the whole operation, the volar–ulnar fragment is handled with care to avoid further splitting and comminution. The fragment, often either impacted or rotated, is carefully reduced by longitudinal traction to restore the continuity of the palmar cortex at the metaphyseal area. An oblique Kirschner wire is inserted at the tip of the teardrop in an oblique volar-to-dorsal direction (see Fig. 15.35K), and if the fragment is large enough, gently used as a joystick for derotation and temporary fixation. Today there are a few special implants for the volar rim, none of them perfect but all designed for a distal position allowing the flexor tendons, which inevitably are in contact with the implant, to glide between the protruding implant parts. The volar buttress pin was the first special implant to be used. It is tricky to insert since the two pins are inserted through the volar capsule without direct vision, but this implant, in the author’s hands, provides the most reliable stable fixation (Fig. 15.62). The more recent volar hook plate (Fig. 15.63) is easier to use, with a guide that can slide over the pin, substantially facilitating the insertion. The volar hook plate is an alternative in larger fragments but has a tendency to fracture the fragment if not handled with utmost care. The two prongs of both implants serve as a fixed-angle support for the subchondral bone simultaneously catching the volar capsule and can therefore be used also in the dorsal dislocations. The proximal part of the implant is rigidly secured to the intact bone proximally with washers and 2.3-mm screws and restores the length of the intermediate column, upon which the dorsal and radial fracture components are built. A radial pin plate is *always added* for multiplane fixation and often sufficient for fixation of the scaphoid facette, but an additional volar buttress pin or a fixed-angle volar plate may be added.

If no special plates are available, solutions can be applied using bent Kirschner wires, which are then hidden under the standard volar locking plate. Moore and Dennison reported maintained reduction in 9 of 9 patients treated in this fashion. Also, a Kirschner wire plus tension-band wire has been shown to be successful in a case series. The wire is augmented with a suture or figure-of-eight wire placed through the proximal metaphyseal cortex in a tension band configuration. Tension wiring alone is not sufficient in a volar shearing fracture but may be an alternative in the dorsal dislocation with a volar avulsion fracture. One can also use miniature 1.5-mm and 2.0-mm locking plates to buttress and stabilize small volar ulnar rim fractures.

**Dorsal plating.** Next the dorsal fragments of the intermediate column are addressed. The incision is centered slightly ulnar to the Lister tubercle. The dorsoulnar fragment can be reached between the third and fourth compartments, without opening the tendon compartment, if only the dorsoulnar corner is aimed for. The fourth dorsal compartment is opened if a wider exposure is needed, to address the dorsal wall or intraarticular joint surface fragments. The radial metaphysis can be widely exposed by elevating the second or fourth (or both) compartments in a subperiosteal fashion.

Technique. The radius bone ulnar to Lister is exposed and the comminuted dorsal cortical wall can be lifted to expose the radial metaphysis. An elevator is used to disimpact the articular fragments and elevate the articular surface en bloc against the proximal carpal row bones. The reduction is checked by the fluoroscope in the frontal plan, both regarding the DRUJ and the radiocarpal joint, and in the lateral plane estimating the AP distance. A 0.045-inch Kirschner wire is inserted in an oblique, dorsal-to-volar, distal-to-proximal direction, exiting on the volar side. A three-hole ulnar pin plate or a 2.0- to 2.4-mm low-profile locked plate is contoured and applied so it fits over the fragment with care taken to not overlap the radioulnar joint. The pin plate is secured with one unicortical screw in the proximal hole. A second wire is driven through one of the distal holes and the two wires are sequentially measured, cut, and bent 180 degrees at their tips before being impacted back into a free hole in the plate (Fig. 15.64).

In general, the dorsal capsule is not opened for direct inspection unless needed to ensure articular congruity. If necessary, in particular to lift depressed articular fragments not possible to address using an elevator, the ligament sparing technique can be used, as described by Berger, not to injure the dorsal ligament. Bone graft or structural bone graft substitute are rarely needed using the modern plates but may be used to augment the reduction of impacted articular fragments.

**Radial pin plate.** The final fragment to fix is the radial styloid, and if not done before, a straight radial incision is made from the tip of the styloid and proximally. The length of the incision is depending on the intended plate to be used, in turn depending on the comminution of the radial styloid. In general, if multiple incisions and multiple plates have been used, it may be wise to use a longer plate, since the space is limited in the metaphysis for the last screws. The superficial radial sensory nerve branches are identified and followed both distally and proximally. The nerve branches in a random fashion and always more or less blocks the access and needs to be properly dealt with, as any injury prompt leads to symptoms. Once the full understanding of the nerve branching is reached, the first dorsal compartment is split longitudinally. The ideal position for a radial column implant is directly beneath and oblique to the first dorsal compartment tendons, with the distal tip of the plate dorsal to the tendons and the proximal end of the plate palmar. Since the plate thus will cross underneath the abductor pollicis longus (APL)/extensor pollicis brevis (EPB) tendons, these are lifted, and the BR is split longitudinally. The periosteum is elevated in a dorsal and palmar plane to visualize the radius and the plate is threaded onto the distal pin and squeezed under the APL/EPB tendons. The position is checked in both the frontal and lateral planes and the proximal screw is inserted. The second pin is inserted and the pins backed out, shortened and impacted. The remaining screws are inserted, and any coronal shift malposition is now reduced as the screws are tightened.

The DRUJ and the SL ligament are then assessed for stability, and appropriate treatment is rendered. The wrist and forearm are immobilized in a volar or dorsal splint for 10 to 14 days. During the first 2 weeks, the main focus for the patient is to regain full finger motion and flexion, being able to make a full composite fist. After removal of the sutures, the patients can start a program of active, unresisted wrist motion exercises. Strengthening exercises are begun after fracture consolidation, generally at 6 to 8 weeks postoperatively, and a radiogram is useful to evaluate plate and pin position. Removal of the fragment-specific implants is more common than when using a volar plate.

#### Other Fixation Methods

Virtually all fractures of the distal radius can be managed with the previously detailed fixation strategies—namely, percutaneous/external, low-profile dorsal plate, angular stable volar plate, and fragmentspecific. A well-trained surgeon versed in each of these techniques can apply them in isolation or in combination to effectively reduce a DRF and maintain fragment stability until healing. However, a highly comminuted DRF may not be amenable to complex periarticular reconstruction for various reasons, for example, in multiple extremity trauma when other injuries may be more urgent or when the surgeons performing the primary surgery may be less experienced with wrist trauma. As an alternative to temporary external fixation, which is known to be troublesome and far from perfect in these complex fractures, the technique of distraction plating was described by Burke and Singer. A bridging or spanning plate is used as an “internal” bridging fixator to grossly but rapidly restore the radial alignment and length. The method has become increasingly popular, and highly satisfactory results have been published with a low complication rate for a very difficult injury cohort. No randomized studies have been published. The comminuted articular fragments are allowed to reduce under ligamentotaxis, with the plate providing a buttress for the dorsal cortex of the distal radius. In addition, a long dorsal plate bridges any metaphyseal comminution, less easily addressed with external fixator or volar plating. Initially thought of as a temporary method, it often remains the final treatment, and if well reduced, there is no need for a secondary revision. Today, radiocarpal distraction plates have been designed specific for the distal radius and are available from most manufacturers. The length has increased, compared with the previously used wrist arthrodesis plates, enabling better control in metaphyseal comminution. The plates have a straight design with contoured ends to facilitate passage under the extensor tendons, the holes are threaded for locking screws, and fewer central holes increase the stiffness. The dorsal distraction plate today is technically an easier operation to perform than volar plating for complex articular fractures.

**Distraction plating technique.** The fracture is reduced and pinned. Intraarticular depression, volar and dorsoulnar fragments are not attached to extrinsic ligaments and may not disimpact by ligamentotaxis. These may need special attention and fixation, percutaneously or open. Otherwise, a two-incision technique is used with one 4-cm incision over the dorsal surface of the second or third metacarpal, and a second 5-cm incision over the dorsoradial shaft, approximately 4 cm proximal to the fracture. The second metacarpal is preferred if possible. The plate is passed bluntly underneath the extensor tendons and across the fracture site and placed along the floor of the fourth dorsal compartment. Care is taken to allow the EPL free passage (Fig. 15.65). The plate is fixed to the metacarpal with a single screw, to enable fine-tuning of the fracture reduction. The hand and forearm are supinated approximately 60 degrees to avoid fixing the fracture in pronation, and a provisional fracture-reduction clamp is applied to hold the plate to the radius proximally. The forearm is taken through a full range of rotation and gross alignment is assessed with fluoroscopy. Care needs to be taken to prevent excess radiocarpal or midcarpal distraction. Proximal and distal fixation is finalized with a minimum of three bicortical screws. Locking screws may be preferred in osteoporotic bone.

### The Ulnar Column

The most frequent complaints of residual disability and functional loss following a healed fracture of the distal radius emanate from the ulnar column, the third of the three columns. A thorough evaluation is therefore indicated also in the acute stage, although the ulnar column injuries are often overseen and rarely treated. Type I consists of *stable* DRUJ lesions, which means that adequate reduction of the radius will render the joint clinically stable without disruption of the articular surface. Such lesions include: (1) avulsion of the tip of the ulnar styloid and (2) stable fracture of the neck of the ulna. In both, the primary stabilizers of the joint, in particular the TFCC foveal attachment, are intact.

Type II consists of *unstable* DRUJ lesions. Despite satisfactory reduction of the radius, the DRUJ, compared with the contralateral, is unstable as a result of (1) a massive tear of the TFCC or (2) an avulsion fracture of the ulnar styloid through or below the fovea. These injuries may require supination casting, repair of the TFCC, or fixation of the avulsed ulnar styloid.

Type III includes comminuted articular injuries of either the sigmoid notch or the ulnar head, and these injuries require reduction and stabilization to restore articular congruency and prevent degenerative changes.

To assess radioulnar stability following completion of radial fixation, a manual “shuck” test (Fig. 15.67) of the ulna is performed by grasping the ulnar head between the examiner’s thumb and index finger and translating it dorsally and palmarly within the sigmoid notch. Gross instability is manifested by frank dislocation, but more subtle instability can be appreciated by the loss of a firm endpoint to translation and by increased subluxation of the ulna relative to the uninjured wrist. The shuck test is performed in all rotation positions to specifically test both the palmar and the dorsoradioulnar ligaments (see DRUJ section). It is further helpful to have assessed the stability of the contralateral DRUJ before preparing and draping the injured wrist.

#### Ulnar Styloid Fixation

There is considerable controversy concerning the need to repair fractures of the ulnar styloid, in particular the *fracture of the base of the ulnar styloid.* Just as a foveal detachment of the TFCC can occur, an avulsion of the base of the styloid may lead to laxity of the DRUJ. A substantial number of studies in the last decades have questioned the relevance of an ulnar styloid fracture to radioulnar stability and subjective outcome. In a comprehensive metaanalysis in 2243 patients, using DASH at 12 months as outcome, a concomitant fracture of the ulnar styloid process did not significantly affect subjective outcome, nor range of motion, grip strength, or pain in patients with a DRF. Although DASH was found to be three scale steps better in the patients without a styloid fracture, the difference was well below the minimal clinically important difference of DASH. In other studies, the level of the ulnar styloid process fracture or nonuniondid not seem to change the functional outcomes. It appears that ulnar styloid fracture as a separate finding does not influence the outcome after a DRF, but the finding should be a part of the evaluation of the DRUJ stability.

#### The Coronal Shift and DRUJ Stability

Although the coronal shift can be viewed in anatomic drawings already at the end of the 19th century (Fig. 15.68), it was first described in the context of a DRF by Hagert. Its importance in the anatomic reduction of a DRF and the influence on DRUJ stability has been vigorously discussed in the last decade. Investigators have identified the distal interosseous membrane (DIOM) and in particular the DOB, first named by Noda in 2009, as a major restraining structure, stabilizing the ulna to the sigmoid notch even when the TFCC is disrupted (Fig. 15.69). The DOB originates from approximately the distal one-sixth area of the ulnar shaft, coinciding with the proximal border of the pronator quadratus muscle, and running distally toward the DRUJ. The fibers blend into the capsular tissue of the DRUJ and insert onto the inferior rim of the sigmoid notch of the radius. Some fibers extend more distally along the anterior and posterior ridges of the sigmoid notch, in continuity with the dorsal and palmar radioulnar ligaments of the TFCC. A more recent anatomic study defined the ligament to insert distally/dorsally on the radius and proximally/palmarly on the ulna, suggesting its primary role in resisting dorsal translation of the radius on the ulna in supination. The DOB can be identified and evaluated by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound.

Nakamura and colleagues demonstrated that coronal translation, loss of radial inclination, ulnar shortening, and a 4-mm radial translation of the ulnar styloid were all predictive of a foveal avulsion of the DRUJ ligaments; however, coronal translation was the only independent predictive variable. Most often the dorsal compression is reduced simultaneously. After having secured the distal screws, but only inserted the most proximal screw, the latter is reversed half a turn and the shaft pulled radially for a final fine-tuning before locking the plate with the rest of the proximal screws. Other methods using similar techniques but different tools have been described using an “Army-Navy” retractor or a Hohman retractor. Surgical methods to reconstruct the DOB ligament have been published, mainly cadaveric but also some clinically used.

#### Distal Radioulnar Joint Injury Treatment Options

For type I stable injuries, *functional after-care* with early active forearm rotation exercises and minimal external support is recommended. Early motion is likewise recommended for patients with fractures of the distal ulna or ulnar styloid in which radioulnar stability has been restored with stable internal fixation, and for type III comminuted fractures of the ulnar head that are not amenable to internal fixation to allow fracture remodeling.

Type II subluxation can be treated in a number of ways. Many prefer 4 weeks of cast or splint immobilization with the forearm in the most stable rotation position; most often in partial to complete supination. In grossly unstable DRUJ dislocations, the osseous reduction is reevaluated, and if anatomically reduced, the TFCC should be repaired. Percutaneous transfixion of the ulna to the radial shaft is a last resort and should be avoided if possible. Otherwise, the DRUJ is transfixed in the position of greatest stability with dual 0.062-inch Kirschner wires. The wires are placed just proximal to the joint, and it is helpful to leave their tips protruding through both the radial and ulnar cortices in the less likely but troublesome event that wire breakage occurs in the interosseous space. The wires may be removed at 4 weeks postoperatively and rotation exercises begun.

For type II *unstable* DRUJ lesions with a large ulnar basostyloid fragment, fracture fixation should be considered. The styloid is exposed with a 3- to 4-cm incision midway between the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) and the flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU). Care is taken to identify and preserve the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve, which courses through the ulnar “snuffbox” just distal to the styloid tip (Fig. 15.70). A 24- or 26-gauge wire or nonabsorbable suture can be passed either around or preferably through the styloid fragment and then through the ulna at the axilla of the shaft and the articular surface. The suture/wire is tensioned and tied with the forearm in neutral rotation. Alternatively, the fragment can be stabilized with one or two 0.035-inch Kirschner wires and a tension band wire passed proximally through the ulnar shaft. The forearm is immobilized in neutral rotation for several weeks, depending on the degree of stability attained.

### AUTHOR'S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ULNAR COLUMN INSTABILITY

Anatomic restoration of length, inclination, tilt, and coronal alignment of the fractured radius is paramount for the stability of the DRUJ. Even in total TFCC injuries, low- and medium-demand patients will have a good outcome provided anatomic reduction of the radius fracture is achieved, probably due to sufficient stability provided by the DOB.

After fixation of the radius fracture, the DRUJ stability is tested. Unstable fractures of the dorsal and volar margins of the sigmoid notches should have been rigidly stabilized since they may lead to radioulnar subluxation. If an ulna neck or head fracture is present and the DRUJ is unstable, the fracture is preferably reduced anatomically and fixed with 2.0-mm, 2.4-mm, or 2.7-mm plates; interfragmentary screws; cerclage wiring; or a tension band, depending on the nature of the fracture and the degree of comminution. The surgery is technically demanding, and the fractures are often more comminuted than appreciated in the fracture radiograms. Basilar styloid fractures are also fixed if the DRUJ is unstable, whereas styloid tip fractures are always left unfixed. If open reduction and fixation have been successful, early range of motion is started. Open or arthroscopic repair of the TFCC in the acute phase in my hands is rarely indicated in the acute setting unless the patient is an athlete or high-demand. In conservatively treated fractures with unstable DRUJ, I always start with a correction osteotomy for anatomic position, even in mild malunions, before contemplating TFCC repair (Case Study 15.11).

### Open Fractures

Most fractures of the distal radius are closed injuries, but any fracture that communicates with the external environment should be treated as an open fracture. Sometimes it is obvious, and an easily visible skin injury is found. Other times the diagnosis is less obvious and there may be only a small wound with a minor fluid exudate. All open fractures preferably are treated as surgical emergencies and should not wait for several days. Antibiotics should be started as early as possible. The wound and the fracture are irrigated already in the ER after swab or specimen for culture has been obtained, and intravenous or oral antibiotics are initiated. The fracture is temporarily stabilized in a splint and prepared for surgery. Grossly contaminated wounds should receive additional anaerobic and aminoglycoside coverage or according to local guidelines. In these cases, the patient is transported to the operating room, where the wound is enlarged, the skin and fracture margins are debrided, and the wound is irrigated with large quantities of saline. Restoration of the stability is paramount to control infection; thus if the fracture is unstable, there is adequate soft tissue coverage, and the wound has been suitably cleaned, I prefer to stabilize the fracture with internal or percutaneous fixation, depending on the fracture’s particular characteristics. If the wound cannot be suitably cleaned or if it has been open for more than 12 hours, the fracture should be stabilized with an external fixator, the wound left open, and the patient returned to the operating room at 48 hours for definitive treatment and wound closure.

## TREATMENT OF THE ELDERLY

Over the past few years, interest has increasingly focused on the treatment of DRFs in older individuals, partly due to a radical rise in the numbers of elderly, but also partly due to a focus on cost containment in medical care. When asking patients, a preference was identified for internal fixation over other types of fracture treatment modalities, with patients being more concerned about the short-term rehabilitation than the long-term outcome. Also, elderly patients wish to be involved in the decision-making, and using qualitative methods, a deeper understanding of the hidden motives and expectations of the elderly patients can be acquired. Also, the definition of who is “elderly” is gradually changing. When “elderly” is defined as an age starting at 60 to 65 years, the conclusions become difficult to interpret. The younger elderly today are, in many cases, as active as the individuals in the younger cohort and see themselves entitled to a fully functioning and pain-free wrist. More sedentary, elderly individuals >75 to 80 years, however, also deserve a pain-free and well-functioning wrist in order to keep them independent in their later years. Outcome studies in this group are rare.

In a healthy population, age correlates with the DASH score and worsens almost linearly with age. The elderly as a group have inferior DASH functional scores and take longer to return regarding function. The elderly have an inferior global health-related quality of life than their younger counterparts, which implies that the lack of differences between treatments within this group may be related to nonorthopedic factors. Conventional wisdom ascribes the lack of differences to the older cohort’s “low functional demands,” but no study has stratified outcome by activity level in the senior population and to date no prospective studies have utilized a physical activity scale for the elderly to differentiate patients.

Several prospective studies demonstrate a lack of significant functional outcome differences among elderly patients after 1 year and imply that treatment options for unstable DRFs in this cohort are interchangeable. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) appropriate use panel concluded that firm conclusions for DRF treatment in the elderly were confounded by the lack of studies that “distinguished patients based on infirmity, *functional demands,* bone quality, or energy of injury.” While Grewal et al. identified no significant risk relationship between fracture alignment and outcome in their entire older cohort, their relative risk data suggest that their *active* elderly patients achieved better outcomes when the parameters of radial reduction were met. Recently, two high-quality, randomized studies have been published, showing improved outcome after surgical treatment in the elderly. In both studies a high number of fractures in the conservative groups lost reduction, and the radiologic end result clearly did influence the subjective outcome. A recent metaanalysis implies the same. Hopefully more studies will come in the next few years studying elderly, not as a single homogenous group, but divided into subgroups depending on prefracture function and demands.

### AUTHOR'S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THE ELDERLY

Even though restoration of mobility, lack of pain, and return of function is the universal goal of treatment also for the elderly, consideration must be given to the physiologic age and, in particular, functional need and activity level, to provide customized care. For the relatively inactive or sedentary patient, a treatment combining minimal morbidity with emphasis on maintenance of digital and forearm mobility is preferable; nonsurgical treatment is likely to result in acceptable outcomes despite imperfect radiographic outcomes, and deficits in grip strength and range of motion are likely to be well tolerated.

For the highly active patients with high functional demands on the wrist, in contrast, pain and loss of strength are less likely to be compatible with their lifestyle. In these individuals, age is an unimportant factor in the decision making and the same efforts should be made to restore articular congruency, radial alignment, and a normal radioulnar relationship as in younger patients. It is wise to ask the elderly patient’s opinion. Percutaneous methods have an increased risk of loss of reduction in osteopenic bone, and for elderly, especially those with decreased bone mass, the increased rigidity of volar locked-plate fixation is preferable to pinning or external fixation. Even comminuted C3 fractures, difficult to fix in osteoporotic bone, can be safely fixed also in the elderly, as shown in a prospective study investigating the outcome after surgical fixation.

My preference for the inactive, low-demand, super elderly patient >80 to 85 years is conservative treatment, unless major displacement is present. In the more active super elderly group, stable internal fixation with a fixed-angle volar plate or fragment-specific fixation is in my view preferable, but after careful discussion with the patient. Those who elect an earlier return to function may opt for internal fixation, knowing there is an additional risk of complications, and that the long-term outcome may not be measurably different to any type of treatment.

## ASSOCIATED INJURIES

### Associated Median Nerve Injury

With the median and ulnar nerve in close vicinity to the distal radius, it may not be surprising that these nerves are commonly involved and need attention. The ulnar nerve is more often involved in the acute situation, in particular in open ulnar fractures or in combined radius ulna metaphyseal fractures, which may have been severely displaced at the moment of fracture.

Median nerve dysfunction is usually caused by blunt contusion or stretching of the nerve over the angulated distal radius and may accompany an acute DRF. A fracture hematoma may compromise the nerve within the carpal tunnel, particularly in patients who may have mild or nocturnal symptoms preceding the injury. Iatrogenic median nerve injuries should be avoided, with the median nerve at risk in the Henry incision by the retractor, including the palmar cutaneous branch (Fig. 15.72). It is wise to record the degree of nerve involvement with careful measurements of two-point discrimination and, if possible, thenar motor function before treatment.

![[Fig.15.72.png]]
> **Fig. 15.72** A to C, Dangers by the Henry approach.

In case of median nerve symptoms after fracture, closed reduction is performed under adequate local or regional anesthesia. If satisfactory reduction is obtained, observation plus careful follow-up is all that is necessary in most instances. If the reduction is maintained, the nerve compression syndrome generally improves substantially over the subsequent 24 to 48 hours. If neurologic symptoms get worse or show no improvement during that period, or if the reduction cannot be obtained or maintained in the presence of median nerve compression, early operative stabilization of the fracture and a carpal tunnel release are preferable. In this situation, one must never connect a Henry incision with a carpal tunnel incision. Always leave a skin bridge between, where the palmar cutaneous median nerve runs (Fig. 15.73). There are no data to support routine release of the carpal tunnel at the time of operative fixation in patients without preoperative evidence of median nerve injury.

### Associated Carpal Ligament Injuries

Carpal ligament disruption can occur in both intraarticular and extraarticular fractures of the distal radius and may influence the subjective outcome. Certain fracture patterns like a radiocarpal fracture–dislocation or severely displaced radial styloid fractures entering the ridge between the scaphoid and lunate fossae are particularly at risk for associated carpal ligament injury. The incidence of associated intercarpal ligament disruption with fractures of the distal radius has been documented with arthroscopy, ranging from 30% to 78% for partial or complete scapholunate tears and 15% to 54% for lunotriquetral tears. In the latter study, the subjective outcome in the group of patients with untreated SLiL tear Geissler grade I–III did not differ from the ones without when reexamined at long term 13 to 15 years,and no wrists progressed to a scapholunate advanced collapse (SLAC) wrist (Fig. 15.74). However, no total tears (Geissler grade IV), which we know may progress into a SLAC wrist, were observed (Fig. 15.75).

## COMPLICATIONS

Until recently it was believed that all patients with DRFs get well. It is partly true; the vast majority do achieve a good outcome. It may take longer than the patient believes, and complications are more common than generally perceived, both in surgically as well as conservatively treated fractures. With the increasing number of patients being surgically treated, new types of complications are encountered. It should also be emphasized that conservative treatment can lead to complications as well, maybe less noted and less dramatic. These are often seen as acceptable consequences of the fracture itself and not as a result of suboptimal conservative treatment. It is clear that our treatment can be improved, both for conservative and surgical options (Case Studies 15.12 and 15.13). We cannot accept 15% to 20% of the patients ending up in DASH values exceeding 35, implying pain at rest and substantial limitations in function and activity.

The rate of complications can be reduced for surgical treatment by improved training, hands-on workshops, and educational meetings, and maybe most importantly, leaving the difficult cases to the most experienced surgeons. Also, conservative treatment can be improved by better knowledge and better guidelines of what types of fractures are doomed to redisplace, eventually needing surgery. Coming to the decision to operate earlier and not delaying with weekly radiographs may play a role, but also having a team that can identify complications such as chronic regional pain syndrome (CRPS) early. For conservative treatment, the majority of the complications that continue to plague the patients are related to malunion. With axial collapse, ulnar impaction is not uncommon and causes painful rotation and ulnar-sided pain.Radial and/or dorsal compression alone may cause similar symptoms, and a TFCC injury by speculation may be more prone to be symptomatic with a combination of these. Tendon rupture, in particular the EPL, is still common but curable, in contrast to CRPS or radiocarpal or radioulnar arthrosis, which are less likely to fully resolve.

### Tendon Rupture

Cast treatment of nondisplaced or minimally displaced DRFs is complicated by rupture of the EPB in up to 5% of cases. Patients with painful thumb extension, especially if radiographs demonstrate an irregularity in the region of the Lister tubercle, should be examined by dynamic ultrasound for signs of tendon irritation or fraying. On occasion, decompression may be warranted to prevent this complication; however, so far clinical guidelines are lacking.

Apart from the spontaneous EPL ruptures, the increased use of volar plating has increased the incidence of EPL ruptures due to screws protruding dorsally in the EPL groove (Fig. 15.78A). It needs to be emphasized that screw length measurement is important in volar plating, and it is better to stay short than long. Bicortical purchase should not be aimed for distally, and instead at least 2 mm should be subtracted from the measured length. Biomechanically, the shorter length has no negative influence on fixation strength. The use of specialized radiographic views can be used intraoperatively, but postoperatively a CT is preferable if screw protrusion is suspected, to verify or dismiss the diagnosis. In a systematic review including 6278 patients from 56 studies, the incidence of extensor tendon rupture after plating was 1.5% in volar and 1.7% in dorsal plates. The rate of EPL rupture was 0.7% and 0.3%, and in EDC 0.05% and 1.3%, respectively.

![[Fig.15.78.png]]
> **Fig. 15.78** A and B, Radiogram of a patient presenting with an FPL rupture 6 years after fracture. Note the protrusion volar to the teardrop. The plate was extracted and the thumb IP-joint fused. C, The screw protrudes dorsally as seen already in the perioperative radiogram. The screw was extracted after 4 weeks and attrition of the EPL tendon was found but no rupture.

Also, flexor tendon ruptures are far from uncommon following volar plating (see Fig. 15.78B). The FPL is predominantly involved, with a frequency of 0.6% in volarly fixed fractures, with tenosynovitis being more common. The rupture may appear late. The average time to rupture in a systematic review was 9 months, but ruptures have been reported as late as 10 years following surgery.

If radiographs demonstrate Soong grade 1 or 2 positioning, plate removal is recommended to prevent rupture if the fracture is fully healed. Ruptured tendons require hardware removal and reconstruction with direct repair, tendon graft, tendon transfer, or a thumb/finger interphalangeal (IP)/distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint fusion (see Chapter 31).

### Distal Radioulnar Joint Contracture

Capsular contracture of the DRUJ may be responsible for limitation of forearm rotation after DRFs. Having ruled out joint incongruity, subluxation, radioulnar synchondrosis, interosseous membrane contracture, or derangement of the PRUJ as other possible causes of limited forearm rotation, surgical release is helpful if the condition does not improve after a trial of physiotherapy.

The volar aspect of the joint is exposed through a longitudinal incision just ulnar to the FCU tendon. Having exposed and protected the dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve, the tendon and the ulnar neurovascular bundle are retracted radially. Next, the PQ is sectioned longitudinally 5 mm radial to its ulnar insertion. The contracted palmar capsule is exposed. It is useful to place a 25-gauge needle between the TFCC and the ulnar head and confirm placement under fluoroscopy before resection of the palmar capsule. A longitudinal capsulotomy just proximal to the volar radioulnar ligament close to the sigmoid notch is performed and then continued proximally to the neck of the ulna. The entire palmar capsule is excised to expose the articular surface of the ulnar head.

A more radial approach between the ulnar neurovascular bundle and the digital flexor tendons can also be used for this purpose. A “silhouette” capsulotomy of the dorsal capsule is then performed with a counter incision through the fifth dorsal compartment if one has to release the dorsal capsule to recover forearm pronation. Postoperatively, it is important to begin immediate mobilization of the forearm and use dynamic supination bracing if necessary.

### Malunion

Malunion of DRFs is common and may or may not result in pain at motion or at rest, decreased rotation and distal radioulnar instability, or any combination of these. The radiographic threshold criteria for malunion grossly follow the thresholds for acceptable reduction as discussed earlier, and the literature appears on a group level to support a correlation between malunion and symptoms. However, it remains enigmatic that symptomatic malunion may occur also in a fracture healed within the limits, as well as being totally asymptomatic in severely malunited fractures. A significant correlation has been found between outcome and adverse radiographic parameters across all age groups. Factors most closely correlated with outcome included ulnar variance, radial inclination, and volar tilt.Greater disability was identified when two factors were present in combination and the effect was still found significant at the 2-year follow-up.

#### Corrective Radius Osteotomy

The indication for corrective osteotomy thus is based on symptoms in the presence of a malunion, not the radiograph by itself. In general, the prime indication for surgery is ulnar-sided wrist pain, the major factor leading to patients with malunited DRFs seeking help. DRUJ instability may be presented as ulnar-sided wrist pain at rotation, sometimes with clicking or pain at a loaded rotation like opening a door handle or twisting a wrench. The radiograms are carefully examined and correlated with the clinical symptoms. Volar compression and shortening become symptomatic with minor malunion (see Fig. 15.26) and may, just as combinations of radial, axial, and dorsal/volar compression, lead to malalignment of the sigmoid notch and pain at rotation. Changes in ulnar variance may lead to ulnar impaction, which isolated can be successfully treated with an ulnar shortening osteotomy. Patients with malunion and decreased forearm rotation may benefit from intervention, whereas patients having mainly limited flexion/extension are less likely to improve their range of motion.

TFCC injuries are common and tempting to repair when symptomatic hyperlaxity is found using the shuck test. However, the secondary stabilizers, in particular the DOB, often stabilize the DRUJ sufficiently and a radius or ulna osteotomy typically precedes any attempt to repair the TFCC. Also, malunions close to the threshold values may improve radically with an osteotomy (Fig. 15.79), in particular in patients with symptomatic DRUJ instability. If an impeding intraarticular malunion or a subluxation is recognized in the subacute stage (i.e., before complete bony healing), early intervention provides easier realignment, but the risk of finger stiffness and pain is high if the soft tissue has not fully recovered. In patients with substantial swelling, it may be prudent to wait until the soft tissues have healed and finger motion regained before corrective osteotomy.

With limited experience of the procedure, there are many sources of errors that might jeopardize the results, but in experienced hands a standard extraarticular osteotomy or a shortening ulnar osteotomy are safe procedures with predictable and consistent improvement. However, the results of a successful osteotomy may objectively not be on par with the standard uneventful fracture when comparing the DASH values after healing. In a group operated with radius osteotomy for malunited DRFs, the median DASH at 1 year was 24, compared with a median of 9 in the whole DRF population. But, coming from a situation with pain at rest and painful rotation severely affecting the daily life, most patients are grateful and truly appreciate the substantial improvement.

The aims of a radial osteotomy are to alleviate pain and restore function by correcting the deformity at the level of the fracture site. Radiographs of the uninjured wrist should be acquired to determine the physiologic ulnar variance and calculate the restoration of radial length. The osteotomy should reorient the joint surfaces to restore normal load distribution, reestablish the mechanical balance of the midcarpal joint, and normalize the anatomic relationships of the DRUJ. I prefer an opening wedge osteotomy performed from the volar side, transverse in the frontal plane and parallel to the joint surface in the lateral view (Fig. 15.80). Radial lengthening, correction of dorsal/volar tilt in the sagittal plane and correction of the radial inclination in the frontal plane are all easy to plan and template from the radiographic films. Correction of rotational deformity in the axial plane is more difficult and may require a whole-arm CT scan with three-dimensional reconstruction (Case Study 15.14).

The results in the published studies in general are excellent.Even in displaced, dorsally compressed malunions, the length can be restored by simply correcting the dorsal tilt, retaining the volar soft tissue hinge (see Fig. 15.79). With larger defects, causing the volar cortices to separate, a cancellous (or corticocancellous) bone graft should be used to fill the bone defect and induce osseous healing. Autograft has a superior osteoinductive potential compared with allograft or bone substitutes, which in my opinion should be avoided in large defects. Angular–stable plate fixation is ideal for corrective osteotomies, especially in osteopenic/porotic bone or when using nonstructural bone grafts. An alternative to bone grafting is the combination of a radius osteotomy and an ulnar shortening osteotomy. In patients in which osseous healing is not obviously foreseeable, dual osteotomies are best avoided. A cessation of smoking is required in most centers for the procedure. In severely displaced and shortened malunions, sometimes the only solution is a corrective radius osteotomy combined with an ulnar head resection, as a salvage procedure (Fig. 15.81). The resected ulnar head can then be sculpted and used to fill the radial defect.

### AUTHOR'S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: VOLAR APPROACH FOR OSTEOTOMY OF A MALUNITED DISTAL RADIUS FRACTURE

Malunited, volarly angulated fractures are corrected through a volar approach. In the majority of cases, dorsally compressed fractures are also approached volarly. With the wedge opening dorsally, some conditions must be considered. Although extensor tendon irritation associated with dorsal plate fixation is avoided, instead the sharp edges of the osteotomized bone ends may abrade the tendons dorsally. Excess callus protruding dorsally is best resected through a separate dorsal incision but can be morsellized and reused in the gap as an autograft to enhance osseous healing.

A fixed-angle volar device is designed to match the normal anatomic contours of the palmar–radial surface. This can be used to precisely restore the palmar as well as the radial tilt, since the volar surface distal to the fracture often is intact and can be used as a guide. Although, the degree of correction is best measured in the preoperative films in the sagittal and coronal planes, the plate can be applied to the volar distal surface, as one would have in a fresh fracture. The plate is positioned to follow the contour of the watershed line, reaching all the way to the volar ulnar corner. When in the right position, Kirschner wires or screws are used to temporarily secure the plate using the two middle distal pin holes. In a standard dorsally and radially compressed malunion, the proximal plate once temporarily fixed to the bone distally should be found elevated from the shaft and deviated ulnarly, at the same degree as measured in the preop films. Once the ideal position is reached, the distal screws are drilled and measured. The plate is then removed. A Kirschner wire is inserted, parallel to the joint surface in the sagittal plane, to guide the direction of the cut. Using another Kirschner wire, the cut in the transverse plane is prepared. It should be horizontal, but care should be taken not to violate the sigmoid notch. Before making the cut, check in the lateral view, in particular in severely dorsally compressed malunions. The cut is made using large amounts of saline as cooling. If excess callus interferes with the plate volarly, the callus is resected and used as graft. The osteotomy is completed dorsally with an osteotome. The saw places the extensor tendons, namely the EPL and ECRL/ECRB at risk for injury. Alternatively, one can make a small counter incision and isolate the extensor tendons before the osteotomy for protection. The plate is then reapplied to the distal fragment and secured to the shaft with a pin or clamp. The position is evaluated under fluoroscopy, and if the dorsal/volar tilt and the radial have been corrected, a screw is inserted in the proximal sliding hole. If additional lengthening is required, the BR has to be cut. Cutting the brachialis is commonly required in malunions. The plate is pushed distally and stabilized with a Kirschner wire or clamp until final fixation. If the two volar cortices meet, no bone graft may be needed, but one can fill the dorsal gap with graft. In larger defects, iliac crest or olecranon graft is used. The PQ is sutured back, but before, some bone graft is preferably placed in the ulnar recess of the PQ covering the osteotomy.

#### Ulnar-Shortening Osteotomy

Ulnar shortening osteotomy was first described by Milch in 1941. In patients with a pure axial compression causing an ulnolunate impingement, an isolated ulnar shortening osteotomy is sufficient to relieve pain at resisted forearm rotation (Fig. 15.82). With the threshold set at 20 degrees dorsal extraarticular radial malunion and simultaneous ulnar-positive variance, marked improvements were found in 18 patients with isolated ulnar-shortening osteotomy, regarding wrist and forearm range of motion and visual analog scale (VAS) and DASH scores. Several systems are available for precision cutting and exact plate fixation and preferred to previously used free hand techniques. If the osteotomy is made too far proximally, there is a risk of nonunion, especially if a power saw is used without cooling. It is a good advice to allow muscle tissue to be in contact with the osteotomy at closure, supplying mesenchymal stem cells for osseous healing (Case Study 15.15).

The bone is prepared sharply, and the plate attached preferably volarly, with three screws on one side and one screw in the oval hole on the other. The jig is applied, and the cut is made using cutting blocks as predetermined. The osteotomy is compressed longitudinally with an oblique lag screw. The rest of the screws are inserted (Fig. 15.83). #### Dorsal Approach for Osteotomy of Malunited Distal Radius Fractures

A dorsal approach to radius malunions is preferred in some centers,in particular in aggressive lengthening using large corticocancellous iliac crest grafts. The radius is exposed through a universal dorsal incision between the third and fourth extensor compartments. The healed fracture site is identified with fluoroscopy. The BR is released. If dorsal plate fixation of the osteotomy is planned, the Lister tubercle is removed with a rongeur or else left undisturbed. A 25-gauge needle is introduced into the radiocarpal joint and along the articular surface of the radius. In accordance with the preoperative plan, two 2.5-mm Kirschner wires are inserted so that the angle of correction in the sagittal plane is subtended on both sides of the future osteotomy. These wires control intraoperative angular correction but also help manipulate and maintain the distal fragment in the corrected position.

The osteotomy is performed with an oscillating saw cooled with saline, without cutting the volar cortex completely. The gap is instead opened up by applying a laminar spreader dorsally, until both wires are parallel in the sagittal plane. Opening up the osteotomy on the radial side may be difficult, with the radial shaft tending to move ulnarly during distraction of the gap. A complete tenotomy of the BR tendon is then necessary to facilitate realignment.

The osteotomy is pinned, and the quality of correction and radial lengthening is controlled with the image intensifier. With the elbow in 90 degrees of flexion, intraoperative forearm rotation and wrist motion are checked before definitive internal fixation of the osteotomy is undertaken. Corticocancellous or cancellous bone graft can be inserted into the defect and secured by a 1.6- or 2.0-mm Kirschner wire, driven obliquely from the radial styloid across the graft and into the proximal fragment. The osteotomy can be stabilized by a variety of methods. Preferably, stable locking plate fixation is used (e.g., 2.4mm condylar plates or any of a number of dorsal fixed-angle devices), allowing early wrist motion and with less need for bone grafting. The use of low-profile implants has helped to minimize extensor tendon irritation. A radial column plate and a fixed-angle wireform dorsal implant, spanning the opening wedge osteotomy, provides rigid fixation but should be combined with a corticocancellous graft (Fig. 15.84). #### Articular Osteotomies

The role of an osteotomy correcting an articular malunion of the radiocarpal joint after a DRF is limited, both by time elapsed from injury and type of injury. The osteotomy should be performed as early after the fracture as possible, as soon as full finger flexion is recovered. Up to 4 to 8 weeks, the fracture plane is soft enough to be developed by open debridement using an elevator or dental instrument. Radiographically, the fracture plane can readily be identified in up to 8 to 12 weeks. A high-resolution CT with multiplanar reformatting is particularly helpful in identifying the fracture plane and planning the osteotomy. Custom sterile cutting guides can be created to ensure precision correction of articular and combined defects. MRI with cartilage-sensitive sequences or concomitant wrist arthroscopy may play a useful role in evaluating the amount of cartilage damage and articular incongruence. The presence of areas that lack subchondral bone represents a formal contraindication to osteotomy and instead total or limited arthrodeses considered.

The most feared for osteotomy is the one with a displaced palmar–lunate facet fragment after a palmar carpal subluxation. This fracture is a fracture-subluxation, and additional ligament injuries are commonly present. An arthroscopy may therefore be indicated in high-energy injuries to identify SL and RSC ligament injuries adding to the instability. Even without finding these ligament injuries, the competence of the dorsal and radial stabilizers is questionable, and Orbay and colleagues describe an osteotomy leaving the volar/dorsal tilt at neutral to compensate and avoid a redislocation.

### Nonunion

The tissue volar to a DRF is probably one of the best vascularized parts of the human body. Both the radial and ulnar arteries pass by with branches supplying the PQ, which in turn is directly attached to the volar radial cortex. Nonunion of an uncomplicated DRF is therefore extremely rare. In contrast, nonunion of seemingly harmless distal or midshaft diaphyseal ulna fractures seems to be more common. In high-energy, closed, or open ulna fractures, but also after surgical fixation, the periosteum at the fracture ends has been injured. Since no muscle covers the distal ulna, both osteogenic progenitor cells and sufficient oxygen are lacking, and nonunions are therefore not infrequent.

In high-energy DRFs, the PQ acts as the secondary line of defense. Vessels grow into the fracture gap from the muscle, and with the osteogenic cytokine bone morphogenic protein leaking from the fracture surfaces, the satellite cells of the muscle differentiate to osteoblasts. In comminuted radius metaphyseal fractures extending proximal to the PQ, the radius fracture lacks muscle coverage and union may be delayed. Today, long volar locking plates are used to bridge these fractures, but sometimes the plate fractures before healing. Bone grafting and replating most often is sufficient to allow healing.

Although nonunions of DRFs are extremely rare, they do occur after complex open and/or infected fractures in patients treated with immunosuppressive drugs or iatrogenic after radius osteotomies (Fig. 15.85). Treatment follows the basic rules of any orthopedic nonunion, with infection control, stabilization, and bone grafting. In larger defects, like after a resection of an osteomyelitis, the defect may be too large to be bridged by an iliac crest graft. Instead vascularized fibula grafts are used, and recently the Masquelet technique has also been used, as a two-staged technique.

Nonunion after ulnar shortening osteotomies is not uncommon. It is good advice to be extremely careful when cutting the bone, cooling with copious amounts of saline. Avoid making the cut too proximal and try to leave muscle tissue, if possible, in contact with the fracture line at closure. Also, osteotomies of the radius should be performed with caution, with the cut in the metaphysis and within the PQ muscle sleeve. Prepare the muscle as in fracture fixation. Detach radially, elevate the flap, and suture back so at least some part of the muscle is in contact with the fracture gap. Avoid more than 2 mm gap and avoid bone substitute.

### Chronic Regional Pain Syndrome

Although full-blown reflex sympathetic dystrophy is a relatively infrequent problem, milder variants are surprisingly common in conjunction with DRFs. Early recognition, preferably during the first or at least the second week and attention to patients with extraordinary pain, finger stiffness, swelling, allodynia, or paresthesia may prevent this serious complication. Removal or splitting of a dressing or cast to relieve pressure, elevation of an edematous hand, and intensive hand therapy are frequently helpful in preventing the development of fullblown CRPS. These patients should be followed frequently until the acute stage is resolved.

Frequently, an irritated or entrapped median nerve is the cause of a CRPS. The surgeon should have a low threshold for performing electrodiagnostic studies and/or surgical decompression for suspected nerve entrapment (see Chapter 53).

A thorough anamnesis may reveal previous pain history or ongoing musculoskeletal pain as risk factor for developing pain behavior and CRPS. Also, these patients should be monitored frequently in the early phase, and adequate pain management is important. For patients who have a previous history of CRPS, there may be value in preemptive treatment with a long-acting sympathetic block or indwelling catheter for a regional nerve blockade for any proposed surgical procedure.

### CASE STUDY 15.3 The Slippery Slope

A 42-year-old right-handed female presents after a fall onto an outstretched hand (eFig. 15.10). The fracture is reduced in the ER.

![[eFig.15.10.png]]
> **eFig. 15.10** The fracture before and after primary reduction.

Is the fracture stable?

The fracture appears stable in the reduced radiograms, but the malposition was severe on the prereduction films and the volar cortices have not been aligned after reduction. The fracture line continues into the DRUJ with a small step-off. The fracture was initially left for conservative treatment.

After a week (eFig. 15.11), a further loss of position was observed and surgery scheduled. What type of implant would you choose?

![[eFig.15.11.png]]
> **eFig. 15.11** The fracture after 1 week.

A. Volar plate?

Note the rather distal position of the fracture line in the lunate facet. With the volar lip comminuted into a scaphoid and a lunate facet fragment, a volar plate is unlikely to cover the fragment distally.

B. Pinning?

Considering the relatively young age and good bone stock, a well-executed pinning fixating the separate fragments may be feasible.

C. External fixation?

With the fracture well reduced, an external fixation may be sufficient but additional pinning advocated.

D. Fragment-specific fixation?

Due to the distal location and the comminution of the volar rim, fragment-specific fixation was chosen (eFig. 15.12). A volar buttress pin captures the volar rim fragment. A radial pin plate triangulates the radial styloid, protecting the fracture from a coronal shift and also anterior rotation and fracture collapse.

![[eFig.15.12.png]]
> **eFig. 15.12** shows the fracture after healing. Note the congruency in the DRUJ and absence of coronal shift.

### CASE STUDY 15.4 Augmented Fixation

Severely displaced four-part intraarticular fracture (eFig. 15.13A and B). ### CASE STUDY 15.6 The Feared Volar Shearing Fracture

A 22-year-old right-handed athlete fell from a height (eFig. 15.15). What type of fracture does this patient have according to the Fernandez and the column theory?

![[eFig.15.15.png]]

The position is maintained after healing. An SL injury can be feared for with this kind of fracture, but SL integrity was tested during surgery. No dorsal angulation of the lunate (DISI) was found after healing.

### CASE STUDY 15.11 Osteotomy of a Subtle Malposition

A 44-year-old woman fell during ice skating. The fracture was not reduced but immobilized in a plaster in situ (eFig. 15.23). The patient returned 6 months later complaining of pain at rotation. A moderate DRUJ instability was found compared with the uninjured side. A new radiogram revealed a very subtle axial and radial compression in the frontal plane and a neutral position, indicating a 10- to 15-degree dorsal compression in the lateral plane. After a volar osteotomy, the length of the radius and the radial compression was corrected and the DRUJ stable. In distal radius fractures anatomic, correction often should precede a TFCC reconstruction.

### CASE STUDY 15.12 Fixation Failure and Malunion: Salvage Procedure With Wrist Arthrodesis and Darrach

A high-energy fracture in a 68-year-old man after a bicycle accident. The fracture was comminuted with a substantial radial shortening (eFig. 15.24). The ligaments of the DRUJ were obviously torn. The fracture was surgically fixed and an apparently acceptable position was obtained.

![[eFig.15.24.png]]
> **eFig. 15.24** A comminuted fracture with a severe axial and radial compression (A and B) was reduced and fixed by a volar plate (C and D).

After 6 weeks the patients complained of increasing pain after initial prompt progress. The preoperative malposition had returned and all screws had fractured (eFig. 15.25). In hindsight, the postoperative radiograms showed insufficient reduction and fixation of the radial styloid. By speculation, with a hybrid fixation and an additional radial pin plate or perfect reduction with radial cortical support and more than two screws, the fracture would have ended up differently.

![[eFig.15.25.png]]
> **eFig. 15.25** At 6 weeks the screws had fractured and the fracture collapsed.

With a damaged DRUJ, a radically shortened radius necessitating an ulna shortening osteotomy, and a joint surface of questionable quality, wrist fusion and ulnar head resection were chosen (eFig. 15.26). The patient became pain-free at both rest and activity and could return to a rather strenuous job as a gardener.

### CASE STUDY 15.13 Fixation Failure and DRUJ Malunion

A metaphyseal fracture of both the distal radius and ulna with severe comminution and displacement was diagnosed in a 54-year-old woman (eFig. 15.27). An acceptable reduction was not achieved by closed reduction. Primary external fixation was chosen but later converted to a volar plate of the radius but not the ulna.

![[eFig.15.27.png]]
> **eFig. 15.27** Double trouble with both a distal radius and ulna metaphyseal fracture (A and B), which could not be brought into a stabile position (C and D). An external fixation (E and F) was used, changed to a volar plate after a week (G and H) with a suspected nonreduced ulna.

After collapse of the ulnar metaphysis (eFig. 15.28), an ulnar osteotomy was performed but the patient continued to have pain at rotation and limited range of motion. After 2 years a DRU prosthesis was inserted as a rescue operation. Range of motion improved and pain was acceptable.

### CASE STUDY 15.15 Ulna Osteotomy

This is a classic three-part fracture (eFig. 15.36) with the radial styloid fragment intact but the lunate facet divided in a volar and a dorsal part. Due to the distal fracture line ulnarly, a volar buttress pin was chosen, and the fracture adequately reduced surgically. The patient returned due to pain at rotation and a new radiogram revealed an axial compression with a potential impingement between the lunate and the ulnar head. In the retroscope it becomes obvious that the pins went cut too short and the dorsal fragment left unsupported. The dorsoulnar corner tipped volarly and subsided. An intraarticular osteotomy is difficult, and a simple 3 mm ulnar shortening was performed with good outcome regarding the pain.

![[eFig.15.36.png]]
> **eFig. 15.36** Trauma films of a comminuted fracture (A and B) scheduled for surgery (C and D). Due to the small volar ulnar, fragment-specific fixation was chosen. The prongs, which are cut not to penetrate the dorsal cortex, are too short to support the dorsal joint surface as seen in the lateral projection. At 6 weeks the position appeared to be adequate, but the patient returned and a new radiogram (E and F) showed substantial axial compression with ulnar impaction and a DRUJ malunion. An ulnar shortening was performed (G and H) and the ulnolunate impingement disappeared (I and J).
