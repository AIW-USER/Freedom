Any child with congenital joint contractures in two or more limbs can be designated as *arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC);* the term does not specify any certain etiology for the contractures. Arthrogryposis can result from environmental or genetic causes. *Amyoplasia,* however, the most common form of AMC, is an idiopathic nongenetic disorder with a wide range of levels of severity and anatomic involvement (Fig. 39.1). *Distal arthrogryposes (DAs)* are genetic disorders with 11 discrete patterns, each with variable penetrance (Figs. 39.2 to 39.4; Table 39.1). The most common DAs are types 1, 2A, and 2B (DA1, DA2A, and DA2B). Arthrogryposis can also result from myriad disorders that compromise the central or peripheral nervous system or the musculature. Currently, over 300 types of arthrogryposis have been identified.

Although the cause of amyoplasia is unknown, the clinical findings result from a localized or generalized underdevelopment of the neuromuscular system. Muscles are either absent or hypoplastic and often replaced by fibrofatty tissue. Tendons and overlying fascia are often preserved even when no muscle is present, leading to tethering at the joints. It is unclear whether the primary insult occurs at the central nervous system (motor cortex or the anterior horn cells), the target organ (muscles), or a combination of the above. Aplasia or hypoplasia of any of these systems in utero would result in concomitant loss of the other systems. However, the high coexistence of hypodactyly and the preservation of tendons even when the muscle is absent suggests that the origin may be from muscle cell line hypoplasia. Maternal bicornuate uterus and oligohydramnios have been historically thought to increase the risk of amyoplasia, yet recent studies have not found such a link.

Children with amyoplasia have a characteristic appearance with high cheeks, button noses, and “stork bite” hemangiomas over the occiput and the bridge of the nose. Interestingly, we have evaluated a few identical twins with indistinguishable facial features, yet only one displays clinical signs and symptoms of amyoplasia. The hemangiomas are usually flat and small (Fig. 39.5) but can be protuberant and even extend through the skull into the cranium. Even though the lesions are benign and most resolve or diminish over time, raised lesions should be imaged to evaluate for intracranial extension. Despite or perhaps as a result of diminution of the motor cortex and expansion of the remaining cortex, children with arthrogryposis tend to have average to above average intelligence.

## PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

### History

Most children with arthrogryposis have no other medical conditions. Some children, however, have respiratory and gastrointestinal system involvement. If the respiratory involvement is severe, mechanical ventilation will be required after birth. These children can typically be weaned to a tracheostomy and supplemental oxygen by their second or third year of life. A small percentage of children will have bowel atresia, abdominal wall defects, or gastroschisis. Terminal limb deficits such as hypodactyly are more common in amyoplasia patients than in the general population.

In DA, the family history is usually positive. Quite a few children with arthrogryposis are adopted because their biologic parent is unable or unwilling to care for them. In these cases, the family history is often unavailable. The history should gauge the depth and resilience of the family support structure and the resources available.

### Physical Examination

Building trust is critical to coaxing a meaningful examination from any child but even more so for children with arthrogryposis as many have been traumatized by extensive medical interventions prior to seeing the upper extremity specialist. The examiner should begin with a global assessment of the child’s cognitive, social, and physical abilities. Some children are severely affected, requiring feeding tubes and mechanical ventilation. A thorough intake worksheet can help obtain a more complete picture of the child’s needs (Fig. 39.6). All of the joints of the upper limb should be examined for both active and passive motion. Watching the child play with a variety of differently shaped and sized toys is an effective way of judging the child’s function. If possible, observe how the child eats finger foods or drinks from a bottle. There is no substitute for spending a few minutes playing with the child to gain an understanding of the child as a person and comprehending what the child requires as a patient.

![[Fig.39.6.png]]
> **Fig. 39.6** An intake worksheet can be very helpful to get a thorough assessment of the child.

A more methodical examination of the upper limbs, when possible and age appropriate, proceeds from the shoulder to the fingertips. For the child who is unable or unwilling to respond to commands, having the child reach for toys and stickers followed by object manipulation may be the only way to examine the child. Assess every joint on both arms, recording active and passive motion as well as the joint’s resting position. Internal rotation and adduction contractures of the shoulder are common. Children with more than 90 degrees of active abduction and active external rotation past neutral rarely require any intervention about the shoulder. Shoulder rotation is assessed by flexing the elbow to 90 degrees and placing the arm in adduction. Passive rotation of the shoulder assesses the degrees of passive internal and external rotation. Finding the axis of rotation of the elbow in a child with less than 30 degrees of passive elbow flexion can be difficult.

Elbow flexion and/or extension contractures are also common and imply underlying deficiencies in the elbow flexor and extensor muscles. Most often, the triceps is preserved and the elbow flexors are deficient, resulting in the more common elbow extension contractures. Occasionally, the triceps is weak or absent, causing an elbow flexion contracture. Absence of both the triceps and the elbow flexors often leads to a fixed or minimally mobile elbow joint that positions around 60 degrees of flexion.

Historically, the forearm has been described as in pronation in the majority of these children; however, most forearms are positioned in neutral or slight supination. The illusion of a pronation contracture is created by the internal rotation contracture of the shoulder combined with an extended elbow. As the child becomes older, he or she pushes the hand against a hard surface to achieve passive hand-to-mouth activities. This action yields elbow flexion, forearm supination, and wrist flexion. Continued repetition of this functional activity gradually positions the forearm into more supination over time.

In patients with DA, the wrist is most often in extension. In contrast, patients with amyoplasia typically have fixed wrist flexion contractures (see Fig. 39.1). In the absence of active wrist extensors, some children will use the viable extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tendons to extend the wrist. This results in active extension to just shy of neutral but with concomitant metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint extension.

Children with arthrogryposis often have lower limb involvement. A pediatric orthopedic surgeon familiar with arthrogryposis should examine the lower extremities. The current status and potential ambulatory status are critical during formulation of an overall treatment strategy.

Children with arthrogryposis will typically visit the operating room several times for spine, upper extremity, and/or lower extremity correction before the appropriate age for muscle/tendon transfers (usually 4 years old or older). If the child is undergoing any procedures requiring sedation, an examination under anesthesia can be performed to determine the true extent of contractures. Percutaneous needle stimulation of individual potential muscle donors (e.g., pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and gracilis muscles) can also be performed to determine their availability.

### Diagnostic Imaging

Routine radiographs are unnecessary for patients with arthrogryposis. Neither computed tomography (CT) nor magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is necessary for the diagnosis. However, evaluation of donor muscles for transfers (e.g., pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and gracilis muscles) is difficult to quantify via clinical examination and often requires an MRI or CT scan (Fig. 39.7). Electrodiagnostic studies are rarely necessary other than in evaluating alternative neurogenic contracture syndromes such as spinal muscular atrophy. Muscle biopsy does not yield useful information unless an alternative diagnosis is being considered.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

Arthrogryposis affects the entire musculoskeletal system. Children with arthrogryposis have absent or hypotrophic muscles but may retain the associated tendons and overlying fascia. As the child grows, these noncontractile tissues can tether the joints leading to joint contractures. The bones may be osteopenic because they do not have the normal load of muscle activity or weight bearing. Cortices in children with arthrogryposis tend to be quite thick, but the bones are small in diameter and the medullary canal is nearly absent. The motor and mixed nerves are also small in caliber compared with other unaffected children of the same size. With thin bones and a hypoplastic musculature, the subcutaneous fat encompasses a large percentage of the bulk of the arms.

## SURGICAL PROCEDURES

The goal of any treatment plan for a child with arthrogryposis is to maximize function and independence. For the lower extremities, the goals are ambulation and sitting. Traditionally, ambulation has trumped all other treatment goals. In many centers, the lack of expertise and familiarity with upper limb surgery relegates upper limb function to an afterthought. This dismissal is a disservice to these children and to their caregivers. Basic upper limb function is equally as important to the well-being of these children as ambulation.

If the child does possess limbs and joints in optimal position for function by the first or second year of life, surgery should be performed to reorient and/or increase joint motion (Fig. 39.8). For nearly all children, shoulder rotation should be centered at 15 to 30 degrees of internal rotation. If this shoulder position is not present, the surgeon should consider a humeral rotational osteotomy. If elbow flexion is insufficient to reach the mouth, a release the posterior elbow should be performed. Elbows should be released within the first 2 years of life to provide maximal passive motion, with an optimal range between 30 degrees of extension and at least 100 degrees of flexion. For the minority of children that lack sufficient extension, soft tissue releases, osteotomies, and tendon transfers may be required.

![[Fig.39.8.png]]
> **Fig. 39.8** The goal of treatment for children with AMC is to optimize function. Having the child’s hands in front of them, both for visual afference and for bimanual interaction, improves function in nearly all children. This may require rotational osteotomies of the humerus and/or one-bone forearm/rotational osteotomies.

For the upper extremity, the primary goals are to have “one hand to eat and one to wipe.” These goals can be achieved by the same hand. The rate-limiting step in optimizing the child’s independence is uniformly finger and thumb motion. Independent self-feeding requires at least some hand function. The child must be able to acquire the food, grip firmly enough to bring food to the mouth, and release the food by opening the digits. In addition, the wrist and the elbow combined must have enough extension to reach the plate or the table and sufficient flexion to allow the hand to reach the mouth. Forearm and shoulder rotation need to allow the hand to face the plate and then the mouth.

In very impaired children, independent feeding may be an unrealistic goal. A skilled therapist and the use of adaptive equipment may achieve independence with some daily tasks even in severely impaired children (Fig. 39.9).

![[Fig.39.9.png]]
> **Fig. 39.9** Adaptive equipment to facilitate feeding. A, Spoon in mouth for scooping food. B, Spoon placed in adaptive holder. C, Food placed in mouth. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospitals for Children, Philadelphia.)

Perineal care can be performed through the legs or around the back. Both require sufficient hand function to hold toilet paper firmly. Children who wipe their perineum through the legs require wrist flexion, elbow extension, and shoulder internal rotation. Children who wipe their perineum around the back require forearm supination, elbow extension, and shoulder extension.

The next priorities on the functional ladder are bathing and dressing, followed by food preparation, driving, and other activities of daily living. Recreational activities and sports participation may be achievable in mildly affected children.

### Humeral External Rotation Osteotomy

#### Indications

Children with arthrogryposis typically present with internal rotation and adduction contractures. Elbow extension contractures can create the appearance of a pronation contracture, but most often the posture results from internal rotation at the shoulder with the forearm postured in neutral. The deltoid, rotator cuff, pectoralis, latissimus dorsi, and periscapular muscles may be weak or absent. Although rarely responsive to passive stretching, a course of therapy is recommended. Capsular releases have resulted in unpredictable results. Internal rotation contractures are best treated with a humeral external rotation osteotomy. The medial approach requires visualization and retraction of the median and ulnar nerves but is more cosmetic and provides a better bony surface for plate application than the anterolateral approach (Fig. 39.10). However, the medial approach is difficult to access if the child has an adduction contracture of the shoulder. We currently prefer the anterolateral approach because the lateral arm dissection is more accessible and straightforward, and the only neurovascular structure in harm’s way is the radial nerve at the distal extent of the incision. For the child with a severe elbow extension contracture, distinguishing between a shoulder internal rotation contracture and a forearm pronation contracture can be difficult. For these children, we will perform an elbow release first, followed by a humeral rotational osteotomy if needed. We routinely perform bilateral humeral rotational osteotomies in these children; however, for surgeons unfamiliar with this patient population, we recommend one side at a time.

![[Fig.39.10.png]]
> **Fig. 39.10** Children without elbow flexion or who have inadequate shoulder external rotation will use a crossover grasp pattern. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospitals for Children, Philadelphia.)

#### Contraindications

Relative contraindications include (1) insufficient internal rotation for perineal care, (2) older age with established crossover grasp (see Fig. 39.10), and (3) absence of pectoralis function. Children who lack pectoralis function but retain triceps function will use an obligatory crossover grasp pattern that requires internal rotation.

![[Fig.39.10.png]]
> **Fig. 39.10** Children without elbow flexion or who have inadequate shoulder external rotation will use a crossover grasp pattern. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospitals for Children, Philadelphia.)

#### Preoperative Planning

Plates and screws of the appropriate size are required and are often a size smaller compared with a humerus of an unaffected child of the same age. Our preferred fixation technique is two orthogonal staggered plates (one with six holes and another with three holes) to provide stable fixation for early motion and to minimize postoperative periprosthetic fracture risk. For example, a typical young child will require two 2.0-mm limited-contact dynamic compression (LCDC)- type plates: one with six holes and the other with three holes. An older child may require two 2.4-mm plates, and an adolescent, two 2.7-mm plates. A fracture brace will be required after surgery, which should be fabricated prior to surgery and available on the day of surgery.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: HUMERAL EXTERNAL ROTATION OSTEOTOMY

Place the patient supine on a standard operating room table without a hand table. Allow enough room on the operative side of the table for the arm to sit comfortably on the operating room table. Regional anesthesia may be used to control postoperative pain along with general anesthesia. Because the approach is so proximal on the humerus to avoid the radial nerve at the distal extent of the incision, a tourniquet cannot be used. Instead, bupivacaine with epinephrine is injected subcutaneously along the length of the intended incision. Prepare and drape the arm from the shoulder girdle to the fingertips.

Perform a longitudinal incision along the deltopectoral and anterolateral approaches, with the proximal extent just distal to the metaphyseal flare and the distal extent as long as is necessary to place the hardware. Raise full-thickness subcutaneous flaps while preserving the cephalic vein. Incise the fascia between pectoralis and the deltoid muscles. Regular palpation is required to feel the humerus because muscle planes may not be clearly evident. The biceps and brachialis are often absent, leaving no guide to identify the radial nerve. We routinely perform blunt dissection at the distal extent of the wound until either the bone is encountered or the radial nerve is found. If the nerve is identified, the nerve can be retracted in a posterior and distal direction out of harm’s way. Using a periosteal elevator, expose the humerus in an extraperiosteal fashion to a length sufficient to place a plate. Plates of 1.6 mm to 2.7 mm are used for this operation and should be an appropriate size for the bone as discussed above. Gently precontour the six-hole plate to provide compression on the far side of the bone following osteotomy.

Apply the plate provisionally to the distal osteotomy fragment as far anterior as the soft tissues will allow using the three most distal screw holes. Remove the plate. Split the periosteum at the site of the osteotomy and apply Hohmann retractors to protect the surrounding neurovascular structures. Perform a transverse osteotomy using a reciprocating saw while preserving the periosteal sleeve. Reapply the plate to the distal fragment. Place a fracture reduction clamp around the proximal fragment and the plate. Externally rotate the arm to the desired position of between neutral and 15 degrees of internal rotation. Provisionally clamp the distal humeral fragment to the plate and assess the arm position and rotation. Sufficient internal rotation should be preserved to allow for midline function, such as perineal care. Fix the plate to the proximal fragment with a single screw and reassess the position one more time (Fig. 39.11). If the position of the arm is optimum, apply the remaining proximal screws. Confirm the screw lengths and plate position using fluoroscopy. Trim a four-hole plate to three holes and apply this plate orthogonal to the first plate. Make sure to stagger the screw holes between the plates so that the screws from the first plate do not block the screws for the second plate (Fig. 39.12). Close the subcutaneous layer and the skin in standard fashion. Place the arm in a fracture brace. If the fixation was somewhat tenuous or the child is very active, a triangular wedge can be used to place the arm in a “gunslinger position” (Fig. 39.13).

Alternative approaches to a humeral osteotomy include the posterior and medial approaches. The advantages of the medial approach are primarily cosmetic because children keep the shoulders adducted and conceal the scar. However, the majority of children with amyoplasia have adduction contractures that make this approach not feasible. The posterior approach is likewise cosmetically appealing but must be performed in a lateral or prone position, which complicates the assessment of the optimum rotation.

Fixation with pins and fixation with single plates have also been described. Pins have the advantage of decreasing the late refracture risk, but they require a longer time for union and have a higher infection rate. A single larger plate improves healing time compared with pins but does have a higher risk of periimplant fracture. Using two orthogonal plates of a smaller size may reduce the risk of refracture compared with a single plate and decreases the time to union compared with pins.

We have demonstrated that concomitant elbow release and humeral rotational osteotomy using a single plate or pins had worse outcomes compared with patients undergoing an isolated elbow release. The use of two plates may improve the outcomes of concomitant elbow release and humeral rotational osteotomy. Without evidence, we recommend a staged approach with elbow release at a separate setting. Our paradigm for patients who require bilateral elbow releases and possibly humeral rotational osteotomies is as follows: (1) elbow release on the dominant side at the first operation, (2) elbow release on the nondominant and a humeral osteotomy on the dominant side at the second operation, and (3) humeral rotational osteotomy on the nondominant side at the third operation. This measured approach takes into account that (1) the rotation of the humerus is difficult to assess in children with severe extension contractures, (2) elbow releases should prioritize the better hand to ensure reaching the mouth, (3) elbow releases have the best results when performed between the first and second birthday, and (4) elbow releases have better results when performed without a concomitant ipsilateral humeral osteotomy.

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** Union is typically achieved between 4 and 6 weeks, although occasionally it may take up to 10 weeks. Maintain the fracture brace (Fig. 39.14) until union has been achieved. Begin passive range of motion while maintaining the brace at all other times until union. Patients can expect improvements in hand-to-mouth and hand-to-neck activities if there is adequate elbow and shoulder motion. Bimanual tasks are also more easily performed because the hands should face each other after the osteotomy. Hand function typically improves because the patient acquires an unobstructed view of the thumb and index finger.

![[Fig.39.14.png]]
> **Fig. 39.14** A fracture orthosis can be used to protect the osteotomy site until union is achieved. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospitals for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

**Complications.** Overrotation or underrotation is the most common complication. Typically, younger children will correct for overrotation but will not correct for underrotation. Older children, particularly those with minimal shoulder rotation, have a lesser margin for error. Neurapraxia of any of the major nerves can also occur, but more considerable nerve injuries are rare. Infections and nonunions are rare, but delayed unions do occur, especially if there is a gap at the osteotomy site or if the periosteum was stripped. Fractures at the ends of the plates are an ongoing concern as these children tend to fall frequently.

### Elbow Extension Contracture Release (Video 39.1)

#### Indications

Most children with arthrogryposis lack adequate elbow flexion to oppose the working triceps, leading to in utero extension contractures. Some children have active elbow flexion but no extension, leading to flexion contractures; a pterygium is common in this group. Other children have only a jog of passive motion at the elbow. If the child cannot flex the elbow more than 30 degrees, the true axis of rotation of the elbow joint can be difficult to determine. Caretakers and health care professionals are discouraged from performing passive range of motion of these elbows because inadvertent collateral ligament damage secondary to varus/valgus stretching or physeal injuries can result.

Impressive gains in elbow range of motion can be achieved in some children who can flex the elbow beyond 30 degrees. Progressive static splinting, as can be accomplished with an hourglass splint (Fig. 39.15), will augment and preserve the gains made with therapy. Passive handto-mouth motion can allow the child to perform feeding and grooming tasks by pushing the arm against the knee or other arm, a desk, or a table or by abducting the shoulder and allowing gravity to flex the elbow (Fig. 39.16).

![[Fig.39.15.png]]
> **Fig. 39.15** An hourglass splint (A) can be used to help gain more elbow flexion both before and after surgery, provided the patient has at least 60 degrees of flexion to begin with. Alternatives include an anteroposterior splint (B) or an adjustable static progressive splint (C).

![[Fig.39.16.png]]
> **Fig. 39.16** Children can use passive flexion of the elbow to feed themselves by pushing the forearm against the knee (A), a table (B), or the other arm (C) or by abducting the shoulder and allowing gravity to flex the elbow (D).

Indications for surgical release include less than 30 degrees of elbow flexion by 12 to 18 months of age and failure to achieve sufficient elbow flexion for self-feeding at any age. We have demonstrated that posterior elbow releases have the best outcomes when performed in children less than 2 years of age. Based on our results, the natural history of elbow extension contractures is improvement with stretching only 15 to 30 degrees after the first year of life. Any child with more than a 60-degree elbow extension contracture at 1 year should be considered for a posterior elbow release, triceps lengthening, and ulnar nerve transposition. Elbow flexion contractures are more difficult to manage (Fig. 39.17).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ELBOW EXTENSION CONTRACTURE RELEASE

There are multiple ways to lengthen the triceps and release the capsule. Some surgeons prefer a “W”- type triceps tendon flap, or the “V-Y” flap. I prefer the “Y” flap procedure because it is simpler to perform and requires fewer suture lines. The key concept is to make the flaps the length of the triceps tendon to allow adequate overlap once the elbow is flexed. If the lengthened ends of the triceps do not meet, a turndown flap or allograft/autograft is required.

To augment excursion of the triceps muscle itself rather than simply to elongate the tendon, I also perform a slide of the medial head of the triceps to the level of the spiral groove. The muscle origin is released from the humerus above the periosteum with a bipolar cautery or blunt dissection. This usually allows the medial head to reach the olecranon, which enhances the tendon repair.

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** The results of triceps lengthening, posterior capsular release, and ulnar nerve transposition have been consistent across multiple studies. Terminal extension loss and triceps weakness are possible, but most children achieve sufficient passive flexion to feed themselves. The results are most predictable in children under the age of 2 years, but releases in older children are still possible with more variable outcomes. Children between 1 and 2 years of age can expect a 70-degree increase in range of motion, with an average flexion contracture of about 30 degrees. Surgery after 2 years of age is more variable, with some patients losing overall motion and others gaining up to 50 degrees of additional motion. Active elbow flexion has been seen in some children after a release without tendon or muscle transfer, but this is rare and should not be expected.

Because of the potential loss of elbow extension after a release, bilateral releases are discouraged. Begin with the arm most likely to have an adequate hand for feeding (i.e., the dominant hand). Do not release the nondominant side until the child demonstrates at least the potential to carry out perineal care with the arm that has already undergone a release. Remember the doctrine “one hand to wipe and one hand to eat,” but it can be the same hand. We have seen some patients continue to gain flexion in long-term follow-up, usually worsening their flexion contractures in the process, but most patients stabilize their arc of motion after 2 years.

**Complications.** Complications such as ulnar and/or radial nerve injury are rare, but triceps insufficiency and elbow stiffness do occur.

### Bipolar Latissimus Dorsi Flexorplasty (Video 39.2)

#### Indications

Active elbow flexion is rarely achievable in children with arthrogryposis. On occasion, an elbow release alone will allow a previously restrained biceps or brachialis to flex the elbow. The “new” active flexor function is typically a Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 1 or a weak grade 2 and rarely provides enough active flexion to truly enhance function. In a child with passive motion who would benefit from active elbow flexion, there are several options for achieving active flexion. Transfer of the entire triceps to the biceps has proven to be a poor option, with many children developing disabling elbow flexion contractures.

Transferring only the long head of the triceps may avoid the flexion contracture that results from transferring the entire triceps. However, proponents of this technique have yet to demonstrate a substantial improvement in function from this transfer. Additional experience with this transfer is needed before it can be recommended for widespread use.

Other options for restoring active elbow flexion are a Steindler flexorplasty, a unipolar or bipolar lower pectoralis major transfer, a bipolar latissimus dorsi transfer, and a free muscle transfer. When present, the latissimus dorsi is the best choice for restoring active elbow flexion. The pectoralis major transfer has also been described; however, there is a possibility for functional loss and cosmetic disfigurement. Transfer of only the sternocostal portion of the pectoralis major may be a better option from the standpoint of appearance, but it may not be of sufficient strength or length. Results of monopolar lower pectoralis muscle transfer from Olga Agranovich, MD, and her team at the Turner Institute in Russia are promising but inconsistent.

#### Contraindications

Poor triceps function is a contraindication to any transfer for active elbow flexion. Patients who have had a previous triceps lengthening typically have compromised triceps strength and are not candidates for active elbow flexion transfers unless they demonstrate at least 3/5 MRC elbow extension strength. If the latissimus is difficult to palpate, it is probably not suitable for transfer. Poor passive elbow flexion of less than 90 degrees is another contraindication.

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** Outcomes depend almost entirely on the quality of the latissimus muscle. More than half of our patients achieve 4/5 MRC strength in midrange, but there is typically a small lag in terminal flexion and an acceptable flexion contracture. The biggest difficulty has been preventing flexion contractures, particularly if the patient had undergone a posterior elbow release and triceps lengthening. Flexion contractures can be progressive, with some of our patients progressing from a 30-degree to a 90-degree contracture over the long term. For this reason, we have heeded the advice of Olga Agranovich, MD (personal communication), and tensioned our transfers to allow full passive elbow extension at the time of surgery. Interestingly, there has been no reciprocal loss of elbow flexion strength from relaxing the tension. A recent study of 13 latissimus transfers that were tensioned at 90 degrees of elbow flexion reported no elbow flexion contractures. However, the authors did not report postoperative range of motion, and many patients had preoperative flexion contractures, making their results unclear.

**Complications.** Loss of the latissimus is possible but rare as long as meticulous dissection is performed, there is adequate pedicle length, and there is no torsion on the pedicle during passage. The posterior wound is prone to seroma formation, which can be mitigated using postoperative drains and dead space closure.

### Radial Osteotomy With and Without Ulnar Osteotomy

#### Indications

As stated previously, a pronation contracture in a child is uncommon with arthrogryposis. Rather, most children have shoulder internal rotation contractures, creating the illusion of forearm pronation. The forearm is most often positioned in neutral. Forearm osteotomies have been mistakenly performed to correct shoulder rotation and, conversely, humeral osteotomies have been performed to correct forearm malrotation. The risk of misdiagnosis is greatest when the elbow is contracted in full extension. Prior to humeral or forearm osteotomies in these children, we typically perform an elbow release that allows a better position to evaluate shoulder and forearm rotation. If the malrotation is at the forearm, a separate surgical procedure is performed to correct the rotation.

Children with limited elbow flexion and forearms fixed in pronation struggle with self-feeding. This difficulty is worsened with concomitant wrist ulnar deviation. However, pronation can be an asset to a child who goes through the legs for perineal care. Supination straps and passive range-of-motion exercises are first-line treatments to improve supination. If these nonoperative measures are unsuccessful, surgical correction can be achieved by a radial osteotomy, ulnar and radial osteotomies, or a one-bone forearm. Rarely, if the child has an active biceps muscle, a working supinator muscle, and passive supination, biceps rerouting can be considered to augment pronation. A therapist who is familiar with the child can be very helpful in determining the best functional forearm position.

A radial osteotomy is usually sufficient for rotational correction below 45 degrees. Greater corrections require a concomitant ulnar osteotomy that can achieve corrections up to 90 degrees. Alternatively, a one-bone forearm can achieve even greater correction and is more predictable.

Repositioning the forearm reorients the wrist position. Pronation allows gravity-assisted wrist flexion, and supination augments gravityassisted wrist extension.

#### Contraindications

A child who requires pronation for perineal care should not undergo a forearm osteotomy into supination. Likewise, a child who requires supination to eat should not be rotated into pronation.

#### Preoperative Planning

Any standard hand plating system can be used for osteotomy fixation. Compression plates usually range from 2.0 to 2.4 mm. Minifluoroscopy is also recommended.

#### Technique

Place the patient supine on a standard operating room table. Regional anesthesia may be used to control postoperative pain along with general anesthesia. Confirm the patient’s forearm rotation once asleep. If the shoulder is internally rotated on examination, consider a humeral osteotomy first and subsequent reassessment of the forearm position.

The arm is prepared and draped to the axilla. Exsanguinate the limb and apply a tourniquet on the upper arm. Incise the skin along the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon just proximal to the wrist crease. Raise medial and lateral full-thickness subcutaneous flaps. Beware of the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve. Mobilize the tendon and incise the floor of the FCR sheath. Elevate the flexor pollicis longus (FPL) and the proximal portion of the pronator quadratus to expose the distal radial diaphysis. Apply a contoured six-hole compression plate of appropriate size to the volar distal aspect of the radius with the distal three holes. Make sure that the plate is proximal to the volar metaphyseal flare to avoid physeal injury. Remove the plate.

Split the periosteum longitudinally at the osteotomy site and place Hohmann retractors deep to the periosteum. Perform a transverse osteotomy and reapply the plate to the distal fragment. Clamp the proximal fragment to the plate after rotating the distal fragment and reassess the rotation of the forearm. If there is some active wrist extension or the wrist is contracted in extension, position the radius to between neutral and 30 degrees of pronation. If the wrist has no active extension, place the forearm in neutral. Keep in mind the optimal forearm position necessary for wiping and/or eating. If the child has insufficient shoulder rotation, elbow movement, and wrist motion to achieve both tasks, determine the function that is best delegated to the contralateral hand and optimize the opposite function for the surgical arm. This determination is best done prior to surgical intervention. Once forearm position is deemed to be adequate, fix the plate to the proximal fragment and reassess rotation a final time.

If the radial osteotomy was insufficient to provide the desired amount of rotational correction, add a proximal ulnar osteotomy or convert to a one-bone forearm. For the ulnar osteotomy, incise the subcutaneous border of the ulna distal to the level of the coronoid. Insert the distal three screws of a six-hole plate. Remove the plate. Split the periosteum in a longitudinal direction at the osteotomy site and place Hohmann retractors deep to the periosteum, keeping the periosteum in continuity. Perform a transverse osteotomy and reapply the plate to the distal fragment. Rotate the ulna and clamp the proximal fragment to the plate. Fix the plate to the proximal fragment and reassess one more time. If the position of the arm is optimized, confirm screw lengths and plate position using fluoroscopy.

For a one-bone forearm, the approach to the radius depends on the deformity. A volar FCR approach is used to gain pronation. A dorsal approach distal to the first extensor compartment muscles is used to gain supination. After isolation of the volar radius, place the plate on the dorsal distal radius with the distal three screws. Make a transverse cut proximal to the most proximal screw hole. Make a second incision along the subcutaneous border of the ulna. Dissect a path along the interosseous membrane for the radius to be reduced onto the ulna. Make a transverse ulnar osteotomy 1 cm proximal to the radial osteotomy (Fig. 39.20). The staggered osteotomies create bony overlap to encourage union. The shortened forearm relaxes soft tissue tension to decrease the risk of compartment syndrome. Reapply the plate to the distal fragment of the radius. Clamp the proximal fragment of the ulna to the plate and assess the rotation of the forearm. Fix the plate to the ulna and reassess one more time. If the position of the arm is acceptable, confirm screw lengths and plate position using fluoroscopy.

Close the subcutaneous layer and the skin. Do not close the fascia. Apply a sterile dressing with the elbow in flexion and the forearm in the corrected position. Apply a sugar-tong splint or a long-arm cast (bivalved to accommodate swelling) to prevent forearm rotation.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: RADIAL OSTEOTOMY WITH AND WITHOUT ULNAR OSTEOTOMY

A one-bone forearm is the most predictable operation for correcting forearm rotational deformities. Union in children is rapid and robust, and there is no risk of loss of correction over time. If a small amount of pronation is required in a forearm with passive motion, biceps rerouting can performed. Prerequisites include an active supinator and a working biceps muscle. If a small amount of supination is required, a radial osteotomy can also be effective but may lose correction over time if there is no active supination.

```
CRITICAL POINTS: Radial Osteotomy With and Without Ulnar Osteotomy
- Make sure that the deformity truly resides in the forearm and not the shoulder before correcting forearm rotation.
- Confirm that self-feeding and perineal care will still be possible after the change in forearm position.
- Perform an isolated radial osteotomy for corrections of less than 45 degrees.
- For larger corrections, either create a one-bone forearm or perform osteotomies of the radius and ulna.
```

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** Correction of a pronation deformity can be a life-changing event as the child is able to self-feed for the first time. If the surgery negates function, such as bathroom independence, the family will be disappointed, and the consequences can be lifelong.

**Complications.** Nonunion is rare, but malunions can occur from inadequate positioning in the operating room. Intraoperative position must be carefully confirmed and scrutinized prior to definitive fixation. Malrotations of a few degrees are common, but clinically meaningful malrotations are rare.

### Carpal Wedge Osteotomy

#### Indications

The carpal wedge osteotomy is a common upper limb procedure for children with arthrogryposis. However, the indications are few and the contraindications are many. Decisions about altering a child’s wrist position require a thorough assessment of the child’s overall functional goals and limitations. In addition, the active and passive motions about the shoulder, elbow, forearm, and hand are critical components in the decision-making process.

The wrist position of children with arthrogryposis varies from child to child, with some children having one wrist in flexion and the contralateral wrist in extension. Most patients with DA tend to have wrist extension contractures, whereas patients with amyoplasia have flexion contractures. Extension contractures at the wrist are well tolerated if the following criteria are met: (1) Finger extension is sufficient to acquire and release objects, (2) forearm supination and elbow flexion are sufficient to get food to the mouth, and (3) forearm supination, shoulder extension, and elbow extension are sufficient to be able to wipe the perineum from behind. In some children, wrist flexion contractures can enhance function if the above noted criteria are not met. In these children, a carpal wedge osteotomy is contraindicated.

Initial treatment consists of passive motion and night splinting. Unless splinting enhances function, splinting is avoided during the day to allow the child to play and to promote cortical/muscular development. Because surgical interventions in the wrist and hand have not been demonstrated to have better outcomes at a younger age, surgery can be delayed until the child’s functional needs and capabilities are better defined. On occasion, the decision is delayed until the child is potty trained to determine the wrist flexion requirements for perineal care. Wrist flexion is so critical to perform perineal care from the front, it is important for the child to be able to wipe from around the back before considering any procedure that lessens wrist flexion.

Serial casting has not been shown to make a substantial difference in the long term. Extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) to extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon transfers after serial casting may delay recurrence, but we have had minimal success with this strategy. The ECU muscle is often affected, particularly if the EDC is nonfunctional.

If the child has good finger extension, has sufficient elbow flexion to reach the mouth without the need of wrist flexion, and can perform perineal care around the back, the most reliable procedure to provide wrist extension is a carpal wedge osteotomy with ECU-to-ECRB transfer. The best candidates have a reciprocal deformity of wrist flexion and MCP joint extension (Fig. 39.21). In cases where one hand is used for wiping and the other for eating, the eating hand can be considered for a carpal wedge osteotomy as long as finger extension, forearm supination, and elbow flexion are adequate. The maximal amount of wrist extension that is desired is limited by the ability of the EDC to extend the fingers. Wrist extension past 30 degrees is unnecessary even if there is good finger extension beyond that amount of wrist extension.

> **Fig. 39.21** Children with limited wrist extension will use the extensor digitorum communis to extend the wrist, leading to a zigzag deformity of wrist flexion, metacarpophalangeal extension, and interphalangeal flexion. These patients are excellent candidates for a carpal wedge excision and tendon transfer for wrist extension.

#### Contraindications

Contraindications to correcting a child’s wrist flexion contracture include (1) poor finger extension in maximal preoperative wrist extension, (2) obligatory wrist flexion for hand-to-mouth movement, (3) obligatory wrist flexion for perineal care, and (4) use of the back of the wrist for scooting along the ground for ambulation or for balance during standing or sitting. Most parents want their child to have their wrists in extension in the hopes of normalizing their appearance. However, the loss of the ability to ambulate, engage in perineal care, and self-feed is too high a price for esthetic improvement.

#### Preoperative Planning

Large Kirschner wires and Steinmann pins should be available for fixation. A mini-C-arm should be available.

#### Technique

Place the patient supine on a standard operating room table with a hand table. Regional anesthesia may be used to control postoperative pain along with general anesthesia. Apply a tourniquet to the upper arm.

Prepare and drape the arm to the tourniquet. Begin with an ulnarsided incision just volar to the subcutaneous border, extending through the mid third and distal third of the forearm. Identify and isolate the ECU tendon and muscle immediately adjacent to the ulna. If the ECU is inadequate for transfer, consider transferring one of the wrist flexors if present. Palpate the flexor compartment to identify tight structures, which uniformly includes the flexor carpi ulnaris. Make a volar oblique incision over the distal third of the forearm. Identify the ulnar nerve and artery, the median nerve, and the radial nerve and artery prior to releasing any tendons. If the wrist flexors have no muscle present, resect the tendon and fascia of the wrist flexor. If the tight tendon has excursion and its muscle is present, lengthen the tendon.

Reassess the patient’s passive range of motion. Typically, soft tissue releases alone can increase extension up to 30 degrees. If the wrist can be extended to 30 degrees past neutral, transfer the ECU or other tendon to the ECRB and do not perform a carpal wedge osteotomy. If passive motion is still inadequate after the volar release, proceed with the carpal wedge osteotomy.

Incise the skin either longitudinally or transversely across the dorsal wrist (Fig. 39.22A). The dorsal sensory branches from the radial and ulnar nerves are protected. Identify the ECU in the sixth compartment and trace to its insertion at the base of the fifth metacarpal. Release the overlying sixth extensor compartment (see Fig. 39.22B). In the proximal ulnar incision, identify the ECU muscle and tendon. If there is minimal to no ECU muscle or the excursion is very limited, consider an alternate donor tendon. Release the ECU from its insertion to maximize length. Retrieve the tendon into the proximal wound and release the soft tissue adhesions from the forearm fascia to the ECU that may limit muscle excursion. Pass the ECU via a subcutaneous tunnel to the midline of the distal dorsal incision and hold the tendon with a small hemostat. Place a moist sponge over the tendon for hydration and close the proximal ulnar-sided wound.

When indicated, the carpal wedge osteotomy and tendon transfer is the most predictable option for improving wrist extension. We have found serial casting to not be helpful without a volar release first. Recurrence begins as soon as the cast is removed, and splinting is mandatory to maintain wrist extension.

```
CRITICAL POINTS: Carpal Wedge Osteotomy
- Confirm that there are no contraindications to positioning the child’s wrist in extension.
- Release any volar structures that are tethering the joint into flexion.
- Design a biplanar osteotomy to correct both ulnar deviation and flexion.
- Use transosseous sutures for fixation.
- Transfer the ECU or any other available expendable tendon to provide active wrist extension.
- Use a modified Pulvertaft technique with a single Pulvertaft pass to minimize tendon bulk.
- Continue splinting for as long as possible after the osteotomy has healed.
```

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** The wrist position is reliably improved such that the wrist is positioned in greater extension even without an active wrist extension transfer. However, the correction can be underwhelming and can deteriorate over time if there is no active wrist extension.

**Complications.** Nonunions are rare, but undercorrections are common. Loss of finger extension and increased finger stiffness can also occur.

### Thumb Reorientation Osteotomy and/or Metacarpophalangeal Chondrodesis

#### Indications

The classic clasped thumb in arthrogryposis consists of extension at the first carpometacarpal (CMC) joint and flexion at the MCP joint. This is more commonly seen in DA, however, as the CMC joint generally follows the position of the wrist. Children with wrist flexion contractures, as are more commonly seen with amyoplasia, typically have CMC joint flexion contractures with the MCP joint in extension. The MCP joint can either be passively correctable or fixed in flexion. Interphalangeal joint motion is typically limited but most often positioned in extension. A flexion posture at all three joints is less common (see Table 39.2).

Initial treatment is passive range of motion until the child is old enough to be fitted with a splint. We prefer nighttime splints, but daytime splints can be used in severe cases or if the splint enhances function. Splints should expand the first web space, correct the thumb posture, and provide simultaneous correction of wrist and finger contractures. Tremendous gains can be made with passive motion and splinting in infants, and the parents are encouraged to be active participants in their child’s care. Gains can be seen up to approximately 5 years of age, but most children plateau after 2 years of age.

The goal of surgery is to position the thumb to provide the best pinch and/or grasp, regardless of appearance. The surgical algorithm (Fig. 39.23) depends on the posture of the CMC and MCP joints, as well as on the passive motion of the joints. In general, fixed contractures require a fusion whereas passively correctable contractures can be treated with a tendon transfer. The preferred donor for tendon transfers to augment thumb extension is the extensor indicis proprius (EIP), but alternate donors such as the palmaris longus, flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), and brachioradialis can be used. If the CMC joint is in extension and the MCP joint has a flexion contracture that is not correctable, MCP chondrodesis is the best option.

![[Fig.39.23.png]]
> **Fig. 39.23** Surgical algorithm for thumb contractures in children with arthrogryposis. Subtype A is passively correctable, and subtype B is not correctable.

The more common thumb posture for children with amyoplasia is flexion at the CMC joint and slight flexion at the MCP joint. The thumb may oppose to one or several of the fingers, or typically only to the palm. Palm pinch is the only option if the fingers are stiff and fixed in extension. Some children have neither finger nor thumb active motion, and do not use their hands at all other than to push objects around. Patients with no digital motion usually lack any active motion in the upper extremity. If FPL function is preserved but the thumb orientation prevents useful prehension, the patient is a good candidate for a thumb reorientation osteotomy. The goal of the osteotomy is to place the thumb into a position to provide pinch and/or grasp. The reorientation osteotomy typically results in a “sock puppet” type of position for the thumb, but other positions may be optimal depending on the child’s digital motion.

Both type 1 and type 2 thumbs benefit from a first web space contracture release at the same time as a chondrodesis or reorientation osteotomy. Options for expanding the first web space include the fourflap Z-plasty, the Abdel Ghani dorsal rotation advancement flap, and the index rotation (stiletto) flap. For mild web space contractures with a passive extension at the MCP joint, a four-flap Z-plasty will suffice (Fig. 39.24A). More severe web space contractures with no or minimal MCP joint flexion contractures require the Abdel Ghani flap (see Fig. 39.24B). For patients with both fixed MCP flexion and first web space contractures, the stiletto flap remains the best option (see Fig. 39.24C). When necessary, the stiletto flap can be extended into an Abdel Ghani type of flap to expand a very tight first web space (see Fig. 39.24D) or further modified as described by Mahmoud et al. Usually the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis muscles are tight or have been replaced with fibrous tissue and must be either lengthened or divided.

More severe contractures may benefit from a dorsal advancement flap (B). If the thumb is clasped and the first web space is contracted, the index rotation (stiletto) flap is an excellent option (C). #### Contraindications

Contraindications include a fixed extension contracture at the CMC joint that would preclude opposition after the MCP chondrodesis. Children with stiff fingers that would not reach the thumb even after reorientation or MCP fusion are not candidates for thumb surgery. Reorientation osteotomy before the age of 7 has a high risk of recurrence in our practice. However, the procedure can be repeated at an older age if necessary.

#### Preoperative Planning

Kirschner wires and a reciprocating saw are required to perform both the reorientation osteotomy and the MCP chondrodesis.

#### Technique

Place the patient supine on a standard operating room table with an arm table. Apply a tourniquet to the upper arm. Prepare and drape the arm to the tourniquet. Begin with a stiletto flap if appropriate (see Fig. 39.24C; Video 39.3). Mark the radial apex of the index finger’s proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint crease with a dot, and the thumb MCP joint flexion crease with a line. Connect the dot to the most ulnar aspect of the line, and draw a divergent line from the dot to the dorsal aspect of the index MCP joint. All limbs should be of equivalent lengths. Remember that the stiletto flap is merely a modified Z-plasty. Curving the more dorsal line provides a better contour for the web space (Fig. 39.25A). Pinch the “V”- shaped flap together to confirm that there will be sufficient skin remaining on the index finger to close the donor site.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THUMB REORIENTATION OSTEOTOMY AND/OR METACARPOPHALANGEAL CHONDRODESIS

For children with fixed thumb-in-palm deformities who can flex their fingers over the thumb, the standard operation is the thumb MCP chondrodesis with first web space expansion. The functional and esthetic gains are substantial, and most patients will request the same operation on the contralateral side.

For children whose fingers do not reach the thumb and a thumb that does not reach the palm, pinch is not possible without reorientation of the thumb. The fingers must actively flex sufficiently to reach the thumb in its planned position in order to achieve pinch.

The stiletto flap is the workhorse flap for the thumb in arthrogryposis, allowing both expansion of the first web and resurfacing of the volar MCP joint. For children that require only first web space expansion, a four-flap Z-plasty or an Abdel Ghani flap can be used.

```
CRITICAL POINTS: Thumb Reorientation Osteotomy and/or Metacarpophalangeal Chondrodesis
- Ensure there is adequate finger flexion to merit a thumb reorientation or chondrodesis.
- Thumb reorientation osteotomy is used to correct CMC flexion.
- Thumb MCP chondrodesis is used to correct MCP flexion.
- MCP chondrodesis alone will not address a CMC flexion contracture. A combination of MCP chondrodesis and reorientation osteotomy may be required.
- Prior to surgery, assess the need for skin augmentation at the MCP flexion crease and the first web space and choose the most appropriate skin flap.
- Release or lengthen the first dorsal interosseous and the adductor to allow expansion of the first web space and rotation of the first metacarpal.
- Make sure that the thumb volar neurovascular bundles are not under tension after performing a chondrodesis.
- Lengthen the FPL if the interphalangeal joint remains tight after chondrodesis or reorientation.
```

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** A thumb extension chondrodesis and first web space widening can increase function by improving pinch and grasp. The thumb reorientation osteotomy can be similarly gratifying for the same reasons. However, some children who undergo reorientation osteotomies have minimal hand function prior to surgery and the subsequent functional gains are modest. Also, young children (<7 years) will often remodel their reorientation osteotomy over time and lose their correction.

**Complications.** Nonunion is rare in these cases. Malunions are possible and are usually the result of malrotation of the thumb. Nerve injury and vascular injury are rare. However, neurovascular injury can result in loss of the thumb. The neurovascular structures must be protected during the dissection and excessive tension must be avoided during joint reduction. If there is tension, resect more metacarpal prior to fixation.

### Camptodactyly Release

#### Indications

Almost all children with upper extremity arthrogryposis have some degree of contracture of the fingers (Table 39.4). The fingers are typically rigid at the interphalangeal joints and fixed in extension. MCP motion (at least, passive movement) is often preserved. Some children have reciprocal deformities with the wrist in flexion and the fingers in extension at the MCP joints. Children with amyoplasia with no muscle function in the hands often present with symphalangism fixed in extension (Fig. 39.28). Rigid fingers have minimal function and only push objects around on a surface. There is currently no successful treatment for mobilizing stiff digits other than the occasional osteotomy to change the resting posture.

Camptodactyly is more common in DAs and can be a defining characteristic of specific syndromes such as *Beals syndrome* (contractural arachnodactyly). Camptodactyly can interfere with capturing objects in the hand, with some children developing a compensatory pinch-and-grasp pattern against the dorsum of the contracted fingers (Fig. 39.29A).

Initial treatment is passive range of motion until the child is old enough to be fitted with a splint. We prefer nighttime splints, but daytime splints can be used in severe cases or if they improve function. Splints should provide correction of wrist and finger contractures as well as simultaneous expansion of the first web space and improvement of the thumb posture. Gains can be made with passive motion and splinting, and parents are encouraged to be active participants in their child’s care. Gains can typically be seen up to about 5 years of age, but most children plateau after the age of 2.

Radiographs are not useful for camptodactyly in young children. In older children, the PIP joint condyles will often appear hypotrophic (see Fig. 39.29B). Children with passively correctable camptodactyly are more likely to have well-formed condyles than children with fixed flexion contractures. In general, the more severe the condylar hypoplasia, the less correctable the deformity.

Another option for gradual correction of camptodactyly, with or without volar Z-plasty and/or FDS tenotomy, is the Agee Digit Widget (Hand Biomechanics Lab, Sacramento, CA). We have used the Digit Widget in children as young as 5 years and up to skeletal maturity with mixed results (Fig. 39.30). The recurrence rate is high likely related to extensor mechanism deficiency. Correction is usually achieved within 3 weeks of application, but the device is kept on as long as the child tolerates the widget to minimize the risk of recurrence. A passive flexion protocol is performed while the Digit Widget is in place to avoid extension contractures.

#### Contraindications

There are no practical contraindications to a volar Z-plasty. A relative contraindication for FDS tenotomy is lack of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) and intrinsic function.

#### Preoperative Planning

No special equipment is required.

#### Technique: Camptodactyly Release

Place the patient supine on the operating room table with an arm table. Apply a tourniquet to the upper arm. Begin with an examination under anesthesia. If the PIP joint is contracted, consider adding a joint release or Digit Widget as long as the neurovascular structures will tolerate additional extension. Determine whether the FDS is tight by comparing the joint contracture in wrist flexion and extension. Lastly, examine the finger for the presence or absence of extensor mechanism tenodesis.

Prepare and drape the arm. Exsanguinate and inflate the tourniquet. Make a Z-plasty incision beginning along the ridge of maximal skin tension crossing the PIP flexion crease. Extend the incision in a “Z” shape to the apices of the PIP flexion creases at 60-degree angles. The “Z” limbs should have equal length segments.

Identify the neurovascular bundles and retract out of harm’s way. Occasionally, the bundles may limit the ability to gain extension. If the bundles can accommodate further PIP joint extension, test for FDS tightness by extending the wrist. If the child has a functioning FDP and a tight FDS, consider an FDS tenotomy just proximal to the fourth annular pulley.

In high-functioning patients with good FDS excursion and without a PIP joint contracture, consider transferring the FDS to augment intrinsic function. A Zancolli lasso and a formal intrinsic-plasty have been described.

After FDS release, any remaining contracture is likely from the joint itself. Depending on the tension of the neurovascular bundles, consider dividing the checkrein ligaments and the attachment of the accessory collateral ligament to the volar plate. Gently manipulate the joint to avoid a physeal fracture at the base of the middle phalanx. Kirschner wire placement is optional to hold the joint in maximal extension.

Close the wound with absorbable sutures and apply a sterile dressing. Deflate the tourniquet and check the finger for adequate perfusion. Make sure that perfusion of the digit is not compromised by excessive extension. The arteries may be in spasm, and capillary refill may be delayed at first. If the fingers remain ischemic after 5 minutes, consider step-wise warming of the room, removing the dressings, blocking the sympathetic nerves with a lidocaine injection, and removing the pin (if used), in that order. Venous outflow is rarely an issue. Once perfusion is confirmed, apply a long-arm or short-arm cast or splint to maintain the fingers in extension.

Remove the splint or cast after 2 to 3 weeks, and remove the pin if used. Use an Orthoplast splint full time except during motion exercises and bathing for a further 6 weeks, followed by nighttime splinting until the contractures are stable.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: CAMPTODACTYLY RELEASE

Despite a few reports of good results with camptodactyly release, we have not had uniform success with anything other than releasing the pterygium and cutting the FDS. We therefore perform a Z-plasty as the standard treatment for camptodactyly, adding an FDS tenotomy routinely if the FDP is functional and the FDS is tight. We do not have sufficient experience with the Digit Widget to routinely recommend its use into the treatment algorithm, but we have enough promising early results to continue exploring this option.

If the child has the rare combination of inadequate active wrist extension, passive wrist extension past neutral, good finger extension, and camptodactlyly, the FDS can be transferred through the interosseous membrane to the ECRB tendon.

```
CRITICAL POINTS: Camptodactyly Release
- Make the longitudinal portion of the Z-plasty along the crest of the pterygium (ridge of maximal skin tension).
- Extend the digit only as much as the neurovascular bundles will tolerate.
- Tenotomize the FDS if the FDS is contributing to the contracture and the FDP is functional.
- Release the volar plate checkrein ligaments if the joint is tight.
- Consider a Digit Widget as an adjunct or alternative treatment.
```

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

**Expected outcomes.** The results of camptodactyly release are unpredictable, despite some favorable results in the literature.The result is often inadequate correction or recurrence. Transferring the FDS to the extensor mechanism can impair grasp and should be performed judiciously.

**Complications.** Stiffness is the most common complication after any camptodactyly release. Digital nerve injury and vascular injury are possible and are most often traction injuries caused by straightening a bent finger.

## SUMMARY

Arthrogryposis remains a challenging disease for the patient, the parents, and the treating physician. Fortunately, the initial joint contractures tend to improve over time with stretching and splinting. Each child follows his or her own path, however, with some returning to full function and other less fortunate children never developing any muscle tone. The type of arthrogryposis often suggests the nature and severity of the child’s contractures. However, each child is affected differently and adapts to their limitations in a unique way. When a child is unable to adapt, guidance from an experienced therapist and communication with other affected children can help. The arthrogryposis community has accumulated many adaptive maneuvers or tricks to optimize function, with assistive devices and novel methods for achieving routine activities of daily living. The role of upper extremity treatment is to enhance the abilities of these children without downgrading existing function. There is little margin for error in these children, and parents should be aware that any surgical intervention may risk compromising existing function. Surgery should be pursued only if the potential functional benefits outweigh the possible losses. Operating on one arm at a time provides a mechanism for the surgeon, patient, and family to assess the outcome.

Although the options are often limited and the results of surgery are not always predictable, helping these children transition from dependence to independence is tremendously rewarding. Sterling Bunnell’s famous saying, “To someone who has nothing, a little is a lot,” particularly applies to these children.
