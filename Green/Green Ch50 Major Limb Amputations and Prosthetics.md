# Major Limb Amputations and Prosthetics

*Rafael J. Diaz- Garcia, Paul S. Cederna*

---
Amputation of the upper extremity takes its toll on both the patient and the surgeon. The patient is devastated at the prospect of life with a functional disability and laments the potential loss of the independence we all take for granted. For the surgeon, the toll is much less, but there remain feelings of sympathy and regret, as we wish we had another option. Nonetheless, it is important that surgeons who care for the hand and upper extremity have an appreciation of the finer points of amputations. In addition, they should have a comfortable understanding of the treatment goals in postoperative rehabilitation and functional restoration of the upper extremity to maximize outcomes.

## MAJOR LIMB AMPUTATIONS

While the amputation of a digit is by far more common, major upper extremity amputations comprise up to a quarter of all amputations.In the United States alone, the prevalence of transradial amputations was approximately 41,000 people in 2005. This has risen from previous estimates due to improved trauma and critical care medicine, which is preserving the life of severely injured patients; it is likely to continue to rise due to an aging population of patients with a higher incidence of comorbidities including diabetes and vascular disease. The human hand has 23 degrees of freedom and its utility is closely linked to our abilities to be gainfully employed or to perform simple activities of daily living. The effects of an upper extremity amputation are significant and greatly impact quality of life. Up to one- third of patients who undergo amputation of an upper extremity require a change in occupation, and almost two- thirds discontinue hobbies and vocations they participated in prior to their amputation.

The technical principles of upper extremity amputations are fairly constant, regardless of indication for surgery or level of amputation:
- Preservation of functional length
- Durable coverage of the residual limb to allow for long- term prosthetic use
- Prevention of symptomatic neuromas
- Prevention of adjacent joint contractures
- Early prosthetic fitting (“golden period”)

### Indications

Unlike in the lower extremity, the majority of upper extremity amputations are performed for acute trauma. In fact, in a Scandinavian epidemiologic study, up to 90% of upper extremity amputations were trauma- related. Amputations may be performed acutely when the hand surgeon believes that the functional potential of the salvaged limb would be substantially worse than prosthetic limb rehabilitation. At times, the surgeon will elect to perform multiple operative procedures in an attempt to salvage a hand or extremity but are unsuccessful in these attempts. It is even possible that these attempts to salvage a severely damaged limb can be detrimental to the health of the patient. In these cases, there is often severe and irreparable damage to the neurovascular supply to the extremity or severe injuries to the skeleton, either of which may make meaningful recovery nearly impossible. It is also possible that amputations are performed in a delayed or staged fashion, such as when replantations and other reconstructive efforts ultimately fail.

Major upper extremity amputations may also be required following burns or infections or as treatment for malignancies. Up to 26% of electrocution injuries require some form of major amputation.Deep thermal burns result in amputations approximately 3.8% of the time, usually due to nonviable tissues, but at times for control of a septic source (Fig. 50.1). While most infections can be managed with antibiotics and surgical debridement, invasive fungal infections, like mucormycosis, almost uniformly result in amputation, particularly in the solid- organ transplant patient population. There has been only one case report published of a salvaged upper extremity in an immunocompromised patient with a mucormycosis infection. The mortality in this patient population has been reported as high as 92%. Advances in radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and limb salvage surgical techniques have considerably reduced the need for upper extremity amputation in sarcoma patients over the past 40 years, yet radical amputation remains a mainstay of treatment in recurrent disease and large tumors.

### Wrist Disarticulation and Transradial Amputation

After decades of research and clinical experience, controversy remains regarding the ideal level of amputation in patients that require removal of the hand. Wrist disarticulation preserves limb length, thus providing a longer lever arm with which to perform work. More importantly, the longer residual limb preserves the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ), resulting in better pronation and supination. The metaphyseal flare of the distal radius also provides a contour that aides in suspension of a terminal device (Fig. 50.2). However, these benefits come at a cost. The prominence of the distal radius and ulnar styloids can become pressure points, leading to breakdown of the soft tissues of the residual limb, usually along the incisional scar. Though the additional length in a wrist disarticulation provides a longer lever arm, it leads to a limb- length discrepancy with the uninvolved side when a prosthesis is worn. A myoelectric terminal device may add several centimeters of additional length to the end of a residual limb, making the limb length discrepancy even more prominent. This not only presents an esthetic concern but also makes it more difficult for a patient to reach the anatomic midline, requiring greater shoulder abduction and scapular retraction.

![[Fig.50.2.png]]
> **Fig. 50.2** The increased width provided by the metaphysis of the distal radius allows for easier prosthetic suspension with a wrist disarticulation in comparison with a transradial amputation.

For these reasons, patients with wrist disarticulations are more likely to abandon their prostheses than patients with transradial amputations. Ideally, the patient is afforded the opportunity to have a preoperative consultation with a physiatrist and a prosthetist so that they may make an educated decision between a wrist disarticulation and a transradial amputation. Given the usual indications for transradial amputation, however, this is unfortunately rarely the case. Amputation 8 to 10 cm proximal to the ulnar styloid provides the greatest variety of prosthetic options for functional restoration of the extremity.

This preserves an adequate arc of pronation and supination and a reasonable lever arm for flexion at the elbow. However, one form of amputation is not ideal for all patients, making the preoperative consultation critically important in the process. For instance, patients who perform heavy labor frequently prefer a wrist disarticulation due to the increased durability of a body- powered prosthetic, the increased lever arm length, and the preserved DRUJ.

As the transradial amputation becomes more proximal and approaches the elbow, the anticipated pronation and supination may be minimal. In fact, there is minimal pronation and supination once half of the original forearm length is removed. Yet preserving at least 5 cm of ulna is sufficient for successful prosthetic fitting. The addition of an elbow joint to the prosthesis greatly increases the amount of work required to use it and dramatically increases the likelihood of abandonment. As a result, preserving the elbow joint should remain a high priority, even when complex reconstructive efforts such as free flaps are required to maintain this joint.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: TRANSRADIAL AMPUTATION

The skin incisions are designed with equal- length flaps along the volar and dorsal aspects of the forearm, in what is often referred to as a “fish- mouth” pattern (Case Study 50.1). The incisions are designed so that the osteotomies in the radius and ulna will lie at least 1 to 2 cm proximal to the level of skin incision (Fig. 50.3). The arm is exsanguinated, and tourniquet control is used for hemostasis. The skin and subcutaneous tissues are elevated off of the underlying antebrachial fascia proximally past the level of proposed osteotomies. The antebrachial fascia is then incised at the level of skin incision, and all of the neurovascular structures are individually identified. The radial and ulnar arteries are ligated with suture ties or vascular clips, while the interosseous vessels are cauterized. The flexor and extensor musculature is divided 1 cm distal to the level of osteotomies. The periosteum is incised, and a small cuff is elevated proximally. An oscillating saw is used to make the osteotomies, and the edges of the bone are smoothed with a rasp.

![[Fig.50.3.png]]
> **Fig. 50.3** Artist’s rendition of a planned, elective transradial amputation. A, A fish-mouth incision is designed 1 to 2 cm distal to the level of the planned osteotomies, which are usually 8 cm from the ulnar styloid. B, Soft tissues are elevated, and osteotomies completed with an oscillating saw. C, A myodesis is completed with the deep musculature to the bone. D and E, Superficial musculature is sutured in agonist/antagonist pairing over top, trying to re-create physiologic tension. (From Marchessault JA, McKay PL, Hammert WC: Management of upper limb amputations, J Hand Surg Am 36[10]:1718–1726, 2011.)

Regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces (RPNIs) or targetedmuscle reinnervation (TMR) should be performed at the time of amputation to reduce phantom pain and neuroma pain postoperatively (see Frontiers in Prosthetic Rehabilitation section). Each of the major mixed motor/sensory and pure sensory peripheral nerves should be controlled with either RPNIs or TMR. To create an RPNI, the peripheral nerve is sharply transected with a 15 blade at level of the bony cuts. A 30 × 15 × 5 mm muscle graft is harvested from the palmaris longus, flexor carpi radialis, or a distant site like the vastus lateralis. The muscle graft is harvested in the direction of the muscle fibers. All fatty tissue, fascia, and fibrous tissue are then removed from the muscle. The terminal end of the peripheral nerve is secured into the muscle graft with interrupted sutures of 6- 0 nylon between the epineurium and epimysium (Fig. 50.4). The edges of the muscle graft are then wrapped around the terminal end of the peripheral nerve and secured to itself similarly with epimysial sutures of 6- 0 nylon. The RPNI construct is then placed deeper in the soft tissues away from the incision line. To perform TMR, a peripheral nerve stimulator is used to identify a motor branch to one of the residual innervated muscles of the forearm. The motor branch is then divided, and the terminal end of the peripheral nerve is coapted in an end- to- end fashion to the motor branch as it enters the muscle.

![[Fig.50.4.png]]
> **Fig. 50.4** A, A major peripheral nerve is split along fascicular planes with careful microsurgical dissection. B, Muscle grafts are sutured to the terminal nerve ends to create regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces (RPNIs). (Copyright © 2017 Alexandra B. Hernandez.)

Once the peripheral nerves have been controlled with either RPNIs or TMR, a myodesis is then performed. The deeper muscle bellies are fixed firmly to the underlying skeleton to establish durable bony coverage and to prevent bursitis from excessive excursion of the muscles over the end of the bones. The superficial musculature is sutured with Ethibond in agonist/antagonist pairing over the end of the osteotomies. Care is taken to recreate physiologic tension to

superficial muscle units to maximize postoperative contractions. The force of these contractions will ultimately be used to control a myoelectric prosthesis. The wound is closed in layers, and a bulky compressive dressing is applied.

### Elbow Disarticulation and Transhumeral Amputation

The loss of the elbow joint has a significant adverse impact on the function of the residual limb and greatly diminishes a patient’s adaptation to meaningful use with a prosthetic. In these more proximal amputations, the elbow must be replaced with a prosthetic component that not only adds weight but also, more importantly, adds additional functional requirements that need to be replaced with fewer residual innervated muscles. Above- the- elbow prosthetics require the exertion of more energy to perform daily functions, and thus patients with transhumeral amputations are more likely to abandon their prosthetics than their transradial counterparts.

The discussion regarding elbow disarticulation versus transhumeral amputation is similar to the previous discussion regarding preservation of the distal forearm. There are advocates for either approach, as they each have their own benefits and drawbacks. Preservation of the distal humerus conserves the flare of the condyles, which not only aids in the suspension of the device but also allows for transmission of rotation. These benefits come at fairly high price, however. When an elbow disarticulation is performed, the greater length of the residual upper arm creates limb length discrepancy, which is particularly problematic when a patient utilizes a prosthetic device with an elbow articulation. In these situations, it is more difficult for the patient to reach the anatomic midline, and the asymmetry with the unaffected arm is fairly noticeable, unesthetic, and functionally limiting.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: TRANSHUMERAL AMPUTATION

It is our preference to perform a transhumeral amputation rather than an elbow disarticulation, as the benefits of the latter seem fairly minor. The exception is in the pediatric patient, where there is a high incidence of bony overgrowth after amputation through the metaphysis, which ultimately may require surgical revision. A sterile tourniquet is utilized to limit blood loss and facilitate identification of key structures in a bloodless field. After exsanguination, anterior and posterior flaps are designed 2 cm distal to the level of planned osteotomy, with a medial extension for exposure of all major nerves. Ideally, the osteotomy is planned 10 cm proximal to the olecranon tip to maximize prosthetic options. The brachial artery is ligated with a suture ligature and the radial, median, and ulnar nerves are divided at the level of the osteotomy. The osteotomy is performed with an oscillating saw and all bone edges are carefully rasped smooth. The tourniquet is removed and hemostasis ensured. Occasionally, if there has been preoperative discussion with a prosthetist and it is recommended, we will perform an angulation osteotomy of the distal humerus to provide rotational control of the prosthesis. This is not done routinely in all patients as it can create a soft tissue pressure point in the residual limb. If it is planned, the amputation is performed more distally to account for the length lost with the angulation. The ends of each of the major mixed motor/sensory and pure sensory nerves are then controlled as previously described with either RPNIs or TMR. There are well-described benefits of RPNI and TMR, to reduce neuroma pain and phantom pain following amputation. As a result, we are strong supporters of performing one of these procedures at the time of amputation to minimize the postoperative and long- term pain the patient may experience. The median, ulnar, and radial nerves are transected sharply with a 15 blade at level of the osteotomy. To perform RPNIs, a series of 30 × 15 × 5 mm muscle grafts can be harvested from the residual limb or harvested from the vastus lateralis, based on surgeon preference. The terminal end of the nerves should be secured into the muscle graft with 6- 0 nylon sutures as previously discussed. The edges of the muscle graft are then wrapped around the terminal end and secured to itself similarly with microsurgical suture (Fig. 50.5). This RPNI construct is then placed deeper in the soft tissues away from the incision line. For TMR, we use the technique as described by Dumanian et al. A proximally based adipofascial flap is raised to separate the two heads of the biceps after TMR. Using a stimulator, a motor branch to the short head of the biceps is found, and the terminal end of the median nerve is coapted in an end- to- end fashion to that motor branch as it enters the muscle. Similarly, the ulnar nerve is coapted to the motor branch to the brachialis and the radial nerve is transferred to one of the triceps motor branches. Once the nerves have been controlled with RPNIs or TMR, a myodesis is performed with the musculature of the anterior compartment secured to the muscles of the posterior compartment to cover the distal humeral edge. Unlike in transradial amputations, a closed suction drain is placed to avoid fluid collections. The skin is closed in layers, and a bulky compressive dressing is applied (Fig. 50.6).

### Interscapulothoracic Amputation

Interscapulothoracic (forequarter) amputation is the most radical and, luckily, least frequent amputation in surgical practice. This disfiguring procedure requires disarticulation of the entire shoulder girdle from the thoracic cavity. Although initially described in the setting of trauma in 1808, the surgical technique has not evolved much over two centuries due to the rarity of the procedure. Its main clinical role today is in the treatment of aggressive malignancies as a last recourse. The use of forequarter amputation has continued to decline from 32% in the 1970s to 5% in current studies, largely due to advances in radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and limb salvage surgical techniques.Due to the fact that most of these amputations are done for aggressive malignancies, outcomes are generally poor. Over a 12- year- span at a major orthopedic oncology center, overall survival at 1- year was 42% and less than 20% by year 2.

Planning the closure of the eventual defect after amputation is critical since the amputation cannot be performed any more proximally if there is tension on the skin and difficulty with closure (Fig. 50.7).

This is particularly true in cases of recurrent sarcoma where radiation damage to the surrounding tissues may present a dilemma. If the area of skin resection is large or the tissues are indurated from previous radiation therapy, the surgeon may need to bring in additional tissue to allow a tension- free closure. This goal can be accomplished by performing a fillet of forearm flap or utilizing a fasciocutaneous free flap based on the brachial artery.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: FOREQUARTER AMPUTATION

The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position, with all points of pressure appropriately padded. Like most amputations, previous scars or recurrent malignant disease may affect the ultimate incision design. However, classically, the design of the incisions is an ellipse about the base of the shoulder and around the scapula with a cephalad extension along the clavicle up to the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) to obtain proximal control of the subclavian vessels (Fig. 50.8). The anterior portion of the incision is extended from the deltopectoral groove inferiorly, while the posterior portion crosses the acromion and runs along the medial border of the protracted scapula. The natural skin redundancy over the retracted scapula is preserved to allow for a tension- free closure (Case Study 50.2).

![[Fig.50.8.png]]
> **Fig. 50.8** Anterior (A) and posterior (B) incision design for elective interscapulothoracic amputation. This allows for proximal access to the subclavian vessels and sufficient soft tissue for closure of the amputation site. (Redrawn with permission from Qadir R, Sidhu S, Romine L, et al: Interscapulothoracic [forequarter] amputation for malignant tumors involving the upper extremity: surgical technique and case series, J Shoulder Elbow Surg 23[6]:e127–e133, 2014.)

The operation is commenced anteriorly, exposing the entire clavicle by releasing the muscular attachments with electrocautery. An osteotomy is completed just lateral to the SCM origin with a Cobb elevator protecting the underlying vascular structures. The osteotomy allows for control of the subclavian artery and vein, which are suture ligated and divided. If a free flap is planned for coverage, then vascular control is maintained with a combination of vessel loops and vascular clamps to preserve the stumps of the subclavian artery and vein for microsurgical anastomoses. In most tertiary medical centers, this is completed with the assistance of a vascular surgeon. Once control of the major vessels is obtained, the pectoralis major tendon is divided and retracted medially. The origins of the coracobrachialis and short head of the biceps are divided off of the coracoid along with the insertion of the pectoralis minor muscle. Vascular branches are ligated as they cross the surgical field with care taken to ensure that the transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries, from the thyrocervical trunk, are controlled. In all patients, either RPNIs or TMR should be performed to reduce the incidence of neuroma pain and phantom pain. RPNIs can be performed as previously described on all major branches of the brachial plexus. If it is oncologically acceptable, muscles can be harvested from the amputated part to easily create the RPNIs. Otherwise, muscle grafts can be harvested from the vastus lateralis muscle for creation of the RPNIs. TMR can also be performed in these patients by transferring the major branches of the brachial plexus to divided motor branches of the pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and serratus anterior muscles (Fig. 50.9). A medially based adipofascial flap is raised to separate the two heads of the pectoralis major after TMR. Using a stimulator, the motor branch to the clavicular head of the pectoralis is found, and the terminal musculocutaneous nerve is coapted in an end- to- end fashion to that motor branch as it enters the muscle. The sternal head of the pectoralis is split based on the contractile and vascular patterns into two halves. Care must be taken to completely denervate the pectoralis due to the ansa pectoralis. Similarly, the median nerve is coapted to the motor branch of one portion of the sternal head, and the ulnar nerve is coapted to the other portion of the denervated pectoralis. The radial nerve is transferred in an end- to- end fashion to the divided end of the thoracodorsal nerve.

![[Fig.50.9.png]]
> **Fig. 50.9** Terminal ulnar nerve transferred to the motor branch along undersurface of split pectoralis major, in targeted-muscle reinnervation (TMR) case.

Attention is then directed posteriorly. The incision is made along the medial border of the scapula, dissecting medially toward the muscular insertion of the rhomboid muscles. As the muscle fascia is incised, the shoulder is flexed and adducted to bring the scapula off of the thorax. If one does not have sufficient help in the operating room, a hydraulic arm holder can be of great assistance. All of the muscular attachments are released superiorly and medially off of the scapula, leaving only the serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi as attachments to the specimen. These tendons are divided, and the specimen is passed off for pathology. The skin is closed in a layered fashion over closed suction drains. A bulky compression dressing is then applied.

## CURRENT PROSTHETIC REHABILITATION

The loss of an upper extremity is devastating for the patients. Unfortunately, current prostheses do not provide functional restoration that approximates that of the normal human hand. Not only do they lack adequate degrees of freedom, but they also lack sensory feedback,

which is critical for restoration of naturalistic function. Every activity requires visual cues for direction, as there is minimal proprioceptive and tactile feedback to guide use of the prosthetic device. Thus the patient sustaining an upper extremity amputation must expend greater energy—both physical and cognitive—to perform the activities of daily living. The ultimate goal is to provide patients with functional independence through prosthetic rehabilitation, allowing them to participate in the vocational and recreational activities they did prior to surgery. However, as mentioned previously, 33% of patients require a change in occupation and 58% discontinue recreational activities. These suboptimal outcomes are in part due to the performance measures of the prosthetic devices but also are related to the ability of the patient to provide high- fidelity signals to control the device.

There are a multitude of factors that influence prosthetic rehabilitation including the level of amputation, comorbidities, range of motion, strength, patient goals/preferences, and health insurance coverage. The level of amputation probably has the greatest impact on prosthetic rehabilitation since it determines: (1) the functions that need to be replaced, (2) the length of the residual limb to provide stability and control of the terminal device, and (3) the prosthetic components that will be required to reestablish function. There are a wide variety of choices when developing a restorative plan after upper extremity loss—from passive, cosmetic devices to futuristic robotic units with multiple functions and several degrees of freedom (Table 50.1). Comprehensive coverage of all the options is a massive topic and beyond the scope of this chapter. However, we hope to provide you with a basic understanding of prosthetic rehabilitation and how it pertains to the surgeon.

### Rehabilitation After Upper Extremity Amputation

Ideally, prosthetic rehabilitation commences before the major limb amputation even takes place. The patient should meet with a physiatrist prior to surgery, and there should be collaboration between the surgeon and physiatrist to create a rehabilitation care plan. Level of amputation should be discussed, focusing on the impact each potential level will have on functional outcome, prosthetic restoration, and rehabilitation. The physiatrist can also provide counseling that may better prepare the patient for their new disability. Obviously, since most amputations of the upper extremity occur traumatically, this is usually not a possibility. However, when possible, this is greatly beneficial to all patients physically and psychologically.

If there are no contraindications, range- of- motion exercises of the residual limb should be initiated soon after surgery. Occupational therapy can provide gentle range of motion of the surgical limb and training in using the noninvolved arm in eating, dressing, and self- care. They can also provide thermoplastic extensions, which depending on the level of limb loss, will allow the patient to use the residual limb for some bimanual skills before the prosthesis can be fitted. In this early postoperative period, graded compression of the residual limb will help create a well- shaped recipient for the socket liner. Good pain control is of great importance, as its absence will delay the recovery and participation in rehabilitation.

Barring any wound healing issues, a provisional prosthesis should be fitted as soon as possible. After upper extremity amputation, patients immediately begin using compensatory movements to be more functional—some which persist even after completion of rehabilitation.Motivation and interest peaks early after limb loss, and one hopes that the patient is in their prosthesis prior to adapting to life without the extremity and developing poor compensatory habits. This is often referred to as Malone's “golden period”—the first month after amputation. The successful rehabilitation rate drops to 42% for patients fitted after the initial 30 days postoperatively. The main purpose of the provisional prosthesis is to acclimate the patient to the use of a prosthesis, hopefully providing a positive experience. As edema resolves, a final prosthesis can be designed and fitted—those who do so within 6 months are 16 times more likely to use their prosthesis long term. It is critically important that the patient undergoes counseling regarding all of the various types of terminal devices, their capabilities, and functionality so they can select the device that is best suited to their needs. However, even with good education and fitting, many patients ultimately abandon their prostheses due to discomfort, lack of functional gains, appearance, weight of the device, lack of sensory feedback, short battery life, and financial constraints. A review the literature shows that rejection rates are greater for passive- power prostheses, and approximately 20% of adults with amputations abandon all prosthetics and routinely use no device.

## PROSTHETIC COMPONENTS AND OPTIONS

### Prosthetic Designs

Upper extremity prostheses can be categorized in a variety of ways. The most obvious categorization is by level of amputation. As the amputation becomes more proximal, it is more difficult to suspend the prosthesis without the use of a harness and more functions need to be replaced with either locking or powered joints. At each amputation level, there are multiple options for a patient to choose from. For an individual patient, prostheses are best categorized by their source of power—passive, body- powered, externally powered, and hybrid. The passive prosthesis is considered a cosmetic terminal device, since that is why they are most commonly selected. They are usually lightweight and can look fairly lifelike if fitted with a cosmetic silicone glove. Interestingly, they can also be quite functional, either by acting to stabilize objects or by having a recreation- specific design.

Body- powered prostheses are the most commonly prescribed prosthetic in the upper extremity. They are lightweight and much less expensive than externally powered devices, particularly for people with transhumeral amputations. They harness scapular and humeral motion, and since they work through a relatively simple system of metal cables and joint locks, they are durable and require very little maintenance. These qualities make them attractive to manual laborers who are more likely to have suffered a traumatic amputation (Fig. 50.10). Unfortunately, they do appear “mechanical” and have a bare hook, which is not esthetically pleasing to some patients.

![[Fig.50.10.png]]
> **Fig. 50.10** A body-powered hook terminal device is durable and requires very little maintenance; it is therefore attractive to manual laborers. (Courtesy of Advanced Arm Dynamics, Torrance, CA, with permission.)

While the designs of body- powered devices have not changed much in a century, externally powered prostheses have undergone extensive research and development. While early designs used pneumatics and hydraulics, current myoelectric prosthetic devices are run by batterypowered motors that are constructed in the artificial limb itself. Surface electrodes detect electrical activity in the superficial musculature upon volitional contraction, allowing the patient to open and close the terminal device by activation of agonist and antagonist muscle groups. Externally powered prostheses have greater prehension strength (>30 pounds of pinch) than body- powered devices, which close using a series of rubber bands (Fig. 50.11). However, each additional function greatly increases the cost of the device, weight, and required maintenance. Prototypes tested by the Department of Defense for transhumeral amputees cost upwards of $100,000.

![[Fig.50.11.png]]
> **Fig. 50.11** The new Michelangelo Hand from Otto Bock has multiple specific grip functions for mastering tasks such as gripping a key or holding a pen, enabling it to function more like a human hand. (Courtesy of Advanced Arm Dynamics, Torrance, CA, with permission.)

Once the patient is proficient in the use of the provisional prosthesis, limb size has stabilized, and functional needs have been determined, decisions about the definitive prosthetic restoration can be made. As stated previously, some patients may need more than one device to achieve their goals. While the final prescription is an individualized decision, there are some general trends. *Myoelectric devices* are preferred for most patients with transradial amputations due to their improved cosmesis, pinch strength, and anatomic suspension. *Hybrid* *prostheses* are usually preferred for proximal amputations, including transhumeral amputations and shoulder disarticulations (Fig. 50.12). This is due to the lack of sufficient and distinct proximal muscle signals to power a complete myoelectric device. However, passive and body- powered devices can be fashioned for any level of amputation, the latter being the device of choice for the manual laborer due to their durability.

### Sockets and Suspension Systems

The socket is the very foundation for prosthetic rehabilitation. It must be comfortable to wear or the patient is sure to discontinue its use. A test socket is created during the initial fitting of the provisional prosthesis. Based on patient feedback, a customized permanent socket is constructed out of carbon fiber and Kevlar to fit the final prosthesis. Sockets may be self- suspended or require some harnessing. Selfsuspended sockets have an intimate fit that has direct translation of motion from the residual limb to the socket, and in turn, the prosthesis. Conversely, non–self- suspended sockets are easier to don but require a highly visible harness that is less cosmetically appealing. That harness is necessary in more proximal amputations, such as in a patient with a transhumeral amputation. The greater the weight of the distal prosthetic, the more likely that weight must be distributed away from the end of the residual limb and shared through a harness. Suspension depends not only on the level of amputation but also on the control method for the prosthetic joints. For instance, a body- powered prosthesis requires an external suspension system to harness the scapular motion and translate it down a cable to the terminal device.

## FRONTIERS IN PROSTHETIC REHABILITATION

In recent years, there has been an increase in the awareness of the limitations of current prosthetic designs, as wounded servicemembers have attempted to reintegrate into society after limb loss. This awareness has led to significant increases in research funding and scientific discovery in the field. Although advances have been made, limitations abound, with upper extremity prosthetic users uniformly dissatisfied with the prehensile function of the available terminal devices. There is insufficient control of multiple degrees of freedom and a lack of sensory feedback, both of which are key in optimizing use of the upper extremity prosthetic for daily activities. These pitfalls are further compounded in patients with more proximal amputations, as the loss of the elbow joint decreases the muscles available for prosthetic activation and adds another function that must be replaced with the terminal device. Current and future research are designed to address these shortcomings and create a prosthetic that will more adequately replace the human hand.

### Improved Prosthetic Control

In an attempt to create the ideal prosthetic, the largest obstacles originate from the lack of motor control and true sensory feedback. The perfect mechanical extremity would be useless without the ability to control it consistently and precisely. Without proprioceptive and tactile feedback, there is a significant cognitive burden in doing everyday tasks, as visual cues must be used for every act. An ideal control system would not only provide that crucial feedback to the user but also allow for simultaneous motion at multiple joints with volitional control. A high- fidelity neural- machine interface—both for motor control and sensory feedback—is the ultimate goal. To achieve this, five research paradigms have been developed to record efferent motor action potentials to provide volitional control of a robotic extremity: (1) TMR, (2) RPNIs, (3) neurovascular muscle transfers, (4) traditional peripheral nerve interfaces, and (5) central nervous system (CNS) interfaces.

TMR is a novel approach designed to address the paucity of myosites in patients with proximal amputations. In conventional myoelectric devices, patients with transradial amputations will use their residual innervated wrist flexors and extensors to control their terminal device. However, in a more proximal amputation, there are minimal residual innervated muscles to provide an electromyography (EMG) signal for prosthetic control; in a transhumeral amputation or shoulder disarticulation, there are no innervated wrist flexors and extensors to provide myoelectric signals to control prosthetic hand function. In these circumstances, there is a limited ability to control hand function and elbow function simultaneously. Dr. Dumanian and colleagues have created a solution for this problem with TMR. Target proximal muscles are denervated and then provided with an alternative signal from a transferred nerve. The reinnervated muscles serve as biologic amplifiers of the previously lost motor commands. The muscle contractions are detected by surface EMG electrodes, which then control the myoelectric prosthesis.

TMR patients have had great clinical success, though the number of patients remains small. They have demonstrated a sevenfold increase in efficiency in completing tasks with their prostheses, allowing patients with proximal amputations to control their device at multiple articulations simultaneously. Patients report that the control is more natural and intuitive, reducing the amount of necessary training with their prosthesis.

Over the past decade, the RPNI has been developed to provide more independent control signals for higher fidelity motor control of a prosthetic, without the need for denervation of any existing residual muscles. RPNIs are created by wrapping the end of a divided nerve or nerve fascicle with a free skeletal muscle graft and securing it into this position. The skeletal muscle graft becomes reinnervated and becomes a bioamplifier of the efferent motor action potentials of the peripheral nerve. It is possible to create RPNIs on numerous nerve fascicles thereby creating many control signals to provide many degrees of freedom. In addition, there is no need for denervation of existing innervated muscle in the residual limb. This is important because those innervated muscles could be used as control signals independent of the control signals provided by the RPNIs. For instance, in a patient with a transhumeral amputation, surface EMG can be used to record control signals from the residual innervated biceps and triceps to provide elbow flexion and extension. RPNIs can be used to control independent finger flexion and extension by interfacing with median and ulnar nerve fascicles, and intrinsic hand function can be created by interfacing with ulnar nerve fascicles.

TMR and RPNIs have been designed to provide higher fidelity control of prosthetic devices, particularly with the increasing demand from wounded warriors returning from combat. However, one of the greatest results has been in the incidental findings in pain control. Both TMR and RPNIs have been found to mitigate phantom pain and prevent neuroma formation. The implications of these effects are immense in improving the quality of life of our patients, particularly in the current setting of an opioid epidemic.

Neurovascular muscle transfers are also currently under investigation for improved control of upper and lower extremity prostheses. The “Starfish procedure” involves isolating the dorsal interosseous muscles on their neurovascular pedicles and then transferring them to a more superficial location for EMG signal capture. This technique is very helpful in providing control signals for myoelectric prostheses in patients sustaining partial hand amputations. Unfortunately, these muscles are frequently in the zone of injury and are not salvageable when more proximal amputations are performed.

The agonist- antagonist myoneural interface (AMI) is a novel surgical technique designed to provide motor control signals and sensory feedback for more precise control of neuroprosthetic devices. The tendons of the agonist and antagonist muscles are secured to each other so that when an agonist fires, it will shorten and place tension on the antagonist muscle. In this way, not only can signals be recorded for prosthetic control but also the patient can receive proprioceptive feedback through the interplay between the two antagonistic muscles. To date, this technique has focused on the lower extremity with the ultimate goal of providing a more natural and controlled gait. The potential to re- create the degrees of freedom needed for improved upper extremity function after amputation remains to be determined.

For many years, scientists and engineers have been developing various types of peripheral nerve interfaces to improve prosthetic control. These interfaces have been developed to record efferent motor action potentials directly from motor nerves or mixed motor/sensory nerves. “Cuff electrodes” are implanted extraneural electrodes that surround nerves and record several fascicles. They have been shown to be safe and effective in humans, but they have limitations in selectivity, as the small amplitude signals can be difficult to isolate from adjacent muscle contractions. In addition, with biofouling of the cuff electrode over time, it is possible that the efferent motor action potentials can no longer be discriminated from the electrical noise, thus making it less effective for prosthetic control. The longevity of cuff electrodes has been nicely demonstrated when the electrodes are used as stimulating devices to provide afferent sensory feedback. Intraneural electrodes, such as longitudinal intrafascicular electrodes (LIFEs) and rigid multielectrode arrays, are placed within the peripheral nerve with direct axonal contact (Fig. 50.13). This increased contact area with the axons improves the selectivity of the individual signals. However, the location of intraneural electrodes within the peripheral nerve leads to fibrosis, both from initial placement and micromotion over time. Scarring in the nerve causes progressive axonal damage, axonal pruning, and biofouling of the electrode, which ultimately results in loss of signal.

Brain interfaces have been the focus of research for many years. With the possibility of direct interactions with the brain, CNS interfaces are attractive in their potential ability to both capture motor commands and provide sensory feedback. Brain signals can be recorded with electroencephalograms (EEGs) on the scalp, with electrocorticograms (ECoGs) on the cerebral surface, or directly from electrodes implanted into the brain. These electrical signals from implantable electrodes have been shown to control a number of devices including prosthetic arms. There are significant limitations and some potential benefits to this approach. EEG signals represent a large number of neurons, rather than individual cells, so there are limits in the resolution of the signal. This makes it difficult to code movement for volitional intent consistently. Since derived directly from the motor cortex, ECoG signals are specific and have much better signal resolution, but gaining access requires a craniotomy and these implants have a limited functional life. Much like the intraneural electrodes placed in the peripheral nerves, ECoG arrays lose fidelity and signal strength over time due to the fibrosis at the cortex/electrode contact area. Nevertheless, there has been success in subject animals to execute complex tasks with these implanted interfaces, and one team has successfully achieved control of a prosthetic limb with ECoG in a human. These CNS interfaces are unlikely to be the answer in patients with upper extremity amputations but may serve a purpose within the spinal cord injury population.

### Improved Prosthetic Design

In the past 20 years there have been amazing advancements in the field of robotics. While some of these advances have carried over to prosthetic technology, there is still a substantial amount of development that is required. Prosthetics are very complex devices that need to become lighter, have more degrees of freedom, have sensory feedback capabilities, have longer battery life, and be esthetically pleasing. Functional gains must be balanced with the added weight and size that come with them. Furthermore, the typical patient with limb loss lacks the means to pay for the advanced, complex neuroprosthetic robotic devices available. Nevertheless, advances are constantly on the horizon, and improvements can be made to the current devices available.

Sockets and liners provide a critical interface between the prosthetic device and the residual limb. Not only must they allow for the transfer of work from the organic to the mechanical, but also they must remain comfortable to allow for use over a full day. New materials are always under development in the hopes of greater durability and less weight. Polymers will continue to be developed with greater strength and pliability. Computer- aided design and computer- aided manufacture (CAD/CAM) has recently become an area of increased interest in prosthetics. Custom- fitted devices can be designed and produced more quickly and at a fraction of the cost. Northwestern University's ProstheticsOrthotics Center is at the forefront of the CAD/CAM movement with its “Squirt Shape” technique. On the design side, the Center is using new technology such as the Inspeck 3D Megacapturor optical system and Shapemaker software. The former can digitize complex shapes such as residual limbs with the aid of up to 7,800,000 sampled points from a single camera, while the latter creates three- dimensional models that can be digitally modified to relieve pressure in a sensitive area by applying it in a more tolerant position. On the manufacturing side, the Center uses a deposition modeler, which can extrude hundreds of layers of plastic, thermally bonded together, in any complex three- dimensional shape, including prosthetic sockets. Fabrication is totally automated, and thus a finished product can be created in less than an hour with a dollar’s worth of raw materials. One can envision that centralized prosthetic centers could easily provide sockets and liners for patients with decreased fabrication errors and inconsistencies on a large scale. This is a dramatic shift from the current state of prosthetic rehabilitation, which is more art than science and is taught in an apprenticeship model.

Actuators convert available potential energy to actual work and, much like batteries, are valued in terms of their power density. Engineered actuators are much more efficient than human muscle, yet they have higher expectations still given the limited space for stored energy. The current gold standard is the brushless DC motor, which requires less maintenance and delivers a higher power output at a smaller size than its predecessors. However, electromagnetic motors waste significant energy in the speeding up and slowing down with each movement. Potential alternatives are under development to address this issue. Mesofluidic actuation uses pressurized fluid for the creation of force and has a much greater power density than conventional electric motors. While traditional hydraulics lose their mechanical advantage when miniaturized, researchers have linked antagonistic joint movements and recently patented a fully mesofluidic actuated digit.Another potential solution to the energy lost with brushless motors is cobotics. In cobotic actuation, a central electromagnetic motor spins continuously and has multiple outputs, which can actuate motion individually or in unison. This approach is ideal for the intricate, complex, and multidimensional motions the upper extremity must perform at multiple joints to have more naturalistic movement. Cobots preserve the energy lost in stopping/starting conventional electric motors, but current advances in development have yet to produce a cobot small enough to make a device feasible for a prosthetic.

## SUMMARY

A major amputation of an upper extremity can be severely disabling. The human hand is a true marvel that engineers have been trying to recreate since the Industrial Revolution. Our hands not only play a role in our ability to perform our daily activities but also are integral in how we communicate with others and how we view our own humanity. Early prosthetic rehabilitation is critical in maximizing patient outcomes, as there is significant evidence that delays result in greater prosthetic abandonment. Current upper extremity prosthetic options have significant limitations, but they can often result in a patient’s return to a fulfilling life at work and play.

The field of prosthetics and biomechanical engineering has seen a surge in interest and research funding alongside the surge in young men and women with limb loss returning from overseas conflicts. Lighter materials, smaller/faster processors, and newer batteries have contributed positively to this patient population over the past decade. Yet significant challenges remain, as new devices are prohibitively expensive and still fail to provide the necessary haptic feedback of the human hand. Neural- machine interfaces are an exciting area of research and development but require much more study prior to integration into routine clinical practice. Surely, the next decade will be an exciting time to be in the field of prosthetics, and, hopefully, the loss of a limb will become more of a hurdle than a disability.
