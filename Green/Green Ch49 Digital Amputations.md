Traumatic injury is the most common cause of an amputated finger or thumb. In other cases, digital amputation may be necessary because of congenital deformity or acquired pathologic conditions, such as malignant tumors or chronic infection. Sequelae of trauma may also require amputation, such as stiffness, debilitating pain, or revision surgery to improve fit of a prosthesis. Functional recovery is the primary consideration after an amputation. For this reason, the surgeon should be aware of the patient’s occupation and vocations. The surgeon should express empathy and gauge the patient’s emotional response following the amputation. Failure to appreciate the patient’s attitude and ability to adjust to the injury may compromise an otherwise successful operative procedure.

As surgeons, we tend to be influenced by the experience of mentors early in our careers, and later, our own expanding experience. We hold fast to methods that prove satisfactory in our hands and quickly seek alternatives to procedures that yield unsatisfactory outcomes. The numerous articles referenced in this chapter attest to the reasoned contributions of many authors who treat similar amputation situations by disparate methods. For some surgeons, the more technically demanding procedures have great appeal. Other surgeons prefer the simplest yet most effective procedure, such as shortening of bone followed by primary closure, or healing by secondary intention. There is no gold standard technique for every clinical scenario.

The *goals* of amputation surgery in the upper extremity should be (1) preservation of functional length, (2) provision of durable skin coverage, (3) preservation of useful sensibility, (4) avoidance of symptomatic neuromas, (5) prevention of adjacent joint contractures, (6) prevention of morbidity by minimizing complications, (7) allowance of early prosthetic fitting when applicable, and (8) allowance of early return to work, play, and activities of daily living.

Any amputation in a child, whose ultimate role in life is yet undefined, should be approached with a conservative attitude. Later reconstruction is generally preferable to any early radical ablative procedure. Adults with well-defined functional roles may be better served by a more definitive approach that will accelerate their functional rehabilitation.

At times, deciding whether to perform limb salvage or amputation may be difficult. Consultation with an experienced and knowledgeable colleague is helpful and should be documented in the patient’s medical record. It may help reassure the patient and family. Photographs of the injured extremity before amputation are useful to document the severity of the injury. Although replantation or attempted salvage of a severely traumatized limb is an option at times, it may be more of a technical triumph than a procedure done in the patient’s best interests. The enthusiasm for the successful technical accomplishment of replanting an amputated finger part versus closure and early rehabilitation has been dampened by poor patient outcomes at a greater financial cost, including lost wages. Cold intolerance can be a substantial problem for patients following an amputation, but the symptoms tend to improve over time.

## DIGITAL TIP AMPUTATIONS

An amputation of the fingertip is the most common type of amputation seen in the upper extremity and at the same time provokes the greatest controversy because of the multitude of treatment options. Multiple techniques have been developed to advance local skin or transfer soft tissue to ensure coverage of an area where bone is exposed. The surgeon, in consultation with each patient, must choose the type of coverage that appears to be most appropriate for that individual’s needs and within the technical abilities of the surgeon. Regardless of choice of treatment, the goals of preserving functional length and restoring adequate sensibility remain constant. When one is comparing the various treatment options available or looking for guidance, the injury zone classification systems of Hirase,Allen, and Tamai provide a useful frame of reference (Fig. 49.1).

### Digital Tip Amputations With Skin or Pulp Loss Only

When the fingertip is amputated, the geometry of the defect dictates the various treatment possibilities. The loss may be transverse or oblique, with more volar skin loss than dorsal skin loss, or the reverse may be true. Some slicing amputations may result in skin loss primarily from the ulnar or radial side of the digit and may spare the distal tip.

#### Nonmicrosurgical Reattachment or Composite Graft

If the amputated part is available and not highly contaminated, it may be cleansed and reattached. This can be an effective approach in children with injuries distal to the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint. The tip is reattached as a composite graft or defatted and sutured to the residual digit as a full-thickness skin graft (Fig. 49.2). The concept of using it as a “biologic dressing” has been advocated by some; however, this is not often appropriate in adults due to the lack of success, and the concept of placing dead tissue into a contaminated field is flawed. If there is bony involvement of the composite graft, stability may be afforded by repair of the amputated tip with absorbable sutures, Kirschner wires, or a hypodermic needle. The nail plate is removed and the nail plate repaired. Timing of repair may be important. Moiemen and Elliot noted that those repaired within 5 hours after injury had a 61% survival rate compared with none after this time point. The tip may become necrotic but should be left in situ as long as there is no developing infection because the tissue acts as a biologic dressing. Butler and colleagues conducted a retrospective review of 120 patients under 16 years of age who underwent composite graft of digital tip injuries and noted 10% complete and 34% partial graft survival rate. Patients younger than the age of 4 years were likely to have complete graft survival. However, 48% of patients developed a hook nail deformity, 17% had cold intolerance, and 95% had some degree of functional limitations in the long term.Similar success has not been noted within adults. Heistein and Cookreported on 53 patients who underwent composite tissue grafting of injuries distal to the DIP joint. The success rate was 43% for injuries between the DIP joint and the eponychium and 58% for injuries distal to the eponychium. Factors linked to suboptimal results were smoking, age older than 18 years, alcohol use, presence of diabetes, and crush injury. In an effort to improve survival, Chen and colleagues reported on a technique whereby the distal amputated tip was defatted and deepithelialized and the bone excised in patients who sustained either a crush injury or a sharp transection injury. The survival rate was higher than 93% with an average two-point discrimination of 6.3 mm after 6 months. Over 90% of patients were happy with the cosmetic appearance and 86% were able to use the finger normally at work.

#### Dressing Changes and Healing by Secondary Intention

Healing by secondary intention with dressing changes is one of the best options for a tip amputation without exposed bone. This technique is especially applicable when the amputated part is unavailable or cannot be used. The technique is simple, inexpensive, and effective, regardless of patient age (Fig. 49.3). Healing by secondary intention is most applicable for wounds with skin loss of 1.5 cm or less; healing will occur by wound contracture and reepithelialization over 3 to 4 weeks with return of normal sensation and two-point discrimination. Indeed, recovery of sensation tends to be better than with other forms of surgical reconstruction. Dressing changes can also be used to treat wounds with additional loss of subcutaneous tissue of similar size, but it may take 1 to 2 months to heal by this method. The patient performs daily dressing changes and local wound care. Protective splinting may be used to protect the healing wound. Patients can be referred to hand therapists within the first few days after injury to use modalities such as MIST ultrasound therapy (Celleration, Inc., Eden Prairie, MN) to encourage granulation tissue formation. Ultrasound works by reducing the bacterial load and promoting tissue regeneration by increasing angiogenesis, release of growth factors, and collagen deposition.Patients are encouraged to work on the range of motion to avoid joint stiffness and/or contractures.

Bojsen-Moller and colleagues reviewed a series of 134 digital tip injuries in 110 patients who were treated by dressing changes, shortening of the digit, or skin grafting. They observed that nonoperative treatment was uniformly compared with surgical cases that developed a variety of complications such as infection or graft failure that prolonged treatment. In addition, there was no significant difference in time off work between the treatment groups. The authors concluded that management of an amputation by skin grafting or dressing changes was superior to surgical shortening and primary closure. Allen reported on 46 patients with 49 fingertip injuries from the lunula and distal. If the distal phalanx was exposed, it was trimmed back to allow soft tissue to cover it. Forty-five patients felt their results were very good or good, 3 believed their outcome was acceptable, and 1 deemed the result as poor. Four of the 49 injuries required secondary surgery, 3 for nail growth problems and 1 because of lack of ample soft tissue coverage. The average two-point discrimination was 6 mm, and the average time off work ranged from 18 to 26 days. None of the patients had any joint stiffness, although 50% did experience cold intolerance that was marked in 10%. Weichman and colleaguescompared the outcomes of patients with fingertip injuries treated nonoperatively with those treated operatively (nail removal, revision amputation, or grafting with full-thickness grafts or local flaps, such as the “V-Y” advancement, cross-finger, thenar, or first dorsal metacarpal flap). Those who had surgery had larger wounds (3.3 cm versus 1.75 cm), volar oblique lacerations (50% versus 9%), exposed bone (81% versus 35%), and a distal phalanx fracture (81% versus 47%). The authors found that patients requiring operative treatments required more time off work (4.3 weeks compared with 2.9 weeks), which was likely related to the extent of injury.

#### Primary Closure

Primary closure may be performed if the repair is without tension across the wound edges. This can be difficult because there tends to be a lack of sufficient mobile dermis for a tension-free primary repair. Primary closure, when possible, was shown in the series by Holm and Zachariae to give results equivalent to conservative healing by secondary intention. However, in the series reported by Sturman and Duran, 51% of the patients complained of tenderness with some disability. Shortening of the bone may allow primary closure; however, the surgeon should perform traction neurectomies if the amputation is proximal enough to identify nerve branches to prevent a symptomatic neuroma. A nail bed ablation is performed if the injury is at or through the lunula. The entire germinal and sterile matrix is excised. This is performed by excising the tissue on the undersurface of the proximal nail fold as well as removing all nail matrix from the residual distal phalanx. Use of a curette to scrape the dorsal cortex of the distal phalanx is helpful. The patient should be informed of the potential risk of a symptomatic neuroma and a bothersome nail horn that can be present several months after the procedure.

#### Split-Thickness Grafting

Split-thickness skin grafting has been a popular method for coverage of the exposed pulp when there is a well-vascularized recipient bed.Split-thickness grafts contract more than full-thickness grafts, and both are limited by the fact they cannot be placed directly onto bone or tendon devoid of its paratenon. In a study by Holm and Zachariae, only 56% of the patients who had undergone skin grafting considered their results to be good after 5 years of follow-up, compared with 90% of patients treated conservatively with a wound that was permitted to heal by secondary intention. The most notable complaints after skin grafting were induration and fissuring of the skin and reduced sensibility in the graft, as well as complaints about the donor site. Cold sensitivity was present in 39% of the patients treated conservatively and in 33% of those who received a split-thickness skin graft. Hypoesthesia was present in 26% of those treated conservatively and in 67% of those who were treated with a skin graft. The authors concluded that splitthickness skin grafting offered no advantages for fingertip coverage.

Sturman and Duran monitored 235 patients with a fingertip amputation for a minimum of 1 year and reported their late outcomes. Of the patients who had split-thickness skin grafts, 70% reported tenderness in the area of the graft, and in 41%, the tenderness was considered to be marked. Cold sensitivity was present in 49% of the patients who had split-thickness skin grafts, including 27 of the 53 who had thumb or index tip amputations. Fifty-nine percent of the latter group avoided the injured digit when the pickup test was administered. Thirty-two percent of patients noted diminished touch sensibility. The average two-point discrimination measured was 5 mm, considerably greater than the normal 2 mm.

### Digital Tip Amputations With Exposed Bone

When bone is exposed at the level of amputation, the question becomes whether length should be preserved (necessitating coverage of the site) or whether sacrifice of length is justifiable. However, small areas of well-vascularized bone may support healing if desiccation is prevented with appropriate wound care (such as with semiocclusive dressings or matrix bilayer dressings). For larger areas, flap coverage versus revision amputation via skeletal shortening and closure is the dilemma. An important variable is the extent of nail matrix injury and the possibility of developing a problematic hook nail deformity. The development of a hook nail is related to the wound closure and amount of bony support for the nail bed. Closure of a fingertip amputation by pulling the nail bed over the distal phalanx should be avoided. If a satisfactory amount of distal phalanx is absent, the nail bed should be trimmed back to the same level at the end of the bone so that it does not curve over the end of the bone and result in a hook nail. A nail bed ablation is performed if the injury is at or through the lunula. Since the primary aim is to expeditiously restore function, some wounds with minimal exposed bone are allowed to heal secondarily with appropriate dressing changes to avoid bone desiccation. Minimal skeletal shortening with a rasp or rongeur can allow adequate soft tissue coverage. Our suture preference is an absorbable suture to avoid the need for suture removal as the sutures become covered during the healing process and are painful to remove. Alternatively, the wound may be left to heal by secondary intention, ensuring that the nail bed is supported by underlying bone. Antibiotics are not routinely prescribed if there has been adequate wound debridement without gross contamination. Patients are seen within a week and are sent to occupational therapy to work on edema control and range of motion. Tip desensitization is started once the wound is healed and stable. Many of these injuries are work-related, and most manual workers are able to return to duty without restrictions by 6 to 8 weeks after injury. It is important to emphasize that skin-to-skin closure is not necessary. Flap coverage is reserved for cases with considerable bone exposure. There are a variety of described techniques that will be discussed, each with its own merits and limitations.

#### Semiocclusive Dressings

Studies have demonstrated successful treatment with the use of an occlusive dressing. The dressing should be applied within a few days of the injury. Self-adhesive OpSite or Flexifix film (Smith and Nephew, London, UK) is used. The dressing is changed weekly until epithelialization has been completed. Hoigné and associates reported on a series of 17 patients with an exposed distal phalanx; patient satisfaction was good with regeneration of approximately 90% of the original soft tissue volume with an average two-point discrimination of 4 mm. The average duration of dressing use was 6.5 weeks. There were no cases of bone or soft tissue infection. Complications included a single case of a nail horn and neuroma formation.

#### Bilayer Matrix Wound Dressing

The Integra Bilayer Matrix Wound Dressing (BMWD) (Integra Life Sciences, Plainsboro, NJ) is an acceptable alternative to flap coverage. The graft consists of an acellular dermal matrix composed of bovine collagen covered by a silicone layer. It is designed to act as a skin regeneration scaffold that allows the host’s cells to grow into the dermal template. It can be placed directly onto bone or tendon once the wound bed is clean and devoid of contamination. It can be sutured in place and its “take” encouraged with bolster dressings. The graft is normally applied as part of a two-stage procedure in which autologous skin graft is applied 3 to 4 weeks after matrix application. Taras and coworkers used two-stage grafting in digital injuries with exposed bone, joint, tendon, or hardware in 21 digits. The area of Integra application ranged from 1 to 12 cm. Twenty of 21 digits demonstrated complete incorporation of the dermal matrix, and following skin grafting, 16 of 20 digits demonstrated complete incorporation. Single-stage use avoids the morbidity of skin grafting and is the same as healing by secondary intention because the healing process relies on both wound contraction and reepithelialization. In a series of 9 patients with an average defect of 2.3 cm (range, 1 to 3.2 cm), Jacoby and colleagues demonstrated high patient satisfaction, with a mean static two-point discrimination of 9.6 mm (4.6 mm for the opposite unaffected finger). One patient developed an early infection, followed by a hook nail deformity 18 months after treatment. There were no other complications. The Integra BMWD is more expensive than a semiocclusive dressing or healing by secondary intention. However, the matrix may be placed directly on bone, preserving critical length and obviating the need for flap coverage with the associated inconvenience and morbidity (Fig. 49.4).

#### Atasoy-Kleinert Volar “V-Y” Flap

In 1970, Atasoy and associates described a triangular volar “V-Y” advancement flap (Fig. 49.5) for reconstruction of the distal pad with preservation of length when bone is exposed. It is indicated for dorsal oblique or transverse distal fingertip amputations beyond the midpart of the nail. The flap is contraindicated in oblique amputation injuries with more palmar skin loss than dorsal skin loss and injuries with extensive skin loss.

Digital anesthesia is administered before preparation of the wound. After surgical preparation of the hand, a Tourni-Cot (Mar-Med, Grand Rapids, MI) is used to exsanguinate the digit and simultaneously provide tourniquet ischemia. Alternatively, the finger is elevated and a Penrose drain secured at the base of the digit. A pattern may be made to cover the dimensions of the defect and then transposed proximally to the cut edge of the skin that is to be advanced. The base of the triangle will be the distal cut edge, and an appropriate triangle of skin is made according to the pattern, with the apex of the triangle being at the DIP flexion crease. The two sides of the triangle should be at least 1.5 times the length of the desired advancement. Only the full thickness of the skin is cut. The digital nerves and blood vessels of the flap are preserved. Its blood supply is presumed to be axial from proximal small arterial branches of the distal digital arteries. Separation between the flexor sheath and subcutaneous tissue is performed by dividing the fibrous septae that anchor the subcutaneous tissue to the bone, which facilitates advancement of the flap distally. The skin of the flap must be connected to the subcutaneous tissue to maintain its viability. The base of the triangle is carefully contoured and sutured to the nail bed or remaining nail, and the resulting “V” incision on the palmar aspect of the digit is closed, thus converting it to a “Y” (see Fig. 49.5). The Tourni-Cot or Penrose drain is removed prior to flap insetting to assess viability. The flap can be adequately mobilized up to 1 cm and if necessary, defatted to facilitate tension-free skin closure. Postoperative management consists of sterile dressings that allow early motion of all adjacent joints, especially the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint. Active motion and digital usage are advanced as healing ensues over the next few weeks.

In their initial report, Atasoy and associates stated that 56 of 61 patients available for follow-up had normal sensation and motion.However, a follow-up study conducted by Frandsen found hypoesthesia or dysesthesia in 7 of 10 patients with triangular volar flaps. Four of the 10 patients had subjective complaints of cold intolerance, and 5 of the patients also had difficulty grasping objects. Conolly and Goulston had suboptimal results in 4 of 7 patients treated by this technique.The results were deemed unsatisfactory because of persistent paresthesias or impaired sensibility. Tupper and Miller reported diminished sensibility in all 16 of their patients. Eight of their patients reported hypersensitivity, particularly cold intolerance.

#### Kutler Lateral “V-Y” Flaps

Kutler described the use of “V-Y” advancement flaps for digital tip injuries (Fig. 49.6). The technique involves advancing two triangular flaps from lateral positions to cover the tip of the digit.

![[Fig.49.6.png]]
> **Fig. 49.6** A, Triangular flaps are marked, centered over the midlateral aspect of the finger. B, The skin is incised and fibrous septa divided, allowing mobilization of the flaps distally. C, Distal advancement of the flaps. D, The wounds are closed from distal to proximal.

Preparation of the digit is similar to that used in the Atasoy technique. The tip of the digit is debrided as necessary. Two triangular flaps are developed from the midlateral aspect of each side of the digit, and the incisions are made just down through the dermis. The subcutaneous tissue is similarly mobilized from the phalanx on a deep plane by dividing the fibrous septae. Both triangles are advanced to the midline distally and sutured together. The “V” apex of the triangle is closed in a “Y” fashion, and the surrounding edges and nail bed, or nail, are carefully sutured to the distal edges of the advanced flaps (see Fig. 49.6). This flap can only be advanced up to 3 to 4 mm. Haddad reported on a series of 20 patients treated with this technique and stated that slight skin necrosis occurred at the edges if the flaps were too large. Subjective evaluation in a series of 22 patients was reported by Freiberg and Manktelow, who noted mild hypersensitivity and numbness in 7 of 22 patients. Frandsen reported a follow-up study of 14 patients who underwent Kutler’s technique and found subjective complaints of coldness in 8 patients (57%), tenderness on percussion in 10 (71%), difficulty in grasping small objects in 6 (43%), and slight hypoesthesia or dysesthesia in 10 (71%) when objectively measured. Patients also reported an average time off work of 61 days.

Shepard reported excellent results with a modification of Kutler’s technique in 28 traumatic amputations and 9 reconstructive procedures by dividing the dorsal pedicle and advancing the flap 10 to 14 mm. The nerves, vessels, and subcutaneous tissues are preserved under the palmar skin incision. The dorsal incision is made down to bone and the fibrous septa divided. On the palmar side, the skin is incised only and the flap advanced distally. Shepard suggested that the Kutler technique is most applicable to oblique palmar and transversely oriented amputations.

Given the limited advancement of the Kutler lateral “V-Y” flaps, others have described modifications by either raising the triangular flap on its neurovascular pedicle or extending the flap to the PIP joint crease to increase its size and ability to advance distally. The flap could be advanced up to 2 cm, although patients were at risk of cold intolerance

(49%), numbness (78%), PIP joint flexion contractures (17%), and DIP joint flexion contractures (22%). These “V-Y” advancement flaps are at risk of necrosis if tension-free closure is not achieved. The tension placed on them at closure could cause a hook nail deformity as it pulls the nail bed palmarly and could also diminish sensation due to tension on the digital nerves. These problems can be overcome by allowing the proximal wound to heal by secondary intention.

#### Volar Flap Advancement

Moberg is credited with the technique of volar flap advancement for coverage of thumb tip amputations. The flap is a bipedicled, axialpattern, cutaneous advancement flap. Snow advocated the technique for fingertip amputations when length is to be maintained. Advancing volar skin on its neurovascular pedicle provides the dermis with sensibility without losing length. Arons has modified this with the addition of a terminal dermal graft for padding, and sometimes a skin graft may be added at the base of the digit to gain length.

Regional anesthesia is obtained, the limb is exsanguinated with an Esmarch bandage, and a pneumatic tourniquet is inflated. The distal tip is appropriately debrided, and midaxial incisions just dorsal to the flexion crease at each of the interphalangeal (IP) joints are made on both sides of the digit. The flap is completely separated from the underlying flexor tendon sheath, advanced to the distal tip, and sutured to the nail bed and the adjacent sides of the digit. The tip of the flap may need to be shaped to fit the distal defect.

This method is only appropriate for the thumb (Fig. 49.7) as the fingers lack the robust dorsal circulation compared with the thumb and the thumb is able to tolerate a mild IP flexion contracture without substantial morbidity. Flap advancement is limited to wounds measuring 1 cm in longitudinal dimension. Reported complications are fixed flexion deformity of the IP joints and skin necrosis on the dorsum of the finger. The studies of Caplan and coworkers, Leffert and associates,and Armenta and Lehrman suggest that the blood supply to the flexor tendon apparatus is compromised using this flap. Reports of tendon nutritional complications, however, are lacking.

#### Cross-Finger Pedicle Flap

The cross-finger pedicle flap technique, first described by Gurdin and Pangman in 1950, is indicated when other techniques for local flap coverage are not possible and maintenance of length is important.Examples include distal amputations of the index finger or thumb and amputations of multiple digits where remaining length is critical (Fig. 49.8). The flap, however, is associated with stiffness and is considered contraindicated in patients with preexisting arthritis or in patients over the age of 50 years. Paterson noted an increased incidence of stiffness in patients older than 41 years.

![[Fig.49.8.png]]
> **Fig. 49.8** Cross-finger flap for loss of palmar skin to index finger after crush injury to index and long fingers. A and B, Radiographs demonstrating index and middle finger tip injuries. C, Index finger tip skin loss and open wound. D to G, Radial based dorsal flap from middle finger to index finger for tip coverage with dorsal sensory nerve from ulnar/dorsal middle finger and coaptation to distal index radial digital nerve. The flap provides coverage for an oblique volar defect of the pulp region or for extensive soft tissue loss on the volar fingertip with exposed bone and tendon (see Fig. 49.8). A piece of an Esmarch bandage is used to template the defect and to design the flap. It is important to measure the defect or make a template rather than estimate the flap size. We will make the flap 1 to 2 mm larger than the defect to allow for tissue contracture and ensure tension-free insetting of the flap.

The donor site is outlined with a marking pen on the dorsum of the neighboring digit. The broad pedicle will be on the side adjacent the digit with the wound. With careful tissue handling, the flap is reflected with preservation of its venous drainage at the base of the flap. The dissection is carried down to the plane between the paratenon of the extensor mechanism and the subcutaneous fat. The dissection should not violate the paratenon to preserve a viable bed for skin grafting of the donor site. The three margins of the flap are sutured in place on the recipient site. The fourth margin is to be sutured at the time of flap detachment. A full-thickness skin graft from the ipsilateral upper extremity (i.e., hypothenar, antecubital, or medial forearm skin) is used to cover the defect on the extensor surface of the donor digit. Skin without hair is used to prevent later hair growth on the donor digit. To avoid undue tension on the vascular pedicle of the flap, the two fingers may be sewn together or a transdigital Kirschner wire can be placed. A bulky soft dressing reinforced with a plaster splint is applied. At 3 weeks postoperatively, there should be sufficient circulation from the recipient wound bed to the flap. However, if there is concern about division of the flap, a Penrose drain can be placed around the base of the donor digit to assess flap perfusion from the recipient finger. If adequate, the flap is detached and inset into the recipient site. Cohen and Cronin modified the technique to include a dorsal sensory branch of the digital nerve that is coapted to the recipient digital nerve at the time of flap division. They reported an average two-point discrimination of 4.8 mm in 88% of the patients in their series. The nerve can also be coapted at the time of flap coverage to provide sensation in the flap, which is our preference (see Fig. 49.8).

After detachment of the flap, motion of the digits is encouraged to mobilize all the adjacent joints. Follow-up studies by Sturman and Duran, Kleinert and associates, and Johnson and Iverson have indicated that the return of sensibility to cross-finger flaps as measured by two-point discrimination tests showed excellent flap reinnervation that demonstrated progressive improvement over time. Lassner and colleagues reported an average two-point sensibility of 3.6 mm and cold intolerance in 6 of their 15 patients using a bilaterally innervated sensory flap.

#### Thenar Flap

In 1926, Gatewood described the technique of a thenar flap for coverage of digital tip injuries with exposed bone. This was subsequently expanded on by Flatt in 1957 and modified by Smith and Albin with a technique that they describe as a thenar “H”- flap.

The indications for the random-pattern thenar flap are similar to those for a cross-finger flap, namely, when preservation of length is important and other techniques are not applicable. The flap has been advocated for use in the index and long fingers only and in younger patients without any preexisting arthritis or joint injury. The proposed advantages are (1) better skin color and texture match, (2) abundance of subcutaneous tissues, and (3) return of some sensibility. To avoid permanent joint stiffness or unsightly scarring in the donor area, one must keep in mind the three cardinal technical principles outlined by Melone and colleagues: (1) Design the flap near the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) crease of the thumb and avoid the midpalmar area, (2) fully flex the MCP joint with whatever amount of flexion is required in the IP joints of the recipient finger, and (3) detach the pedicle 10 to 14 days postoperatively and begin immediate active range-of-motion exercises.

**Thenar “H”- flap.** Fig. 49.9 shows the thenar “H”- flap as described by Smith and Albin. The area of contact of the injured digital tip with the thenar eminence is outlined. An “H” is drawn on the skin approximately 20% wider than the defect. The transverse limb of the incision is made at the most distal contact point of the fingertip with the thenar eminence (see Fig. 49.9A). Square proximal and distal flaps are elevated, including the subcutaneous tissue. The proximal flap is sutured to the fingertip, and the distal flap is sutured to the proximal margin of the defect on the volar side of the injured finger (see Fig. 49.9B). The proximal flap is advanced distally and the distal flap advanced proximally to close the donor defect. A soft dressing is applied, and 2 weeks later the flap is detached. The fingertip is closed with the proximal flap, and the distal flap is advanced into the thenar defect (see Fig. 49.9C). This closes the donor site primarily and avoids the potential problem of an unsightly scar in the thenar eminence.

An alternative technique is to elevate a proximally based flap and suture it to the injured tip similar to that just described but to use a full-thickness or split-thickness skin graft or Limberg-type flap for the thenar defect when the flap is divided and inset.

Satisfactory outcomes with the thenar flap have been reported in several clinical series. Porter compared the results of 56 pedicle flaps with 44 free grafts. He found sensibility was better in crossfinger and thenar flaps and that these flaps resulted in excellent cosmesis. Porter also concluded that full-thickness grafts were superior to split-thickness skin grafts. In terms of subjective and objective sensibility evaluation, of the five methods evaluated in his study, the results were superior with the thenar and cross-finger flaps. Thanik et al. used a combination of the thenar flap in combination with a bone and split thickness nail bed grafts to reconstruct bony, soft tissue, and nail bed injuries and reported the functional and cosmetic results in over 50 patients. Flexion contracture is a particular concern with the thenar flap as the IP joints of the finger must be flexed to a greater degree during the period of flap “take” than the other options discussed in this chapter.

#### Island Flaps

A number of elegant and ingeniously conceived island flaps have been described for coverage of fingertip amputations. These consist of flaps raised on their neurovascular pedicle, and advantages include avoiding prolonged digital immobilization, single-stage reconstruction permitting early rehabilitation, adding an independent blood supply to provide a good soft tissue bed for nerve grafting or repair, restoring a well-padded sensate digital pulp, offering the potential for a composite tissue transfer via wide selection of donor sites, and allowing a greater arc of rotation over longer distances. These include homodigital and heterodigital island flaps and can be anterogradely or retrogradely based. However, island flaps are more technically demanding and are associated with complications such as flap failure, joint contracture, and cold intolerance. However, the degree of joint contracture and stiffness is less than the other procedures as these flaps do not require immobilization of the digit in a flexed position. In addition, the homodigital flaps eliminate the morbidity of the neighboring digits. Therefore the use of these island flaps may offer decreased morbidity and improved outcomes in select cases.

#### Free Flaps

Free tissue transfer has been used to treat complex digital defects, including medial arm free flaps, anterolateral thigh flaps, wraparound toe flaps, and arterialized venous flaps. These can be complicated by their bulky appearance necessitating revision surgery, donor site morbidity, and unpredictable survival rates. A fasciocutaneous free flap based on the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery has been reported for medium-to-large volar soft tissue defects, including the fingertip region. The use of a free thenar flap has also been reported by Kamei and Sassu and associates. Coverage of complex digital defects using a proximal first dorsal metacarpal artery free flap was recently described in 10 patients. The proximal first dorsal metacarpal artery flap is particularly applicable when the defect is large or when scars or injuries prevent the use of a pedicled homodigital or heterodigital flap. Ulnar artery perforator free flaps have been described for managing complex digital defects because they are thin, have esthetic and textural characteristics similar to those of the finger, and tend to be free of hair. These can be either proximally or distally based. Zheng and colleagues described the use of a distal ulnar artery perforator bilobed free flap in the treatment of 15 patients with complex digital defects. Advantages include a cosmetic appearance similar to digital skin, donor and recipient sites within the same surgical field, preservation of the main trunk of the ulnar artery, similar size vessels to that of the proper digital artery, accompanying superficial veins, and sensation via the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm or dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve. The authors reported satisfactory outcomes in 14 of 15 patients (1 patient had partial flap necrosis) with good digit range of motion and two-point discrimination ranging from 6 to 11 mm.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: DIGITAL TIP AMPUTATION

We prefer to treat all digital tip injuries with minimal areas of exposed bone nonoperatively with dressing changes to prevent dessication. Ultrasonic MIST therapy is helpful in some cases to cleanse and gently debride the wound to facilitate healing by secondary intention. Educating the patient and the family with photographs from previous cases has proven to be invaluable. Pictures from similar case examples allow one to gain the trust of the patient and help the patient engage with therapy.

In single-digit injuries with a small protruding portion of the distal phalanx, we will trim the bone back and allow healing by secondary intention. Exposed bone is covered with available soft tissue or skin (if possible). The small “V-Y” flaps are very rarely indicated and are mostly of historical interest. They offer very little in terms of coverage, have associated morbidity, and compromise the functional, esthetic, and pain outcomes. For large defects and volar oblique wounds of the finger, we prefer a cross finger or homodigital island flap. For such wounds on the thumb, we prefer the first dorsal metacarpal artery flap as discussed in Chapter 44. We tend not to favor the thenar flap for concern of developing IP joint stiffness but may consider it in young patients with index or long fingertip amputations. If the defect is large, we will use a cross-finger flap. If the defect also involves a large portion of the volar tissues, we use a neurovascular island flap, although we have become more and more impressed with the results obtained with bilayer matrix wound dressings and have been increasing their use in our practice.

## AMPUTATIONS FROM THE DISTAL INTERPHALANGEAL TO THE METACARPOPHALANGEAL JOINT (EXCLUDING THE THUMB)

### Distal Interphalangeal Joint Disarticulation

Our preferred method of management for an amputation through the DIP joint or just distal with loss of flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) function is skeletal shortening and primary closure. We preserve the FDP attachment when possible to maintain some length, IP joint flexion, and pinch stability (Fig. 49.10). If the insertion of the FDP is not salvageable, we pull the tendon as far distal as possible and transect it under tension. Whitaker and colleagues presented experimental and clinical data suggesting that there was less inflammation if the articular surface of the middle phalanx was preserved. However, we believe that the shape and contour of the residual limb should be fashioned to resemble the normal distal phalangeal tuft. This is accomplished with a rongeur removing the volar and lateral condylar prominences. The digital nerves should be dissected and cut under tension to allow retraction away from the cutaneous scar to minimize symptomatic neuroma formation. The flexor and extensor tendons should be inspected and debrided of any contamination. The tendons should not be sutured to each other as this inevitably limits the excursion of both and compromises function of the remaining digits. Wang and associates reported on the outcomes of revision amputation at or distal to the DIP joint and noted that the average arc of motion of the PIP joint was 94 degrees, 24% of patients experienced cold intolerance, mean two-point discrimination was 5.6 mm, and return to work averaged 7 weeks after surgery.

#### Complications of Digital Amputation: The Lumbrical-Plus Finger

Distal release of the FDP tendon to the fingers may lead to a “lumbricalplus” deformity. As the FDP and its lumbrical move in a proximal direction, tension increases in the lumbrical tendon, which is an intrinsic extensor of the PIP joint. As active flexion of the digit is attempted, further proximal migration of the lumbrical may put sufficient tension along the lateral band to cause paradoxical extension of the PIP joint. Sectioning the lumbrical tendon allows the superficialis to regain control of the PIP joint and will alleviate the problem. In our experience, this lumbrical-plus deformity rarely occurs, and routine sectioning of the lumbrical is unnecessary at the time of the amputation.

### Amputation Through the Middle Phalanx

If amputation has occurred proximal to the DIP joint of the index finger, most patients will transfer pinching and picking up of small objects to the tip of the long finger (Fig. 49.11). Therefore efforts to preserve index finger length by flap coverage may be necessary. Preserving length in other digits may also be important in certain occupations such as a professional musician or stenographer who uses a computer keyboard. For injuries not amenable to preserving length, the bone should be shortened sufficiently to allow primary coverage with available skin without tension (Fig. 49.12). The flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) insertion should be preserved to allow the middle phalangeal segment to participate in grasping activities; however, PIP flexion is always limited when the middle phalanx is short. If amputation has occurred proximal to the superficialis insertion or if the tendon has been transected, active flexion of the middle phalanx will be absent. Preservation of the middle phalanx is more of a cosmetic issue and less of a functional consideration (Fig. 49.13). An exception to this rule is if toe transfer reconstruction is planned. In these cases, preservation of the base of the middle phalanx is critical to simplify the toe transfer and improve the functional outcome.

The amputation is designed as a “fish-mouth” incision with equal palmar and dorsal skin flaps (Fig. 49.14). The phalanx is cut using a reciprocating saw and the ends are smoothed with a rasp. The tourniquet is deflated, hemostasis is obtained, and the wounds are closed with a 4-0 absorbable suture. A soft dressing is applied and range-of-motion exercises are encouraged. Once the wounds have healed, edema control and desensitization are initiated.

#### Amputations Through the Proximal Interphalangeal Joint

Amputations through the PIP joint should be carried out in a manner similar to those through the DIP joint, without denuding of articular cartilage, shaping of the condyles of the proximal phalanx, and coverage with local skin.

#### Amputation Through the Proximal Phalanx

If an amputation has occurred proximal to the PIP joint, the remaining proximal segment is under motor control of the intrinsic muscles and the extensor digitorum communis. This provides active MCP joint flexion of approximately 45 degrees. The remaining segment will participate in gripping and maintaining small objects in the palm. To improve flexion at the MCP joint, tenodesis of the FDS tendon to the end of the proximal phalanx can be performed. Kirschner wire holes are made through the distal end of the proximal phalanx and the FDS tendon is sutured at its resting length to the phalanx, which provides almost 90 degrees of flexion with grasp (Figs. 49.15 and 49.16) (Case Study 49.1). O’Shaughnessy and Kakar reported the outcomes of FDS tenodesis in 12 digits in 8 patients and noted an average of 82-degree flexion arc of the affected MCP joint and an average grip strength of 70% of the contralateral extremity. If the amputation has occurred near or at the MCP joint level, particularly in the long and ring fingers, functional problems such as dropping small objects through the defect will result. Options include a prosthetic replacement or a ray amputation. If the amputation involves the index finger, immediate ray amputation should be considered.

## RAY AMPUTATIONS

A ray amputation involves excision of a digit from its metacarpal base. Indications include trauma, tumor resection, infection, failed replantation, debilitating Dupuytren disease, and macrodactyly. When considering a ray amputation, its effects on overall hand function need to be considered including the three functional units: (1) an opposable thumb, (2) the index and long fingers for pinch, and (3) the ring and small fingers for grip. Ray amputation can narrow the palm span by 10%, with a resultant decrease in grip strength. However, the narrower palm must be weighed against other impairments related to the space created by the missing digit, especially for the central digits, because small objects may fall through the space. In addition, loss of hand span can decrease hand width, which decreases forearm rotation, strength, and grip stability. Ray amputation may be indicated at the time of initial trauma, but in most instances, the surgery is performed as an elective procedure secondary to functional impairment or unremitting pain. In most injuries, the principle of spare parts surgery and saving all viable tissue should be followed. Tissue that may appear at first glance to be of little functional benefit may serve as a good source of material for subsequent reconstruction (e.g., bone graft). Additionally, patients may benefit from retaining length of the metacarpal for function or prosthetic fitting.

Peimer and colleagues conducted a retrospective review of 12 patients who underwent primary ray amputations (within 2 weeks) and 13 patients who had secondary ray amputations for either traumatic or oncologic reasons. They noted that patients who underwent primary ray resection had better strength and dexterity and required less time off work. On average, patients lost 28% of grip strength, 26% of opposition, and 13% of key pinch strength. The vast majority of patients, excluding those involved in workers’ compensation or litigation cases, were satisfied with the functional outcome, with most returning to the preinjury occupation. Melikyan and colleaguesreported on 20 patients at an average of 32 months following surgery. Grip strength decreased by 27% and three-point pinch by 22%. Patients who had an amputation of border digits recovered better than those who had amputation of the central digits, with the latter group having impaired manual dexterity.

Targeted-muscle reinnervation (TMR) is a technique whereby the amputated nerves are coapted to specific muscle motor branches in the residual limb. The technique was first described for proximal amputation at the transhumeral and shoulder disarticulation levels. TMR provides additional signals for prosthetic control. With experience, it was realized that TMR also decreases neuroma formation and can be used to treat them when painful. We prefer to perform TMR whenever possible for these reasons. For most hand amputations, certainly distal to the wrist, TMR is performed for neuroma prevention. At the wrist disarticulation level and proximal, TMR has the added benefit of additional myoelectric signals for prosthetic control. We believe that TMR will be the “standard of care” in patients in the near future. In the Authors’ Preference section below, we include TMR as well as other techniques for nerve management.

### Index Finger Ray Amputation

Murray and colleagues reviewed 41 patients who underwent index finger transmetacarpal amputation. The level of amputation was through the metacarpal head in 13 patients and proximal to the head in 28. They reported a 20% reduction in power grip, key pinch, and supination strength. Pronation strength, which is a measure of grasp stability, decreased by 50% owing to the narrowing of the palm. With loss of the index finger, the pronosupination fulcrum is decreased by 25%, resulting in a loss of stability and a mechanical disadvantage for forearm rotation (Fig. 49.17). There was no significant difference in strength between those who had proximal or distal amputations. Patients noted that the overall hand function had improved, despite the loss in strength, because of the ability to maintain thumb prehension. Cold intolerance was the most common persistent symptom (35 of 41 patients). Fifty-nine percent of patients had suboptimal results owing to hyperesthesia in the thumb/long finger web space. This was attributed to excessive mobilization of the index finger radial digital nerve during the amputation process and interfered with hand function in 37% of patients. Fisher and Goldner reported a similar complication in five patients and noted that it appeared 6 to 8 weeks after surgery. Although this problem may resolve with additional surgery, the symptoms may recur over time. In the series of Murray and associates, 34 patients underwent elective ray amputations. Twenty-one complained of pain in the residual limb before the transmetacarpal amputation, and only 3 experienced complete relief after the procedure. This information is important when advising a patient preoperatively about the possibility of persistent postoperative neuroma-like symptoms. Ay and Akinci described a technique of primary transposition of the index finger digital nerves into the interossei to decrease neuroma formation. After amputation of the digit, the radial and ulnar digital nerves are mobilized in a proximal direction and inserted within the interossei of the long finger. A modification of this technique would be to perform TMR of the residual digital nerve(s) to functionless intrinsic muscle branch(es).

In an effort to restore strength to the long finger, several tendon transfers have been described, including transfer of the first dorsal interosseous muscle to the second dorsal interosseous tendon or extensor hood, the extensor indicis proprius to the long finger extensor, or the FDS to the proximal phalanx of the long finger. Murray and associates found no differences in key pinch strength in patients who had or had not undergone transfer of the first to the second dorsal interosseous tendon. In 11 of 28 cases in which the second dorsal interosseous tendon was augmented by transfer of either the first dorsal interosseous tendon or one of the long flexor tendons of the index finger, there was an intrinsic-plus deformity. From these data, it would appear that procedures designed to augment the second dorsal interosseous tendon are unnecessary and may lead to additional complications.

Germann and colleagues reported on 120 patients with ray amputations (42 of the index finger). The majority of patients noted minimal functional deficits in performing activities of daily living. There was no significant loss of strength compared with patients who had undergone an amputation through the middle phalanx with respect to tip, key, and palmar pinch and sustained grip. Unlike those who had undergone digital amputation, 90% of patients with a ray amputation were happy with the cosmetic appearance. Similar results were reported by Melikyan and associates in an evaluation of ray amputations. Eight of the 20 patients underwent index finger ray amputation. Grip strength was decreased by a mean of 19% compared with the contralateral hand. Interestingly, 6 of 8 patients transferred manual dexterity precision skills that are commonly performed between the thumb and index finger to the long finger without difficulty.

Many cases of ray amputation are performed following trauma. Wilkins and colleagues studied the risk factors that led to secondary procedures in 114 patients aged 18 years or older who underwent immediate repair versus amputation of the injured index finger. Results showed that 44% of patients in the repair cohort compared with 21% of primary amputation patients required additional procedures. Those patients who underwent a primary ray amputation had more extensive injury of the index finger, and those that required a skin graft for wound coverage had a higher risk of secondary surgery due to infection, chronic pain, web space contracture, and skin graft complications.

Additional indications for ray amputation include tumors. Puhaindran and coworkers reported on the outcomes of single-ray amputation (7 of the index finger, 5 of the long finger, 6 of the ring finger, and 7 of the small finger) for tumors of the hand that included soft tissue malignant tumors, metastases, giant cell tumors of bone, and primary bone sarcomas. Ten patients underwent postoperative radiation treatment (2 for positive margins and 8 for close margins). None of the patients had local recurrences. Patients who received preoperative radiotherapy had a decrease in functional outcomes and grip strength that averaged 66% compared with the contralateral hand.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: INDEX FINGER RAY AMPUTATION

For trauma cases, transmetacarpal amputation is not performed in the acute setting unless there is injury proximal to the MCP joint.

In cases of multiple-digit injuries, we try to preserve as much finger length as possible. For ray amputation, regional anesthesia is preferred and a tourniquet is utilized. A proximally based “V” incision is fashioned on the volar surface at the midproximal phalangeal level of the index finger. Dorsally, this is joined by a “Y”- shaped incision with proximal extension over the index metacarpal (Fig. 49.18A). The longitudinal dorsal incision is made through the subcutaneous tissue, and the veins are ligated as necessary. If indicated, dorsal sensory branches of the superficial radial nerve should be divided under tension to allow proximal retraction. The extensor digitorum communis and extensor indicis proprius tendons are divided at the level of the second metacarpal base and reflected distally to expose the underlying periosteum. The dorsal periosteum is sharply incised, and a subperiosteal sleeve is raised. The index finger metacarpal is transected using a reciprocating saw distal to the insertion of the extensor carpi radialis longus (maintaining wrist strength) and flexor carpi radialis tendons with care taken to protect the underlying radial artery as it passes beneath the base of the metacarpal (see Fig. 49.18B). A rasp is used to address any rough edges of the transected metacarpal base. Dissection is carried around the distal circular portion of the incision. The first dorsal interosseous tendon and lumbrical muscle to the radial side of the index finger are divided. One next proceeds around the palmar aspect of the finger, and the neurovascular bundles to the index finger are identified. Care must be taken to identify and protect the radial digital nerve to the long finger and its accompanying digital artery. The vessels to the index finger are ligated and the digital nerves dissected distally into the middle phalangeal segment and divided. The flexor tendons are transected under tension to promote retraction into the palm. The tendon of the first palmar interosseous tendon is also divided.

The volar plate, deep transverse intermetacarpal ligament (DTIL), and proximal portion of the flexor tendon sheath are sharply incised, permitting removal of the amputated digit. The distal end of the digital nerves that have intentionally been transected in the finger are now carefully dissected proximally, with their connections with the palmar skin left undisturbed (see Fig. 49.18C). The distal ends are transposed proximally into the interosseous space between the first and second dorsal interossei to provide protection from external compression (see Fig. 49.18D). Alternatively, the nerves may be coapted or transferred to the first lumbrical and first dorsal interosseous motor branches as a TMR. The tourniquet is deflated, hemostasis is obtained, and the periosteal sleeve is closed. The skin flaps are trimmed, inset, and closed with interrupted sutures. A well-padded soft dressing is applied, and early motion is encouraged. Strengthening is started at 6 weeks following operation. Patients are followed to ensure an uneventful recovery as complications such as neuroma formation and carpal tunnel syndrome may occur. The latter may occur as a result of proximal migration of the transected flexor tendons into the carpal canal.

### Long Finger Ray Amputation

Given its central location, the long finger plays an important role in hand function, especially in lateral pinch, chuck pinch, and power grip. Any loss of length distal to the PIP joint can lead to small objects falling between the gap of the index finger and ring finger(Fig. 49.19) and a loss of three-point chuck pinch. Angular deformity of the index finger can occur when the amputation is at the level of the MCP joint. Key pinch strength is diminished because the origin of the adductor pollicis muscle is removed from the long finger metacarpal. One of the main complications following central ray deletion is scissoring of the adjacent index and ring fingers due to instability of the DTIL and altered intrinsic muscle function. Given this, two techniques of long finger ray amputation have been popularized, namely, repair or reconstruction of the DTIL and ray transposition.

### Long Finger Ray Amputation Without Transposition

Melikyan and colleagues reported on the outcomes of patients who underwent central ray amputation compared with those who underwent border digit ray amputation. Amputations were performed through the base of the metacarpal, and the DTIL was closed for central deletion cases. Compared with patients who underwent border digit deletion, those who underwent long finger ray amputation had weaker three-point pinch, decreased manual dexterity, and weakness in torque power and work output of up to 38% compared with the contralateral hand. The authors did not find any cases of gapping or scissoring by primarily repairing the DTIL (Fig. 49.20). One of the concerns with imbrication of the DTIL when the metacarpal base of the long finger is left in situ is the scissoring that may develop as the metacarpal heads of the index finger and ring finger are drawn together. This was highlighted by Steichen and Idler, who stated that the procedure may be better suited for ring finger ray amputation because in the ring finger, the metacarpal is thinner and gap closure is more readily achieved due to the mobility of the fifth carpometacarpal (CMC) joint. In the long finger, the metacarpal is wider and the index finger CMC joint stiffer. Nonetheless, in their report of 13 patients (4 of whom had long finger surgery and 9 of whom had ring finger surgery), satisfactory functional and cosmetic results were noted in all but 1 patient, who complained of continued palmar tenderness and narrowing of the palm. For cases with an absent or deficient DTIL, Gong and coworkers described a technique of reconstruction using a free tendon graft. Following metacarpal base osteotomy, a free tendon graft (either a graft from the amputated digit or an autograft) is looped around the index and ring finger metacarpal necks and sutured to itself and to the adjacent periosteum (Fig. 49.21). An alternative method of fixation would be to secure the graft with biotenodesis screws in the index finger and ring finger metacarpal necks.

Patients were satisfied with the functional and esthetic results. Additionally, Lyall and Elliot described a technique whereby the entire middle finger ray is amputated at the CMC joint without capitate osteotomy. Care must be taken to protect the underlying motor branch of the ulnar nerve. Subperiosteal dissection of the extensor carpi radialis brevis from the dorsal aspect of the long finger metacarpal base is necessary to preserve its function. Reconstruction of the hand involves closing the gap between the index finger and ring finger metacarpals while avoiding angulation and malrotation. From proximal to distal, sutures are placed between the periosteum/muscles of the index finger and ring finger metacarpals, followed by their respective sagittal bands and DTIL (having excised the palmar plate of the long finger MCP joint, which can act as a barrier to preventing closure of the dead space). Dorsal skin excision or dermadesis helps to further support the reconstruction. A palmar “V” flap of excess skin is excised.

The distal ends of the incision are carried over the proximal phalangeal skin to preserve sufficient skin for closure of the commissure between the index and ring fingers. This technical point is important when performing ray amputations of the middle two rays to preserve skin and prevent iatrogenic web contracture. Alternatively, a web-saving incision that avoids placing the scar in the new web space can be used (Fig. 49.23).

### Long Finger Ray Amputation With Transposition

Despite careful surgical technique, long finger ray resection with repair of the DTIL may still cause malrotation and scissoring. To avoid this problem, transposition of the index finger to the long finger has been advocated. The advantage of index finger ray transposition is that it provides immediate closure of the intervening space and allows the surgeon to control rotation at the osteotomy site.

Colen and coworkers reported on the functional outcomes after ray transposition for central digital loss. Eleven patients had an index finger transfer for long finger deletion and 8 had a small finger transposition for ring finger amputation. Total grip was 80% compared with the contralateral hand, with greater digit range of motion, pinch, and grip strength in those who underwent small finger to ring finger transfer than in those who underwent index finger to long finger transfer. Posner reported on a series of 14 patients who underwent 9 index finger and 5 small finger transpositions. In 13 of 14 patients, the stepcut osteotomy had healed by 9.5 weeks. There were no instances of malrotation, and all patients were happy with the prehension function and cosmetic result. Hanel and Lederman treated 10 patients with index finger to long finger ray transposition. Rigid internal fixation was used, and patients were mobilized immediately. The authors reported a union rate of 100%, no loss of range of motion, and no cases of malrotation. The average strength of grip and pinch was 65% and 78%, respectively, compared with the contralateral hand. The size of the web space in the reconstructed hand was within 2 mm of the uninjured hand. Six patients involved in workers’ compensation cases returned to the same occupation. Patients were uniformly pleased with the hand function and esthetic results. Carroll also reported his results in 20 cases in which the entire index metacarpal was transposed to the base of the capitate and an arthrodesis was performed between the base of the index metacarpal and the capitate. He noted no difference between this procedure and transposition to the base of the long finger metacarpal. Carroll preferred osteotomy at the index metacarpal base and osteosynthesis to the base of the third metacarpal.

Complications of ray transposition include malunion, nonunion (Fig. 49.24), and stiffness of the transposed digit related to extensor tendon adhesions at the osteotomy site. In addition, the transfer results in a large first web space and hinders precision pinch between the thumb and the index finger.

## RING FINGER RAY AMPUTATION

The ring finger plays an important role, forming the keystone of the palmar arch and participating in power grip. In contrast, the ring finger plays a minimal role in precision pinch and is considered by some to be the least important digit with respect to overall hand function.Ring finger ray resection, however, can have negative effects on hand function. Nuzumlali and colleagues compared patients who underwent amputation (through the proximal phalanx) with those who underwent ray resection for ring finger avulsion injuries at the PIP joint. Compared with the amputation cohort, the ray resection patients had substantially decreased key and chuck pinch strengths. In addition, central ray deletion can have a negative impact on manual dexterity.

As with the long finger, two techniques of ring finger ray amputation have been described, namely, performing the procedure with or without bony transposition.

### Ring Finger Ray Amputation Without Transposition

In the ring finger, unlike in the index finger and long finger, there is 15 to 30 degrees of mobility between the ring and small finger metacarpal bases and the hamate at the CMC joint. The ring finger and small metacarpal bases also share a common articulation with the hamate. It is therefore possible for the base of the fifth metacarpal to slide radially if the entire fourth metacarpal is excised. This possibility is enhanced by the lack of tendinous attachments to the fourth metacarpal base, unlike in the remaining metacarpals in the hand. This concept was demonstrated by Steichen and Idler, who reported on patients who underwent central ray resection without transposition. They noted that it was easier to close the intermetacarpal space between the long finger and small finger compared with the space between the index finger and ring finger after ray amputation. Cosmetic and functional results were satisfactory in 12 of 13 patients. Morgan and colleagues conducted a cadaveric study comparing suture button stabilization versus repair of the transverse intermetacarpal ligaments after ring finger ray amputation and noted that the suture button was able to provide a more stable gap that permitted earlier range of motion. Transmetacarpal ray resection of the ring finger can leave an open space between the long finger and small finger as the remaining metacarpal base cannot converge. This problem can be avoided by complete ray amputation, but complete deletion can lead to a loss of small finger abduction and possible scissoring.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: RING FINGER RAY AMPUTATION WITHOUT TRANSPOSITION

A racket-shaped incision is created distally over the proximal phalanx or its remnant (Fig. 49.27). A proximal longitudinal incision is made over the fourth metacarpal, continuous with the racket incision. Alternatively, a web-saving incision that avoids placing the scar in the new web space can be designed (see Fig. 49.23). The incision is carried down through the subcutaneous tissue, beginning dorsally. The extensor digitorum communis tendon to the ring finger ray is transected proximally, and the tendon is reflected distally. An incision is made through the periosteum around the metacarpal shaft. Subperiosteal dissection is performed, and the metacarpal base is identified at its articulation with the hamate. The dorsal CMC ligaments are incised and the CMC joint disarticulated assisted by palmar flexion of the metacarpal to allow division of the volar CMC ligaments. Care must be taken to avoid damage to the underlying ulnar nerve and artery. The metacarpal base is elevated and dissected from its underlying bed. The lumbrical, second volar, and fourth dorsal interosseous tendons are divided. Dissection is carried out around the volar aspect of the metacarpal. The digital vessels are identified and ligated. The digital nerves are addressed with traction neurectomy and muscle burial, coapted together or transferred to residual intrinsic motor branches as a TMR. The flexor tendons are sharply divided under traction. The DTIL is identified and transected close to the volar plate with careful preservation of the digital nerves. The palmar fascial attachments are incised, and the ray is removed.

### Ring Finger Ray Amputation With Transposition of the Fifth Metacarpal

There are advocates for transposition of the small finger to the ring finger avoiding the risks associated with convergence of the long finger and small finger, such as impaired mobility and objects falling through the space. Le Viet described a technique that involved complete ring finger ray resection, an intracarpal wedge-shaped osteotomy, and a capitohamate arthrodesis for small finger translocation (Fig. 49.30). He proposed four main advantages: (1) convergence between the small finger and long finger is avoided, (2) the integrity of the ulnar side of the hand is maintained, (3) the fifth CMC joint is preserved, and (4) the action of the interossei is unchanged. In a series of 17 patients, 14 were satisfied with preservation of motion at the CMC joint. The author acknowledged that there is a learning curve with this technique. Others have also reported favorable results with intracarpal osteotomy.Another concern of small finger transposition is that the finger will be shorter than the ring finger. To mitigate this issue, Posner described a step-cut osteotomy that adds height to the small finger during transposition to the ring finger metacarpal base. The small finger metacarpal is cut at a more proximal level than the ring finger metacarpal and can lengthen the small finger by 1.5 cm at the time of transposition (Fig. 49.31). This lessens the length discrepancy between the long finger and transposed small finger with improved hand function and cosmesis.

## SMALL FINGER RAY AMPUTATION

The small finger plays an important role in palmar grip because of the mobility of its CMC joint and the action of the hypothenar muscles.It also increases the span of the hand for grasp owing to its abduction moment. These two facts have led some to believe that the small finger has the most functional value after the thumb.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: SMALL FINGER RAY AMPUTATION

For small finger ray amputation (Fig. 49.33), a dorsal incision is fashioned over the small finger metacarpal and curved around the base of the proximal phalanx. Care is taken to ensure that the incision is not placed directly over the ulnar border of the hand to prevent scar sensitivity. After the dorsal skin is incised, care is taken to identify and protect the dorsal sensory branches of the ulnar nerve, which lies within the subcutaneous tissue. The extensor digitorum communis and extensor digiti minimi tendons to the small finger are transected proximally and reflected distally. Subperiosteal dissection is carried out around the base of the metacarpal, and an osteotomy is performed with a reciprocating saw at its metaphyseal flare, with care taken to protect the insertions of the extensor carpi ulnaris and flexor carpi ulnaris tendons (Fig. 49.34). The metacarpal is reflected distally, and the conjoined tendon of the abductor digiti minimi and flexor digiti minimi is divided distally, as well as the third volar interosseous and lumbrical tendons. Attention is next diverted to the palmar side. The digital vessels are ligated and the digital nerves divided. The nerves can be either transposed later into the interosseous space to protect them from direct trauma or sutured directly to each other in an end-to-end manner. Alternatively the digital nerves are coapted to residual intrinsic motor branches as a TMR. The extrinsic flexor tendons are transected under tension, and the DTIL is transected between the ring and small fingers. The hypothenar muscles are not sutured to the fourth interossei but are used to fill the remaining defect, providing satisfactory contour and cushioning to the ulnar side of the hand. It is not advisable to reattach the hypothenar muscles to the fourth dorsal interosseous because this is unnecessary and the results are unpredictable. The tourniquet is deflated, hemostasis is obtained, and the skin incision is closed. The hand is placed into a bulky dressing, and the patient is encouraged to begin early active range-of-motion exercises for all remaining digits.

CRITICAL POINTS: Ray Amputations
- Although single-ray amputation of the index, long, ring, or small finger affects the biomechanics of the hand, it does not result in substantial loss of hand function.
- An acute ray amputation following trauma should be performed sparingly as delayed ray resection can be performed to address functional or esthetic concerns.
- Ray amputations can improve the appearance of the hand.
- Ray resections without transposition avoid complications such as nonunion and minimize postoperative immobilization.
- Ray resections with transposition narrow the resultant open space and avoid complications such as malrotation leading to scissoring.
- Amputation of the index, long, and small fingers requires preservation of the metacarpal base to protect the insertion of the flexor and extensor tendons.

## MULTIPLE-DIGIT AMPUTATIONS

Amputations that involve multiple digits may occur following trauma, infection, hypothermia, or vascular injuries. The incidence is about 3 to 6 times greater in men than in women and is 4.9 times greater in lefthanded than in right-handed individuals. The basic principle is to preserve all viable tissue for possible reconstruction at a later date.Thumb amputation is associated with substantial disability because the thumb provides over 40% of the hand function. Thus valiant attempts are appropriate to replant or salvage the thumb. If the thumb is deemed nonreplantable, Soucacos and colleagues advocated transpositional digital microsurgery, in which the least damaged digit is replanted to the injured thumb. Preservation of a sensate thumb and another digit allows basic opposition pinch, and the addition of a third digit further improves chuck pinch, hook grip, and power grasp (Fig. 49.35).This concept was emphasized by Rose and Buncke when they performed selective replantation in four patients with multiple-finger amputations. The least traumatized fingers were replanted to the more ulnar metacarpal rays and the intervening metacarpal shafts were resected. This resulted in a wider hand span for grasp, improved precision handling, and better chuck pinch with the thumb. Additional digits may be added by microvascular toe transfer. Gordon and coworkers reported on double microsurgical toe transfers for reconstruction of the thumb and an ulnar digit. When the thumb was present, two-finger reconstructions were performed with bilateral second toe transfers. Patients reported improvement in hand function, with an average pinch of greater than 9 lb. None of the patients reported difficulties with activities of daily living, although the majority did experience symptoms of cold intolerance.

Partial hand amputations may be necessary for the treatment of malignant tumors (Fig. 49.36). The tumor surgeon must balance the importance of obtaining negative surgical margins, which is associated with improved survival rates, and the preservation of critical structures that are important for hand function. Puhaindran and colleagues reported on eight patients with soft tissue sarcomas of the hand who underwent partial hand resection followed by postoperative radiotherapy. Six of the eight patients were disease-free and had a mean Musculoskeletal Tumor Society score of 26 (range, 19 to 29). Patients who had undergone double-ray amputations (index finger and long finger or ring finger and small finger) had lower functional outcome scores. In their series of double-ray amputations, four of five patients with high-grade soft tissue sarcomas of the hand were disease-free with a mean grip strength of 24% compared with the contralateral hand. Aside from the weakness, patients maintained good key, tip, and tripod pinch when the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve was preserved.

## AMPUTATIONS THROUGH THE CARPUS

Amputations through the carpus require special consideration. Coverage may be possible with local or distant skin, but the intrinsic musculature is absent (Fig. 49.37) and the patient is incapable of pinching and grasping. Preservation of the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) and the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) maintains forearm rotation. A potential concern about keeping the carpus has been the added length to the arm following prosthetic fitting and has led some to advocate for transradial amputation as the treatment of choice. However, we believe this is no longer appropriate, especially in the acute setting. First, the hand prostheses are improving to allow for fitting at a distal level. Importantly, the “starfish” procedure, whereby the intrinsic muscles are salvaged and mobilized to a more superficial level to allow for electromyography (EMG) signal capture, permits a discrete signal from each interossei and the possibility for independent prosthetic finger motion with a myoelectric device. Second, additional length with native intact sensation may be preferential for some people depending on work and vocation. If the prosthetic is ultimately rejected, the additional length may provide improved body function. Third, if hand transplantation is considered, the added length may make a substantial difference in the functional outcome of the transplanted hand. Finally, at the time of the initial traumatic event, the future surgical and nonsurgical options are not yet defined. Therefore we strongly advocate for length preservation in the acute setting. If it is determined that shortening of the limb will provide some advantage, this can be performed in a delayed fashion to the ideal functional level.

## WRIST DISARTICULATION

Similar to transcarpal amputation, the main advantages of a wrist disarticulation is preservation of the DRUJ and forearm rotation. Preserving the length of the forearm and the metaphyseal flare of the distal radius aids in prosthetic suspension. Despite these advantages, prosthetists historically have argued that fitting may be more challenging than for transradial amputees because conventional prostheses may be too long, the prominence of the radial and ulnar styloids can lead to pressure necrosis, and myoelectric fitting is more difficult. This was highlighted by Wright and colleagues in 1995 in their review of prosthetic usage in patients with upper extremity amputations. The authors found a 94% utilization rate in patients with a below-elbow amputation compared with only 54% in those with a wrist disarticulation. However, one of the main reasons for the lower utilization rate could be the added length of the forearm in patients with a wrist disarticulation who can use the residual limb for object manipulation without use of the prosthesis. In addition, by maintaining sensibility, the limb may actually be more useful than an insensate prosthesis. Again, we do not advocate for early limb shortening for prosthetic reasons as articulated in the above section. Other factors linked to poor utilization of prosthetics include the type of device, late fitting, the cost of repairs, and the perceived limited functionality of the device.

With improvements in prosthetic technology, preoperative consultation with a prosthetist regarding the preferred level is worthwhile. Hand transplantation may be an option and should be a part of the decision as to whether to shorten the residual limb to accommodate a prosthesis as previously discussed. The decision to shorten the limb to a transradial amputation should be made later after other surgical options (e.g., hand transplantation or starfish procedure) have been declined and prosthetic fitting has proven too difficult.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: WRIST DISARTICULATION

The surgical approach varies depending on the indication for amputation and the status of the patient. In settings of compromised circulation with an unhealthy patient, the operation is simplified and nerves are managed with traction neurectomies to minimize surgical time. In contrast, in the trauma or tumor setting with a healthy patient, TMR is performed to minimize neuroma formation and provide additional myoelectric signals for prosthetic control. After satisfactory anesthesia, the limb is exsanguinated using an Esmarch wrap and the tourniquet inflated. If the surgical indication is infection or tumor, the arm is elevated and drained using gravity exsanguination and digital occlusion of the brachial artery. If possible, skin flaps are marked and raised at a level distal to the DRUJ such that the palmar flap is longer and the dorsal flap shorter for closure over the residual limb (Fig. 49.38). Once the skin has been incised, flaps are raised in a proximal direction and all veins ligated (Fig. 49.39). Our preference is to start on the dorsal surface and identify all the appropriate nerves, vessels, and tendons. The superficial branch of the radial nerve (SBRN) and the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve (DSUN) are dissected and divided under traction. Care is taken to ensure that they adequately retract in a proximal direction away from the amputation residual limb to minimize the chance of symptomatic neuroma. If there is viable, innervated muscle tissue from the hand available, this can be used for TMR in the residual limb for neuroma prevention and/or prosthetic control. If there is none available, TMR can be performed in the proximal forearm with coaptation of the amputated nerves to select motor branches of the no longer functioning finger and/or wrist flexors and extensors. The major median, ulnar, and radial nerves would be the most important options to consider; however, their sensory branches (SBRN, dorsal sensory ulnar nerve) or medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (MABC) and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (LABC) are also considerations. If the TMR is planned for prosthetic control, it is critical that the targeted muscle be otherwise completely denervated as the native innervation will overpower the nerve transfer. In addition, superficial muscles should be selected to improve surface capture of the myoelectric signals. The posterior interosseous neurovascular bundle is identified and divided using electrocautery. The extensor tendons are divided at their resting physiologic tension and fixed to the distal radius. This has two main purposes: (1) Contraction of the extensor muscles can be used to drive a myoelectric prosthesis, and (2) the tendons are readily accessible if the patient becomes a candidate for hand transplantation. Attention then focuses on the palmar surface, where the medial antebrachial cutaneous, median, and ulnar nerves and the anterior interosseous neurovascular bundles are divided in a similar manner. Again, TMR is performed unless contraindicated due to surgeon or patient factors. The radial, ulnar, and median arteries (when present) are doubly ligated proximal to the wrist. After transection of the vessels, flexor tendons are secured to the distal radius in a fashion similar to the extensor tendons. Wrist disarticulation is performed through a transverse dorsal incision of the radiocarpal joint that provides visual access to the radiocarpal joint. By identifying and protecting the distal radioulnar ligaments as well as the TFCC, the radiocarpal and ulnocarpal ligaments are divided, allowing amputation of the hand with the carpus. The prominent ulnar and radial styloid processes are rounded off with a rasp to prevent pressure necrosis at the wound edges. The skin flaps are fashioned to eliminate redundancy. The tourniquet is deflated, hemostasis is obtained, and the subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed in layers. A suction drain is used as necessary. A soft dressing is applied with compression in a distal-to-proximal direction to prevent edema. The patient is seen at 2 weeks following operation for suture removal and referral to a skilled upper extremity prosthetist for prosthetic fitting.

## HAND PROSTHETICS

Improvement of hand function after multiple-digital amputation can be provided by modern-day prosthetics. Early prosthetic fitting of the upper extremity amputee has many advocates. Biddiss and Chaunoted that when fitted within 6 months of an amputation, patients were 16 times more likely to continue using the prosthesis. In contrast to patients with lower extremity amputations, in whom vascular insufficiency and problems with wound healing are major concerns, most upper extremity amputees have excellent vascularity in the residual limbs and are much less prone to wound breakdown and other complications. The major advantage of the application of modified upper extremity prostheses in the early postsurgical period is the early performance of bimanual activities. In addition, early prosthetic fitting allows for patient education in prosthetic use during the woundhealing phase and hastens functional rehabilitation. Prosthetic fitting improves patients’ ability to perform many activities of daily living, such as hygiene and dressing, and enhances their self-esteem.

When deciding on the type of prosthesis, the use of a trial device can help determine whether it will meet the needs and expectations of the patient. Factored into the decision are variables such as age, gender, profession, patient motivation, and unilateral versus bilateral involvement. The level of amputation plays an important role in prosthetic fitting. The thumb, for example, affects 40% of hand function, and any type of device should afford length, stability, and opposition.Preserved sensation is an important determinant of thumb function because a partially amputated thumb with preserved sensory function may be more useful than one that is covered by an insensate prosthesis. In these cases, procedures such as distraction osteogenesis, web space deepening, pollicization, or microsurgical toe transfer may be more beneficial in providing a functional thumb.

### Types of Prostheses

There are four main types of partial hand prostheses: *passive devices, opposition posts, body-powered designs,* and *externally powered designs.* Passive devices are high-definition silicone prostheses that provide the most esthetically pleasing appearance of the hand and provide important psychological benefit. The color tone and skin creases are matched with those of the recipient’s hand (Fig. 49.40). A nail is applied to the digital prosthesis. The prosthesis can be designed to fit all levels of injury from the fingertip to a complete hand. In addition to improving the overall appearance of the hand, this passive prosthesis can protect areas of hypersensitivity and serve as a stable post with other functioning digits. These devices are primarily cosmetic and provide little in regard to functional benefit.

*Opposition posts* are used in patients who are missing a thumb but have all the other digits. They add length and can help hold objects with the remaining functioning digits. Most are rigid, but advancements in the development of articulating hinges have allowed the joints to be manually positioned for task-specific activities.

*Body-powered prostheses* use movement from a more proximal joint to drive distal function by means of a harness and cables. They claim improved sensory feedback from the cable system and tend to be relatively durable, yet they are limited with respect to their range of motion and prehensile strength. Traditional body-powered prostheses had a terminal hook voluntary opening or closing device (Fig. 49.41). There are newer devices and include the X-finger (Didrick Medical, Naples, FL) and M-finger (Liberating Technologies, Holliston, MA) (Fig. 49.42A and B). The latter uses a cable system where wrist flexion causes prosthetic finger flexion at the MCP and PIP joints (the DIP joint is fixed). For patients with partial digital amputation, power from the native MCP joint can drive prosthetic PIP joint flexion by means of a cable from the dorsal side of the MCP joint to the volar side of the prosthetic PIP joint. As such, this prosthesis allows for independent finger motion that improves hand dexterity.

With advancements in modern-day biomaterials and engineering, there have been considerable improvements in *externally powered prostheses* for the hand. The devices are lighter, more functional, and more durable than ever before. The prosthetic hands have mobile joints and individually powered fingers that can perform power gripping, tip and key pinching, and three-point chuck gripping. They currently fall into the main categories: *single-grasp, three-digit hands,* and *multigrasp anthropomorphic hands* (Fig. 49.43 and Tables 49.1 and 49.2). Although the former are often easier to control, lighter in weight, and more affordable than their more advanced counterparts, they are also more limited in function because they are able to form only a single, rigid grasp. Examples of the multigrasp anthropomorphic hands include the iLIMB (Touch Bionics, Mansfield, MA) and the Michelangelo hand (Ottobock, Minneapolis, MN). The iLIMB has a thumb and independently powered fingers. The thumb must be positioned manually to the required position. The Michelangelo hand is similar to the iLIMB prosthetic hand, except that it has its own independently powered thumb, obviating the need for manual positioning.

The Contineo MultiGrasp hand (Orthocare Innovations, Oklahoma City, OK) was developed in the Revolutionizing Prosthetics program of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency and offers powered thumb abduction and adduction. Stein and Walley compared patients who had undergone amputation of one upper extremity limb and were using a body-powered cable hook device, a myoelectric hand, and a normal remaining hand. Patients with the hook device tended to wear the prosthesis longer (14 hours versus 9.6 hours) and could complete functional tasks twice as fast as patients with a myoelectric hand. Despite these factors, patients with myoelectric devices preferred the function, range of motion, and esthetic appearance compared with the conventional body-powered prostheses.

Myoelectric fingers, such as the ProDigits (Optimus Prosthetics, Columbus, OH), Vincent fingers (Vincent Systems, Karlsruhe, Germany), and iLIMB (Fig. 49.44), have a power supply and a series of linked motors and struts that drive finger motion. For cosmesis, the myoelectric fingers can be covered with a material that resembles natural skin (Livingskin, Touch Bionics, Hilliard, OH). They can be driven by myoelectric activity via surface electrodes placed on the remaining musculature. These myoelectric control sites can be within the hand or forearm. Commonly used muscles of the hand include the thenar, hypothenar, and dorsal interossei; there is the advantage of allowing individual finger function independent of wrist motion.In the absence of a myoelectric signal, small switches can be incorporated within the device to activate the digit. Because each finger is self-contained, a variety of digital amputations can be accommodated. The device can provide up to 30 lb of grip strength, compared with 8 lb with the body-powered devices. Despite these advantages, the motors and batteries add weight and cost to the device. To improve the ability of the prosthesis to perform the complexity of hand movements, the number of myoelectric sensor points needs to increase.To accomplish this goal, TMR (as described previously) can be performed. The nerve transfer provides additional signals, whereby the recipient muscle acts as a biologic amplifier of the transferred nerve. For example, in a transradial amputation, the ulnar nerve can be transferred to the flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) motor nerve. When the patient attempts to fire intrinsic hand muscles, the FCU muscle will contract. The prosthetic can then capture this signal with a surface electrode. The patient will thereby have an additional myoelectric signal with improved intuition for prosthetic hand control. In this situation, the distal median nerve can undergo TMR and be utilized for additional prosthetic control. The TMR will also decrease the neuroma formation. The distal sensory radial nerve can undergo TMR for neuroma prevention.

Another area of research for prosthetic control has focused on the use of direct brain wave control. Cerebral control of a prosthetic device has the major advantage of being independent of the peripheral nervous system within the residual limb, which may be damaged or nonfunctioning. Da Paz and Braga used functional magnetic resonance images of the brain to study the relationship between cerebral activation and control of a myoelectric prosthetic hand. They noted similar activation in the areas of the brain that controlled prosthetic and contralateral hand function, suggesting that it may be possible to control myoelectric devices through cortical regulation.

Hopper and colleagues studied the use of digital prostheses in workers’ compensation cases and noted that 28% of patients did not use or seldom used the prosthesis (low use) and only 24% used it for more than 4 hours a day (high use). Those in the former group included male patients, patients with residual limb complications such as cold intolerance, patients with hypersensitivity, and patients with amputations at the more distal digital level. Those with a ring finger or thumb prosthesis reported the least use. Patients who tended to wear the prosthesis were workers who did not do manual labor and had either an index finger or a small finger amputation. Women were more likely to be long-term users of the prosthesis. Lifchez and coworkers noticed an improvement in hand function in patients with multiple-digit amputations who used their prostheses. Ten patients who had undergone amputation of two or more fingers proximal to the DIP joint were given a series of hand function tests. Patients reported using their prostheses on an average of 4.3 days a week for over 7 hours a day. Five of the nine employed patients returned to their preinjury occupations. When patients used the device, they had increased tip pinch, lateral pinch, and grip strength including better function in activities of daily living. Burger and colleagues investigated whether patients with a partial hand amputation were able to return to their previous occupations and whether they used their silicone prostheses. The index finger was most commonly affected, and most fingers were amputated through the middle phalanx or PIP joint. Less than 50% of patients were able to return to the same job. Those involved in nonmanual labor and those who had undergone amputation of only one finger had a better chance of keeping the same job. None of the patients with amputation of three or more fingers were able to keep their previous jobs. The greatest loss and functional impairment were caused by partial or complete thumb amputation. Less than a third of patients used the prosthesis at work and only wore the prosthesis an average of 5.3 hours a day (women tended to wear it longer than men, for 7 hours versus 3.5 hours). Loss of tip sensibility while using the prosthesis was the main impediment for use at work.

## REHABILITATION PROCESS OF THE HAND AMPUTEE

Successful rehabilitation of the amputee requires the input of a comprehensive multidisciplinary team. The members of the team should include the surgeon to provide surgical expertise, occupational therapist to provide rehabilitation, prosthetist to fabricate upper extremity devices, psychiatrist for psychological support, and pain specialists to manage the pain. Equally as important are other upper extremity amputees, who can provide camaraderie and understanding. A support network of amputees is vitally important for the recently injured patient undergoing various psychological stressors that can lead to low self-esteem, disability, denial, and a low rate of prosthetic use.The Amputee Coalition of America provides important information and support.

The rehabilitation process has four stages that may overlap to ensure maximum recovery of the patient. These include the *postsurgical stage, preprosthetic training stage, basic prosthetic stage,* and *advanced functional training stage.* The *presurgical stage* could be considered an additional stage. Whenever possible, we have our patients meet with an occupational therapist and prosthetist to educate the patient, prepare them mentally and emotionally, and set expectations. The goal is to provide the patient with the necessary physical and psychological skills to meet functional goals and requirements. Many of these principles have been derived from past experiences gained from our military servicemembers in recent conflicts.

The *acute postsurgical phase* lasts from the time of surgery until all wounds have healed. During this period, emphasis is placed on achieving a stable wound, edema control, desensitization, scar management, and pain control. Depending on the nature of the wound, serial debridement may be needed if the wound has been heavily contaminated or has substantial tissue damage. Once the wound has been definitively closed, occupational therapists focus on controlling edema by using distal-to-proximal compression wrapping followed by silicone sleeves that provide continuous compression that also aid in minimizing scar formation. These dressings also help with the desensitization process, which advances to massage and the use of texture devices as the scar matures. During this time, the therapist also works on the range of motion of adjacent joints to minimize the development of joint contractures. Training in activities of daily living to promote independence, such as feeding and hygiene, is encouraged by means of custom adaptive aids as needed. During this period, patients can have residual or phantom limb pain. In a study by Jensen and colleagues, 57% of patients had residual limb pain 8 days after surgery that lasted up to 2 years in 21% of patients. Involvement of a pain specialist may be necessary if symptoms do not resolve within the first few weeks after surgery. Within the immediate postoperative period, a patient-controlled analgesia pump can be used or an indwelling catheter can be placed. As medications are weaned, the patient can be given long-acting opioids and then transitioned to short-acting medications, such as short-acting opioids, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory medications, anticonvulsants, or tricyclic antidepressants. Other modalities such as transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and thermal treatments can improve pain control because they have a synergistic effect with other pharmacologic and psychological interventions.

The provision of psychological support, through work with trained psychologists and psychiatrists as well as family members, is started early in the rehabilitation process to ease patient fears and promote independence. These issues are gaining increased scrutiny because early psychological therapeutic regimens can reduce medical bills, disability payments, loss of income, and mental health costs. Psychological stress can be triggered by the sudden and unexpected nature of the injury, the alien hospital environment, fear of loss of bodily function, helplessness, and humiliation. Early in the recovery process, many patients experience symptoms suggestive of posttraumatic stress disorder. Grunert and colleagues showed that up to 94% of patients suffered from flashbacks, nightmares, poor concentration, cosmetic concerns, and the fear of death within 2 months of their injuries. These symptoms can persist for up to 6 months after injury. Therefore, patients, as well as family members, require psychological support early and throughout the rehabilitation. The hand surgeon plays an important role in the adjustment phase following injury as he or she is often the first health care professional involved with the patient. A positive preoperative and postoperative relationship with the surgeon can result in improved patient compliance, satisfaction, and recovery.

Psychological adjustment during recovery from devastating hand injuries has three interlinked phases. *Functional acceptance* is the process whereby the patient comes to terms with his or her altered form. This is dependent on several factors, such as the patient’s motivation, nature of the injury, and type of functional disability. The next phase is acceptance of the *esthetic appearance* of the hand and is affected by age, gender, underlying personality, and perception of how others see the hand. Early involvement with prosthetists can be important in improving the patient’s cosmetic concerns following a mutilating hand injury. The final stage of adjustment is related to the *emotional acceptance of the injury.* This is characterized as the time necessary for the patient to accept the loss of a finger, hand, or other body part. This stage has a variable length related to numerous prognostic factors such as occupation, personal and family relationships, feeling of self-guilt as a cause of the injury, previous history of mental health issues, whether the injury is work-related, severity of the injury, and whether there has been early or delayed psychological therapeutic intervention.

Therapeutic modalities include psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy such as tricyclic antidepressants, monoamine oxidase, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. The former includes cognitive behavioral therapy (e.g., systematic desensitization, graded exposure) and management of life skills, such as relaxation therapy, anger control, and cognitive restructuring to address pain, anxiety, and depression.Weis and colleagues investigated the efficacy of imaginal exposure, a form of cognitive behavioral therapy that asks the patient to imagine the feared situation, when given early (within 30 to 60 days) or later (after more than 120 days) following a severe work-related injury. The authors noted that the therapy had a beneficial effect on symptom reduction in the early treatment group so that fewer therapeutic sessions were required than in the group for whom treatment was delayed. Eighty-three percent of patients were able to return to work after 6 months. Despite receiving psychological therapy, the remaining 17% who did not return to work still cited psychological distress rather than physical disability as the main reason for not returning.

During the 2 to 3 weeks after definitive surgical management, the *preprosthetic phase* occurs, when time is spent readying the patient to accept a prosthesis. This coincides with the “golden window” for upper limb prosthetic fitting during the first 30 days after amputation.Forty-seven patients underwent immediate, early, or late postoperative fitting after upper extremity amputation. Those fitted within 30 days of amputation had a 93% rehabilitation success rate and a 100% return to work rate within 4 months after injury compared with a successful rehabilitation rate of only 42% and a return to work rate of only 15% in those fitted with a prosthesis after 30 days from injury. During this period, changes in limb volume and sensitivity will occur, and the occupational therapist will address range of motion and physical conditioning of the injured limb. Psychological support is ongoing and adapted to meet the changing demands of the patient. This may include interaction with other amputee patients as well as participation in occupation-based activities. Change of hand dominance training may be introduced in patients with substantial mutilating dominant hand trauma. Limitations of fine motor skills with the available prosthetics require the patient to learn to use the contralateral uninjured hand independently.

If a patient is to be fitted with a myoelectric prosthesis, the patient begins electrode site identification and training. The goals during this phase are to teach the patient how to effectively contract the residual limb muscles to activate a basic myoelectric device. In general, forearm flexors control closing and pronation of the prosthesis, and extensors control its supination and opening movements.

Patients gradually advance into the *prosthetic training stage* of rehabilitation. The goal during this phase is to ensure that the prosthesis becomes an integral part of the patient’s life as the patient learns the actions required for prosthetic control and incorporates the device into activities of daily living. Each patient trains with a variety of prostheses to determine the best fit. A patient often chooses different prostheses to match particular tasks at hand. Patients are also taught principles of skin care to avoid breakdown.

The final stage of the rehabilitation process is the *advanced prosthetic training stage.* Patients learn efficient prosthetic use as therapy is tailored to meet specific needs. They are challenged through a series of tasks that require precise motor control, such as using a tool, musical instrument, or cooking utensil. This training enhances knowledge about the prosthetic components, limb care, and how to don and doff the prosthesis. Furthermore, postural and strengthening programs emphasizing educational awareness of altered body patterns will prevent overuse injuries. This process is highly individualized and is based on several factors including but not limited to the level of amputation, patient desires, patient abilities, level of prosthetic expertise, pain/discomfort, and insurance approval. It is often the case that more than one prosthetic is appropriate for the patient based on their individual needs and lifestyle. Advanced training with a therapist and prosthetist guides the patient to facilitate appropriate selection via operation and control of the prosthesis to engage in complex tasks. It is important to consider the prosthetic as a tool. The prosthetic (tool) allows the patient to perform certain tasks that would otherwise be more difficult or impossible. There is no replacement for lost parts.

## PAIN MANAGEMENT AFTER AMPUTATIONS

Despite the surgeon’s expertise, 7% to 49% of patients will develop residual pain in the limb after an upper extremity amputation. This pain can have a deleterious effect on recovery and function, including causing difficulties with prosthetic training, depression, reduced likelihood of employment, and overall disability. The two types of pain commonly seen after an upper extremity amputation are phantom limb pain and residual limb pain. *Phantom limb pain* is pain felt in the amputated limb, whereas *residual limb pain* is pain felt in the remaining proximal body part. After an amputation, patients may develop the nonpainful phenomenon of *phantom limb sensation* because they feel that the amputated part is still present. The mechanism for this bothersome phenomenon is ill-defined. Phantom limb pain typically resolves over time, except in major limb loss.

An early approach to pain management may prevent long-term impairment of function. The treating surgeon should begin pain management immediately with either postoperative patient-controlled analgesia or long-acting opioids. Involvement of a pain specialist is required to facilitate the prescribing of short-acting narcotics and anticonvulsants. Nonpharmacologic therapies such as thermal treatments, massage, or TENS are synergistic to pharmacologic measures. Psychological support is equally as important to address any developing issues such as low self-esteem, concerns about disability, and denial.

Desensitization is recognized as an effective way to decrease pain following amputation. The physiologic basis for dysesthesias and their response to desensitization techniques is not entirely understood. Sica and associates demonstrated that the amplitude of the cortical-evoked response of the contralateral somatosensory cortex was diminished in patients with partial limb amputations. Interestingly, they found an increase in amplitude of the evoked response in the proper area corresponding to the uninjured limb on the same side as the partial amputation. These data suggest that the cerebral cortex adapts when its peripheral afferents are interrupted. Exactly how this information relates to dysesthesias after amputation is unclear. Empirically, desensitization techniques appear effective because of this adaptability of the central nervous system to repetitive stimuli. Several techniques of desensitization have been suggested, but most are focused on an early return to functional activities as advocated by

Fisher and Boswick or intensive supervised programs of progressive resensitization to textures and objects. On many occasions, patients are unable to visit regularly with a hand therapist. Given this dilemma, it is important to provide a home program of desensitization. Patients and their family members are shown how to rub and tap the fingertip against various smooth and rough surfaces, such as sandpaper and cloth, for a minimum of 6 weeks. The patient is educated on the importance of participating in the home program and routine visits with a hand therapist to prevent the functional consequences and limitations of the hypersensitive part.

Optimizing the care of the patient with a major limb loss requires a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach, including the help of a pain management specialist. Residual limb pain and phantom limb pain occur in 56% to 85% of amputees and have a considerably negative impact on long-term functional outcomes. Hanley and colleagues reported on the incidence of chronic pain in 104 patients with upper limb amputations at 6 months after surgery. They noted residual limb pain in 71% and phantom limb pain in 79% of their patients. Interestingly, the patients also complained of pain in the back (52%), neck (43%), and uninjured contralateral arm (33%). Men reported a greater amount of neck, phantom limb, and residual limb pain, and those who used a prosthesis were significantly more likely to have phantom limb pain (p < 0.05). Patients who complained of pain in the nonamputated limb reported a high level of disability and days off work. Overall, 90% of patients experienced at least one type of postamputation pain. This study serves as an important reminder to the surgeon to actively address these issues with the help of a multidisciplinary team.

Phantom limb pain remains a persistent and poorly understood problem that is extremely difficult to treat. Montoya and colleaguesstudied cortical regulation in healthy controls, patients with a congenital upper limb amputation, and traumatic amputees. The authors noted that traumatic amputees who had symptoms of phantom limb pain demonstrated reorganization of their sensory and motor cortical maps compared with the other patients. Li and associates noted that digital amputation caused changes with synaptic transmission within the anterior cingulate cortex, a region that relays pain within the central nervous system, as possibly contributing to phantom limb pain. Jensen and colleagues noted that the presence of preoperative limb pain plays a role in the development of phantom limb pain immediately after surgery. Fifty-eight patients who primarily underwent elective lower limb amputations for peripheral vascular disease were interviewed preoperatively and postoperatively about their residual limb and phantom limb pain. The incidence of residual limb pain was 57% at 8 days, 22% at 6 months, and 21% by 2 years following amputation. The incidence of phantom limb pain was much higher (72% at 8 days, 65% at 6 months, and 50% at 2 years) in patients who had preoperative pain. Lambert and coworkers conducted a prospective randomized trial comparing preoperative epidural medications (bupivacaine and diamorphine) with intraoperative perineural analgesia (bupivacaine) of 30 patients undergoing lower limb amputations. The authors noted that at 3 days following amputation, there was a significant reduction in residual limb pain in the epidural group but no real differences in phantom limb pain with either regimen. Aggressive pain management with multimodal pharmacologic management in conjunction with integrative and alternative measures such as biofeedback, hypnosis, relaxation techniques, or acupuncture may contribute to a successful outcome.

### Neuromas

A peripheral nerve injured during amputation will attempt to regenerate. Regeneration is initiated by axonal growth (sprouting) from the proximal nerve end. There are a host of neurotrophic factors that are released to stimulate the repair process, including substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and fibroblast growth factors. Because there is no end organ present, a neuroma will form (Fig. 49.46). Even when repair is possible, some regenerating axons can grow outside the repair site and become symptomatic. The overall incidence of neuroma formation following amputation varies from 2.7% to 30%.Most neuromas are asymptomatic. Only 3% to 5% become symptomatic; however, they can be frustrating for the patient and the surgeon, resulting in dissatisfaction and disability.

Patients tend to present with a history of trauma from a sharp, crushing, or stretch injury. Symptoms include hypersensitivity and an aching or throbbing pain. The pain may or may not localize to a single peripheral nerve distribution. The diagnosis is made clinically, with patients usually having a positive Tinel sign over a localized site yielding lancinating pain. Selective local anesthetic injections can be used to facilitate the diagnosis. Complete symptom relief after the injection will help the patient to realize the area of numbness that will develop after neuroma excision, transposition, or grafting. Initial management requires nonoperative measures, such as scar massage, desensitization, and TENS. Oral medications, including opioids, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory medications, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, and topical analgesics, have had varied success. There are many surgical techniques for symptomatic neuroma, and they should be considered in patients refractory to nonoperative treatment who have had a positive response to a local anesthetic injection and symptom resolution. In patients whose pain is not aligned with the anatomic distribution of a nerve, caution is necessary when considering surgery because these patients may have secondary gain issues and/or underlying psychological condition. Surgical treatment of neuromas is centered around four main options: (1) neuroma resection; (2) primary microsurgical endto-end coaptation or coaptation using a vein graft or neural tube; (3)

containment of the neuroma, including translocation into muscle or bone (Table 49.3); and (4) nerve translocation.

### CASE STUDY 49.1 Multiple Traumatic Digit Amputation Treated With Revision Amputation and FDS Tenodesis

A 15-year-old right hand dominant boy sustained a table saw injury to his left hand at school. Examination demonstrated dorsal degloving injuries to the index, long, and ring fingers through the proximal phalanges, with bone loss (eFig. 49.1A to D).

### CASE STUDY 49.2 Traumatic Amputation of Bilateral Hands

A 3-year-old girl was run over by a lawnmower, sustaining traumatic amputations of bilateral hands (eFig. 49.2A-J). The left thumb, index, and middle finger rays were completely amputated at the carpus. The right hand was amputated at the radiocarpal joint. The amputated parts were brought in piecemeal (see eFig. 49.2F). The largest and most usable part was the index, middle, and ring fingers from the right hand. The middle finger was used to reconstruct the left thumb, and the index and ring fingers were filleted to provide skin coverage and web space reconstruction. The "thumb" was positioned in opposition and despite a lack of intrinsic thumb muscles proved to be quite valuable for hand function that was present 6 weeks from injury (see eFig. 49.2K to M). The right-sided amputation was closed with residual skin and skin grafting. Preservation of the length allows for this extremity to be used as a helper without the need for a prosthesis (see eFig. 49.2M). Length preservation is important for all patients, especially in children as the residual limb can be incorporated into function. eFig. 49.2P to Q show the appearance of the left hand at 3 months, but subsequently the patient was lost to follow-up.
