In the early 19th century, well before microsurgical equipment and techniques were developed, surgeons published encouraging results of brachial plexus reconstruction. Subsequently, others reported poor results and high complication rates. Little else was published on reconstruction of the brachial plexus until the 1970s and 1980s, when surgeons began to apply the fascicular nerve grafting techniques described by Millesi to birth and traumatic injuries of the brachial plexus. These pioneers established indications and techniques for plexus reconstruction and reported better recovery in operated patients than in those observed without surgical reconstruction.

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, exciting diagnostic and microsurgical advances further advanced the field. New surgical procedures and experimental nonoperative treatments were introduced internationally. In the late 1990s, aggressive reconstructions using extraplexal sources for reinnervation of vascularized muscle transfers were reported. In the past 20 years, the role of nerve transfers has expanded. The introduction of novel distal nerve transfers has changed the way in which brachial plexus surgery is being performed. Although full recovery of function after brachial plexus reconstruction still remains unachievable, the exciting developments of the past two decades have considerably improved surgical outcomes, and future directions for research are promising.

### SURGICAL ANATOMY

The brachial plexus is formed from five cervical spinal nerves, typically C5, C6, C7, C8, and T1 (Fig. 34.1), and is generally divided into five anatomic sections: nerves, trunks, divisions, cords, and terminal branches. The spinal nerves are formed by coalescence of the ventral and dorsal nerve rootlets as they pass through the spinal foramen (Fig. 34.2). The dorsal root ganglion holds the cell bodies of the sensory nerves and lies within the confines of the spinal canal and foramen. Each spinal nerve sends a small dorsal ramus to the paraspinal musculature. The remaining large ventral rami of the lower four cervical (C5- C8) and T1 spinal nerves emerge from the foramina as the first section of the brachial plexus.

The C5 and C6 and the C8 and T1 nerves merge and form the upper and lower trunks, respectively. C7 continues distally as the middle trunk, with no clear demarcation point for this change in nomenclature. The point of convergence of the C5 and C6 nerves, known as Erb’s point, marks the spot at which the suprascapular nerve emerges. Nerves and trunks are located above the clavicle and collectively form the *supraclavicular plexus.* Each trunk then divides beneath (dorsal to) the clavicle into anterior and posterior divisions (the *retroclavicular* *plexus* ). The posterior divisions from all trunks merge to become the posterior cord, and the anterior divisions of the upper and middle trunks merge to form the lateral cord. The anterior division from the lower trunk continues distal to the clavicle as the medial cord. The lateral cord splits into two terminal branches, the musculocutaneous nerve and the lateral cord contribution to the median nerve. The posterior cord forms the axillary nerve and the radial nerve, and the medial cord gives off the medial cord contribution to the median nerve, as well as the ulnar nerve. The cords and terminal branches arising from them collectively make up the *infraclavicular plexus.*

Additional branches of clinical importance at the proximal nerve level include a C5 contribution to the phrenic nerve, the dorsal scapular nerve (C5), and the long thoracic nerve, with contributions from C5, C6, and C7. The only trunk branches are the suprascapular nerve and the nerve to the subclavius muscle, both from the upper trunk. The lateral pectoral nerve arises at the division level in the retroclavicular plexus. In the infraclavicular region, the posterior cord gives off branches (proximal to distal) that include the upper subscapular nerve, the thoracodorsal nerve, and the lower subscapular nerve; the medial cord gives off the medial pectoral nerve, the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve, and the medial brachial cutaneous nerve.

## CLASSIFICATION OF NERVE INJURIES

Nerve injuries are generally classified according to the Seddon and Sunderland classifications. Both classifications overlap; Seddon classified nerve injuries into three different categories: neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis; Sunderland expanded Seddon’s axonotmetic category, for a total of five degrees of nerve injury: firstdegree (neurapraxia), second- to fourth- degree (axonotmesis), and fifth- degree (neurotmesis) injuries. Basic knowledge of the different degrees of nerve injury is necessary to understand the need for observation in some patients and for surgical intervention in others. (See also Chapter 30.)

In neurapraxia (first- degree injury), there is a focal conduction block at the site of injury. Wallerian degeneration does not occur, and as the conduction block resolves, neurologic function returns. These injuries do not require surgical intervention, and recovery occurs within hours to weeks. In axonotmesis (second- to fourth- degree injury), the axons are disrupted and Wallerian degeneration occurs distal to the injury. However, the nerve remains in gross continuity. Stretch injury is the most common mechanism. Depending on the severity of the injury, regeneration may occur (at a rate of 1 mm/day or 1 inch/month) or not. To better describe axonotmetic lesions, Sunderland further classified them into second- degree injury (axon disrupted, but intact endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium), third- degree injury (axon and endoneurium disrupted, but intact perineurium and epineurium), and fourth- degree injury (axon, endoneurium, and perineurium disrupted, but intact epineurium). In second- degree injury, regeneration and neurologic recovery are expected but will require several months or even years. In very proximal injuries with distal targets (such as the proximal ulnar nerve or lower trunk lesions), muscle atrophy or motor endplate degeneration may occur before regeneration is completed and prevent functional recovery. In third- and fourth- degree injury, regeneration may occur to a variable degree or may not occur at all, and these lesions may require surgical intervention. Finally, in neurotmesis (fifth- degree injury), the nerve is completely disrupted and there is no continuity. These lesions cannot spontaneously recover and surgical intervention is necessary. In brachial plexus injuries, neurotmetic lesions include postganglionic ruptures and preganglionic avulsions.

## MECHANISMS OF TRAUMATIC BRACHIAL PLEXUS INJURY

### Traction Injury

Most traumatic injuries of the brachial plexus are due to traction or stretch injury. These brachial plexus injuries are caused by varying amounts of energy imparted to the plexus that may result in different injuries ranging from mild stretch to rupture or avulsion (see Fig. 34.2). Low- energy traction trauma, not severe enough to cause rupture or avulsion, typically causes injuries with a potential for spontaneous regeneration, such as neurapraxia (Sunderland type I) or lower degrees of axonotmesis (Sunderland types II and III). Experimental evidence also suggests that low- energy injuries may interfere with brachial plexus microcirculation and cause ischemic injury. High- energy injuries are associated with more significant damage to the plexus that may include rupture of plexal elements (Sunderland type V) or avulsion of nerve roots from the spinal cord. When a combination of incomplete rupture and avulsion occurs, some spontaneous recovery may take place, but early microneurosurgical reconstruction generally improves the functional outcome. If the shoulder- neck angle is forcibly widened by downward traction on the arm, damage is imparted first to the upper spinal nerves (C5 and C6)/upper trunk and/or C7/middle trunk (Fig. 34.3); if the scapulohumeral angle is forcibly widened, damage is imparted first to the lower nerves (C8 and T1) and the lower trunk. If the impact is extreme, all levels can sustain damage. Anatomic studies have determined that the supporting tissue anchoring the upper nerves (C5 and C6) to the vertebral foramina is significantly stronger than the supporting tissue of the lower nerves (C8 and T1). This anatomic arrangement predicts that the more caudal structures of the brachial plexus (i.e., the C8- T1 nerves) are more likely to be avulsed from the spinal cord, whereas the more cranial structures of the brachial plexus are more likely to stretch or rupture after exiting the neural foramina (i.e., the C5- C6 nerves). In approximately two- thirds of traction injuries involving the brachial plexus, the injury is located in the supraclavicular region; in the other third, the injury is retroclavicular or infraclavicular and involves the divisions, cords, or terminal nerves. In some patients, a double- level (supraclavicular and infraclavicular) injury can occur and should be considered. The principal factors determining the extent of injury are the energy imparted by the blow and, to a lesser degree, the direction and the relationship of the arm to the neck during the injury.

An individual patient may have different degrees of injury at different nerve levels; for example, the C8 and T1 nerves may be avulsed, the C7 nerve or middle trunk may be ruptured (Sunderland type V), and the upper trunk may remain intact but with varying degrees of internal damage (Sunderland types I to IV). Within an individual brachial plexus element, individual axons and fascicles may also suffer various degree of injury. This variability in the degree of injury affecting each plexal element explains why spontaneous recovery may occur in some plexal elements but not in others. Extensive longitudinal injury is also common and at times results in segmental injuries to the retroclavicular and infraclavicular plexus (divisions and cords or terminal branches, respectively), as well as the roots, nerves, and trunks cephalad to the clavicle.

The same high- energy traction injury that damages the brachial plexus may also cause other injuries. Approximately 75% of traumatic brachial plexus injuries are associated with head injury, thoracic trauma, and fractures or dislocations (or both) of the cervical spine or ipsilateral upper extremity; 20% of patients have associated vascular trauma. These associated injuries, such as fractures, dislocations, and vascular injuries (from either an associated hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, or arteriovenous fistula), can further damage the brachial plexus.

### Gunshot Injury

Most commonly, the plexal elements are in continuity following a gunshot injury, but in a small proportion of patients, some elements may be transected. The injury will depend on the caliber, velocity, and angle of incidence of the missile. Low- velocity missiles usually damage nerve elements by direct impact; high- velocity missiles can injure nerves by direct impact (which transects the nerves) or, more commonly, by shock wave and cavitation effects (which contuse or stretch the nerves). Because the injury is often in continuity, some patients will have spontaneous recovery. Others do not recover, however, and will require surgical exploration; high- velocity injuries are more likely to cause extensive damage and to fail to recover spontaneously. Gunshot wounds may cause associated vascular injuries that may require emergency or delayed repair; the latter may occur over time as a result of the formation of a pseudoaneurysm, which can compress the plexus, produce progressive loss of function, and require an intervention on the vessel(s) or nerves, or both.

### Penetrating Trauma

The brachial plexus is occasionally injured by sharp (caused by knives or glass) or blunt (caused by fan and motor blades, chainsaws, or animal bites) penetrating trauma. Iatrogenic injuries have also been reported from various surgical procedures. As expected, most patients have brachial plexus elements in discontinuity (i.e., transected); however, despite the mechanism of injury, some patients have a lesion with a degree of continuity. Because of the high suspicion of transection of one or more nerves, these patients require nerve exploration. A third of these patients require vascular surgery, which is often performed concomitantly. In these patients, as in patients with gunshot wounds or traction injuries, an associated hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, or arteriovenous fistula may compress the plexus and require intervention because of progressive neurologic deterioration.

## COMMON PATTERNS OF BRACHIAL PLEXUS INJURY

In brachial plexus injuries, any combination of stretch, rupture, and avulsion can occur. However, certain patterns are more frequent, such as supraclavicular injuries (including the roots, nerves, and trunks, and often described as C5- C6, C5- C7, C8- T1, or pan- plexus), retroclavicular injuries (divisions), and infraclavicular injuries (cords and terminal branches).

### C5- C6 Injury

Approximately 15% of patients have injured the C5 and C6 nerves, often extending to the junction where they form the upper trunk (Erb’s point). These patients have deficits in shoulder stability, abduction, and external and internal rotation (supraspinatus and infraspinatus, deltoid, subscapularis), as well as in elbow flexion (biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis) and forearm supination (biceps, supinator). Sensory deficit will be present in the C5 and C6 distributions. Elbow extension is normal, as is wrist and hand function. This pattern of injury is commonly referred to eponymously as Erb or Erb- Duchenne palsy in recognition of the early work of Wilhelm Heinrich Erb and Guillaume Duchenne on peripheral nerve injury.

### C5- C7 Injury

In approximately 20% to 35% of patients, weakness of muscles innervated by C5 and C6, as described earlier, will be accompanied by a partial or complete C7 or middle trunk injury. In these patients, there will be variable weakness of the elbow, wrist, and sometimes finger extensors. The C7 contribution to wrist and finger extension and even to the flexor digitorum profundus muscles varies between patients and leads to different degrees of weakness. Sensory disturbances in the proximal part of the arm, as well in the thumb and index and middle fingers, may be present. This at times is referred to as an Erb- plus pattern. Some cases of C5- 7 injury may mimic C5- 6 injury.

### C8- T1 Injury

Approximately 10% of patients (even less in our experience) have supraclavicular lesions isolated to the C8 and T1 nerves at presentation (typically the upper elements have recovered). These patients will have weakness of the hand intrinsics, as well as variable weakness of the hand extrinsics and finger extensors, depending on the C7 contribution to these territories. Sensory loss in the ulnar digits, medial aspect of the forearm, and distal part of the arm may be present. Involvement of the lower roots can result in Horner syndrome, which is noted on examination by meiosis (constricted pupil), ptosis of the upper eyelid, anhidrosis, and enophthalmos (see the section Physical Examination). This rare pattern of injury is often referred to as Klumpke or DejerineKlumpke palsy.

### Pan- Plexus (C5- T1) Injury

Traumatic injury to the entire brachial plexus (C5- T1) occurs in 50% to 75% of patients with supraclavicular brachial plexus injuries. These patients most commonly have a completely flail arm and insensate hand. Occasionally, certain elements may be partially injured. Even with complete pan- plexus injuries, postganglionic injury (particularly of C5) is often present, with preganglionic lesions affecting other nerves.

### Infraclavicular and Terminal Branch Injury

Retroclavicular, infraclavicular, or axillary region brachial plexus injuries may involve injuries at the division, cord, or terminal branch level. Clavicular and/or scapular fracture is often present in these cases. A few patterns are more commonly seen, including injuries to the posterior cord (radial and axillary nerve distributions), as well as isolated axillary or suprascapular nerve injuries.

## PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

A comprehensive preoperative evaluation that includes a detailed history, physical examination, imaging, and electrodiagnostic evaluation is essential to determine the location and severity of the brachial plexus injury and appropriate management. The goal is first to determine whether there is potential for spontaneous and functionally significant recovery, which warrants further observation. If no recovery is seen within the first 2 to 3 months in patients with stretch injury, surgery is indicated. In cases in which surgery is indicated, determining whether the injury is preganglionic or postganglionic will also help in planning the surgical approach and possible reconstructive options. In preganglionic injuries, the nerve root is nonfunctional (avulsed from the spinal cord) and, for practical purposes, cannot be repaired or grafted. The presence of a preganglionic injury at one or more levels indicates a more severe injury with a lower probability of spontaneous recovery;

thus earlier surgical intervention may be warranted. No single finding or evaluation can tell the surgeon whether the injured plexus requires a surgical intervention. All the information must be analyzed and each patient requires an individualized approach.

Patients at our institution are evaluated in a multidisciplinary clinic, first by a neurologist and then by a team of surgeons skilled in primary and secondary reconstruction. A physiatrist (with expertise in pain management) and a physical/occupational therapist are also involved in the care of each patient. They teach and emphasize the importance and timing of passive and active range- of- motion exercises before and after any operation. Some groups have also incorporated social workers and psychological evaluation. Decisions regarding management are discussed and planned collectively. We believe that this type of team approach best addresses the short- and long- term needs of these patients with complex injuries.

### History

A good history of the injury mechanism is helpful in determining its severity. High- energy traction injuries, such as motor vehicle or motorcycle injuries, will probably be more severe, with less potential for spontaneous recovery than low- energy traction injuries, such as falls. Still, some falls, especially those associated with musculoskeletal injuries, may result in severe nerve lesions that do not recover spontaneously. Nerve deficits from lacerations, either sharp or blunt, will not recover spontaneously (except in the rare case in which the nerve elements were not transected) and should be explored acutely or subacutely (see the section Timing of Surgery). On the contrary, the majority of gunshot wounds associated with brachial plexus injury should be observed because most lesions are in continuity and have the potential for spontaneous recovery.

When a patient with brachial plexus injury is evaluated acutely in the emergency room, the airway, breathing, and circulation should be evaluated and stabilized, and the standard trauma evaluation guidelines should be followed. If the patient sustained high- energy trauma, a thorough search for associated life- and limb- threatening injuries should be conducted. In patients with head or spinal cord injuries,brachial plexus injury is often underdiagnosed; the opposite may also occur if the surgeon focuses on the upper extremity and fails to diagnose an associated head or incomplete spinal cord injury. Every patient should therefore be asked about loss of consciousness and paresthesias or weakness affecting the other extremities.

The presence of associated injuries (especially vascular injury, ipsilateral upper extremity musculoskeletal injury, scapulothoracic dissociation, rib fractures, pneumothorax and hemothorax, lung contusion, spine fracture and spinal cord injury, and traumatic brain injury) should be considered and documented. Associated injuries may indicate a more severe injury with less potential for spontaneous recovery and may influence the available reconstructive options. Severe pain in an anesthetic extremity may be due to deafferentation, as seen in patients with root avulsion injuries. When evaluated weeks to months after the injury, the patient should be questioned about any improvement in function or pain since the injury.

### Physical Examination

The examination of patients with brachial plexus injury must be carried out in a standardized and detailed manner. The objective is to establish the location of the nerve injury (preganglionic [i.e., root] or postganglionic [i.e., nerve, trunk, division, cord, or branches]) and the severity of the lesion (i.e., partial or complete for each element of the brachial plexus) (see Figs. 34.1 and 34.2). Careful systematic motor and sensory examination of the entire limb should be performed and recorded (Fig. 34.4). Serial examinations over the first few months after injury may help determine the presence or absence of ongoing nerve regeneration and the prognosis for functional recovery.

The motor and sensory innervation of the upper extremity is well known and assists the surgeon in predicting the location of nerve lesions. Terminal branches can be assessed by examination of the fingers and wrist (median, ulnar, and radial nerves), elbow (musculocutaneous and high radial nerves), and shoulder (suprascapular or axillary nerves). Involvement of the latissimus dorsi (thoracodorsal nerve), sternal head of the pectoralis major (medial pectoral nerve), and clavicular head of the pectoralis major (lateral pectoral nerve) implies a lesion at least at the posterior, medial, and lateral cord levels, respectively. Involvement of the suprascapular nerve (supraspinatus and infraspinatus for shoulder abduction and external rotation, respectively) indicates a lesion at this terminal branch or at the upper trunk level. Finally, loss of function of the rhomboid (dorsal scapular nerve derived from C5) and serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve from C5 to C7) is an important indicator of a proximal lesion or preganglionic injury.

There is variability in plexus anatomy and nerve level contribution to individual muscle innervation. For example, a prefixed plexus receives significant innervation from the C4 nerve and a postfixed plexus receives innervation from the T2 nerve; these variations are reported commonly by anatomists. The dorsal scapular nerve may on occasion receive innervation from C4, such that rhomboid activity may be identified by electrical studies in the presence of a C5 avulsion injury. Unusual motor and sensory impairment that is not distributed in the proper territory of the cervical and peripheral nerves may indicate incomplete nerve injury and some recovery.

Certain findings on physical examination suggest preganglionic injury of the brachial plexus; if present, these findings imply a lower probability of spontaneous recovery and a higher probability of finding an avulsed root at exploration. As stated earlier, injury to the rhomboid and serratus anterior indicates a proximal nerve injury and suggests a preganglionic injury of the upper neural elements, whereas the presence of Horner syndrome (Fig. 34.5) on the affected side strongly correlates with avulsion of the lower elements (i.e., the C8 or T1 roots, or both). Because the T1 sympathetic ganglion is close to T1, an avulsion injury may interrupt sympathetic outflow and be manifested as Horner syndrome. Absence of a Tinel sign or of tenderness to percussion in the neck also suggests a preganglionic injury. Finally, muscular atrophy of the paraspinal muscles, which is associated with root avulsion, may be associated with shifting of the head away from the injured side.

Physical examination findings suggesting a postganglionic or more distal injury include the presence of tenderness to percussion in the supraclavicular or infraclavicular region, absence of sweating in the distribution of the injured nerve (sympathetic interruption), and minimal preservation of movement (partial injury). An advancing Tinel sign is suggestive of a recovering lesion.

The examination should also include testing of the distal spinal accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI), which supplies the trapezius muscle. It is frequently used for reconstruction options as a nerve transfer but can occasionally be injured concomitantly at the time of traumatic brachial plexus injury. In all patients, stability and active and passive range of motion of the joints should be assessed preoperatively. To rule out a concomitant spinal cord injury, a lower extremity neurologic examination (that includes reflexes) should be performed. The vascular system of the affected upper extremity should be assessed by palpation or Doppler examination of distal vascular flow, as well as for thrills or bruits in the infraclavicular and supraclavicular fossae.

### Imaging Evaluation

Radiographs of the cervical spine, chest, shoulder girdle, and humerus should be obtained in patients with high- energy traumatic brachial plexus palsy to rule out associated upper limb fractures and dislocations. Chest radiographs should include anteroposterior inspiration and expiration views and should be examined for the presence of rib fractures and for activity of the diaphragm. A paralyzed (i.e., elevated) hemidiaphragm indicates a proximal (probably preganglionic) brachial plexus injury because it is supplied by the phrenic nerve, which originates from the proximal C3- C5 nerves. The presence of cervical transverse process fractures is also associated with brachial plexus avulsion injuries. First or second rib fractures are also associated with preganglionic brachial plexus injuries. Rib fractures may be important if the intercostal nerves are to be considered for nerve transfers (10% of the intercostal nerves will be damaged by rib fractures in our experience.

Myelography and computed tomographic (CT) myelography,although invasive, have been considered the most reliable methods for detecting root avulsions (Fig. 34.6). Myelographic findings have been classified by Nagano and colleagues’ modified scheme into six categories, from normal roots to ventral or dorsal root avulsions (or both), to the presence of traumatic pseudomeningoceles. Myelography or CT myelography is typically performed at least 3 to 4 weeks after the injury; such a delay will allow a potential blood clot in the area of the root avulsion to dissolve and the pseudomeningocele to form and be visualized on imaging.

More recently, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been used increasingly to evaluate patients with traumatic brachial plexus injury; in some centers it has supplanted CT myelography. MRI can reveal traumatic pseudomeningoceles, large neuromas, rootlet abnormalities, associated inflammation and edema, and mass lesions. Newer techniques have improved the detection of nerve root avulsions by MRI.

Finally, the vasculature to the injured upper extremity should be assessed, especially when a functioning free muscle transfer (FFMT) is being considered. In the authors’ experience, a normal peripheral pulse on examination does not preclude a vascular injury affecting the major vessels. We generally evaluate the vasculature with magnetic resonance angiography; special attention is given to our preferred source of arterial supply for FFMT, the thoracoacromial trunk. Other adequate options to formally evaluate the vasculature include CT angiography and conventional angiography.

### Pulmonary Function Tests

Patients with a history of chest wall trauma (e.g., rib fractures, pneumothorax, hemothorax) and those found to have phrenic nerve dysfunction should have pulmonary function tests performed to better characterize their pulmonary function. These evaluations, with assessment by a pulmonologist if necessary, will help determine whether the patient will tolerate intercostal nerve transfers. Unless the preoperative pulmonary function test results are very low (i.e., <40% of expected, but no definitive criteria exist), a patient can generally tolerate up to four or five intercostal nerve transfers even if concomitant phrenic nerve dysfunction is present.

### Electrodiagnostic Evaluation

Electrodiagnostic studies are an integral component of the evaluation of traumatic brachial plexus injury. These studies can confirm the diagnosis, localize and characterize (i.e., partial versus complete) the nerve lesion, and assess for subclinical evidence of recovery. A baseline examination should be done 3 to 4 weeks after the traumatic injury, when Wallerian degeneration has occurred, to ensure that the electrodiagnostic study reflects the degree of nerve injury. Serial electrodiagnostic studies complement the physical examination when assessing for recovery and reinnervation. The electrodiagnostic evaluation should include electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCSs). EMG evaluates and records the electrical activity of muscles both at rest and with activity. Changes reflective of a denervation injury include the presence of fibrillation potentials at rest and absent (complete injury) or reduced (partial injury) motor unit potentials with voluntary effort. Over time, nascent motor unit potentials (low in amplitude, polyphasic in configuration, and of variable duration) may appear and suggest reinnervation. Several muscles, difficult to test clinically, can be evaluated by EMG to help localize the lesion. For example, the rhomboids, serratus anterior, and cervical paraspinal muscles, if affected and abnormal on EMG, may suggest a very proximal or preganglionic injury. The trapezius muscle should be evaluated, especially if the spinal accessory nerve is being considered as a potential donor for nerve transfer. NCSs, especially sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs), are also helpful to evaluate the level of the nerve injury. With preganglionic injuries, the dorsal root ganglion, where the sensory neuron cell body is located, is intact. The distal axons do not undergo Wallerian degeneration because they remain connected to the cell body; thus the SNAP is preserved. However, the patient is insensate because the sensory neurons are not connected to the central nervous system (i.e., spinal cord and brain). The finding of intact SNAPs in the presence of dermatomal anesthesia is pathognomonic of a root avulsion injury. In postganglionic injuries, the sensory axons will degenerate and the SNAP will be lost. On occasion, a segmental or longitudinal injury will cause both preganglionic and postganglionic lesions, and the SNAP will be absent in the presence of a root avulsion injury. On the contrary, motor conduction will be absent with both preganglionic and postganglionic injuries because the motor axons will have undergone Wallerian degeneration (i.e., because their cell body is located in the anterior horn of the spinal cord).

Whenever possible, a follow- up electrodiagnostic study is performed 6 to 12 weeks after the initial study to assess for recovery. If surgery is being considered, electrodiagnostic interrogation of those muscles innervated by potential nerve transfer donors can be performed to ensure normal or near- normal motor unit recruitment.Although an advancing Tinel sign and the presence of nascent units are encouraging findings, they need to be taken into consideration along with improvement in clinical function; these findings do not necessarily eliminate the need for surgery.

## INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY

Multiple studies on the exploration and reconstruction of brachial plexus traction injuries suggest the value of surgical intervention in many of these patients. Surgery is indicated in patients with functional deficits and no hope for spontaneous recovery or for further recovery. This principle applies to patients with traumatic brachial plexus injuries from any mechanism. All patients with laceration injuries in proximity to the brachial plexus should undergo exploration because in the vast majority of these injuries (sharp or blunt), there is no possibility of spontaneous improvement and surgery is clearly indicated. In patients with gunshot injuries, an initial potential for spontaneous recovery exists because the nerves are usually in continuity. The same applies to patients with traction injuries. After a period of observation (see Timing of Surgery), surgery is indicated if there is no clinical or electrophysiologic evidence of recovery. Signs of electrophysiologic recovery that do not translate to clinical recovery within a short period of observation should not be viewed as meaningful recovery, and such patients should be considered for a surgical intervention. If it were possible to clearly identify patients with complete nerve root avulsion or nerve rupture, a surgical intervention could be performed without the observation period because these injuries cannot recover spontaneously. However, at present, no definitive study or sign is sufficiently reliable; the decision should be made after analyzing the different evaluations (history, physical examination, imaging studies, electrodiagnostic studies) in the context of time.

## CONTRAINDICATIONS TO SURGERY

Absolute contraindications to surgery for the reconstruction of brachial plexus injury are few. Stiffness and contractures, age, medical comorbidity, associated traumatic brain injury, and associated spinal cord injury should be considered in the decision process and may need to be addressed, but they are not generally contraindications. The main contraindication is unrealistic goals or unwillingness of the patient to undergo the procedure. Before a decision to operate is reached, it is therefore crucial to inform the patient about the possible surgical options, risk- benefit ratio, postoperative rehabilitation program, success rate, long recovery time, and possibility of additional surgery. This discussion should be individualized to each patient according to that patient’s specific injury, needs and expectations, and goals and priorities (set by the treating team and patient). Surgery is also not usually indicated for patients with spontaneous ongoing recovery in all involved elements because outcomes will generally be better without surgery in such patients. We believe that the rare patient with a C8- T1 lesion has a relative contraindication to primary nerve repair or grafting because of the limited ability for successful regeneration to reach the hand. Distal nerve transfers or secondary reconstruction with tendon transfer may yield more predictable results. Finally, if more than a year has passed since the injury, primary plexus reconstruction is generally contraindicated, except perhaps in a young patient. In select cases, distal nerve transfers may be considered up to 18 months after the injury.

## TIMING OF SURGERY

Timing of brachial plexus reconstructive surgery is based on three principles: (1) better functional outcomes occur in patients with spontaneous recovery who do not require a surgical intervention; (2) surgical intervention is indicated for patients with no hope for spontaneous recovery or for further recovery; and (3) most series demonstrate that the surgical outcome is inversely proportional to the time interval from injury to surgery (i.e., outcomes are better if surgery is performed earlier). Based on these principles, primary nerve reconstruction may ideally be initiated immediately (within days) after injury or in a delayed fashion (within months). Timing of secondary reconstructive procedures is discussed separately.

### Primary (Nerve) Reconstruction

#### Immediate Surgery

In patients with plexus deficits and a sharp open injury, immediate exploration plus repair is indicated (best within several days). Immediate surgery allows direct repair of the lacerated elements. In patients with blunt laceration injury (e.g., fan or motor blades, auto metal fragments, chain saws, etc.) to the plexus, surgical intervention is also clearly necessary, but the timing of the surgery is controversial and less definite. A subacute (within 3 to 4 weeks) surgical intervention may be preferable; time will allow better definition of the zone of nerve injury. Occasionally, a reconstructive brachial plexus surgeon gets the opportunity to assess the degree of damage to the plexus at the time of injury when emergency vascular reconstruction of an injured subclavian or axillary artery is performed by the vascular surgeons. This may occur with sharp or blunt laceration injuries and with traction injuries. The supraclavicular and infraclavicular regions may be explored, the damage to the plexus mapped, and the plexus reconstruction planned. If the nerves are sharply divided, they can be repaired immediately; if they are bluntly transected or ruptured, they may be tagged with radiopaque vascular clips (to allow identification) and loosely coapted to prevent retraction. The injury can then be reexplored 3 to 4 weeks later to allow the zone of damage to be better demarcated. If the nerves are avulsed, reconstruction can be performed immediately or at a second setting, depending on the situation and condition of the patient; finally, if the nerves are stretched but in continuity, a period of observation will be necessary.

#### Delayed Surgery

Surgery is delayed in patients who have a potential for spontaneous recovery or for injuries that require time to become better defined. In patients with closed traction injuries or gunshot injuries (because most are in continuity), surgery is performed after a period of observation if there is no evidence of spontaneous clinical or electrophysiologic recovery. Even though outcomes are better if surgery is performed earlier, a balance must be reached such that patients demonstrating early spontaneous recovery (i.e., who will probably fare well) do not undergo surgery. The timing of surgery needs to be individualized based on multiple factors, including the mechanism of injury, findings on physical examination and imaging, and the surgeon’s preference. Patients with a low probability of spontaneous recovery may undergo surgery earlier, and patients with a high probability of spontaneous recovery may be observed longer. However, there are no universal guidelines and different opinions exist in the literature. Our preference is to explore and perform primary nerve reconstruction early in patients in whom there is a high suspicion of root avulsion based on the history/mechanism, physical examination, electrophysiologic studies, or imaging studies. At our institution we are not performing surgery ultra- early, as is advocated by some. We operate on patients with pan- plexal lesions 2 to 3 months after injury if they have no evidence of recovery but have imaging evidence of avulsions. In patients without this high suspicion of root avulsion and no adequate signs of recovery, we perform exploration 3 to 6 months after the injury. We may wait longer (5 to 6 months) for patients with a higher chance of spontaneous recovery, such as those with incomplete brachial plexus palsy or complete brachial plexus palsy without evidence of root avulsion. An early intervention may not allow sufficient time for spontaneous recovery; however, we believe that routine use of intraoperative monitoring (such as nerve action potentials [NAPs]) will help determine whether there is early subclinical evidence of recovery at the time of exploration. We seldom wait for more than 6 months because long delays diminish the outcomes of the reconstructive procedure. Still, many patients present to us for initial evaluation more than 6 months after injury.

### Secondary Reconstruction

For adult patients initially seen late (i.e., >12 months after the injury), primary nerve reconstructive procedures are associated with significantly poorer results. In select cases, distal nerve transfers can still be considered even later (i.e., <18 months). However, secondary reconstructive procedures, such as tendon transfer, FFMT (gracilis muscle), and bony or soft tissue procedures, may be indicated and can be done at any time.

## OPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

### Informed Consent

The possible surgical options, risk- benefit ratio, site of incisions, hospitalization time, postoperative precautions and rehabilitation program, expected outcome with or without surgery, and long reinnervation period should be discussed in detail with the patient and his or her family. The patient should have realistic expectations and a clear understanding of the goals and priorities of the planned surgery. The surgical approach should be individualized according to the patient’s comfort level (acceptable risks and set of goals).

### Preparation and Positioning

The surgical procedure, in particular its indeterminate duration and the need to perform intraoperative stimulation and recordings, should be discussed with the anesthesiologist. Long- acting paralytic agents, muscle relaxants, and agents depressing cortical responses should be avoided. Adequate vascular access should be obtained and an indwelling urinary catheter should be inserted. After induction of anesthesia and intubation, the patient is positioned in the supine position. The head is turned to the contralateral side, the upper part of the body is elevated, and a small pillow is placed beneath the ipsilateral scapula to bring the shoulder forward. Proper padding and positioning to protect bony prominences are necessary because brachial plexus reconstructive procedures are lengthy. Surgical preparation and draping should include the ipsilateral neck, mandible, hemithorax, axilla, entire upper extremity, and both lower extremities (for possible nerve grafts). Some surgeons use tourniquets placed on the upper part of the thighs that can be inflated just before sural nerve graft harvesting. Given the long duration of plexus surgeries, it is recommended that the tourniquet’s pneumatic hose not be connected until just prior to inflation and disconnected again after use. The arm is prepared and draped freely; this will permit the response to nerve stimulation to be observed and appropriate changes in arm positioning to be made during the procedure if needed. Tight circumferential elastic drapes are avoided on the extremities because of the long duration of surgery and possible swelling. If planning is being done to use the contralateral C7 nerve for transfer, the contralateral neck and hemithorax should be included in the surgical field. The surgeon should be prepared to expose both the supraclavicular and infraclavicular portions of the brachial plexus, harvest nerve grafts (sural nerve, superficial radial nerve, or vascularized ulnar nerve), or perform nerve transfers if required.

Electrodes required for intraoperative electrophysiologic monitoring (such as motor evoked potentials [MEPs] and somatosensory evoked potentials [SSEPs]) are placed. A bipolar microcoagulator, nerve stimulator, magnifying loupes, and operative microscope should be available for the surgical procedure.

### Surgical Approaches to the Brachial Plexus

The elements of the brachial plexus are exposed through a supraclavicular approach (i.e., nerves, trunks, suprascapular nerve) or an infraclavicular approach (i.e., cords, terminal branches). Depending on the pattern of injury, a combined approach may be necessary. The divisions, which are retroclavicular, can be accessed through one or both approaches. With introduction of the newer nerve transfers, some surgeons do not routinely explore the brachial plexus in all patients, even if a proximal nerve would be available for grafting, believing that nerve transfers provide a more reliable option. We recommend routinely exploring the proximal brachial plexus, except in specific patients who are initially seen late and have a more reliable, distal nerve transfer option. When a pan- plexus injury is present, less than 20% of patients are reported to have avulsions of all five roots of the plexus; we therefore believe that exploration is warranted because a proximal nerve can often be found for grafting.

The variability in surgical approaches to the brachial plexus is mainly in the incisions used to expose the supraclavicular or infraclavicular brachial plexus and in the reconstructive approach used. Traditionally, a zigzag skin incision (Fig. 34.7) was used: The supraclavicular incision begins at the angle of the jaw and follows the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid to the midclavicular area; the infraclavicular incision begins at the clavicular insertion of the sternocleidomastoid (connecting with the supraclavicular incision), courses laterally toward the coracoid process, and extends laterally to the deltopectoral groove and into the arm. These incisions are connected to achieve a wide exposure. However, connecting these incisions increases the risk for scar hypertrophy. An alternative is to expose the supraclavicular plexus with a transverse incision, parallel to the clavicle, and the infraclavicular plexus with an incision over the deltopectoral groove. These two incisions can be connected if required. Some surgeons use a transverse cervical skin incision between the posterior tubercle of the C5 transverse process and the anterior tubercle of the C6 transverse process to expose the C5 and C6 nerves and a second incision that runs transversely over the clavicle to expose the C7, C8, and T1 nerves. Our preference, for exposure of the supraclavicular plexus, is to use a single transverse 5- to 6- cm cervical incision, off midline, parallel to and approximately two to three fingerbreadths above the clavicle (Fig. 34.8). With this approach we can expose the C5 through T1 nerves (and the spinal accessory nerve laterally, as necessary). We expose the infraclavicular plexus with a second incision overlying the deltopectoral interval. The skin incision may be infiltrated with a vasopressor agent to decrease bleeding.

#### Supraclavicular Exploration

After the skin incision, the platysma is incised and the cutaneous branches of the cervical plexus are identified and protected. Skin flaps that include the platysma are carefully raised both superiorly and inferiorly. We generally do not encounter the spinal accessory nerve in the proximal portion of this exposure where it exits beneath the sternocleidomastoid muscle; however, we can easily identify it more distally near the medial border of the trapezius muscle if necessary (see the section Nerve Transfer). The external jugular vein on the surface of the sternocleidomastoid muscle is dissected and retracted. Dissection proceeds along the posterolateral margin of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, which is retracted medially; the cleidal head of the sternocleidomastoid can also be released. The superficial layer of the deep cervical fascia and cervical fat pad are opened and retracted. The dissection is extended to the superior border of the clavicle. The omohyoid muscle and transverse cervical vessels are identified, divided, and retracted medially and laterally. The first brachial plexus element to be visualized is usually the upper trunk (lateral to the anterior scalene). Medially, the phrenic nerve is found on the anterior surface of the anterior scalene muscle (Figs. 34.9 and 34.10). Contraction of the diaphragm after electrical nerve stimulation can confirm identification of this nerve except in patients with phrenic nerve dysfunction (e.g., very proximal or preganglionic C5 nerve injury). The phrenic nerve should be mobilized with care. The anterior scalene muscle can then be resected safely to expose the proximal cervical nerves. When traced proximally, the C5 phrenic contribution will be visualized, thereby confirming identification of the C5 nerve. Careful dissection medial to the phrenic nerve may be necessary in patients with proximal C5 injury; iatrogenic injury to vital neck structures should be avoided. The upper trunk is exposed proximally and its C5 and C6 nerve origin identified. The C5 nerve is usually smaller, superior, lateral, and more vertical than the C6 nerve. The upper trunk is dissected distally to its anterior and posterior divisions, as well as to the origin of the suprascapular nerve. Long thoracic nerve may be identified lateral to the middle scalene (deep to the suprascapular nerve) and traced proximally to identify C6. The C7 nerve and middle trunk can be isolated between the anterior and middle scalene muscles, more medial, posterior, and horizontal than the C6 nerve. Even more inferior and posterior are the C8 and T1 nerves (forming the lower trunk), which are closely associated with the subclavian artery. Mobilization of the clavicle may help improve exposure. Osteotomy of the clavicle in our experience is only seldom performed in treating posttraumatic brachial plexopathies. In most cases, however, the C8 and T1 nerves are not specifically exposed, because their repair is rarely, if ever, indicated in our opinion, given the poor outcome after reconstruction of these lower nerve injuries in adults.

If the upper plexus nerves or the upper trunk or middle trunk (or both) is ruptured, the distal ends typically lie behind the clavicle. The avulsed (often) or ruptured (infrequently) C8 and T1 structures are usually found much closer to their respective foramina than is the case with C5 or C6.

#### Infraclavicular Exploration

When indicated, the infraclavicular approach to the brachial plexus will expose the cords and terminal branches (Fig. 34.11). After the skin incision, the deltopectoral interval is developed to expose the cephalic vein, which is preserved and retracted laterally. Some or all of the pectoralis major may be taken down from its humeral insertion and retracted as needed for exposure. A portion of its tendon is left on the humerus for reattachment, and the tendinous footprint is tagged with sutures for ease of closure. The infraclavicular plexus is explored by detaching the tendinous origin of the pectoralis minor from the coracoid process and tagging and retracting it. The clavipectoral fascia, which envelops the pectoralis minor, is incised to expose the lateral cord, which is superficial and lateral to the axillary artery. The thoracoacromial artery and its accompanying veins are identified and tagged if a FFMT or vascularized nerve graft is planned. The posterior cord is slightly lateral and deep to the artery, and the medial cord is medial and deep to the axillary artery. Care should be taken to preserve the medial and lateral pectoral nerves coursing in this area. Electric stimulation can be helpful for identifying these nerves, which often consist of multiple branches. The lateral cord is identified by following the median nerve from distal to proximal. At this point the musculocutaneous nerve can be seen branching laterally and piercing the coracobrachialis muscle. The median nerve can also be followed proximally to identify the medial cord, which is then followed distally to the ulnar, medial brachial cutaneous, and medial antebrachial cutaneous nerves. Medial and deep to the axillary artery and vein and coursing laterally is the radial nerve. It is followed proximally to the posterior cord; the axillary nerve can be seen branching from the posterior cord. Once the supraclavicular and infraclavicular brachial plexuses are exposed, the retroclavicular area can be addressed. A tunnel is made under the clavicle from the neck to the deltopectoral area with a gloved finger. The most lateral part of the clavicular attachment of the pectoralis major muscle is detached; the subclavius muscle is divided. Crossing vessels, including the suprascapular vessels, can be ligated as needed. By elevating the clavicle with a retractor or heavy tape, the divisions of the brachial plexus are exposed. If and when clavicular osteotomy is needed, predrilling of the screws for plate fixation should be done before the clavicle is divided. Patients with a history of clavicular fracture or hypertrophic callus require special attention; in these cases, blunt dissection underneath the clavicle can be difficult. The risk for major vascular and pleural damage must be considered, especially if the lower brachial plexus elements are exposed medially.

If extensive scarring is present, especially fibrosis related to previous vascular injury or reconstruction, identification of major neurovascular structures in “normal” tissue planes is the rule before proceeding toward the zone of pathology. Release of the insertion of the pectoralis major and in some cases the conjoined tendon may improve the exposure and facilitate safe dissection. ## INTRAOPERATIVE CONCERNS

### Intraoperative Assessment

Operative inspection and palpation of the brachial plexus in our experience has not proven to be a reliable means of predicting preganglionic versus postganglionic injury or of assessing (postganglionic) neuromasin- continuity. For example, in some cases of preganglionic injury, there is little scarring of the brachial plexus, which might suggest a minor injury but in fact reflects a more proximal injury (i.e., at the root–spinal cord interface). In other cases, a large neuroma- in- continuity may be recovering but may not have yet reached the distal musculature.

For these reasons, intraoperative electrodiagnostic assessment can be performed; we recommend and routinely use it. Surgical strategies are tailored to the intraoperative findings. Our intraoperative assessment includes the use of SSEPs, MEPs, and NAPs. Spinal evoked potentials can replace SSEPs. Histologic evaluation by determining choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity is also used by some groups. If a spinal nerve or peripheral nerve is divided surgically in preparation for nerve grafting, frozen- section histologic analysis of the transected portion can be performed quickly for assessment of relative fascicular preservation and intraneural scarring.

#### Somatosensory and Motor Evoked Potentials

SSEPs and MEPs help determine whether the spinal nerves are in continuity with the spinal cord; they evaluate the integrity of the intraforaminal and intraspinal sensory and motor pathways. SSEPs are based on the principle that it is possible to record very low amplitude potentials on the scalp (over the parietal cortex) after stimulating a contralateral peripheral nerve in the upper or lower limb. MEPs are motor compound action potentials recorded from surgically exposed spinal nerves after central stimulation. Both SSEPs and MEPs have been used to identify root avulsion during brachial plexus surgery.

For SSEPs and MEPs, an electrode (which can act either as a stimulator or as a recorder) is placed on the available proximal cervical nerves and stimulations are performed. We rely partly on this examination to confirm the integrity of a proximal nerve that is being considered for grafting.

An important limitation is the fact that the sensory (posterior) roots and motor (anterior) roots may be differentially injured. When an SSEP is present, it does not signify that the motor roots are intact, and vice versa. Another limitation is that SSEPs are easily affected by the depth of anesthesia and technical difficulties; a useful normal control may be established by testing the SSEPs in the unaffected upper limb simultaneously. MEP recording and interpretation are also sometimes challenging and require a team experienced with the technique.

#### Nerve Action Potentials

NAPs evaluate the presence of functioning axons, both sensory and motor, over a segment of nerve. Although they can be used to assess preganglionic injury, we routinely use SSEPs and MEPs instead for this purpose. We do use NAPs to assess all postganglionic neuromas- incontinuity. Stimulating and recording electrodes are placed directly on the nerve, proximal and distal to a neuroma- in- continuity (Fig. 34.12). At least 4 cm of separation is required to record reliable NAPs. If a good NAP is present across a neuroma- in- continuity, the likelihood of significant functional recovery over time is high and the lesion should undergo neurolysis but not resection. We do not routinely use compound muscle action potential recordings in assessing incomplete lesions. Based on a large basic science and clinical experience, Kline and colleagues have shown that when no NAP is obtained across a neuroma- in- continuity, this lesion has minimal potential for spontaneous recovery and should be excised and grafted.

While performing intraoperative electrodiagnostic testing, we also observe the effects of direct nerve stimulation on muscle contraction. The phrenic or other functioning proximal nerves can serve as a normal positive control.

#### Choline Acetyltransferase Activity

ChAT activity has been used clinically to differentiate between motor and sensory fascicles intraoperatively during nerve grafting procedures and also to determine the availability of the proximal nerve stump as a donor motor nerve during brachial plexus surgery. ChAT is an enzyme of acetylcholine synthesized in the nerve cell body and transported by axonal flow to the nerve terminals. Its activity is higher in motor fascicles than in sensory fascicles, and this differential expression can be used to identify motor fascicles of good quality (ChAT activity >2000 cpm). Some investigations have demonstrated that ChAT may not be as reliable as previously reported. This fact, in addition to the prolonged time of testing, makes the use of this test difficult in the clinical situation.

### Intraoperative Decisions and Priorities of Repair

Primary brachial plexus nerve reconstruction is based on functional priorities, depends on intraoperative assessment of the nerve damage and available motor axon donors, and is individualized intraoperatively. When only some elements of the brachial plexus are injured or the nerve gaps are short, we prefer to reestablish continuity to all injured parts by using every available donor nerve. When all brachial plexus elements are injured or the nerve gaps are long, this approach becomes impossible; autologous donor nerve tissue is not available to restore continuity to all parts of the brachial plexus, and priorities for reconstruction should be set. The priorities for reconstruction are based on three factors: (1) the functional importance, (2) the likelihood of regaining function after nerve reconstruction (proximal muscles are reinnervated more successfully than distal muscles), and (3) the degree of difficulty in restoring function by secondary reconstructive surgery.

#### C5- C6 Injury

For a patient with an injury limited to the upper plexus nerves (C5 and C6), the priorities are regaining elbow flexion and shoulder stability, abduction, and external rotation. Elbow flexion is generally achieved by reinnervation of the biceps and brachialis muscles (via the anterior division of the upper trunk, the musculocutaneous nerve proper, or the motor branches to the biceps and brachialis). Shoulder stability, abduction, and external rotation are usually obtained with reinnervation of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles (via the suprascapular nerve) and the deltoid (via the posterior division of the upper trunk, the axillary nerve proper, or the anterior division of the axillary nerve).

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: C5- C6 INJURY

In the majority of patients with C5- C6 loss of function, we explore the brachial plexus and perform intraoperative recordings. In a patient with a nonrecovering postganglionic injury (i.e., a postganglionic neuroma- in- continuity with a negative NAP or a postganglionic nerve rupture), we typically reinnervate the shoulder by using interposition grafts between the C5 nerve stump and the suprascapular nerve and the posterior division of the upper trunk. Another option that we may use for shoulder reinnervation is an interposition graft between the C5 nerve stump and the posterior division of the upper trunk combined with transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve. In patients in whom a functional C5 nerve stump is not identified or in those explored late (>6 to 9 months after injury), we perform doublenerve transfers (to suprascapular and axillary nerves) for shoulder reinnervation. Our preferred technique includes a spinal accessory nerve transfer to the suprascapular nerve and a triceps motor branch transfer to the anterior division of the axillary nerve. Consistent with recent literature authored by others, for elbow reinnervation, we typically perform fascicular nerve transfer(s) preferentially over C6 nerve grafting. Our preferred combination is transfer of an ulnar nerve fascicle to the biceps motor branch, sometimes combined with a second nerve transfer of a median nerve fascicle to the brachialis motor branch. Several reports have demonstrated that the addition of the second transfer has not statistically improved outcomes.

#### C5- C7 Injury

Patients with C5- C7 injuries have deficits in shoulder motion and elbow flexion (as do patients with C5- C6 injury), as well as variable deficits in elbow extension (triceps) and wrist extension. The priorities for primary nerve reconstruction are similar to those in patients with C5- C6 injury: regaining elbow flexion and shoulder stability, abduction, and external rotation. In addition, if elbow extension (uncommon) or wrist extension is severely affected, these problems should be addressed. The approach for nerve reconstruction differs because of the variable degree of weakness and the available donor motor axons. Generally, a combination of nerve grafts (if proximal nerves are available) and nerve transfers (spinal accessory, intercostals, pectoral, thoracodorsal, ulnar and median nerve fascicles) is performed.

#### C8- T1 Injury

Patients with this pattern of injury have weakness in the extrinsic and intrinsic muscles of the hand and more variable wrist weakness. The indications, timing, and approach to reconstruction are controversial. As stated previously, nerve grafting is not appropriate for these rare injuries because of the long reinnervation distances. Some surgeons are performing distal nerve transfers in patients with C8- T1 lesions when seen early. Because patient numbers are small, the results of such procedures have yet to be firmly determined. One can certainly perform early or delayed tendon transfers for an isolated C8- T1 injury.An individualized approach is required, with careful clinical and electrodiagnostic assessment of the strength of potential available donors. Reconstruction of thumb flexion, finger flexion, opposition, and intrinsic- minus claw deformity, as well as finger and thumb extension, will be necessary. If C7 is also affected and tendon transfer options are limited, the use of FFMT may also be appropriate.

#### Pan- Plexal Injury

In patients with complete pan- plexal injuries, our priorities of repair, in order of importance, include the following:

1. Elbow flexion by reinnervation of the biceps/brachialis muscle
2. Shoulder stabilization, abduction, and external rotation by rein nervation of the suprascapular, axillary nerves, and long thoracic nerve if possible
3. Hand sensation by reinnervation of the lateral cord (C6- C7 dis tribution)
4. Wrist and finger flexion (When hand reconstruction is per formed, the triceps muscle should also be considered for reinnervation because it is a useful antagonist of elbow flexion; as such, it potentiates the strength of a FFMT that crosses the elbow joint.)
5. Wrist and finger extension
6. Intrinsic hand muscle function (not typically attainable in a pan- plexus injury)

In patients with complete root avulsions of the brachial plexus, no functioning spinal nerves are available proximally to restore function to the extremity. In these situations, there are no local musculotendinous transfers available for restoration of extremity function. Intact extraplexal nerves (spinal accessory, intercostal, C3 and C4 nerves) can be transferred and coapted to the distal peripheral nerve of the brachial plexus as a method for reinnervation of critical sensory or motor nerves.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PAN- PLEXUS INJURY

Conventional goals in patients with pan- plexus injury include a stable shoulder and elbow flexion with or without some hand sensation. Advances in microsurgical techniques, as well as novel application of FFMTs in the acute setting (<6 months after injury), have enabled us to achieve rudimentary prehension in select patients. In all patients, we explore the supraclavicular brachial plexus in the hope of finding some viable and usable nerves from the proximal stumps; even in cases with evidence of multiple avulsions based on preoperative testing, exploration may occasionally reveal a viable nerve stump. In our experience, only rarely do we find more than one proximal nerve stump that can be used for nerve grafting in severe brachial plexus injuries. If available, we use this stump to reinnervate shoulder targets (suprascapular and axillary nerves). Our highest priority for nerve reconstruction in patients with complete injury is elbow flexion. For this reason, we have chosen to doubly reinnervate elbow flexion when possible, often using intercostal nerve transfers to the biceps motor branch in addition to a gracilis FFMT innervated by the intercostals as well. Shoulder abduction, stability, and especially external rotation are provided either by using spinal accessory nerve transfer to the suprascapular nerve (when no available nerves are present) or by leaving the spinal accessory nerve intact and performing a delayed lower trapezius tendon transfer for external rotation. Other options remain for later shoulder reconstruction if these measures prove unsuccessful. When additional nerve resources are available (i.e., more than one viable nerve), we will also target the serratus anterior (with a single intercostal nerve) or pectoralis major.

If hand reinnervation is attempted, a single- stage gracilis FFMT is used for both elbow flexion and finger flexion. The shoulder is stabilized with nerve grafts to either the suprascapular nerve or axillary nerve, or both, from an available cervical nerve stump or with a planned secondary trapezius tendon transfer. The spinal accessory nerve is transferred to a triceps branch with an intervening nerve graft for reinnervation of elbow extension. Sensation in the median nerve distribution of the hand is provided by the transfer of sensory branches of the intercostal nerves to the lateral cord contribution of the median nerve. Secondary surgeries to provide stability to the hand and assist in rudimentary grasp are performed between 4 and 6 months after the index brachial plexus reconstruction. Secondary surgeries include selected fusions and at times, soft tissue balancing. The fusions include wrist arthrodesis, thumb carpometacarpal arthrodesis (to place the thumb in palmar abduction and pronation), and thumb interphalangeal joint arthrodesis. The soft tissue balancing is primarily achieved with passive flexor digitorum superficialis lasso procedures to create a metacarpophalangeal joint contracture for prevention of a claw deformity. Other options for hand reanimation have also been proposed, including a two- stage FFMT and contralateral C7 (entire or half [hemi]) transfer to the median nerve, as well as extended phrenic nerve transfers to the median nerve.

#### Infraclavicular and Terminal Branch Injuries

Variable findings are due to different injury patterns in isolation or combination. Intraoperative NAP recordings are used in each case, and nerve grafting is commonly performed when NAPs are absent, when the zone of injury is relatively short, and provided good proximal and distal nerve architecture is maintained. Concomitant or staged tendon transfers may be considered for denervated muscles with long reinnervation distances. Nerve transfers, if available donors exist, may be preferable in patients initially seen 6 to 12 months after injury. For example, in cases of posterior cord injury, we would typically excise the neuroma and perform nerve grafting to the axillary nerve and to triceps nerve branches, and standard tendon transfers would be used for wrist, finger, and thumb extension.

## PRIMARY NERVE RECONSTRUCTION

### Neurolysis

The indication for neurolysis after traumatic brachial plexus injury is the intraoperative finding of a postganglionic neuroma- in- continuity and a positive (present) NAP. In most of these cases, the integrity of the proximal nerves is confirmed with intraoperative SSEPs and MEPs; however, in patients with a clearly isolated infraclavicular injury, testing of evoked potentials may not be necessary.

Microsurgical neurolysis consists of liberating the nerve from adhesions to neighboring tissue at the epineurial level (external neurolysis). Occasionally, internal neurolysis is indicated and performed under the microscope. This consists of longitudinal incisions made in the fibrotic layers of the epineurium and the epifascicular epineurium; all fascicles are completely separated to remove the surrounding scar tissue.

### Direct Nerve Repair

Direct nerve repair is preferable to nerve grafts if it can be performed without excessive tension. However, neurorrhaphy is rarely possible after traumatic brachial plexus injury, except in cases of sharp transection that are explored early. Mobilization of the nerve stumps may make direct repair possible in some situations. In rare cases, direct repair of individual nerves or nerve branches with focal neuromas that are resected may be possible, provided that the nerve ends can be mobilized sufficiently and sutured without tension.

### Nerve Grafting

#### Indications

Nerve grafting is indicated in patients with postganglionic nerve ruptures or with postganglionic neuromas that do not conduct a NAP (i.e., after resection of which, a gap exists that cannot be sutured directly without tension). In these cases, the nerve is in continuity with the spinal cord and has maintained viable motor axons that can be grafted to specific targets. When nerve transfer options exist, some surgeons prefer them to nerve grafting. Our preference, unless the surgery is performed late, the muscle targets are very distal, or the graft length is long (>10 cm), is to use nerve grafting when proximal nerves are available and in good condition.

#### Operative Procedure

Nerve grafting consists of the coaptation of interposition grafts (e.g., cable grafts of the sural or other cutaneous nerves) between the desired nerve stumps and targets. Generally, the C5 nerve is used to achieve shoulder stability, abduction, and external rotation (suprascapular nerve, axillary nerve, or posterior division of the upper trunk), the C6 nerve to target elbow flexion (anterior division of the upper trunk or musculocutaneous nerve), and the C7 nerve to target elbow extension (posterior division of the middle trunk or radial nerve) (Fig. 34.13).

#### Conventional Nerve Grafting With the Sural Nerve

The most commonly used donor nerve for brachial plexus injury is the sural nerve, a purely sensory nerve that innervates a small area on the dorsolateral region of the foot (Fig. 34.14). If harvested from the popliteal fossa to the lateral aspect of the ankle, up to 35 to 40 cm of donor nerve can be obtained from each leg. The sural nerve can be harvested in the supine position by placing a pad beneath the donor- side buttock to internally rotate the leg because this nerve is located posteriorly in the calf. The sural nerve can be dissected and mobilized with the leg in extension or with the knee flexed. Alternatively, an assistant can hold the leg elevated during harvest. The use of a tourniquet at the upper thigh level is optional; however, if used, surgeons must make certain that the limb tourniquets are deflated and disconnected or removed after harvesting the nerve grafts. Different techniques can be used, including multiple transverse or vertical incisions, a single incision along the course of the nerve (which is our preference), and minimally invasive techniques, using a tendon passer or an endoscope. The nerve is first identified between the lateral malleolus and Achilles tendon in the subcutaneous tissues adjacent to the lesser saphenous vein. Gentle traction is applied, and the remaining portion is harvested up to the popliteal fossa; dissection of the nerve should be as atraumatic as possible. The nerve is transected as far distally and proximally as possible. Communicating branches should be harvested. The skin wound is closed in layers, and the legs are wrapped in elastic bandages.

#### Other Nerve Graft Options

If needed, other cutaneous nerves may be used as interposition grafts, including the superficial radial, medial brachial or antebrachial cutaneous, lateral antebrachial cutaneous, and dorsal cutaneous branch (of the ulnar) nerves. For long defects or when a large- diameter graft is required, some recommend vascularized nerve grafts (pedicled or free). The most common vascularized nerve graft is the ulnar nerve (when it is nonfunctional), as described later with contralateral C7 transfer, but the superficial radial nerve and even the sural nerve may also be used as vascularized nerve grafts.

#### Coaptation

Coaptations should be performed without tension, and the grafts should ideally fill the cross- sectional area of the stumps. Usually, two or three segments of graft are cut and used to fill the area (Fig. 34.15). When possible, different fascicular patterns are matched individually to lead the regenerating axons to the distal nerve, typically prioritizing motor fascicles. An understanding of the anatomic topography of the nerves at different levels can be helpful to align fascicles. Several 8- 0 to 10- 0 nylon epineurial sutures are commonly placed between each nerve graft and the proximal and distal nerves under microscope or loupe magnification. Tissue adhesive can be used to approximate multiple strands of nerve graft and simplify the coaptation with proximal and distal nerves (Fig. 34.16). We generally reinforce the nerve coaptation sites with the use of a bivalved nerve conduit and tissue adhesive.

#### Nerve Conduits

Nerve conduits are increasingly being used in nerve surgery for smallcaliber (more commonly sensory) nerves, for short nerve gaps, or as an adjunct to nerve repair or transfer; however, in proximal brachial plexus injuries, this technique is not recommended by our group in adults at this time as a substitute for nerve grafts.

#### Processed Nerve Allografts

The use of nerve allografts that have been decellularized by a variety of methods has gained popularity in short- gap digital sensory nerve defect reconstruction as well as larger mixed nerve defect reconstruction. Use of these allografts in proximal brachial plexus reconstruction or for long nerve or motor graft reconstruction is not recommended at this time unless nerve autograft is unavailable.

### Nerve Transfer

The terms *nerve transfer, neurotization,* and *nerve crossing* have been used interchangeably in the literature, and it is recommended that the term *neurotization* be used exclusively for transfer of a nerve directly into muscle for purposes of reinnervation. *Nerve transfer* is used most frequently in the current literature and describes the transfer of a normal or nearly normal fascicle or nerve branch to an important sensory or motor nerve that has sustained irreparable proximal damage.

The indications for nerve transfer are irreparable preganglionic injury, selected postganglionic injury, and reinnervation of FFMTs. Important attributes of nerve transfers are as follows: They are closer to the end organ (which means that there is increased likelihood of more rapid recovery or more reliable recovery, or both). There is delivery of a large number of “pure” axons. Repair takes place outside the injured and scarred zone. The use of an intercalated nerve graft introduces a second repair site and should be avoided when possible in nerve transfer. Rehabilitation is more easily accomplished, especially with synergistic transfers. Common intraplexal and extraplexal nerve transfers are described in the following sections.

#### Spinal Accessory Nerve Transfer

### SURGICAL ANATOMY

The spinal accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI) innervates the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles. It originates in the posterior cranial fossa from spinal and cranial nerve roots, passes through the jugular foramen, and divides into an internal branch (containing fibers originating from the “cranial part”), which joins the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X), and an external branch (consisting of fibers from the “spinal part”), which innervates the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles. After supplying the sternocleidomastoid muscle, the nerve descends obliquely in the posterior triangle of the neck between the superficial and deep layers of the deep cervical fascia (Fig. 34.17). In this area the nerve is embedded in loose connective tissue and is in contact with the cervical lymph node chain. This is the most common location for iatrogenic injuries to the spinal accessory nerve during lymph node biopsies. The spinal accessory nerve provides two or three branches to the upper part of the trapezius muscle before passing under its anterior edge. Intramuscularly, the nerve follows an oblique caudal course toward the middle and lower parts of the trapezius and gives off branches to the muscle throughout its course. There is great variability in connections between the spinal accessory nerve and the roots of the cervical plexus.

![[Fig.34.17.png]]
> **Fig. 34.17** The course of the spinal accessory nerve. The nerve can readily be identified in the lateral portion of a supraclavicular incision on the anterior surface of the trapezius muscle several centimeters above the clavicle. (Used with permission of Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research. All rights reserved.)

**Spinal accessory nerve dissection.** As with exploration of the supraclavicular plexus through the described skin incisions, turning the neck to the contralateral side and elevating the shoulder and neck off the surgical table facilitate access to the spinal accessory nerve. Muscle relaxation is avoided to allow the use of electrical stimulation to confirm identification of the spinal accessory nerve.

The spinal accessory nerve is often identified in an incision that parallels the posterior border of the sternomastoid. The nerve may be traced from the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and traced as far as possible along the undersurface of the trapezius to its decussation, where it is transected. The nerve can be transferred to the supraclavicular area for coaptation to any of several denervated targets, most frequently the suprascapular nerve. Although this is the most popular approach to the spinal accessory nerve, there are some disadvantages. First, it creates a large longitudinal scar on the neck. Second, this extensive approach can inadvertently denervate proximal branches to the upper part of the trapezius, which should be preserved.

Our preferred technique uses the lateral portion of the transverse supraclavicular cervical incision described above; this approach makes a less conspicuous scar over the clavicle. The spinal accessory nerve can be identified several centimeters above the clavicle on the anterior surface of the trapezius. This nerve should not be confused with more superficial cervical plexus elements that may run in similar directions. These branches from the cervical plexus can be distinguished from the spinal accessory nerve by their size, which is comparatively small, and by the response of the nerves to electrical stimulation. The spinal accessory nerve should produce vigorous contraction of the trapezius.

The spinal accessory nerve should be dissected as far distally as possible. A proximal branch to the upper portion of the trapezius should be preserved. The nerve can then be divided and transferred. Some authors transect the spinal accessory nerve more proximally, just distal to the sternocleidomastoid muscle; we do not recommend this approach because an intervening nerve graft will be required to reach the distal target, which may reduce the success of the procedure.

Contraindications. Patients with spinal accessory nerve dysfunction preoperatively are not candidates for this procedure. Because trapezius function is difficult to assess on physical examination in patients with paralyzed shoulder muscles, preoperative EMG can help identify an abnormality. Intraoperative stimulation is the definitive assessment of spinal accessory nerve function.

Complications. There are generally no serious complications from this procedure as long as the proximal branch of the spinal accessory nerve to the upper fibers of the trapezius muscle is preserved. If the proximal branch to the trapezius is injured, shoulder depression and dysfunction may occur.

**Transfer to the suprascapular nerve.** The suprascapular nerve, which innervates the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles, originates from the upper trunk 2 to 3 cm above the clavicle. In some patients, its origin can be higher, whereas in those with ruptures or avulsion injuries of the upper trunk, the nerve can retract to the retroclavicular or infraclavicular area. The suprascapular nerve courses laterally and posteriorly, deep to the omohyoid muscle, and enters the supraspinatus fossa through the suprascapular notch, deep to the superior transverse scapular ligament. The nerve then traverses the supraspinatus fossa deep to the supraspinatus muscle and winds around the lateral border of the spine of the scapula (the spinoglenoid notch) to enter the infraspinatus fossa. Its course renders it particularly vulnerable to traction lesions at the suprascapular notch, at the spinoglenoid notch, and within the supraspinatus and infraspinatus fossae. The suprascapular nerve is dissected bluntly with finger dissection to the suprascapular notch to confirm its integrity. This is done even when the location of a lesion proximal to the upper trunk is obvious to ensure that a secondary (distal) lesion is not present. To perform the nerve transfer, the suprascapular nerve is transected at its origin from the upper trunk (Fig. 34.18). If there is no scarring, it may be possible to dissect it further proximally and separate it from the upper trunk to increase the available length. The suprascapular nerve is coapted to the spinal accessory nerve without an intervening nerve graft.

![[Fig.34.18.png]]
> **Fig. 34.18** Transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve. (Used with permission of Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research. All rights reserved.)

Others have proposed a posterior approach when the spinal accessory nerve is used to reinnervate the suprascapular nerve, particularly when a segmental injury to the suprascapular nerve at the suprascapular notch is suspected. The advantage of this approach is a more distal repair to a healthy portion of the suprascapular nerve. The disadvantage is perhaps a reduced number of myelinated axons in the more distal terminal spinal accessory nerve and the need for prone or lateral decubitus positioning to enable a posterior approach to the scapula. Both the spinal accessory and the suprascapular nerves can be exposed via a transverse incision at the superior border of the scapula. The suprascapular nerve is located midway between the superior angle of the scapula medially and the acromion laterally. After identifying the nerve at the suprascapular notch, the superior transverse ligament is divided, and the nerve is dissected as far proximally as possible. The spinal accessory nerve is located at a point 40% of the distance between the dorsal midline (spinous processes) and the acromion. It can be identified deep to the trapezius muscle, which is separated parallel to the course of its fibers. The spinal accessory nerve is divided as far distally as possible to achieve a coaptation without tension.

Expected outcomes. The expected outcome of transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve for restoration of shoulder abduction is influenced by multiple factors, including the type of injury (C5- C6 versus pan- plexus injury) and the number of transfers performed (concomitant axillary reinnervation or not). The range of outcomes reported in the literature is wide. A metaanalysis of the world literature reported that 92% of patients recovered some antigravity shoulder abduction with nerve transfer to the suprascapular nerve.Multiple other groups have reported a range of results, with mean degrees of abduction of between 45 and 122 degrees. Patients with an isolated C5- C6 palsy seem to have better outcomes than patients with a C5- C7 palsy, who seem to have better outcomes than those with a complete brachial plexus injury. The function of the serratus anterior muscle may play a role in these outcomes. Patients with partial innervation of the serratus anterior (from an intact C7 nerve) may achieve significantly better range of shoulder abduction and flexion because of better scapular stability. The variability in results may also be due to the difficulty and complexity of assessing the outcome of shoulder motion (either by range of motion or muscle grading). The outcome of transferring the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve for restoration of external rotation is less frequently reported, but it varies from 0 to 118 degrees of external rotation, depending on the injury pattern and the studies (Fig. 34.19).

**Transfer to the musculocutaneous nerve.** Transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the musculocutaneous nerve is occasionally used to restore elbow flexion (Fig. 34.20). However, we more frequently choose to transfer ulnar nerve fascicles (in C5- C6 injuries) or intercostal nerves (in pan- plexus injuries) to the motor branch of the biceps. Use of the spinal accessory nerve requires an interposition nerve graft, which may degrade results.

Operative procedure. The musculocutaneous nerve may be exposed through an extended deltopectoral or a proximal arm skin incision. The musculocutaneous nerve originates from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus just distal to the coracoid process. After it pierces the coracobrachialis muscle, the nerve runs on the deep surface of the biceps. The musculocutaneous nerve then gives rise to the motor branch or branches to the biceps, which enter the muscle approximately 12 cm distal to the acromion. Two types of distribution of motor branches to the two heads of the biceps are found: a common trunk (pattern 1), which will divide into two branches (to the short and long heads of the biceps), and two separate nerve branches (pattern 2), the branch to the short head generally originating more proximally on the musculocutaneous nerve. Intercommunicating nerve fibers can be found between the motor branches. After traveling a distance within the short and long heads of the biceps (median distance of 1 and 2 cm, respectively, in pattern 1 or 3 cm for each branch in pattern 2), the main branches divide into terminal branches distributed throughout the muscle bellies. The musculocutaneous nerve then gives rise to the motor branch or branches of the brachialis muscle (17 cm distal to the acromion) and continues as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Once the musculocutaneous nerve and its branches are identified and dissected, the nerve is cut just after it branches to the coracobrachialis muscle. A sural nerve graft (typically 9 to 10 cm long) is used to connect the spinal accessory nerve to the musculocutaneous nerve. The nerve graft is usually placed beneath the clavicle, coapted first to the spinal accessory nerve and then to the musculocutaneous nerve. The repair is performed with 9- 0 or 10- 0 nylon without tension under magnification with the operating microscope.

Expected outcomes. A metaanalysis of the outcome of transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the musculocutaneous nerve for restoration of elbow flexion showed that 77% of patients achieved biceps strength of M3 and 29% achieved strength of M4. The results of spinal accessory nerve transfer (with an interposition graft) versus intercostal nerve transfer (without an interposition graft) for restoration of M3- strength elbow flexion were comparable (77% versus 72%), but significantly lower for restoration of M4- strength elbow flexion (29% versus 41%; *P* < .001).

#### Ulnar Nerve Fascicular Transfer to the Biceps Motor Branch

In patients with preserved C8- T1 function, transfer of a functioning ulnar nerve motor fascicle for reinnervation of the biceps muscle has gained popularity because of its technical ease and reliability. This technique has largely replaced the use of intercostal nerve transfers for reinnervation of elbow flexion in cases of C5- C6 and C5- 7 palsy. It is often done in these groups of patients in conjunction with other nerve transfers, such as transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve and the triceps motor branch to the axillary nerve for reconstruction of shoulder function. We and others have not recommended using in patients with C5- C8 injury due to suboptimal results (poorer elbow flexion and higher risk of downgrading ulnar nerve function).

### SURGICAL ANATOMY

The larger of the two terminal branches of the medial cord of the brachial plexus continues as the ulnar nerve, whereas the smaller terminal branch is the medial cord contribution to the median nerve. In the axilla and upper part of the arm, the ulnar nerve runs inferior and medial to the axillary artery. It passes anterior to the triceps muscle and enters the ulnar groove between the medial epicondyle of the humerus and the olecranon. In the upper half of the arm, the ulnar nerve has rich interfascicular connections and 6 to 10 fascicles, each of which contains both motor and sensory nerve fibers. According to Sunderland’s intraneural topography, the posteromedial fascicles contain the motor fibers to the forearm muscles and the anterolateral fascicles tend to contain the motor fibers to the intrinsic muscles of the hand.

**Dissection and transfer.** Both the musculocutaneous and ulnar nerves are exposed through a longitudinal medial skin incision in the proximal upper part of the arm. The musculocutaneous nerve and its three branches (motor branch to the biceps, motor branch to the brachialis, and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve) are identified. After the type of motor branch to the biceps (common or separated branches pattern) is confirmed, the branch is divided sufficiently proximal to be long enough to reach the ulnar nerve (Figs. 34.21 and 34.22). The ulnar nerve is identified and intraneural dissection is performed under loupe or microscope magnification. The ulnar fascicles are tested with a nerve stimulator, and the fascicles that innervate the flexor carpi ulnaris muscle, generally located posteromedially, are identified. Others that do not use nerve stimulation have not reported complications; the ulnar nerve fascicles at this level are mixed and redundant, and all fascicles contain a mix of motor and sensory nerve fibers. Depending on the size of the motor branch to the biceps, one or two fascicles are selected. These chosen fascicles are transected distally under magnification and with enough length to approximate to the biceps motor branch without tension. They are coapted to the motor branch with 9- 0 or 10- 0 nylon epineural suture and reinforced with fibrin glue.

Contraindications. Patients with ulnar nerve distribution weakness and sensory deficit preoperatively are not candidates for this technique.

Complications. Some patients will experience transient numbness in the ulnar nerve distribution postoperatively. These sensory changes will improve over a period of days to months and are rarely permanent. Occasionally, patients will have transient muscle weakness of the ulnar- innervated hand intrinsic muscles. Few permanent deficits have been reported. Most patients gain powerful grip and pinch strength during the postoperative rehabilitation program; it may be better than their preoperative strength. Whether this is due to progressive muscle strengthening exercises or a tincture of time in a patient who is spontaneously recovering is unknown.

Expected outcomes. Most authors emphasize the quick reinnervation of the biceps muscle after this procedure as an important advantage. Many patients have good recovery of biceps contraction beginning several months after the procedure. More than 90% of patients achieved biceps strength of British Medical Research Council (BMRC) grade 3 or 4 (Fig. 34.23).

#### Median Nerve Fascicular Transfer to the Brachialis Motor Branch

In 2005, double- nerve fascicular transfer to restore elbow flexion was described in patients with upper pattern brachial plexus injury.This procedure combines reinnervation of the biceps muscle with reinnervation of the brachialis muscle, the most powerful elbow flexor (Fig. 34.24). Double- nerve transfer has improved outcomes in some series. In patients with C5- C7 injury patterns, single- nerve transfer may be selected over a dual- nerve fascicle depending on the distribution of the hand and forearm weakness, and some authors have recommended not performing this technique in patients with C5- C7 injury. However, we and others have used it in patients with C5- C7 injury and achieved satisfactory recovery without adverse sequelae. The question of whether a single- nerve transfer versus a double- nerve transfer for elbow flexion is necessary has been the subject of recent publications. Our group compared the torque strength of a single- fascicle versus a double- fascicle nerve transfer to the biceps and brachialis motor branches, respectively. Overall, single- nerve transfers did not outperform double- nerve transfers, and the two types of transfers had similar torque strength. In a prospective comparative study, elbow flexion strength did not differ between groups treated with a single- fascicle or double- fascicle transfer. For this reason, the surgeon needs to carefully decide if the second fascicle transfer from the median nerve to the brachialis is necessary in their patients.

**Dissection and transfer.** The same medial arm exposure described for the ulnar nerve fascicular transfer is used to expose the median nerve and the brachialis motor branch. As described previously, the musculocutaneous nerve is identified in the proximal part of the arm. The biceps motor branch is seen, and several centimeters more distally, the brachialis motor branch is identified along with the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Identification of the brachialis motor branch can be confirmed by seeing it enter the muscle. It is distinguished from the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, which is larger and runs more superficially; gentle traction on the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve will tent the skin in the proximal part of the forearm. The brachialis branch can be dissected proximally up to the common stem, where the biceps branch originates. The median nerve is superficial to the brachial artery and vein; it is the largest of the nerves at this level. Intraneural dissection is performed under magnification, and several fascicles are isolated. We prefer to map the nerve with a portable stimulator to identify a fascicle producing wrist flexion (via the flexor carpi radialis [C6- 7]), when it is functioning. Other groups have used fascicles innervating the flexor digitorum superficialis. The chosen fascicle is dissected over a segment long enough that when it is divided distally, it can be transferred and reach the brachialis motor branch. The nerves are coapted with epineural 9- 0 or 10- 0 nylon suture without tension and reinforced with fibrin glue.

Contraindications. Patients with weakness in the median nerve distribution preoperatively, specifically affecting finger flexors or intrinsic muscles, are not candidates for this technique. In patients with C5- 7 injuries, electrodiagnostic testing of the median nerve musculature in the forearm is recommended to identify an appropriate motor donor.

Complications. As for ulnar nerve fascicular transfers, some patients will experience transient numbness in the median nerve distribution postoperatively that will improve over a period of days to months and is rarely permanent. Occasionally, patients will have transient muscle weakness of the anterior interosseous innervated or median innervated intrinsic hand muscles. Few permanent deficits have been reported.

#### Intercostal Nerve Transfer

Intercostal nerves have been widely used over several decades for reinnervation of the musculocutaneous nerve, and their application has been extended to reinnervation of the triceps, deltoid, and serratus anterior muscles; for transfer to a FFMT; and for hand sensation. Historically, transfer to more distal motor targets has been attempted, but the long- term outcomes of these transfers were unreliable, and too frequently they were functionless. Currently, intercostal to musculocutaneous nerve transfers are most frequently performed for reconstruction of elbow flexion in patients with pan- plexus injuries and less commonly for patients with upper brachial plexus injury (C5- C6 or C5- C7). They are likewise used for innervation of the triceps and median sensory reinnervation in the second stage of a double FFMT for prehensile reconstruction (discussed later).

Patients who are being considered for this procedure should be selected with caution if they have a history of rib fracture, thoracotomy, or chest tube placement because there is an incidence of an unusable corresponding intercostal nerve. Although it is possible to use both the sensory and motor components of each intercostal nerve to provide sensory and motor reinnervation, motor reinnervation alone is usually the goal of this procedure. In our experience, the third to sixth intercostal nerves have the most length for reaching the musculocutaneous nerve. By extending the dissection anteriorly to the costochondral junction, sufficient intercostal nerve length is gained, which permits direct coaptation to the target motor branch and thus eliminates the need for interposition nerve grafts. There are anthropomorphic differences between races, and it has been the experience of some authors that direct transfer may be more difficult in Caucasian individuals. The second or seventh intercostal nerve can also be used as needed, but adequate length and good positioning for transfer are less reliable for the musculocutaneous nerve. The second intercostal is readily transferred to the long thoracic nerve in patients with C5- C7 injuries and a denervated serratus. Finally, transfer of the first intercostal nerve to the suprascapular nerve was suggested in an anatomic study and case report.

### SURGICAL ANATOMY

Intercostal nerves run within the intercostal space along the pleural side of the internal intercostal muscles (Fig. 34.25). Posteriorly, these nerves pass between the two layers of intercostal muscles that insert on the posterior thirds of the ribs. Just posterior to the axilla, they enter deep to the intercostal muscles and run anteriorly between the transverse thoracic muscles and the endothoracic aponeurosis. The lateral cutaneous branch pierces the intercostal muscle toward the skin and divides into a dorsal and a ventral branch. The anterior cutaneous branch pierces the pectoralis major close to the sternum and ramifies into the skin.

![[Fig.34.25.png]]
> **Fig. 34.25** Anatomy of the intercostal nerve. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

**Intercostal nerve dissection.** The third to sixth intercostal nerves are explored through an inframammary incision extending from the midaxillary line to the costochondral junction (Fig. 34.26). This incision typically connects with the proximal arm incision used for exposure of the musculocutaneous nerve. Other groups are using different incisions, such as a vertical one in the anterior axillary line or a shorter transverse one on the chest, with tunneling of the intercostal nerves; we have found that with the classic inframammary incision we can achieve dissection of multiple intercostal nerves of adequate length. The subcutaneous tissue and underlying pectoralis major and minor muscles are elevated from their distal insertions so that the muscles themselves are not injured. In the axillary region, the intercostobrachial nerve, a large branch of the second intercostal nerve, is identified and protected. Care should be taken to not injure this nerve because it may be used as a donor nerve for sensory restoration of the hand. The anterior surfaces of the selected ribs, as well as their intercostal musculature, are exposed. When the serratus anterior muscle is functional, it should be preserved with its nerve supply; the long thoracic nerve is found on the chest wall coursing down posterior to the midaxillary line along with the thoracodorsal vessels. The anterior surface of each rib is incised and the periosteum is circumferentially elevated while great care is taken not to injure the underlying pleura. We prefer to mobilize the rib with a periosteal elevator, place an umbilical tape around it, and with the help of an assistant elevating the rib, identify and dissect the intercostal nerve. We do not perform osteotomy of the rib or partial rib resection as is occasionally done by others. The intercostal nerve is identified by carefully incising the periosteal sleeve of the rib in the midclavicular line. This incision is performed at the caudal border of the rib where the motor intercostal nerve is located. The motor intercostal nerve can also be identified by tracing the lateral sensory branch proximally, if it was previously identified, or by finding the flash of bleeding from the intercostal artery and vein, which are in close proximity to the nerve. Electrical stimulation can be used to confirm the motor branch. When identified, the nerve is mobilized in a vessel loop and dissected. The motor branch is traced anteriorly toward the costochondral junction by gentle spreading of the intercostal muscles to gain the required length to reach the target nerves. The nerve is dissected to the midaxillary or posterior axillary line posteriorly. The lateral cutaneous sensory branch of the nerve (larger than the motor nerve) is found at a point between the anterior and midaxillary lines; it may be separated from the motor component as far posteriorly as necessary. Care is taken to open the rib periosteum posteriorly and inferiorly so that it will not compress the nerve once it is reflected for transfer. The technique is repeated for harvesting the other intercostal nerves required for reconstruction. After appropriate dissection, each intercostal nerve is transected distally and passed through the serratus anterior muscle into the axillary region. Pleural tear and air leak should be noted and treated either by pleural repair, air- tight skin closure, or placement of a chest tube, depending on the extent of the tear or leak.

**Transfer to the musculocutaneous nerve.** When intercostal nerves are used for restoration of elbow flexion, three or four intercostal nerves are generally coapted to the musculocutaneous nerve; an alternative is to transfer two to three intercostal nerves directly to the biceps motor branch (Fig. 34.27). To accomplish the nerve transfer, dissection of the musculocutaneous nerve is performed as described in a previous section. Special care is taken when dissecting the motor branch to the biceps, occasionally with the brachialis motor branch, proximally toward its branching from the lateral cord to separate the motor (biceps and brachialis motor branch) and sensory (lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve) portions of the musculocutaneous nerve as far proximally as possible. This technique will maximize the number of motor fibers and minimize the number of sensory fibers that will be targeted by the transfer. Moreover, it will provide a biceps (with or without the brachialis) motor branch with enough length to reach the transferred intercostal nerves without any tension and without the use of intervening nerve grafts. This retrograde dissection of the musculocutaneous nerve should be done carefully, and one should err on cutting on the lateral antebrachial cutaneous portion of the musculocutaneous nerve so that the biceps motor branch portion of the nerve is not injured or thinned. If the length of the biceps motor branch is insufficient for direct transfer, we prefer to perform the intercostal nerve transfer into the musculocutaneous nerve itself. We always try to perform a direct intercostal nerve transfer (i.e., without the use of interposition grafts) because of the improved outcomes with this technique, even if this translates into the use of an intercostal nerve that has been transected distally. Others, knowing that the intercostal nerves have more motor axons in their proximal parts, prefer to use the more proximal portion of the intercostal nerves (e.g., just distal to the lateral sensory branches). Transferring intercostal nerves requires balancing use of the proximal intercostal nerve (more motor axons) and performing direct transfers without the use of intervening nerve grafts (better outcomes). The nerve repair is done with the arm abducted and externally rotated to ensure that these motions will not lead to undue tension on the coaptation site during rehabilitation.

**Postoperative care specific to intercostal nerve to musculocu-taneous nerve transfer.** An early sign of reinnervation is the presence of chest pain when the biceps is pinched (positive squeeze test); this sign may be apparent 4 to 5 months postoperatively. Proximal biceps contraction without elbow joint movement, especially during deep inspiration, is usually recognized by 6 to 8 months postoperatively. With further reinnervation, distal biceps contraction without elbow joint movement is seen and a Tinel sign may appear in the distal part of the arm, lateral to the biceps tendon. Percussion of this area causes tingling in the chest; it develops around 12 months postoperatively. Elbow flexion against gravity generally appears 12 to 18 months after surgery, and voluntary flexion against resistance is recovered gradually.

A relationship between elbow movement and respiration is observed for 4 to 8 months after motor recovery, and volitional small action potentials synchronized with respiratory movement are noted. After 12 months, patients learn to flex the joint voluntarily without relating it to their respiratory rhythm. Twenty- four months after surgery, the respiratory rhythm will still affect the interval of spiked discharges on EMG, but after 32 months, biceps activity is completely distinct from the respiratory cycle. Involuntary muscle contractions continue to occur when the patient coughs or sneezes. Some authors recommend aerobic conditioning programs with deep inspiration exercises (treadmill, stationary bike, abdominal “crunches”) to expedite recovery in the postoperative period, although no definitive studies are available.

**Contraindications.** Patients with a history of chest trauma, rib fractures, or phrenic nerve dysfunction can still be candidates for intercostal nerve transfer. However, both the surgeon and the patient should be aware that rib fractures are associated with a 10% chance of a nonfunctional intercostal nerve. Moreover, preoperative pulmonary function tests may be useful to assess respiratory reserve, especially in patients with concomitant phrenic nerve dysfunction; very rarely do low pulmonary function test results prevent use of the intercostals.

**Complications.** Intercostal nerve harvesting can generally be performed safely. Pleural effusion and acute respiratory distress syndrome are rare. Pulmonary function normalizes by 3 months.Lack of tension at the coaptation site and proper positioning of the shoulder are imperative to prevent rupture of the nerve repair and subsequent failure of the transfer.

**Expected outcomes.** Intercostal to musculocutaneous nerve transfers without interposition grafts for restoration of elbow flexion achieve strength of at least M3 in 72% of patients. If interposition nerve grafts are used, only 47% of patients will achieve strength of M3 or better. A metaanalysis did not show significant differences in outcome with the transfer of two or three to four intercostal nerves without an intervening graft, but the analysis lacked sufficient power. The time interval between the trauma and the reconstructive surgery and the age of the patient may influence the outcome.

#### Contralateral C7 Nerve Transfer

Contralateral C7 nerve transfer remains a controversial option with three potential issues: (1) whether it can be done safely, (2) whether it works well enough to justify the risk, and (3) whether independent function can be relearned in adults. Some surgeons have difficulty accepting that this normal nerve can generally be transected and transferred without neurologic sequelae. Based on a large clinical experience in Asia over a period of several decades, the safety and efficacy of this procedure have become accepted by many. It has been used as a donor nerve for shoulder, elbow, and hand targets (Fig. 34.31) and in some cases as a donor for a FFMT with variable reported outcomes. The ability of adult patients to learn independent function is also unknown. When transfer of the contralateral C7 nerve is being considered, the risks associated with the procedure must be carefully weighed against its potential benefit.

### SURGICAL ANATOMY

Although anatomic variability exists, the supraclavicular plexus usually receives contributions from five spinal nerves: C5, C6, C7, C8, and T1. It is important to realize that each spinal nerve generally sends axons to more than one major peripheral nerve and, in turn, each major nerve contains axons from more than one spinal nerve. Accordingly, muscles in the upper limb are often innervated by multiple nerves. Although the C7 spinal nerve contributes to the posterior cord as well as to the median, musculocutaneous, and even ulnar nerves, C7- innervated muscles are cross- innervated by other spinal nerves, primarily C6 and C8. Because isolated C7 division does not result in significant loss of any individual muscle function, both the ipsilateral and contralateral C7 nerve roots are a potential transferable source of axons. The posterior portion of C7 contains more motor fibers than the anterior portion does. Both the entire nerve and half of the C7 nerve (the latter procedure done to minimize risk) have been harvested with similar, albeit transient, deficits.

**Contralateral C7 nerve dissection and transfer.** When contralateral C7 nerve transfer is planned, the contralateral neck and shoulder should be prepared and draped. The contralateral “normal” plexus is exposed as described earlier. Many groups routinely expose all five nerves of the brachial plexus to determine its anatomic configuration and the contribution of each spinal nerve. The contralateral C7 nerve is identified. It may be divided into two portions at the level of the middle trunk or dissected distally to its anterior and posterior divisions. The motor function produced by the nerve is evaluated with a nerve stimulator. If the entire contralateral C7 nerve is being used as a transfer, elbow and wrist extension with C7 nerve stimulation is evaluated before its division. Recognizing the potential devastating effects of permanent weakness in the one remaining normal upper limb, we believe that if stimulation of C7 produces any contraction of hand muscles, its use is contraindicated. The nerve is transected distally at the level of the middle trunk or at the anterior and posterior divisions. When half of the contralateral C7 is transferred, the half producing maximal pectoralis major contraction is selected and transected distally. A subcutaneous tunnel is prepared in the upper chest region that leads to the skin incision in the ipsilateral brachial plexus. A prespinal or intraspinal (from a separate posterior exposure) route can shorten the distance for subsequent regeneration in comparison with the subcutaneous route.

If the contralateral C7 nerve is used to transfer to the median nerve in a patient with a pan- plexus injury, the ipsilateral ulnar nerve can be used as pedicled graft based on the superior ulnar collateral (SUC) vessels to eliminate the need for vascular anastomosis (Fig. 34.32). The SUC artery arises from the medial surface of the brachial artery at the midhumeral level, 14 to 22 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle of the humerus. It pierces the medial intermuscular septum, courses distally posterior to it, and joins the ulnar nerve on its posterior surface. The artery accompanies the ulnar nerve for 4 to 15 cm and gives rise to an average of three arteriae nervorum, which pass directly into the nerve. The SUC artery descends between the medial epicondyle and the olecranon. It ends deep to the flexor carpi ulnaris muscle by anastomosing with the posterior ulnar recurrent artery and the inferior ulnar collateral artery. Venae comitantes accompany the SUC artery and terminate in the venae comitantes of the brachial artery.

![[Fig.34.32.png]]
> **Fig. 34.32** Vascular anatomy of the ulnar nerve. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

If the ulnar nerve is to be used as a pedicled vascularized nerve graft, its distal end is cut at the wrist and mobilized in its full length to the level of the SUC by dividing the dorsal cutaneous branch as well as the motor branches to the extrinsic finger and wrist flexors (Fig. 34.33). The distal stump is passed under the skin of the upper part of the chest to the exposed contralateral (entire or hemi) C7 nerve. Bleeding should be seen from the end of the vascularized nerve. The C7 nerve is matched and coapted to the distal end of the ulnar nerve. The proximal end of the ulnar nerve is divided proximal to the SUC at a level that allows direct connection to the target nerve, such as the median nerve in the proximal part of the arm. When a vascularized ulnar nerve is not used in cases of C5 to C8 root avulsion or in cases of distal ulnar nerve injury, a vascularized superficial radial nerve or sural nerve graft can be used. For shoulder targets, some groups have recommended using the ulnar nerve as a free vascularized nerve graft. In this case the proximal end of the ulnar nerve is connected to the contralateral C7 nerve first, and the SUC artery and vein are anastomosed to locally available vessels such as the ipsilateral thoracodorsal trunk or the contralateral transverse cervical artery and vein. The distal end of the ulnar nerve is passed into the subcutaneous tunnel in the upper chest region and connected to the target nerve in the supraclavicular or infraclavicular region, such as the suprascapular nerve or the posterior cord. Other groups have used two conventional sural nerve grafts to connect the hemicontralateral C7 nerve to the shoulder targets.

**Postoperative care specific to contralateral C7 nerve transfer.** For the first 3 weeks postoperatively, the ipsilateral upper limb is immobilized. Over time, regeneration into the repaired nerve can be traced by an advancing Tinel sign. Because of the long distance to target muscles, reinnervation may take several years. After reinnervation, reeducation of the targeted muscle or muscles with EMG biofeedback techniques is started. Usually, muscles reinnervated by the contralateral C7 nerve can be activated by adducting, internally rotating, and extending the contralateral shoulder and by extending the contralateral elbow. A rehabilitation protocol for muscle strengthening is then initiated so that patients can try to learn to activate the muscle without assistance of the contralateral limb.

**Complications.** Immediately after surgery, temporary motor deficits can occur in shoulder extension and adduction, elbow extension, forearm pronation, wrist extension, and hand movement. Latissimus dorsi weakness can often be detected. Full functional recovery of the donor limb is usually documented within 6 months, and no long- term functional deficits have been reported in the literature. Sensory abnormalities in the C7 distribution are expected in all patients undergoing contralateral C7 transfer and may resolve after a few months or persist.

When an anatomic variation is present, such as a prefixed plexus with a significant C4 contribution or a postfixed plexus with a substantial T2 contribution (and a diminished C5 component), considerable deficits can occur after C7 transfer. Millesi suggested that preliminary ligation of the C7 nerve with subsequent patient evaluation, before division (at a subsequent stage), prevents this complication. Others recommend exploration of all five nerves and examination for the existence of a prefixed or postfixed plexus by identification of normal anatomic relationships and intraoperative use of electrical nerve stimulation.

The other potential complication is the development of severe neuropathic pain from division of the contralateral, normal C7 nerve. This problem has been particularly noted by surgeons in Europe and appears to be much less common in Asian patients.

If contralateral C7 nerve transfer is being considered, informed consent should be obtained; the complications and expected benefits of this procedure should be exhaustively discussed with the patient and family before a decision is made. Despite reports of minimal complications in the literature, the possibility of complications in the contralateral donor arm and the lack of independent function exist. Contralateral C7 transfer should not be undertaken without a thorough understanding of the potential risks and complications associated with this procedure.

**Expected outcomes.** Despite many authors recommending contralateral C7 nerve transfer to the median nerve, our outcomes have been disappointing, and patients achieve only minimal recovery of forearm motor strength, far from functional recovery. Other authors have recommended using the contralateral C7 for transfer to the suprascapular nerve or posterior cord or musculocutaneous nerve because of a better functional outcome.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: CONTRALATERAL C7 NERVE TRANSFER

Because of other options for achieving hand prehension and our poor results with this technique, we have abandoned the use of hemicontralateral C7 transfer, which was performed in more than 50 adults at our institution. We still occasionally perform a contralateral C7 nerve transfer in children with pan- plexal injuries acquired at birth or caused by trauma.

#### Other Nerve Transfers

Other intraplexal and extraplexal sources of nerve transfer have been introduced or are being investigated in an attempt to expand the available donors for primary nerve reconstruction in patients with brachial plexus injury. These transfers are less commonly used than the ones described earlier and will be discussed only briefly. In patients with partial brachial plexus injuries, intraplexal sources include the ipsilateral C7, medial pectoral, and thoracodorsal nerves; in patients with pan- plexus injuries, extraplexal sources include the phrenic, hypoglossal, platysma motor branch, and cervical plexus nerves, which are also available in patients with partial injuries. The use of a more “aggressive” approach to nerve transfer allows the reconstruction of additional targets, the reconstruction of more distal targets, and the possibility of improving outcomes. However, these transfers are still controversial because of different opinions on their risk- benefit ratio and functional outcome.

**Intraplexal nerve transfers** Ipsilateral C7 nerve transfer. In patients with C5- C6 brachial plexus injury, transfer of a portion (anterior division) or the whole ipsilateral C7 nerve has been suggested and used by some groups.Use of the ipsilateral C7 nerve is an extension of the contralateral C7 transfer as described previously.

Medial pectoral nerve transfer. Transferring the medial pectoral nerve to reinnervate brachial plexus elements is not a novel technique but remains controversial. Several authors have advocated use of the medial pectoral nerve for upper- pattern avulsion injuries to reinnervate the musculocutaneous or axillary nerve, or an isolated long thoracic nerve, with good success. Other authors do not recommend its use and instead prefer to use available alternative nerve transfers and to preserve the innervation of the pectoralis muscles for shoulder adduction. Because there are typically several large branches of the medial pectoral nerve, it is possible to use one branch while maintaining innervation of the pectoralis sternal head. The muscle is synergistic to shoulder abduction, and transfers to the axillary nerve have been reported with success.

Thoracodorsal nerve transfer. The thoracodorsal nerve, which innervates the latissimus dorsi, has been proposed and used by some as a donor nerve for transfer. It originates from the posterior cord and receives nerve fibers from the seventh, eighth, and sometimes sixth cervical nerves. It has been used to transfer to the long thoracic nerve,as well as the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves. The better functional outcomes obtained after nerve reconstruction in patients with preserved serratus anterior function suggest that reinnervation of the serratus anterior may be beneficial. Generally, sufficient length can be obtained (8 to 19 cm) for tension- free direct coaptation with all target nerves.

**Extraplexal nerve transfers** Phrenic nerve transfer. The phrenic nerve, located directly within the surgical field of the supraclavicular plexus, is an attractive source for nerve transfer. Because of its C3 and C4 contributions, it is often functional even in cases of complete brachial plexus avulsion. The phrenic nerve has been used for transfer to the suprascapular, musculocutaneous, and axillary nerves; excellent results have been achieved by several Asian and European groups. The techniques used include (1) supraclavicular harvesting with direct coaptation (e.g., suprascapular nerve transfer), (2) supraclavicular harvesting with the use of intervening nerve grafts (e.g., musculocutaneous or axillary nerve transfer), and (3) harvesting via a video- assisted thoracic surgery approach in which a longer phrenic nerve is obtained, one that can be directly transferred to the musculocutaneous nerve without an intervening nerve graft or extended with an interposition graft to the median nerve.

Despite good results obtained by certain groups, many surgeons have been reluctant to sacrifice the phrenic nerve because of the potential consequences of hemidiaphragm paralysis. The literature reports a 10% initial decrease in vital capacity and pulmonary function test results with few subjective symptoms, which return to baseline within 6 to 24 months. Long- term data are only beginning to emerge. Most studies have included young, thin, Asian patients, and extrapolation of the pulmonary risks to older, heavier patients, for example, is difficult. Our group has used the phrenic nerve as a transfer in 10 patients but stopped after the occurrence of serious but transient postoperative respiratory difficulties in one patient and lack of normalization of pulmonary function test values after 2 years in several others.

Hypoglossal nerve transfer. The hypoglossal nerve, commonly used for transfer to the facial nerve, has been suggested as a possible donor nerve for brachial plexus reconstruction. Both hemihypoglossal transfers and complete hypoglossal nerve transfers (with the use of an intervening nerve graft) have been attempted in small series with disappointing results. A few patients (complete hypoglossal nerve transfer) had morbidity related to the donor site, but despite tongue movement–related EMG activity in target muscles, only a few patients with a complete hypoglossal transfer achieved muscle strength of grade 3 or better. Regardless of the degree of reinnervation, no patient regained voluntary control of the reinnervated muscle. A single patient who underwent hemihypoglossal transfer to the musculocutaneous nerve has been reported to have independent BMRC grade 4 elbow flexion at long- term (8 years) follow- up. Because of the largely poor results reported in the literature, hypoglossal nerve transfer is typically not an option for reconstruction of brachial plexus injuries.

Platysma motor branch transfer. A recent anatomic study and case report proposed the platysma motor branch (from the facial nerve) as a nerve donor. In this single case, the motor branch of the platysma was used for transfer to the pectoralis major muscle with an intervening nerve graft. There was no deficit noted in platysma (lip depressor) function, probably because of supplementary innervation from the cervical plexus, and the patient regained shoulder adduction strength of M4. Further experience with this branch is being reported; its use seems promising because it is an expendable nerve, it can be sectioned without deficit, and relearning of motor control can be achieved.

Transfer of cervical plexus nerves. Many surgeons consider the cervical plexus nerves to be predominantly sensory nerves and therefore not useful for motor reinnervation. However, others have proposed the transfer of anterior cervical plexus nerves to elements of the brachial plexus. Bertelli and Ghizoni successfully transferred the two C3- 4 motor branches of the levator scapulae muscle to reinnervate the triceps with an interpositional graft. These motor nerves demonstrate adequate axon count and redundancy to potentially be used as donor transfers to additional denervated nerves, including the long thoracic and suprascapular nerves.

### Postoperative Care for Nerve Grafts and Nerve Transfers

In the immediate postoperative period after nerve repairs, our preference is to maintain the limb in a shoulder immobilizer for 3 weeks to allow strengthening of the nerve repair. However, others do not immobilize the limb. In all patients, physiotherapy is restarted after this period (3 weeks), with attention initially directed toward maintaining passive movement of all joints in the affected upper extremity. Follow- up is performed at regular intervals for a minimum of 2 to 3 years (preferably 5 years). When early clinical or electrophysiologic signs of motor reinnervation appear, active muscle exercises are started. Physiotherapy focused on reeducation and strengthening, including biofeedback, is very important. With therapy, patients can improve their control of the reinnervated muscles, and in those with antagonistic cocontractions, biofeedback can decrease the problem.

Timing of reinnervation of the target muscles depends on the length from the nerve donor source and the muscle end plates. Visible and palpable muscle contractions are typically not seen for an additional 2 months after early EMG signs of recovery, and the strength of the muscle tends to increase gradually afterward.

In patients with traumatic brachial plexus injury, long- term follow- up is necessary regardless of whether primary nerve surgery has been performed. Over time, patients can be evaluated for secondary reconstructive options once reinnervation has plateaued. Frequently, function can be further improved or refined with a range of surgical interventions, from minor to complex. The possible options are discussed in the section Secondary Reconstruction.

## FUNCTIONING FREE MUSCLE TRANSFER

An FFMT is the microvascular transfer of a muscle, as well as its nerve, for the purpose of restoring limb function. Use of FFMT for brachial plexus reconstruction was first reported by Ikuta and colleagues, who used it to restore elbow flexion in a child. Subsequently, FFMT has become an attractive option for many brachial plexus injuries, certainly in late presentations with absent function due to delay in referral or failed prior nerve surgery. Another valuable role has been to restore grasp in acute cases with avulsion of four or more nerve roots.The use of a long muscle with a proximally located neurovascular pedicle, such as the gracilis, makes such reconstructions possible. A viable gracilis transfer, when fixed to the clavicle proximally and tunneled to the forearm, can animate the wrist or hand. Because the motor point of the muscle is located at the shoulder, a nerve transfer to this area will reinnervate the muscle within 6 months or less. Because of the long distances, conventional or vascularized nerve grafts for reinnervation of the forearm and hand musculature have been demonstrated to be ineffective.

In this section, use of FFMT for reconstruction after acute brachial plexus injuries (primary reconstruction for grasp) is discussed. Its use in chronic situations, primarily for elbow flexion, is provided as part of secondary reconstruction options later in this chapter. The basic science, anatomy, and principles of free muscle transfer are briefly included but are described in detail in articles by Manktelow and colleagues and by Doi and colleagues. We refer the reader to these detailed articles for this important theoretical information.

### Indications

#### Prehension

Free muscle transfers performed in the first 3 to 6 months after brachial plexus injury have most frequently been indicated to restore prehension. Loss of hand and wrist function is the result of injury to C8 and T1 nerves and generally occurs in the global plexal injury. Most often these nerves are avulsed and irreparable. Attempts to graft or transfer for grasp in any case are generally unsuccessful due to the time and distances required to reach a target muscle. While some recent modifications have improved the contralateral C7 procedure, our experience has not been favorable.

Independent hand function requires fingers to independently close for grasp and open for release, with independent thumb positioning for key, precision, and chuck pinch. The complex coordinated activity of the many wrist, thumb, and finger extrinsic and intrinsic muscles required for this activity cannot generally be restored in adult patients with a global or complete plexus injury. The goal of achieving even simple composite grasp by nerve grafting or transfer has proven unreliable. Free microsurgical transfer of one, two, or even three expendable autogenous lower extremity muscles eliminates the problem of any preexisting period of muscle denervation. Proper selection of a transferred muscle, having both sufficient length and a proximal motor point, results in both a shortened reinnervation time and ability to move the wrist and/or finger motors. Motor control is generally provided by an extraplexal nerve transfer, as most acute cases will have four or five nerve root avulsions.

#### Elbow Flexion Alone

In cases where wrist and hand function is preserved, or when grasp function is deemed unachievable, free muscle transfers to provide elbow flexion alone, or elbow flexion with wrist extension may also be considered using a single free muscle. A review of our clinical experience has demonstrated FFMT to provide superior results compared with intercostal nerve transfer for biceps. It is also reasonable to expect improved elbow flexion results if a FFMT is added to simultaneous biceps reinnervation, similar to the double fascicular transfer for both biceps and brachialis in upper trunk injury. We have followed this principle until recently, when review of our results demonstrated that transfer of intercostal motor nerves to both biceps and gracilis FFMT did not improve motor strength compared with FFMT alone.

### Requirements

#### Shoulder, Elbow, and Wrist Stability

Shoulder stability, as well as simultaneous reconstruction of antagonist elbow extension function is essential when restoring grasp with a FFMT. The important concept of “one muscle, one function” for tendon transfers also applies to free muscle transfer. A gracilis muscle fixed to the clavicle and acromion and used to provide finger flexion must first cross the glenohumeral, elbow, and wrist joints. If not addressed by other means, FFMT contraction will first act to reduce shoulder subluxation resulting from paralysis of the rotator cuff and deltoid, diminishing the muscle’s potential excursion and force available for distal joints. Restoration of shoulder stability, whether by nerve graft, tendon transfers, or arthrodesis of the glenohumeral articulation, is imperative.

At the elbow, the need to provide an active stabilizing antagonist muscle is even more important. The same gracilis muscle crossing anterior to the axis of rotation will use most of its available force and excursion for elbow flexion *unless* triceps (extensor) function is maintained or restored.

The wrist must also be positioned by arthrodesis or a separate free muscle transfer if useful finger flexion is to be achieved. These concepts have been clearly detailed by Doi and coworkers.

### Contraindications

#### Vascular Injury

Vascular injuries are common in adult brachial plexus injury, occurring in 10% or more of patients. The incidence is higher when the first rib is fractured. Avulsion of the subclavian or axillary artery is generally considered a contraindication for free muscle transfer, as patency of the thoracoacromial or thoracodorsal vessels is usually needed for FFMT to be successful. At times, collateral flow may permit filling of these vessels. If imaging of the subclavian and axillary branches shows that their blood flow is sufficient, even when a more proximal arterial occlusion is present, FFMT may be successful and provide appropriate function.

#### Joint Stiffness

Some patients with avulsion injury develop stiff and edematous hands with intrinsic- minus posturing (a flattened palm and fixed finger metacarpophalangeal joint extension and interphalangeal joint flexion contractures). Success in these situations will require restoration of supple joints. A dystrophic appearing hand often proves recalcitrant to physical and surgical attempts to permit free joint motion. Already at a significant disadvantage, a single FFMT will generally not restore useful hand function in this situation.

### Preoperative Planning

#### Selection of a Donor Muscle

When planning free muscle transfer, a suitable potential donor muscle must have a vascular pedicle permitting transfer, sufficient length and excursion for its new role, and adequate force and must also be expendable. The latissimus dorsi, gracilis, adductor longus, and rectus femoris muscles have all been used as donor muscles for FFMT and meet the general criteria just listed. When reconstruction is planned for elbow flexion alone, another option is to reinnervate the ipsilateral latissimus dorsi and transfer it as a functioning pedicled flap. This is ideally performed within 6 to 9 months after injury. In addition, it is used occasionally as a free functioning transfer from the contralateral side. The rectus femoris muscle has been used successfully to restore elbow flexion. Transient reduction in knee extension strength occurs after its harvest, so some physiotherapy is required. It provides a good fit for strength but not excursion compared with the biceps.

The gracilis is the most frequently used free functioning muscle. It is a strap muscle rather than a pennate muscle, with excellent fiber length, but it has considerably less cross- sectional area than the biceps that it is intended to replace. Thus data suggest that it is poorly matched for strength but well matched for needed excursion. The gracilis generates sufficient force to flex the elbow against gravity in most cases, however, and has other desirable qualities. Most importantly, the proximal position of its neurovascular pedicle permits rapid reinnervation from either the spinal accessory or intercostal motor nerves, whereas its length and shape facilitate its passage into the forearm to animate the wrist and hand. A single gracilis FFMT, when combined with other nerve grafts or transfers is an extensive procedure. Two such microsurgical procedures performed separately as a double free muscle transfer has the potential to provide both grasp and release, provided shoulder, elbow, and wrist stability can be achieved, as described by Doi. Sananpanich et al. have described the use of a combined gracilis/adductor longus transfer, using a common vascular pedicle and two independent obturator nerve branches to permit a one- stage double- muscle transfer useful for combined finger flexion and wrist extension, for example. The same flap has been used by others to provide shoulder abduction and elbow flexion.

#### Preoperative Examination and Imaging

In addition to the detailed neurologic examination required for assessment of all brachial plexus injuries, particular attention is paid to the vessels and nerves required to provide nutrition and function to the transferred muscle(s). A conventional arteriogram, or more commonly a magnetic resonance arteriogram or CT angiogram, is required to assess the integrity of the axillary and subclavian vessels. The thoracoacromial trunk artery, a branch of the second part of the axillary artery, is most usually selected for arterial inflow. When proximal flow is disturbed by associated trauma, free muscle transfer is generally contraindicated, although collateral flow may at times allow a successful outcome.Injury to the spinal accessory nerve may occur with brachial plexus injury. If used to power the FFMT, its strength and appearance should be assessed by physical exam, and its innervation by preoperative EMG. While chest wall trauma including pneumothorax and multiple rib fractures are common in patients with brachial plexus injury, the risk of intercostal nerve injury is no more than 10% of fractured ribs.

### Operative Procedures

#### Gracilis Harvest

The gracilis is a superficial muscle that lies on the medial aspect of the thigh. A branch of the obturator nerve enters the muscle obliquely just cephalad to the major vascular pedicle, at a point 6 to 12 cm from its origin. The dominant arterial supply is provided by a branch from the profunda femoris artery 8 to 12 cm from the muscular origin and is approximately 4 to 6 cm long. The vascular pedicle is dissected between the adductor magnus (posteriorly) and adductor longus (anteriorly).

Harvest of the gracilis for FFMT (Fig. 34.34) can be accomplished with three short incisions. The dissection starts with identification of the distal tendon at the tibial insertion. This is a longitudinal incision made over the pes anserinus. The sartorius fascia is elevated. The pes anserinus is identified. The superior tendon is that of the gracilis muscle, whereas the inferior tendon is that of the semitendinosus muscle. The gracilis tendon is isolated; its distal attachment is maintained. A distal medial thigh incision is made to identify the myotendinous junction of the gracilis. All attachments to the gracilis tendon are freed at this level. By distal and outward retraction, the muscle is placed on tension and can easily be palpated; the outline of the underlying muscle is drawn on the skin along its length.

The gracilis is routinely transferred with a proximal skin paddle for postoperative flap monitoring. A handheld Doppler device is used to identify myocutaneous perforators in the proximal third of the muscle flap. An elliptical incision is designed to incorporate these perforators in the skin paddle. The anterior incision is made, and dissection is directed toward the adductor longus muscle. The fascia over the adductor longus muscle is incised and mobilized with the skin paddle to preserve any septocutaneous perforators. The adductor longus thus exposed is retracted gently anteriorly to expose the interval between the adductor longus and adductor magnus. The gracilis covers this interval superficially. Its major vascular pedicle can be visualized traversing this interval perpendicularly, entering the gracilis together with its obturator motor nerve. The motor branch from the superficial division of the obturator nerve follows an oblique course toward the gracilis, lying in the same adductor interval cephalad to the vessels and oriented approximately 45 degrees to them. It is identified with a nerve stimulator at this point and mobilized toward the obturator foramen. Additional motor and cutaneous branches encountered may be preserved by blunt proximal mobilization from the nerve, providing a longer gracilis motor nerve stump, often 10 cm or more in length. It is important to maximize the nerve length if a nerve graft is to be avoided. This is particularly true when the spinal accessory nerve is used and the gracilis motor nerve must be passed into the supraclavicular fossa.

The major vascular pedicle is adherent to the adductor longus due to multiple vascular perforators to this muscle. These must be identified and ligated, thus progressively mobilizing the arteriovenous pedicle from medial toward its profunda femoris artery and vein origin. Dissection can be continued to the profunda vessels to obtain the maximal pedicle length. The posterior incision of the skin paddle is made, and dissection is directed posteriorly to the fascia covering the adductor magnus muscle. The adductor magnus fascia is incised and mobilized to form a fascial sling together with the adductor longus fascia that envelops the gracilis muscle and helps to ensure skin paddle viability. The skin paddle is sutured to the adductor magnus and longus fascia mobilized with the muscle to protect these cutaneous perforators.

Before harvest and division of the muscle’s proximal and distal attachments, resting tension may be marked with sutures placed at 5- cm intervals to facilitate appropriate tensioning of the muscle. The distal tendon is divided, passed into the proximal thigh wound, and pulled through. The secondary pedicle is identified and ligated at this time. The fascial sling around the gracilis is preserved by complete release of the fascia from the adductor magnus and adductor longus muscles. The distal tendon is temporarily replaced in its native bed, and the proximal tendon is released from the pubic ramus. The vascular pedicle is not divided until the recipient site is fully prepared, preferably by a second surgical team. This includes dissection of the recipient vessels, donor nerves, as well as fixation in the proximal clavicle and acromion. A subcutaneous tunnel or pocket is prepared in the arm and the distal tendon of insertion exposed, depending on the purpose of the procedure. Only then is the vascular pedicle ligated proximally and divided. Any remaining soft tissue attachments are released, and the muscle is transferred to the arm.

Once transferred, the proximal tendon is expeditiously secured to the anterior surface of the acromion and lateral clavicle with its neurovascular pedicle positioned in proximity to the prepared thoracoacromial trunk artery and cephalic vein. We use suture anchors preplaced for this purpose, to quickly attach the gracilis. The muscle is passed through previously prepared subcutaneous tunnels in the arm and (if indicated) forearm toward its intended attachment point. Distal tenorrhaphy and adjustment of final tension is not performed, however, until the vascular connections and nerve transfer are completed. The vascular anastomoses are typically performed end- to- end to a branch of the thoracoacromial artery and the cephalic vein. Perfusion is reestablished with less than 1 hour of ischemia time. Meticulous neurorrhaphy to two to three intercostal motor nerves or to the spinal accessory nerve is performed with epineural sutures and fibrin glue as close to the muscle as possible to minimize reinnervation time. When intercostal neurorrhaphy is performed, the shoulder is abducted 90 degrees and maximally externally rotated to minimize the effect of later shoulder motion on nerve tension.

Appropriate muscle tension is important for a good result and can most easily be achieved by restoration of the original muscle resting length via superficial sutures placed at measured intervals along the length of the muscle before dissection as originally described by Manktelow and colleagues. Observation of an appropriate tenodesis effect of the transfer is another indication of appropriate resting muscle length.

**Double free muscle transfer.** Our modified double free gracilis muscle transfer technique consists of five reconstructive steps performed in two operative procedures. During the first operation, the steps are (1) exploration of the brachial plexus and reconstruction of the proximal ruptured motor nerves, if possible and (2) transplantation of the contralateral gracilis, innervated by a spinal accessory nerve transfer, for elbow flexion and finger or wrist extension (Fig. 34.35). In the second operation, three remaining steps are performed: (3) a second free functioning gracilis transfer, innervated by the fifth and sixth intercostal nerves, for finger flexion (Fig. 34.36); (4) use of the third and fourth intercostal nerves for transfer to the motor branch of the triceps brachii muscle for elbow extension (Fig. 34.37); and (5) coaptation of the intercostal sensory rami to the lateral cord contribution of the median nerve to restore sensibility of the hand (Fig. 34.38). In addition, secondary reconstruction is often required, including tendon transfers for glenohumeral stabilization and abduction/external rotation, as well as arthrodesis of the thumb carpometacarpal and/or interphalangeal joints and the wrist joint. Tenolysis of one or both of the transferred muscles may also be necessary.

Timing of the various reconstructive procedures is important and guided by several criteria. Steps 1 and 2 are performed at the first stage of the operation, and steps 3 to 5 are performed at the second stage, usually 2 or 3 months after the first operation. The secondary reconstruction is done approximately 1.5 years after the first stage of the operation, depending on the extent of recovery.

Brachial plexus exploration is described in detail earlier. In the first stage, simultaneous elbow flexion and finger extension are reconstructed with FFMT by using the spinal accessory as the motor nerve. We have preferentially connected the muscle for wrist extension rather than the extensor digitorum, as described by Doi and colleagues, for three reasons: (1) The free muscle at times is not able to extend the wrist as well as the fingers, thereby resulting in wrist drop and poor hand function; (2) the development of independent wrist extension augments grasp strength by the tenodesis effect; and (3) the release function of fingers is still possible with passive wrist flexion. The donor gracilis muscle from the contralateral thigh is selected because the orientation and location of its neurovascular bundle match the site of the donor vessels. The length of the gracilis muscle should be sufficient to span the distance between the acromion and midforearm without tension. This is verified by measuring the length of the gracilis muscle from its pubic origin to its tibial insertion. The donor muscle is harvested from its proximal origin to the distal attachment.

The distal portion of the spinal accessory nerve is dissected, divided, and transferred. The motor nerve of the transferred muscle is passed behind the clavicle and coapted to the distal branch of the spinal accessory nerve in the supraclavicular area. The nutrient vessels of the free gracilis muscle are anastomosed to the thoracoacromial artery and vein or to the cephalic vein. The transferred muscle is placed superficially over the anterior part of the deltoid and is attached to the acromion with nonabsorbable sutures or a soft tissue anchoring system. A simple and straight route for the free muscle is created from its new origin to its final insertion to maximize the force of contraction. It is placed on the anterolateral aspect of the arm and dorsal surface of the forearm. To prevent bowstringing, the free muscle is passed deep to the brachioradialis and radial wrist extensor muscles just distal to the elbow.

This position of the muscle is optimal for elbow flexion and finger extension; however, grip strength may weaken when the elbow is flexed. Correct muscle tension is reproduced in the upper limb before final suturing of the muscle to the finger or wrist extensors. The original muscle length is restored by stretching the muscle until the distance between the markers is once again 5 cm. Tension is adjusted with the shoulder in 60 degrees of abduction and 15 degrees of anterior flexion, the elbow in 150 degrees of flexion, the wrist in neutral, and the fingers in full extension. After adjusting the tension, the position of coaptation is marked over the tendon of the extensor digitorum and the donor muscle tendon. The elbow is flexed to 90 degrees, and with the wrist in neutral and the fingers fully flexed, the tenorrhaphies between the donor muscle tendon and extensor digitorum tendon are completed at the previously marked sites. The appropriateness of tension in the transfer is evaluated by using the tenodesis principle.

Reconstruction of finger flexion is done by using a second free muscle transfer. It is performed 2 to 3 months after the first operation, once improvement is noted in postoperative contracture of the elbow and finger joints. The third to sixth intercostal nerves are dissected as far as the midclavicular line and transferred to the axillary region. The fifth and sixth intercostal nerves are used as the donor motor nerve for the second gracilis muscle transplant. The gracilis is harvested from the ipsilateral thigh after ensuring that it will reach the midforearm from the second rib. The proximal end is sutured to the second and third ribs. To activate finger flexion, it is placed on the medial aspect of the upper part of the arm and forearm so that it does not act as a secondary elbow flexor. Prevention of bowstringing across the antecubital fossa requires that it be passed beneath the pronator teres and flexor carpi ulnaris. We do not hesitate to create an additional antecubital pulley using the flexor carpi ulnaris.

The distal gracilis tendon is passed deep to the flexor pronator origin, just distal to the elbow, to lie adjacent to the flexor digitorum profundus muscles and tendons. The flexor digitorum profundi tendons are first interconnected to provide a cascade of increasing flexion from the index finger to the small finger, and they are then joined with the gracilis tendon with a Pulvertaft weave; an additional flexor carpi radialis tendon graft is often required to prolong the gracilis. Muscle tension is determined with the principles described earlier. When the tension is adjusted, the shoulder, elbow, and wrist are in the same position as described earlier. The fingers are kept in full flexion. The nutrient vessels of the gracilis are anastomosed to the thoracodorsal artery and vein. The fifth and sixth intercostal nerves are transferred directly without tension and without an intervening nerve graft to the motor nerve of the second muscle transplant. The third and fourth intercostal nerves are similarly transferred to the motor branch of the triceps brachii muscle to activate the elbow extensors, important to stabilize the elbow to permit grasp.

To restore hand sensibility, the sensory rami of the intercostal nerves are sutured to the lateral cord contribution to the median nerve of the brachial plexus at the second operation (see Fig. 34.38).

**Postoperative protocol.** Postoperative monitoring of the muscle is important because prompt detection of vascular impairment may allow salvage of the gracilis flap. A variety of methods are in clinical use, including implantable Doppler systems, duplex sonography, near infrared spectroscopy laser Doppler flowmetry, and, recently, assessment of muscle contractility. We have generally relied upon skin perfusion for monitoring, together with Doppler examination of the cutaneous perforators. Aftercare is critical for outcomes. We immobilize the elbow for 6 weeks in a position of 100 degrees of elbow flexion and neutral supination of the forearm in a shoulder immobilizer. After this period, passive range of motion is begun, avoiding shoulder abduction greater than 60 degrees if intercostal nerves have been transferred. Once reinnervation has begun, active range- of- motion exercises (with gravity eliminated and aided by electromyographic biofeedback) are begun. Maximum recovery requires 18 to 24 months.

Recently this conventional postoperative regimen has been compared with an early passive mobilization protocol that began immediate elbow and digit passive mobilization. Doi uses compression of the tendinous portion of the transferred gracilis at the elbow to move the fingers beginning 1 week after surgery, with the elbow in extension to apply tension to the transferred muscle. Early motion has prevented the need for tenolysis in the early motion group, compared with a 26% incidence of tenolysis with the prior rehabilitation protocol.

Low- intensity electrical stimulation of the paralyzed target muscles (the two transferred gracilis muscles, the triceps brachii, and the supraspinatus and infraspinatus if the suprascapular nerve has been repaired) can be started during the third postoperative week and may be continued until EMG- confirmed reinnervation is noted in the muscles. The early motion protocol described by Doi may provide superior results compared with 6 weeks of elbow and finger immobilization.

After EMG documentation of reinnervation of the transferred muscle (usually 3 to 8 months postoperatively), EMG biofeedback techniques are started to train the transferred muscles to move the elbow and fingers. Muscular reeducation is indicated when patients display minimal active contraction of an identified muscle or muscle group. The initial goal of reeducation is for patients to reactivate voluntary control of the muscle. As the muscle strengthens, active motion begins, first with gravity eliminated and lastly against gravity. Rehabilitation should continue for at least 2 years after surgery so that use of the reconstructed hand for daily activities can be mastered.

**Subsequent surgery.** Effective grasp following a global plexus injury requires restoration of shoulder stability, elbow extension, and wrist stabilization or active extension. The double free muscle procedure is one such method that at times accomplishes this goal. Supplemental procedures may include a subsequent shoulder arthrodesis or tendon transfer, triceps reconstruction if possible, or consideration of wrist arthrodesis.

Following recovery of extrinsic finger flexion, paralysis of intrinsic muscles innervated by the median and ulnar nerves will result in a worsening claw deformity and impaired pattern of grasp and pinch. Absence of lumbrical function causes the reanimated flexor digitorum profundus tendons to initiate flexion at the interphalangeal joints, preventing an efficient wrapping of the hand around the desired object. This results in a resting claw finger posture that may in time become a fixed deformity with metacarpophalangeal joint hyperextension and proximal interphalangeal joint flexion. Postoperative physiotherapy, including a lumbrical bar or hand- based anticlaw splint, may be helpful, as well as one of a variety of passive anticlaw surgical procedures that is described in the literature. Additionally, the thumb cannot pinch effectively due to unopposed thumb interphalangeal flexion and lack of active motors to position the metacarpophalangeal and carpometacarpal joints. Thumb collapse can be improved by “simplifying” the multiple joints of the thumb, most frequently by arthrodesis of the carpometacarpal and/or interphalangeal joints. (See Chapters 31 and 33 for details of these procedures.)

Basic sensory functions such as protective sensation and position sense should be restored when motor function is being reconstructed in a severely paralyzed limb. Supraclavicular (cervical plexus) or intercostal nerve transfers provide limited sensibility of the hand after double FFMT.

**Complications.** Vascular compromise after FFMT is fortunately infrequent, and reinnervation of the muscle is generally successful. Adhesion of the transferred muscle to the surrounding tissue is a problem that may be minimized by an early range- of- motion protocol. The most important factor affecting the outcome for grasp is the extent of recovery of the triceps, as previously explained. If reinnervation of the triceps brachii fails, secondary reconstruction, such as reinnervated infraspinatus or deltoid transfer to the triceps brachii, is recommended to restore elbow stability.

If the glenohumeral joint remains unstable even after recovery of these muscles, glenohumeral tendon transfers or arthrodesis should be performed. Care must be taken to prevent fractures of the proximal humerus after arthrodesis. After recovery of active finger motion, arthrodesis of the thumb carpometacarpal joint and/or interphalangeal joint may be helpful. In addition, tenodesis flexion of the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints of the thumb and proximal and distal interphalangeal joints of the fingers may augment stable pinch function. A passive Zancolli lasso procedure may be used to minimize the expected claw deformity manifesting as flexor digitorum profundus function improves.

**Expected outcome.** Thirty of 36 patients undergoing reconstruction by the double free muscle procedure and monitored for more than 24 months after the second free muscle transfer (mean follow- up, 40 months) were assessed for the long- term outcome of prehension, including motion and stability of the shoulder and elbow (Fig. 34.39), voluntary and independent motion of the fingers, sensibility, and performance of activities of daily living. The functional outcome of prehension according to the authors’ classification was excellent in 6 patients (which implies restoration of more than 90 degrees of elbow flexion, dynamic stability of the elbow while moving the fingers, and more than 60 degrees of total active motion of the fingers), good in 11 patients (same as excellent, except for total active finger motion of 30 to 60 degrees), fair in 3 patients (total active finger motion of less than 30 degrees), and poor in 10 patients. Satisfactory results (excellent and good) were achieved in 17 of 30 patients (57%).

#### Single- Stage Functioning Free Muscle Transfer for Prehension

Despite reported success with double FFMT for prehension, it remains a daunting undertaking for both the surgeon and patient. Two to three major surgeries followed by significant dedicated rehabilitation may be both financially and emotionally difficult for patients. It has been our current practice to use a single FFMT in one operation that can provide elbow flexion and extension, grasp, and sensation. Only finger extension is not reconstructed. The indications for this method are the same as for a double free muscle transfer: avulsion of four or five nerve roots. It is important that surgery be performed no later than 6 to 8 months following injury so that critical triceps function can be restored.

A supraclavicular exploration is performed, and if a viable proximal stump is available (i.e., postganglionic injury), sural nerve grafts are used to target the suprascapular and axillary nerves. Two intercostal motor nerves (T5 and T6) are transferred to the biceps motor branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, and another pair (T3 and T4) to a free functioning gracilis. The gracilis is attached to the acromion and distal clavicle, and the distal tendon is prolonged with a tendon graft and is woven into the flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus to produce finger flexion and thumb pinch (Fig. 34.40). An antecubital pulley must be used to avoid bowstringing. The intact lacertus fibrosus, reinforced if needed by weaving the flexor carpi ulnaris across the antecubital fossa to the lateral epicondyle and back, is generally used for this purpose. To balance the elbow flexion motion, the spinal accessory nerve is transferred to the triceps branches of the radial nerve with an interposition nerve graft (often the superficial radial nerve) and the sensory intercostals transferred to the lateral cord contribution to the median nerve (Fig. 34.41).

Although the results of the double- stage FFMT described by Doi and associates are anecdotally better as a result, the fiscal and psychological burden of undergoing two extensive microsurgical procedures has been challenging in our patient population. Outcome of either procedure often fails to meet patient expectations and requires a careful preoperative discussion. At times, patients will elect other options, including reconstruction of elbow and shoulder only. In the future, advances in prosthetic designs, targeted muscle reinnervation, and prosthetic control (see the online chapter Bionic Reconstruction and Prosthetic Replacement of the Upper Extremity at ExpertConsult.com) may offer an effective alternative.

#### Nerve Reimplantation

Experimental studies of the implantation of avulsed ventral roots into the spinal cord in rats, cats, and then primates over a period of several decades suggested that spinal motor neuron axons may regenerate.These studies led to small clinical trials. This procedure, pioneered by Carlstedt and colleagues, remains controversial and is still under investigation.

## SECONDARY RECONSTRUCTION

### Shoulder Reconstruction in Adult Patients With Brachial Plexus Injury

Persistent shoulder instability and lack of mobility are common and debilitating problems in adult patients with brachial plexus injury. The lack of shoulder mobility significantly hinders use of the upper extremity even if the elbow, wrist, and fingers are functioning normally. Recovery of deltoid and rotator cuff function is often incomplete, regardless of whether brachial plexus reconstruction has been performed; such incomplete recovery results in poor abduction and limited or no external rotation. Traditionally, recovery of active elbow flexion and reduction of shoulder subluxation, which lead to improvement in glenohumeral stability and pain, are interpreted as clinical successes. However, with the limited or absent shoulder external rotation, most of these patients maintain an internal rotation position of the shoulder, and this in turns results in a “hand- on- belly position.” In these instances, patients cannot position the hand in front of the body in the coronal plane, thereby resulting in significant limitation of the functional use of the limb.

Options for improving shoulder function and stability include fusion and tendon transfers. Shoulder fusion is a reliable procedure for stabilizing the glenohumeral joint; however, it is irreversible and relies entirely on scapulothoracic motion for shoulder motion. The procedure is consequently contraindicated in patients with limited scapulothoracic motion or paralysis of the periscapular muscles.Saha and Goldner both recommended tendon transfer for a paralytic shoulder and used shoulder fusion only as a last resort. We agree with these authors, and it is our preference to attempt shoulder reconstruction with either nerve transfers or tendon transfers and leave shoulder fusion as a salvage procedure when no other options exist.

Because of the complexity of shoulder joint motion, it is important to have a basic understanding of the biomechanics of the shoulder before attempting shoulder reconstruction with tendon transfers. In this section, the basic biomechanics of the shoulder and the anatomy and function of the muscles around the shoulder will be reviewed. Both classic and novel tendon transfers for paralytic shoulder reconstruction will be discussed.

### BASIC BIOMECHANICS AND RELEVANT

### ANATOMY OF THE SHOULDER

Fourteen muscles work around three joints (glenohumeral, acromioclavicular, and sternoclavicular joints) and one scapulothoracic articulation to create three- dimensional shoulder motion. Eight muscles specifically move the glenohumeral joint, including the latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major, deltoid, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis, teres minor, and teres major. The first two muscles originate from the chest wall, spine, and clavicle, and the latter six originate on the scapula and insert on the humerus. Additionally, six muscles are responsible for the mobility and the stability of the scapula on the chest wall and include the serratus anterior, trapezius, rhomboid minor, rhomboid major, levator scapulae, and pectoralis minor. These muscles originate from the chest wall and spine and insert onto the scapula.

Goldner divided the muscles around the shoulder into four functional groups: Group 1, *the prime movers,* includes the clavicular head of the pectoralis major and deltoid; group 2, *the stabilizers,* includes the subscapularis, supraspinatus, and infraspinatus; group 3, *the depressors,* includes the teres major, biceps, latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major, and triceps; and group 4, *the accessory stabilizers of the scapula,* includes the trapezius, serratus anterior, pectoralis minor, levator scapulae, and rhomboids. The concepts described by Saha and Goldner imply that smooth and normal shoulder function requires stable and normal scapulothoracic motion in addition to a normal deltoid and rotator cuff.

The muscle’s ability to contract and move an articulation is related to its tendon excursion. It has been shown that the excursion of the rotator cuff muscles is considerably less than that of the deltoid muscle with simple scapular plane abduction, thus implying that the rotator cuff muscles are the primary stabilizers of the glenohumeral joint during shoulder motion. The stabilizing effect of the rotator cuff muscles (via a force couple created by the subscapularis anteriorly and the infraspinatus posteriorly) resists a relative upward shearing moment at the glenohumeral joint created by the contracting deltoid muscle during early abduction. To illustrate this concept, it should be understood that effective function of the deltoid (prime mover of the shoulder) is significantly compromised if the stabilizing function of the rotator cuff is lost or deficient.

Among the four rotator cuff muscles, the subscapularis and infraspinatus are the most important ones in stabilizing and contributing to the overall abduction moment arm to the shoulder. It has been estimated that the functional contribution of the supraspinatus to the overall abduction moment arm is only 14%; the subscapularis, 52%; and the infraspinatus and teres minor combined, 32%. Similarly, the subscapularis provides 42% of the internal rotation moment, and the infraspinatus and teres minor combined provide 45% of the external rotation moment. The importance of the infraspinatus in abduction and external rotation has been demonstrated by Gerber and coworkers, who showed that a nerve block to paralyze the infraspinatus led to loss of approximately 45% of abduction strength and 70% of external rotation strength of the shoulder independent of the abduction angle.

The accessory stabilizers of the scapula are an important group of muscles that stabilize and mobilize the scapula on the chest wall and are essential to complement shoulder motion in abduction and flexion.The serratus anterior muscle is an important complement to deltoid function. Better shoulder functional outcomes have been reported in patients with brachial plexus injury who had partial or full recovery or preservation of the serratus anterior than in those without serratus function. Therefore, when tendon transfers about the shoulder are planned, the first priority is given to reconstruction of the prime movers and stabilizers, followed by the scapular stabilizers/movers. In most adult brachial plexus cases, reconstruction of active scapular stabilization by tendon transfer is rarely possible because of the scarcity of available adequately functioning muscles.

### Principles of Tendon Transfer

There are well- known important principles that should be followed to improve the chance for successful tendon transfer, and they are discussed in Chapter 31. Understanding the relative tension and potential excursion of the shoulder muscles is essential for preoperative planning. All parameters should be taken into consideration, although a perfect match of donor- to- recipient tendon tension, excursion, and vector is not possible.

#### Tendon Transfer for Reconstruction of Shoulder Function

It has classically been taught that reconstruction of shoulder flexionabduction is the primary priority in reconstruction of a paralytic shoulder. However, in discussing priorities of reconstruction with our patients, we have found that the majority of patients complain most about the lack of external rotation, even more so than the lack of forward flexion or abduction. External rotation of the shoulder is a critically important function in daily activities and is performed by the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles. External rotation allows the humerus to be rotated away from the body so that when elbow flexion occurs, the hand and forearm can clear the abdomen or chest wall. An inability to externally rotate the shoulder to clear the abdomen or place the hand in front of the body can be frustrating, especially when elbow flexion is good or close to normal.

One of the most important factors in determining outcome after shoulder reconstruction in patients with brachial plexus injury is the level and degree of brachial plexus injury. In previous reports on shoulder reconstruction by tendon transfer after brachial plexus injury, detailed evaluation of the available muscles around the shoulder and the feasibility of transfer of these muscles to restore the biomechanics of the shoulder are not adequately addressed. For example, shoulder instability is expected after brachial plexus injury because of paralysis of the rotator cuff muscles (dynamic stabilizers of the shoulder). The techniques described to stabilize the shoulder address the sequelae rather than the biomechanical cause of the instability, and subsequently, glenohumeral fusion for shoulder subluxation has been described to solve this problem.

Prerequisites necessary for tendon transfer about the shoulder include a shoulder that has an adequate soft tissue envelope and is passively mobile. Pain and limb edema must be controlled before surgery. Finally, a cooperative patient who understands the objectives of the surgery and participates in the arduous rehabilitation process is necessary for success. Other relevant questions related to the tendon transfer procedure that should be addressed before surgery include the following:

1. Restoration of which function or functions will yield the greatest functional result?
2. What muscles are available?
3. Are the available muscles expendable, and do they have suffi cient strength (M4 or greater), adequate excursion, and a proper vector to permit function?

Decisions are based on a large number of anatomic, biomechanical, and clinical outcome studies. In the adult shoulder there is little information to guide the surgeon. Relatively few functional muscles are available, and their expendability is unclear. Accurate isolation and determination of strength are difficult because of the complex forces about the shoulder girdle. In plexus injuries, the trapezius, levator scapulae, and rhomboids have been reported to be preserved or recovered in 96% of patients. The trapezius innervation is extraplexal, and the dorsal scapular nerve supplying the rhomboids and levator scapulae receives contributions from more proximal cervical nerves. Additional muscle groups may be available in patients with incomplete plexus palsy as well, which greatly expands the opportunity for reconstruction. Finally, division of multipennate muscles into their functional subcomponents, as described by Herzberg and colleagues, can provide additional tendon transfer options.

#### Upper Trapezius Transfer

Historically, the trapezius is the most common tendon transfer performed for adult patients with brachial plexus injury, including those who undergo spinal accessory nerve transfer as part of their brachial plexus reconstruction. In most of these patients, the upper part of the trapezius is preserved and hypertrophies over time, thereby gaining sufficient strength for transfer. Trapezius transfer was described by Hoffa in 1891, followed by Lewis in 1910 and Lange in 1911. Tendon prolongation with fascia lata led to poor results attributed to stretching and scarring. Bateman reported transfer of the muscle along with its acromial insertion into the proximal end of the humerus in 1955, later modified by Saha, whose name is now associated with the operation. However, Saha originally defined three basic shoulder functions that need to be reconstructed with available nonparalyzed local muscles. He rarely recommended this transfer in isolation, and he was an advocate of multiple tendon transfers in an attempt to restore normal shoulder function. However, it should be mentioned that most of his patients were younger and had shoulder weakness or paralysis as a result of poliomyelitis rather than traumatic brachial plexus injury. There is a significant difference in the pattern of weakness and muscles available for transfer between these two groups of paralytic conditions. The transfers that were performed for polio were selected on the basis of the type of paralysis and residual power of the available local muscles. Most commonly, it was recommended that transfer of the upper trapezius be used to restore function of the deltoid and clavicular head of the pectoralis major, which Saha called the “prime movers.” He transferred the levator scapulae or sternocleidomastoid muscle to replace the supraspinatus, which he described as a vertical “steerer.” The anterior horizontal steerer (subscapularis) can be substituted for by the pectoralis minor, the upper two slips of the serratus anterior, or a residual part of the pectoralis major. The posterior horizontal steerers (infraspinatus, teres minor) can be replaced by a part of the latissimus dorsi or teres major.

Despite Saha’s recommendation for multiple tendon transfers to restore shoulder function in the paralytic polio shoulder, most of what has been published in the literature as the Saha procedure refers to the single, upper trapezius tendon transfer. Kotwal and coauthors reported an average of 60% of good results with upper trapezius transfer using the Saha technique. Nineteen percent of their patients had a poor outcome, with persistent subluxation of the glenohumeral joint. Aziz and associates reported a reduction in shoulder joint subluxation after upper trapezius transfer in all their patients, with an average of 40 degrees of abduction and flexion gained. Karev reported on a single case of severe atrophy of the deltoid, supraspinatus, and infraspinatus with an intact subscapularis after excision of a neurilemmoma of the brachial plexus. The patient underwent upper trapezius transfer 6 months after his original surgery and had almost full return of shoulder function. In this single case, it is likely that the remarkable gains were the result of spontaneous improvement of the native deltoid and rotator cuff. Monreal and colleagues reported a reduction in glenohumeral subluxation and an average gain of 40 degrees of abduction and flexion after upper trapezius transfer in 10 patients with brachial plexus injury.

Rühmann and coworkers repeatedly published the outcome of upper trapezius transfer in adult patients with brachial plexus injury. Originally, the Saha technique was used, although in more recent publications the method has been modified. In Saha’s technique, the trapezius is detached from the spine of the scapula and transferred with the acromion to the proximal humerus just distal to the greater tuberosity. Fixation is provided by two 6.5- mm cancellous screws with the shoulder positioned in 80 to 90 degrees of abduction. In the modified technique, the partially detached deltoid is sutured in a medial direction on top of the trapezius muscle. This technique may transmit additional power by contraction of any remaining deltoid fibers. The authors demonstrated increased function in all patients and decreased multidirectional instability in 95% of patients. The mean increase in abduction and flexion was around 30 degrees. The best results were achieved in patients with a functioning biceps, coracobrachialis, triceps, or pectoralis major muscle, or any combination of these muscles. The improved outcome in this group of patients is most likely related to the additional number of functioning muscles, as well as the less severely injured patient cohort. In these patients the average abduction was 42 degrees and the average flexion was 56 degrees. By contrast, those with a completely flail shoulder (presumably complete brachial plexus injuries) gained just 28 degrees of abduction and 19 degrees of flexion. None of these studies addressed the lack of shoulder external rotation and focused solely on shoulder stability and shoulder abduction- flexion.

#### Latissimus Dorsi Transfer

Latissimus dorsi tendon transfer, with or without teres major transfer, is commonly used in patients with residual brachial plexus birth palsy to restore abduction and external rotation. This transfer has not been used traditionally in adult patients. Most pediatric plexus injuries involve C5 and C6; in such injuries, some latissimus function is preserved. In addition, partial recovery of deltoid and subscapularis function is usually present and is critical for transfer of the latissimus dorsi to be effective. Accordingly, some authors consider paralysis of the deltoid and subscapularis muscles to be contraindications to transfer of the latissimus dorsi. In patients without deltoid and subscapularis function, latissimus transfer to the greater tuberosity may magnify the inferior subluxation and adduction- extension of the shoulder. When latissimus dorsi transfer is performed to reconstruct irreparable rotator cuff tears in the presence of a normal subscapularis and deltoid, however, it functions appropriately as a humeral head depressor and external rotator.

An alternative use of the latissimus dorsi muscle is as a bipolar transfer in which the muscle is rotated about its vascular pedicle to improve its position and vector. For example, reconstruction of anterior deltoid function requires release of the latissimus origin and insertion, rotation of the muscle 180 degrees, passage through the deltopectoral interval, and attachment to the acromion and humerus. Itoh and coauthors reported good results in 10 patients. All of them had a good shoulder contour, six had active flexion of more than 90 degrees, and all but one had M4 power. De Smet and Ferrier and associates used the same technique with similarly good results. It should be emphasized that most of these patients had isolated paralysis of the deltoid and a partially innervated or completely normal rotator cuff.

**Pectoralis muscle transfer.** Hou and Tai reported on seven patients who underwent transfer of the upper portion of the pectoralis either alone (four patients) or combined with upper trapezius transfer (three patients) for deltoid paralysis. They defined the functional anatomy of the pectoralis major muscle as having three parts: clavicular (originating from the clavicle), sternocostal (originating from the first to sixth sternocostal regions), and abdominal (originating from the junction of the lower sternocostal and rectus abdominis fascia). These muscular parts converge laterally and insert along the lateral lip of the bicipital groove. The tendinous portions of the clavicular and upper sternocostal heads make up the anterior tendon. These portions of the muscle are supplied by the thoracoacromial artery and innervated by the lateral pectoral nerve, whereas the lower or abdominal head is supplied by either the thoracoacromial artery or the internal thoracic branch of the axillary artery and innervated by the medial pectoral nerve. During transfer of the pectoralis major muscle, the upper portions (clavicular and upper sternocostal parts) are harvested on their pedicle, and the muscle is then turned over 180 degrees with the deep surface being superficial. The muscular portion is attached to the lateral aspect of the clavicle and the acromion, whereas the tendinous portion is anchored to the deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. In isolation, this technique restored an average of 40 degrees of abduction. When an upper trapezius transfer was added, however, the average abduction improved to 70 to 90 degrees with forward flexion of 60 to 150 degrees. Lin and coauthors reported similarly good results in patients with brachial plexus injury.Shoulder mobility and stability were improved in all their patients. Functioning and preservation of the pectoral nerves are prerequisites in considering and undertaking this transfer. In an anatomic study, Elhassan and colleagues confirmed the feasibility of transferring the clavicular head and upper half of the sternal head of the pectoralis major to reconstruct the anterior deltoid. They also reported on the outcome of pedicled pectoralis transfer with reverse shoulder arthroplasty in patients with deltoid paralysis and rotator cuff deficiency. At the final follow- up, patients had significant improvement in pain, shoulder subjective value (SSV), and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score, with an average improvement of flexion of 83 degrees and external rotation of 15 degrees.

#### Lower Trapezius Transfer

Our original description of lower trapezius transfer to restore shoulder external rotation was in a patient who sustained a brachial plexus injury that resulted in complete paralysis of the deltoid, infraspinatus, and teres minor. The trapezius was prolonged by insertion of an Achilles tendon allograft at the infraspinatus tendon footprint. The patient gained 70 degrees of external rotation and was very satisfied with the outcome of surgery. The authors subsequently reported the outcome of lower trapezius transfer isolated or combined with multiple tendon transfer in 52 patients with brachial plexus injury. All patients had a stable shoulder at the latest follow- up, with significant improvement of shoulder external rotation but only marginal improvements of shoulder abduction and flexion.

If a single tendon transfer is planned to restore external rotation, our original technique involves using an incision placed medial to the scapula, through which the lower trapezius is harvested (Fig. 34.42Aand B). The muscle is lengthened (with an Achilles or posterior tibialis tendon allograft or a hamstring autograft), passed between the posterior deltoid and infraspinatus, and anchored to the infraspinatus insertion site (see Fig. 34.42C and D). However, after the original 52 patients on whom we reported, we performed more than 50 additional tendon transfers using the lower trapezius for shoulder external rotation, as an isolated transfer or combined with other tendon transfers; in the latter cases, the trapezius was transferred directly to the infraspinatus without an attached graft. Because almost all brachial plexus patients have an intact infraspinatus tendon, we were able to dissect and maintain a 5 to 7 cm infraspinatus tendon insertion on the humerus (Fig. 34.43); this allowed direct transfer of the lower trapezius tendon to the infraspinatus tendon without the need for tendon allograft (Fig. 34.44A to C).

#### Contralateral Lower Trapezius Origin Transfer

In the absence of available muscles on the injured side to transfer to the infraspinatus to restore external rotation, contralateral trapezius transfer is a unique option, and it is possibly the only option available to achieve this purpose.

Trapezial length measurements in 20 volunteers and 12 cadaver dissections confirmed the feasibility of transfer of the contralateral lower trapezius, lengthened by its lumbosacral fascia, to the denervated infraspinatus tendon for restoration of shoulder external rotation.In 2014, Satbhai and colleagues demonstrated encouraging results in three patients who underwent contralateral trapezius transfer to restore shoulder external rotation after they regained elbow flexion from free muscle transfer. The patients experienced an average improvement of more than 95 degrees of external rotation arc and an average improvement in DASH scores of 14 points. ### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: SHOULDER RECONSTRUCTION

Consideration of shoulder reconstruction should take place when the initial decision is being made on how to reconstruct the upper extremity, as nerve transfer donors need to be chosen strategically when future shoulder tendon transfers may be required. It is our routine practice to include a discussion of shoulder reconstructive options, as well as priorities of treatment, with each patient. Even after reinnervation and recovery from nerve grafting or transfers, secondary surgery may be needed to improve the stability and function of the shoulder.

Our original reconstruction algorithm was dedicated to reconstruct the force couple around the glenohumeral joint followed by reconstruction of the deltoid. We tried to follow the basic shoulder biomechanics hoping to achieve the best possible function after tendon transfer.

In patients with complete brachial plexus injuries, the trapezius and levator scapulae are usually functioning, and the serratus anterior may be available, depending on the type of injury. We have observed that the three parts of the trapezius (upper, middle, and lower) are activated independently, so they could potentially be used independently as separate transfers. For this reason the upper and middle trapezius can be used for transfer to stabilize and possibly improve shoulder abduction (Fig. 34.47A and B). Additionally, the levator scapulae (reported by Narakas) can be used, and finally, depending on the type of injury, the upper serratus anterior muscle (reported by Saha) may also be available. Our original shoulder reconstruction algorithm was aimed to reconstruct all the rotator cuff as well as the deltoid.

For this reason we performed transfer of the upper serratus or pectoralis minor (if functioning) to the subscapularis insertion for internal rotation, the levator scapulae to the supraspinatus for abduction, the lower trapezius to the infraspinatus for external rotation, and the upper and middle trapezius to the deltoid for abduction. If the latissimus or pectoralis major were functioning with enough strength, they were also added as a pedicled transfer for shoulder contouring and to improve shoulder flexion. These potential transfers were our preferred method, but their combined use has not been substantiated yet in the literature. In our initial cohort of 39 patients who underwent multiple tendon transfers, almost all of them had significant improvement of shoulder external rotation, while improvements in shoulder flexion and abduction were not statistically significant. For this reason we did modify our original algorithm and technique. We started performing three muscle transfers to stabilize the shoulder and improve shoulder external rotation: (1) upper and middle trapezius transfer to reduce inferior shoulder subluxation and possibly improve shoulder abduction, (2) levator scapulae transfer to the lateral spine of the scapula to stabilize the scapula (since we are transferring the entire trapezius to the humerus), and (3) lower trapezius transfer to the infraspinatus to improve shoulder external rotation.

For the transfer of the upper/middle trapezius to the proximal humerus, we no longer perform full acromial osteotomy. We either perform only a small lateral acromial osteotomy or prolong the upper and middle trapezius with an Achilles tendon allograft so that the transfer lays on top of the acromion and inserts on the deltoid tendon footprint (Fig. 34.48A and B). In both of these techniques the acromion is left intentionally to act as a lever that can increase the moment arm for humeral abduction.

It should be also noted that partial or complete acromial preservation maintains an option for future reverse shoulder arthroplasty.

When options are more limited, we prefer to reconstruct external rotation because we believe that the ability to position the hand anterior to the coronal plane restores greater overall function than does abduction. For this reason, in patients who have no muscles available for transfer on the side of injury, contralateral lower trapezius origin transfer is performed to the infraspinatus tendon to restore shoulder external rotation.

### Shoulder Arthrodesis

Shoulder arthrodesis is a well- established operative procedure that involves fusion of the humeral head to the glenoid. Indications include total paralysis after brachial plexus injuries and following failed or neglected plexus reconstructions in conjunction with amputation and prosthetic fitting. We prefer procedures other than shoulder arthrodesis because the decreased arc of shoulder motion after arthrodesis limits use of the reconstructed hand after prehensile reconstruction. In contrast, other brachial plexus centers routinely perform arthrodesis of the glenohumeral joint to use the available nerve donors for elbow flexion- extension and prehension.

Patients have reported difficulty with turning during sleep, and fracture of the humerus is not a rare complication of shoulder arthrodesis. Contraindications to shoulder arthrodesis include extensive paralysis of the trapezius and shoulder stabilizers (levator scapulae, serratus anterior, or rhomboid muscles); if these scapular stabilizing muscles are paralyzed, scapulothoracic subluxation or pain will result. Richards and coworkers reported that if these muscles are nonfunctional, the extremity will be severely impaired despite successful glenohumeral fusion. In these cases, scapulothoracic fusion will be required to alleviate pain. Providing stability of the glenohumeral joint by fusion can be helpful if the patient is to be able to move the elbow and fingers, even if glenohumeral arthrodesis results in some instability of the scapulothoracic joint.

The shoulder is a difficult joint to fuse because the humerus has a long lever arm, the area of contact at the glenohumeral joint is small, and paralyzed muscles that cross the glenohumeral joint cannot contribute compressive forces to facilitate union. The optimum position for arthrodesis of the shoulder is debatable. Patients with total paralysis of the brachial plexus and normal function of the trapezius and serratus anterior muscles should undergo arthrodesis in 30 to 40 degrees of abduction and flexion and 30 degrees of internal rotation. In contrast, in total paralysis of the brachial plexus without serratus anterior or trapezius function, shoulder arthrodesis should be performed only when inferior shoulder subluxation is associated with severe symptoms. In this situation, the position of arthrodesis should be 20 degrees of abduction, 10 degrees of flexion, and 30 degrees of internal rotation to try to lessen scapulothoracic instability.

Many operative techniques for shoulder arthrodesis have been described. Rigid internal fixation with a long 4.5- mm reconstruction plate and compression screws across the glenohumeral joint has been reported with good results. However, it carries the risk of increased devascularization and occasional problems from prominent screws and loosening of the hardware in osteoporotic bone. External fixation has its own complications, including neurovascular injury, pin tract infection, pin loosening, and fractures through pin sites, but it is particularly useful for infected joints.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GLENOHUMERAL ARTHRODESIS

We prefer to use long cancellous screws and a reconstruction plate for transarticular fixation of both the glenohumeral and acromiohumeral joints, supplementing the fixation with autogenous bone graft. The operation is performed in the beach- chair position. Through a posterior incision that follows the spine of the scapula and extends down the lateral humeral head and shaft, the posterior half of the deltoid is detached from the scapular spine and acromion and retracted distally. All soft tissue under the acromion is removed, and the rotator cuff muscles are also detached from the greater and lesser tuberosities to expose the joint capsule, glenohumeral joint, and acromiohumeral joint. Articular cartilage from each joint is removed to facilitate contact of the subchondral bone of the humeral head and glenoid cavity. Bone graft from the iliac crest is then inserted into the subacromial space. The glenohumeral and acromiohumeral joints are transfixed with long cannulated screws of 7.3 mm in diameter in the position of 30 degrees of abductionflexion and internal rotation. Because most patients with brachial plexus injury have osteopenic bones, adequate compression of the acromion on the superior surface of the humerus is important. This allows additional contact surface, which may maximize the fusion rate. A 4.5- mm pelvic reconstruction plate is contoured to the scapular spine and humerus and secured in place, which adds substantially to the stiffness of the fusion construct. Cancellous bone chips or autogenous bone graft from the humeral head are used to pack the vacant space of both joints (Fig. 34.49).

**Postoperative protocol.** A splint is used to immobilize the glenohumeral joint until radiographic union, which may take up to 4 months. Movement of the elbow and distal joints is encouraged. Gentle passive and active range of motion of the scapulothoracic joint is initiated, and radiographs are taken monthly until bone union occurs.

**Expected outcome.** The purpose of shoulder arthrodesis after brachial plexus injuries is to stabilize the glenohumeral joint and permit the patient to move the upper part of the arm through the scapulothoracic joint with the use of functioning periscapular muscles. The postoperative range of shoulder movement depends on the residual strength of the periscapular muscles, such as the trapezius, levator scapulae, serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi, and rhomboid muscles.

Previous reports have shown a satisfactory outcome of range of scapulothoracic motion, with an average of 60 degrees of abduction and flexion, because most of the patients had normal function of the serratus anterior muscle. In a recent study by Atlan and associates of 54 patients who underwent shoulder fusion for a paralytic shoulder in the setting of brachial plexus injury, the authors reported improved abduction of at least 45 degrees in 65% of patients and improved active shoulder rotation of at least 45 degrees in 65% of patients. They recommended the use of abundant subacromial bone graft to decrease the risk of pseudarthrosis. It should be noted that glenohumeral fusion may limit future surgical access for free vascularized muscle transfers or latissimus dorsi bicepsplasty, so priorities and timing of each procedure should be carefully planned. However, in patients with severe pain secondary to chronic subluxation or dislocation of the shoulder, with or without associated arthritis, the priority of restoring elbow flexion first may not be an option, and in this case, we start by performing the shoulder first in order to resolve the pain and stabilize the shoulder.

### Late Reconstruction of Elbow Flexion

Paralysis of elbow flexion resulting from brachial plexus injury should be addressed when feasible by nerve repair, graft, or transfer in the first 6 to 9 months after injury. Thereafter, nerve reconstruction becomes increasingly less reliable and generally fails when delayed 12 or more months because of irreversible ultrastructural changes in muscle. In some cases, even early intervention may be impossible because of extensive damage to the anterior compartment of the arm, including muscle scarring or loss of or injury to the motor nerve at its motor innervation point. In these cases other options are needed to restore elbow flexion. Such options broadly include tendon transfers and FFMT. Examples of tendon transfer include proximal advancement of the flexor- pronator origin (Steindler flexorplasty) and transfer of the pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, sternocleidomastoid, triceps, and latissimus dorsi muscles.

Tendon transfer can generally be used only with incomplete brachial plexus injury because most transfers other than the sternocleidomastoid are innervated by brachial plexus nerves. The final outcome is influenced by a number of factors, including the cross- sectional area, fiber length, and vector of the transferred muscle, as well as fascial connections, the number of joints crossed, and the function of antagonist muscles. In brachial plexus injury, it is common to encounter donor muscles that have partial denervation resulting from the original plexus injury. The adequacy of such a muscle is not necessarily easy to predict preoperatively, although a BMRC grade of 4 (submaximal muscle strength against resistance) is generally regarded as the minimum required. Preoperative evaluation of muscle power can be difficult, however, as when more than one muscle contributes to a given motion (e.g., latissimus dorsi for shoulder adduction) or the muscle has dual innervation (pectoralis major). Unfortunately, BMRC grading is notoriously imprecise in assessing the ability of a muscle to do work, especially following its transfer to a new location. Electrodiagnostic testing is helpful in these circumstances, and appropriate donor muscles should have normal or near normal electromyographic recruitment.

The alternative of FFMT is appealing, although technically more challenging and requiring a lengthy recovery time. Prerequisites include an adequate and stable soft tissue bed; blood vessels in good condition for microsurgical anastomoses; and an available expendable donor muscle with a favorable vascular supply, a single motor nerve, and strength and excursion comparable to one or more elbow flexors. Additionally, motor nerves must be available for transfer to the motor nerve of the transplanted muscle. In brachial plexus cases, the original nerve pathways are injured, and in complete plexus injuries, only extraplexal donor nerves are available. Because rapid reinnervation of the muscle is required to prevent muscle atrophy and loss of motor end plates, nerve transfers from previously uninjured nerves are preferred. Two or more intercostal motor nerves, the spinal accessory nerve, or the fascicles of the ulnar nerve that innervate the flexor carpi ulnaris are the most common choices. Alternatively, free muscle transfer may be delayed until motor nerve reinnervation from a proximal repair or transfer has occurred.

When selecting the best procedure for a given patient from the available choices, one must consider multiple factors, including the specifics of the nerve injury; elapsed time from injury; status of the soft tissue envelope, blood vessels, and muscle; expertise of the surgical team; and desires and expectations of the patient. A careful history, physical examination, and evaluation by electrophysiologic testing and appropriate imaging studies are required. Patient education and informed participation in the decision making are imperative.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: LATE RECONSTRUCTION OF ELBOW FLEXION

**Modiﬁed Steindler procedure.** Arthur Steindler described a method to restore flexor function to the elbow in a classic 1918 article.The flexor- pronator muscles arising from the medial epicondyle are transposed to a more proximal and anterior position on the humerus to increase their moment arm for elbow flexion. As with all potential candidates for tendon transfer, preoperative evaluation of the strength of the donor muscles is critical to the success of the muscle transfer. After transfer, these muscles (the pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor carpi ulnaris, and flexor digitorum superficialis) continue to provide their original function in addition to elbow flexion; consequently, they must have normal or nearly normal power (BMRC grades 4 to 5). Results are generally better in patients who require only augmentation of weak elbow flexion or who can flex the elbow before surgery and achieve wrist flexion and forearm pronation with gravity eliminated (the Steindler effect). In the absence of a demonstrable Steindler effect, the postoperative result is likely to be less optimal. Unlike isolated C5- C6 injury, patients with C5- C7 palsy may have wrist and finger extensor weakness in addition to absent elbow flexion. In these patients, concomitant wrist arthrodesis should be considered.We prefer the modified technique described by Mayer and Green to minimize undesirable pronation during elbow flexion.

Operative procedure. The midpoint of the incision lies just posterior to the medial epicondyle of the humerus. It is directed proximally and anteriorly for 8 to 10 cm over the distal upper part of the arm and distally over the anterior aspect of the forearm for 8 to 10 cm along the course of the pronator teres muscle (Fig. 34.50A). The resulting anterior skin flap is retracted gently anteriorly and laterally, and the posterior margin is undermined to identify the ulnar nerve at the medial epicondyle. The articular branch is sacrificed, but the branches to the flexor carpi ulnaris are preserved and protected while the nerve is mobilized for 5 cm distal to the elbow. The median nerve is similarly identified deep to the proximal border of the pronator teres. Its muscular branches to the pronator originate 2.5 to 5 cm proximal to the elbow on its medial side and must be preserved. The common flexor pronator origin is next detached, along with a 1.5- cm fragment of medial epicondylar bone with an osteotome (see Fig. 34.50B), while protecting the median and ulnar nerves and their muscular branches. It is helpful to predrill (and tap) the medial epicondyle before osteotomy. The anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament must be preserved at the distal, deep aspect of the epicondyle. Once the epicondylar osteotomy is complete, mobilization of the muscle from the ulna continues until the bone fragment and muscle origin can be advanced to a point 5 to 8 cm proximal to the original position with the elbow acutely flexed to 130 degrees.

In the original Steindler technique, the tendon of origin was attached to the medial intermuscular septum and fascia. Mayer subsequently transferred the entire medial epicondyle and moved the attachment point to the anterior aspect of the humerus. This modification is preferred because (1) the attachment to bone is stronger than to fascia and (2) the more anterior position reduces the pronating tendency of the original transfer. Elbow flexion accompanied by marked pronation of the forearm compromises hand function.

The median nerve and brachial artery are mobilized and retracted medially. The brachialis is split longitudinally to expose the humerus. It is prepared with a bur or osteotome, followed by fixation of the medial epicondyle and flexor- pronator group with a 3.5- mm screw and washer to the anterior aspect of the humerus. We augment the screw fixation with two additional suture anchors inserted into the humerus around the drill hole. The anchor sutures are tied to the flexor pronator origin with the elbow flexed 130 degrees and the forearm supinated (see Fig. 34.50C). Alternatively, a suture button technique can be performed to augment the repair in lieu of suture anchors. A nonabsorbable No. 2 Krackow suture is woven through the elevated flexor- pronator tendon. The free ends of the suture are placed through a suture button, passed through a humeral drill hole distal to the screw fixation of the medial epicondyle, and tied securely. The proximal portion of the wound is closed and the dressing is applied.

Postoperative protocol. The elbow is maintained in 100 degrees of flexion until union of the medial epicondyle fragment, generally by 6 to 8 weeks. Passive flexion of the elbow, wrist, and fingers commences just after the operation. Active flexion of the wrist and fingers with the elbow in full flexion is started in the third week. Extension of the elbow, wrist, and fingers is allowed from the seventh week after surgery. Postoperative rehabilitation and muscle reeducation take several months, with the goal of full active elbow flexion and a 30- degree elbow extension lag. The flexion contracture results in an increased mechanical advantage for initiation of flexion. The strength of the transferred muscles, the duration of immobilization, and the strength of the opposing triceps muscle all affect the outcome.

Complications. The main complications of the Steindler transfer are pronation and flexion contractures. Pronation contractures are related to (1) preoperative absence of active supination and (2) an increased mechanical advantage of the pronator teres as a result of its proximal shift. Inserting the transfer more anteriorly and laterally on the humerus diminishes this tendency, but if no active supination is present, pronation deformity may remain a problem. The incidence in the literature varies from 28% to 50%. A flexion contracture in excess of 30 degrees is uncommon and is usually treated by more intensive therapy, including passive range- of- motion exercises, stretching, and splinting.

Dutton and Dawson reported transient ulnar nerve paresthesias in two patients. Lindholm and Einola reported two patients with postoperative ulnar nerve paresthesias, one of whom was still having symptoms at follow- up.

Expected outcomes. Published results of the Steindler flexorplasty reflect a high degree of success in achieving a functional range of elbow flexion against gravity, particularly when performed for poliomyelitis. Steindler achieved 79.5% good results (flexion against gravity of not less than 90 degrees) in 39 cases. Using strict criteria for success, Mayer and Green recorded 11 excellent, 5 good, 4 fair, and 2 poor results in 22 flexorplasties. Lindholm and Einola found that 50 of their 61 patients achieved a range of flexion of at least 90 degrees. Eleven were able to lift a weight heavier than 1 kg at right angles. Dutton and Dawson noted that 20 patients had excellent or good function of the transferred muscles. Eighteen could lift at least 1 to 2 kg through an arc of full elbow motion.

Monreal reported the results of 12 Steindler procedures performed in patients with incomplete C5- C7 brachial plexus palsy who had recovered elbow flexion of M2 or less. Eleven patients were found to have good or very good outcomes based on the criteria established by Alnot and Abols, and one patient had insufficient active flexion of the elbow. All underwent simultaneous wrist fusion. These results emphasize the role of late reconstruction by tendon transfer, when spontaneous recovery or nerve surgery results in insufficient strength. It should be noted that the strength of elbow flexion after a Steindler flexorplasty is limited.

**Triceps- to- biceps transfer.** Use of the triceps muscle to replace the biceps is a technically straightforward method that produces reliable elbow flexion in patients with upper (C5- C6 or C5- C7) plexus palsy. It is considered undesirable by some because of the functional loss of active elbow extension that must necessarily result. It prevents overhead use of the arm should shoulder function recover, will adversely affect the ability to transfer from bed to wheelchair, and will prevent the use of a cane or crutch in others. There are instances, however, when its use is reasonable. In patients with poor elbow flexion and prominent cocontraction of a weak biceps and strong triceps, transfer of the triceps immediately solves both issues. The simplicity of the procedure and the relatively rapid postoperative recovery from it make it more desirable for many patients when other options are more complex.

Operative procedure. The patient is positioned in a semisitting position with the extremity draped free. The incision begins proximally in the posterior midline of the humerus and extends laterally and distally to the olecranon and along the subcutaneous border of the ulna for 5 cm (Fig. 34.51). The ulnar nerve is exposed and protected medially and the triceps tendon is released, along with a tail of periosteum from the ulna. The medial head is mobilized while protecting the radial nerve in the spiral groove proximally. The medial and lateral edges of the tendon are brought together and entubulated, and a No. 2 nonabsorbable Krackow suture is woven through the tendon. The biceps tendon is exposed through a separate antecubital incision and the triceps tendon passed to it laterally through a subcutaneous tunnel. The radial insertion of the biceps tendon is identified and a bur hole is used to create a unicortical hole of 7 to 8 mm. Drill holes are placed through the opposite cortex with a 1- cm cortical bone bridge and the sutures are passed percutaneously using Keith needles through the unicortical side and through the drill holes and out the posterior skin just radial to the subcutaneous border of the ulna. The sutures are tensioned and tied subcutaneously with the elbow in 90 degrees of flexion. Alternatively, a suture button technique can be employed, or the triceps tendon can be woven to the biceps tendon with a Pulvertaft weave and nonabsorbable suture. If the triceps tendon is passed through a bone tunnel and secured to bone, the triceps and biceps tendon should be sewn together side- to- side with large nonabsorbable, interrupted figure- of- eight sutures.

**Latissimus dorsi transfer.** The latissimus dorsi is innervated by the thoracodorsal nerve (C6- C8) and may therefore be partially denervated in brachial plexus injuries requiring reconstruction of elbow flexion (C5- C6 pattern). Thus assessment of latissimus strength is of critical importance. Because the latissimus dorsi generally works in concert with other shoulder internal rotators, such assessment can be difficult. The most accurate assessment is obtained by positioning the patient’s arm over the buttocks, elbow extended, while grasping the patient’s wrist with one hand. The patient is asked to maintain this position while the examiner pulls the arm laterally and anteriorly. It is helpful to use the other hand to palpate the strength of the contraction by grasping the latissimus at the posterior wall of the axilla. The fully contracting muscle must be able to strongly resist the examiner. Comparison with the opposite side is helpful, and electrodiagnostic confirmation of normal or near normal motor unit recruitment is useful for equivocal clinical examinations.

Operative procedure. Latissimus dorsi transfer has been described in two variations: moving the origin alone (unipolar) or moving both the origin and insertion (bipolar). Bipolar transplantation is preferable for several reasons. The mechanical efficiency of the transplant is increased by the more anterior placement of the origin onto the coracoid process, the proper length is easier to determine when the distal insertion is completed first, and there is less chance of the neurovascular pedicle kinking.

**Bipolar transplantation.** The operation is performed in four steps:

1. Division of the origin and insertion of the latissimus dorsi mus cle while preserving its neurovascular pedicle
2. Exposure of both ends of the biceps muscle through separate incisions
3. Transposition of the latissimus dorsi muscle beneath a cutane ous bridge in the axilla to the anterior aspect of the humerus; if necessary, resection of the biceps muscle to provide sufficient room for the latissimus dorsi
4. Fixation of the latissimus muscle to the biceps tendon and its humeral tendon to the coracoid process

The technique described is the preferred approach used by Dr. Milan Stevanovic. The patient is positioned in the lateral decubitus position, and the upper extremity is draped free. A longitudinal incision is made parallel to the lateral border of the latissimus dorsi muscle and extends from the posterior border of the axilla to the iliac crest, with incorporation of a skin paddle beginning 6 to 8 cm distal to the axillary fold. It should be 4 to 6 cm in width with a length equal to that of the biceps muscle belly, elliptical in shape, and centered over the middle of the muscle. Dissection of the muscle is begun along its distal lateral border and proceeds medially and proximally. Initial dissection elevates the posterior and anterior skin flaps from the underlying fascia and muscle. With the muscle thus exposed, the arm is positioned in 180 degrees of abduction and forward flexion. The resting muscle length is marked with sutures at 5- cm intervals to facilitate later tensioning. Deep dissection proceeds from distal to proximal, with division of the origin and inclusion of the overlying fascial tissue. The dissection proceeds by mobilizing the vertebral border and separating the muscle at the inferior angle of the scapula from the deeper- lying rhomboid medially and the teres major and minor laterally. The thoracodorsal neurovascular pedicle is visualized on the deep muscle surface by developing the plane over the anterior border of the muscle. The lumbar fascial and posterior pelvic origins of the muscle are divided and the muscle is lifted off the chest wall with its attached neurovascular pedicle. The pedicle must be sufficiently mobilized to avoid kinking, which requires ligation of its serratus anterior branch and multiple (four to six) more proximal perforating branches. All fascia surrounding the pedicle must be completely released to maximize muscle mobility and minimize the potential for kinking of the pedicle. The tendon of insertion is sectioned at the humerus, and two Krackow 2- 0 nonabsorbable sutures are placed.

A transverse incision 3 to 5 cm long is made at the coracoid, and an additional longitudinal incision is made along the more distal biceps muscle to the level of the antecubital fossa to completely expose the muscle belly and distal tendon. Four mattress sutures are placed in the biceps tendon, medial and lateral at the musculotendinous junction and medial and lateral more distally at the tendinous portion of the biceps, in preparation for securing the latissimus dorsi to the distal biceps tendon (Fig. 34.52).

A subcutaneous pocket is created in the axilla to accommodate the latissimus by elevating the medial flap of the deltopectoral incision in a plane superficial to the axillary fascia, wide enough to admit four fingers. The muscle is tubularized by bringing the vertebral border to the lateral border and is secured with fascial sutures placed every 2 to 3 cm. The humeral insertion is moved proximally and anteriorly to the coracoid by blunt dissection below the tendon of the pectoralis major to create a tunnel for its passage. Fixation is performed after the muscle is anchored distally.

The origin of the latissimus dorsi is passed under the axillary skin bridge into the deltopectoral incision while protecting its neurovascular pedicle from kinking or tension. The muscle is now stretched to restore the previously marked resting length. The elbow should be fully extended. Proximally, the tubularized latissimus should lay superficial to the biceps muscle. Distally, at the musculotendinous junction and biceps tendon, the latissimus dorsi is wrapped 360 degrees around the tendon and secured with the previously placed sutures. The proximal tendon is anchored to the coracoid process or the acromion with suture anchors and adjusted to maintain the elbow in approximately 100 degrees of flexion with full supination. Before skin closure, the pedicle should be checked both with a Doppler device and by direct visualization of the pedicle and skin. Any dark blood in the vein would indicate stretching or compression of the pedicle and requires inspection and correction. Two deep drains are placed and kept at the donor site until output is less than 5 mL in 24 hours to prevent seroma formation.

Postoperative protocol. The arm is kept immobilized in a sling with an abduction pillow for 8 to 9 weeks. At 4 weeks we have the patient start isometric contraction of the transferred muscle while at 90 degrees. After 9 weeks, we have the patient begin working on slowly regaining extension at a rate of 10 to 20 degrees per week. Reeducation of the transferred latissimus dorsi muscle is accomplished initially by simultaneous shoulder extension and elbow flexion. Eventually, the patient will be able to flex the elbow in any shoulder position. Biofeedback by means of EMG is useful during postoperative reeducation of the transferred muscle. Later, exercise against resistance will produce powerful flexion of the elbow.

Complications. Weakness of elbow flexion may occur if the latissimus was partially denervated by the original plexus injury or if tensioning of the transfer was inadequate. Revision of the transfer tension may be performed at a later date.

Expected outcomes. The outcomes reported are all from small patient series or case reports. Zancolli and Mitre reported on eight patients who underwent bipolar transplantation of the latissimus dorsi. The total arc of active flexion ranged from 105 to 140 degrees, and flexion strength varied from 0.7 to 5 kg. Flexion contractures of 10 degrees and 15 degrees occurred in 2, and active supination of 20 to 50 degrees was achieved in 6. Moneim and Omer achieved satisfactory flexion (100 degrees or more) in 3 of 5 patients. The other two initially had paralysis of the latissimus dorsi and achieved only 65 to 70 degrees of flexion. Preoperative EMG evaluation of the muscle and the presence of normal (grade 5) strength were recommended. Botte and Wood noted satisfactory function in 4 of 5 patients treated by unipolar or bipolar latissimus dorsi transfer; flexion averaged 87 degrees (range, 35 to 130 degrees).

**Pectoralis major muscle transfer.** Both unipolar transfer and bipolar transfer of the pectoralis major have been reported. Our preference is the use of bipolar transfer without a fascial graft.

Operative procedure. Prerequisites for this technique are that the sternocostal portion of the pectoralis major muscle be of normal strength and the elbow have full passive range of motion.

**Complete bipolar transplantation of the pectoralis major.** The bipolar transfer was described by Carroll and Kleinman (Figs. 34.53 and 34.54). The patient is placed in the supine position with a flat bolster under the blade of the scapula and the upper extremity draped free. A long, curvilinear incision is made along the anterior thorax from the seventh sternocostal joint proximally to two fingerbreadths inferior to the clavicle. The incision continues laterally to the coracoid process and then distally along the anteromedial aspect of the arm to the level of the axilla. Alternatively, an inframammary incision may be used and extended proximally in the anterior axillary line. The muscle is exposed subcutaneously by elevating a large flap of skin and breast with either approach. Once the acromion and the entire pectoralis major muscle are exposed, a second curvilinear incision is made over the antecubital fossa with its transverse limb across the fossa and the longitudinal limb extending medially and distally for 6 cm. The entire pectoralis major muscle is detached from its origin along the medial half of the clavicle and its sternocostal border. A 12- by 6- cm strip of anterior rectus abdominis fascia is included and used to create a distal tendon of insertion. Care must be taken during dissection to avoid injury to both the lateral and medial pectoral neurovascular bundles, which are easily damaged. The lateral pectoral nerve lies close to the costal margin of the muscle proximally and the deltopectoral groove laterally and passes in proximity to the thoracoacromial trunk. The medial pectoral nerve lies deep to the pectoralis minor muscle; it supplies small but powerful muscular branches that penetrate the pectoralis minor to reach the pectoralis major.

Postoperative protocol. Postoperative immobilization and rehabilitation after pectoralis major muscle transfer are the same as for latissimus dorsi transfer.

Expected outcomes. Botte and Wood reported satisfactory results in all 5 patients who underwent bipolar pectoralis major transfer but cautioned against using it in women because of the cosmetic disfigurement. Matory and associates transferred the lower sternocostal segment of the pectoralis major in 7 patients and achieved a functional range of elbow flexion (mean, 98 degrees) in all. Loss of extension was just 15 to 25 degrees, and sustained flexion strength averaged 8 lb. Tsai and others performed a modified bipolar transplantation of the pectoralis major (with the lateral half of the clavicular origin left intact), supplemented with a unipolar transfer of the pectoralis minor, in 4 patients. Three of the four achieved excellent results (full extension with at least 60 degrees of flexion), and the other patient required a secondary Steindler flexorplasty.

Complications. Doyle and associates reported on 2 patients in whom transient nerve palsy developed after pectoralis major transfer, 1 median and 1 radial nerve palsy. Both problems resolved completely after several weeks. Simultaneous contraction of the triceps, when the elbow is flexed, by the transposed pectoralis major has been reported.All 3 patients had brachial plexus injuries and initially had paralysis of the triceps and elbow flexors. Axonal confusion after regeneration seems to be the probable explanation, but the complication cannot be predicted.

**Free muscle transfer for elbow ﬂexion in delayed cases** Indications. In the complete or upper type of brachial plexus injury, FFMT is indicated to restore elbow flexion when no local muscles of adequate strength are available for tendon transfer or when nerve transfer to the elbow flexors is inadvisable because of chronicity or muscle damage. The operation is technically more difficult in young children and less successful in older individuals. Satisfactory results are not usually obtained in patients older than 65 years because of poor reinnervation of the transferred muscle. Other problems such as elbow joint damage, capsular contractures, vascular injury, poor soft tissues or skin coverage, and systemic injury or illness may limit the indications for this procedure, as well.

Vascular injuries to the subclavian artery, in particular to the thoracoacromial trunk, are a relative contraindication. Dissection through dense scar tissue in proximity to a subclavian artery injury or arterial reconstruction is technically challenging. If attempted at all, surgical confirmation of adequate vessels to permit microvascular anastomoses to the free functioning muscle is required before muscle harvest, and a vascular surgery team should be available in the operating theater if needed. If adequate arterial and venous sources cannot be identified, the muscle transfer should be aborted.

Donor nerve selection. Selection of the donor nerve depends on the availability of functioning nerves after the brachial plexus injury and any subsequent attempt at reconstruction. For late reconstruction of elbow flexion, the spinal accessory nerve is our donor motor nerve of choice (Fig. 34.55). Proximal intercostal motor nerves (third to sixth ribs) are also commonly used because their position relative to the axilla permits direct transfer with sufficient length. We have found the results of these sources to be equivalent in ultimate strength and time to reinnervation of the muscle. When these proximal intercostal nerves have already been used, more inferior intercostal nerves can be harvested. In C5- C6 or C5- C7 paralysis, the ulnar nerve offers an excellent option as a donor motor nerve for innervated muscle transfer. In rare instances, other options may be considered, such as the use of a regenerated local nerve or contralateral C7 transfer.

Donor muscle selection. When selecting a functioning free muscle, one must consider its vascular and nerve innervation, crosssectional area (a measure of ability to do work), and fiber length and excursion (to determine adequate excursion), all in comparison with the biceps brachii parameters. The muscle must also be expendable, with minimal donor site morbidity. It can be expected to function best when used for a single purpose, but it may be used to both flex the elbow and extend the wrist, for example. A variety of muscles have been used as FFMTs, but the gracilis is certainly the most commonly used. The rectus femoris and latissimus dorsi are occasionally selected.

Expected outcomes. More than 90% of patients who have undergone FFMT for elbow flexion have recovered more than 90 degrees of elbow flexion after transfer of the spinal accessory nerve or intercostal nerves. Patients older than 40 years did not consistently recover comparable degrees of elbow flexion despite successful reinnervation to the transferred muscle. The final quantitative assessment of muscle strength showed 10% to 20% of the mean values of normal elbow flexion more than 2 years after surgery.

**Wrist arthrodesis.** Nerve reconstruction for brachial plexus injury seldom restores function of the extrinsic wrist and finger motors. Recovery of grasp, whether spontaneous or the result of FFMT, may also be impaired by wrist extensor paralysis. When tendon transfer is not possible because of the lack of available motors (generally the case in adult traumatic plexus injury), the wrist may be supported with splintage. Long- term use of splints is often poorly tolerated and may result in skin breakdown over insensate skin. A wrist arthrodesis in these instances can be of benefit (see Chapter 12).

## NEUROPATHIC PAIN MANAGEMENT

Last, but not least, is the management of neuropathic pain in patients with traumatic brachial plexus injuries. Despite interventions to improve or restore function in the affected upper extremity, neuropathic pain may remain an important problem; the quality of life of patients with chronic neuropathic pain is significantly reduced. In patients with traumatic brachial plexus injury, the development of severe intractable neuropathic pain is highly variable. For example, intractable pain is very rarely reported to develop in Asian patients, whereas severe neuropathic pain develops in many American and European patients. Pain will develop in up to 80% of patients with a traumatic brachial plexus injury and will persist in 20%. The pathogenesis of the pain is not well understood but may include postganglionic neuromas in some patients and central mechanisms of deafferentation pain attributed to segmental hyperactivity in the dorsal horn cells in patients with avulsion injuries.

Effective management of neuropathic pain in these patients requires (1) early recognition and treatment, (2) a multidisciplinary team, and (3) identification and targeting of physiologic as well as social and psychological factors. Nonsurgical options include psychosocial interventions (such as counseling, relaxation therapy, biofeedback, and support groups), rehabilitation (such as physical therapy and desensitization), and pharmacotherapy (combination of antidepressants, anticonvulsants, and analgesics). If the pain remains intractable over time despite aggressive nonsurgical management, surgical options should be considered. In some patients with postganglionic injury, neurolysis, neuroma resection, or nerve grafting of affected neural elements (or any combination of these therapies) may afford some relief of pain. Patients with root avulsions and chronic neuropathic pain may be candidates for radiofrequency destruction of the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ lesioning) of affected levels. With careful patient selection and adequate technique, the success rate can be as high as 66% to 90% at 4- year follow- up, but it tends to decrease to 50% with longer follow- up. Finally, central nervous system neuromodulation therapies such as spinal cord, motor cortex, or deep brain stimulation are increasingly being used by some groups to treat refractory neuropathic pain and can be considered.
