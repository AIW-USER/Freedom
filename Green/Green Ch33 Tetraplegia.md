Spinal cord injury is devastating to the patient, the family, and his or her friends. In an instance, life has been dramatically downgraded with respect to ambulation, limb usage, bladder/bowel function, and independence. Spinal cord injury has substantial physical, emotional, and psychosocial ramifications. Approximately 54 people per million sustain injuries to the spinal cord each year (over 17,000 new cases per year), with more than half occurring at the cervical spine and 14% resulting in ventilator dependence.

In the United States, there are approximately 300,000 persons living with disability due to spinal cord injury. Cervical- level spinal cord injuries impair arm and hand function. The majority of spinal cord injury research has focused on restoring ambulatory function; however, patients have consistently rated hand function as the most desirable function to recover, prioritizing hand function over regaining sexual function, trunk stability, bowel and bladder control, and walking. Recovering even partial hand function can have enormous impact on quality of life and independence.

Almost 80% of persons with spinal cord injury are young men, and many require lifelong care to complete their activities of daily living (ADLs) such as feeding, dressing, bathing, bladder/bowel care, and mobility. Maintenance cost estimates for care range from $45,000 to $200,000 per year per patient, depending on the severity and level of the injury. This expensive care places substantial physical and financial stressors on the patient and family.

The most common causes of spinal cord injury are motor vehicle accidents (38%), falls (32%), violence (14%), and sports trauma (8%). Tetraplegia refers to a spinal cord injury sustained to one of the eight cervical segments of the spinal cord, whereas paraplegia refers to spinal cord injury in the thoracic, lumbar, or sacral segment of the spinal cord. The most frequent neurologic categories of spinal cord injury have been incomplete tetraplegia (47%), incomplete paraplegia (20%), complete paraplegia (20%), and complete tetraplegia (12%). This chapter will discuss the evaluation and treatment of patients sustaining spinal cord injuries resulting in tetraplegia.

## GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS IN TETRAPLEGIA

### Pathogenesis of the Injured Spinal Cord

The cervical spinal cord contains eight cervical segments encased in the seven cervical vertebrae. The bone and ligamentous structure of the cervical spine allows a wide range of motion, thus exposing the cervical spinal cord to increased risk of injury. Injury occurs most commonly through a flexion deformity with bone or disk compression of the cord after fracture or dislocation or through traction on the cord with translation due to spine instability (Fig. 33.1). Over days and weeks, acute hemorrhage and edema subside, followed by local reparation and finally scar formation.

Following trauma to the spinal cord, the cord is divided into three basic regions: the supralesional segment, the injured metamere, and the infralesional segment (Fig. 33.2). The supralesional segment refers to the region of the spinal cord cephalad to the site of injury, which retains normal function. The infralesional segment refers to the spinal cord below the region of trauma. The infralesional segment consists of normal spinal cord and normal associated peripheral nerves that have been disconnected from descending cortical control either completely (no intentional movement realized) or incompletely (some degradation of control, typically with substantial spasticity).

The injured metamere is the region of the cord that suffered the direct trauma. This region can be discrete and well localized, as may occur from a local disk herniation. Alternatively, the region can be lengthy and involve multiple segments, as may occur following a fracture- subluxation of the vertebrae or gunshot wound. This differentiation is important because the most vulnerable portion of the spinal cord is the central gray matter—the home of the cell bodies of the alpha motor neurons. These cell bodies will be lost at the site of injured metamere. Wallerian degeneration occurs and proceeds in a distal direction down the peripheral nerve. Over time, the associated muscles undergo severe atrophy and fatty degeneration. Similar to a peripheral nerve injury, the associated muscles can only be effectively recovered in the acute period following the injury. In patients who are candidates for nerve transfer, surgery should be undertaken early to optimize the results of the reconstruction. The more time that transpires without innervation in these affected muscles, the less effectively they can recover. After a year from injury, the results following nerve surgery are compromised. After 2 years, useful recovery is not expected. Thus earlier surgical intervention is recommended.

The region of central gray matter destruction may extend partially into an adjacent region, which may not be clinically apparent via denervation atrophy. For example, the cephalad extent can degrade the innervation of the supralesional segment, such that the axon supply to a healthy and normal muscle is reduced. These muscles often have good active control and appear normal. This potential axon depletion must be understood when contemplating nerve transfer surgery and will be discussed in more detail later.

### Considering Spontaneous Recovery

Within the first year following the injury, there is some potential for spontaneous recovery. Surgery must avoid disrupting any natural recovery. There are two types of spontaneous recovery, American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade conversion (Table 33.1) and recovering a cord level or more. Regarding conversion to a different ASIA grade, patients with more favorable injuries have more positive rates of spontaneous recovery. For instance, only about 10% to 15% of patients assessed as ASIA A will convert to ASIA B with an even smaller percentage recovering to ASIA C or D. In contrast, these conversion rates are higher for patients with ASIA B with up to 40% recovering to ASIA C or D. Accordingly, up to 60% to 80% of patients initially categorized as ASIA C will spontaneously convert to ASIA D.In about 80% of patients who demonstrate an improvement in ASIA grade, this recovery will occur within the first 3 months, according to data from the European Multicenter Spinal Cord Injury (EMSCI) Database. Fewer than 20% of patients who improve in ASIA grade first show delayed signs of recovery between 6 and 12 months. After 1 year, this figure drops to only 5%.

| B | Incomplete | Sensory, but not motor, function is preserved below the neurologic level and extends through sacral segments S4-S5 |
| C | Incomplete | Motor function is preserved below the neurologic level, and most key muscles below the neurologic level have a muscle grade of less than 3 |
| D | Incomplete | Motor function is preserved below the neurologic level, and most key muscles below the neurologic level have a muscle grade that is greater than or equal to 3 |

In addition to ASIA grade conversion, persons with tetraplegia can also recover segment levels distal to their site of injury. This recovery can have a major impact on upper extremity function. For example, the difference between a C4 and C5 injury or a C5 and C6 injury translates into tremendous gains in independence. From 57% to 72% of patients with ASIA A injuries will experience recovery of at least one adjacent motor level. Within this subset, 30% recovered two levels. In the setting of complete spinal cord injuries, some authors have described the concept of the “zone of partial preservation,” which designates muscle groups that are partially innervated after injury. Partially innervated muscle groups that possess 1- 2/5 British Medical Research Council (MRC) grade power (Table 33.2) or greater are significantly more likely to recover to 3/5 strength versus muscles groups that are initially 0/5 grade.

As discussed above, the majority of patients even with sensorimotor complete injuries (ASIA A) will regain one or two motor levels within the first year after injury. Recovery occurs most frequently within the first 3 months. By 9 months, the majority of patients have experienced their maximum spontaneous recovery.

The strength of muscles at the level of injury may also improve over time, most commonly within a year of injury. Ditunno and colleagues and Waters and associates reported upper limb strength recovery rates using manual muscle testing and the MRC grading system (see Table 33.2). One- third of muscles classified as grade 0 at 1 month after injury improved to grade 3 at 4 to 6 months after injury; improvement was documented in some cases up to 24 months after injury. All upper limb muscles with an initial strength of grade 1 improved to at least grade 3 by 1 year after injury, with the exception of the triceps. If a muscle’s strength was grade 2 or better at 1 month after injury, the median time for full recovery was 6 months.

Based upon the available evidence, we defer surgery until 6 to 9 months following injury. At that time, patients that fail to demonstrate improvement in ASIA grades or recovery of additional segment levels distal to their site of injury are encouraged to proceed with surgery.

### Referral for Upper Limb Reconstruction

The referral process for persons with spinal cord injury remains disorganized and underutilized. Curtin and colleagues have shown that approximately 65% of the 5000 cervical spine injures per year would benefit from upper limb reconstruction to improve function and increase independence. Despite the potential benefits, less than 500 surgeries are performed annually to improve upper limb function. This disconnect is multifactorial with responsibility levied on physiatrists, surgeons, and institutions.

Published reports of tendon transfers for persons with spinal cord injury have shown improvement in quality of life and overall function. Despite this evidence, many physicians and even spinal cord rehabilitation facilities remain wary or are unaware of the potential benefits. Many patients that would benefit from surgical reconstruction are never referred for surgical evaluation. With the advent of nerve transfers, this problem will escalate unless knowledge dispersion increases across all subspecialties that manage persons with spinal cord injury. A person with spinal cord injury can typically gain one cervical spinal level of function through tendon transfers. Augmenting the current tendon transfer paradigm with nerve transfers can restore two cervical spinal cord levels of function. However, nerve transfers require a viable muscle target for reinnervation, and irreversible motor endplate demise occurs as early as 18 months from injury. Therefore most nerve transfers have limited success after 12 months from injury. This window of opportunity highlights the time sensitivity and necessity of prompt referral for upper limb reconstruction.

### Preoperative Evaluation

The reconstructive surgeon is less concerned with the specific level of cervical injury than with the remaining strength of each muscle in the upper limb. Classically, tetraplegic patients have been classified by the injured cervical spine segment of C1- C7. Previously, it was assumed that the level of paralysis and sensory loss coincided with the bony injury, producing a precise transverse spinal cord lesion. Careful examination of tetraplegic patients has shown, however, little relationship between the level of the skeletal lesion and the spinal cord lesion. In addition, lesions may be asymmetric with unusual patterns of spared sensory or motor function. A classification for spinal cord injury commonly used by surgeons other than hand surgeons is the ASIA classification (see Table 33.1). This classification is based on intact muscle groups.

The classification used in this chapter was developed by an international group of hand surgeons in 1978 in Edinburgh and modified in 1984 at Giens, France. The International Classification for Surgery of the Hand in Tetraplegia (ICSHT) grades the level of the spinal cord injury based on the number of muscles with grade 4 strength below the elbow and includes ICSHT levels 0 to 9 (Table 33.3). The ICSHT level characterizes the most common patterns of presentation, based on the number of functional muscles below the elbow. The majority of persons with complete spinal cord injury will fall into ICSHT motor groups 2 and 3.

Another aspect of patient evaluation includes assessment of the general suitability for surgery. The assessment is based on various factors including age, occupation, level of education, learning capacity, motivation, economic support, family support, and agency support. The patient must have a clear understanding of what can and cannot be accomplished with surgery.

#### Physical Examination

The examination begins with a thorough history of the injury and treatment to date. Subsequently, a meticulous physical examination is performed, including sensory and motor testing to establish the patient’s group (ICSHT level 0 to 9). Manual muscle testing (see Table 33.2) is performed on all muscle groups in the upper extremity. The surgeon can make a working list of tendons that are available for transfer (grade 4 strength or higher and expendable) and muscles that are paralyzed (grade 1 strength or lower). Once the muscles with grade 4 strength have been identified, the patient is assigned a group number (see Table 33.3). For example, a patient with grade 4 strength of the brachioradialis (BR), both radial wrist extensors, the pronator teres (PT), and the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) would be classified as a group 5 patient. One of the difficulties in physical examination is distinguishing between group 2 and group 3 injuries. In group 2 patients, only the extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) has a grade 4 or higher strength, and physical examination reveals substantial radial deviation of the wrist with resisted wrist extension. In group 3 patients, both the ECRL and the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) have strength of grade 4 or higher. In a group 3 patient, the wrist extends with less radial deviation and often grade 1 to 3 strength is present in the PT, the next most caudal muscle. In thinner patients, the ECRL, a flat muscle, and the ECRB, a more bulbous muscle, can be palpated while muscle strength is being tested (Fig. 33.3).

In addition to manual muscle testing, the surgeon must also examine the passive range of motion to evaluate joint and muscle contractures along with the resting posture of the hand. The examiner can induce a tenodesis effect by passively flexing and extending the wrist while observing passive myostatic tensioning of the digital extensors and digital flexors, respectively. The hand must have passive mobility and tenodesis to achieve success. For example, if the thumb rests in a fully supinated position and lacks any tenodesis pinch during wrist extension, tendon transfers for pinch will be ineffective without skeletal stabilization (carpometacarpal [CMC] fusion) in a key pinch position.

The management of the wrist and hand in tetraplegia follows the concepts of “hierarchy of hand function” or the “reconstruction ladder.” The primary fundamental movement is wrist extension, which yields tenodesis for grip as the fingers flex into the palm and tenodesis for lateral pinch as the thumb adducts against the index finger. Wrist extension also aligns the finger flexors along Blix’s length- tension curve for maximum active grip. The second most essential movement is lateral pinch, which is necessary to perform numerous ADLs. Most daily activities are accomplished with lateral pinch, such as holding an object, turning a key, or using a fork (Fig. 33.4). The third essential motion is grasp, which allows the holding of objects (Fig. 33.5). The fourth and last movement is digital opening for object acquisition. The reason to place this function lowest on the ladder is that wrist flexion yields passive digital opening, which is often adequate for object procurement. The next step is to determine the patient’s needs and match the needs with the muscles available for transfer. For example, in a group 4 patient, the BR is available because the patient has the biceps and brachialis for elbow flexion (and, therefore, no function will be lost with the transfer); the ECRL is also available because the patient has the ECRB for wrist extension. The PT can also be used for transfer, but this could result in diminished pronator power and loss of strength for manual wheelchair function. To complete the assessment, the muscles available for transfer (BR, ECRL) are matched with the needed functions according hierarchy of hand function or the “reconstruction ladder” combined with other surgical options such as arthrodesis and tenodesis. In a group 4 patient, pinch and grasp are deemed most important, as these patients have active wrist extension. Thus the BR and ECRL are selected for thumb pinch and finger flexion, respectively. The BR will be transferred to the flexor pollicis longus (FPL) because it has (and needs) less excursion. The ECRL will be transferred to the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendons, which is synergistic and provides greater excursion. Surgical adjuncts to be considered include optimizing the thumb position for key pinch with a CMC fusion and augmenting digital extension for object acquisition via extensor pollicis longus (EPL) and extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tenodesis. If necessary, intrinsic reconstruction can be added during a second surgery. By evaluating what the patient has, what the patient needs, and what is available, the surgeon can determine the optimal combination of tendon transfers and adjunctive procedures to restore function (nerve transfers will be discussed later).

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

Injuries of the spinal cord produce a different nerve deficit than do brachial plexus injuries or isolated peripheral nerve injuries. The pattern seen with a spinal cord injury is based on segmental innervation. This concept means that the anterior horn cells in the spinal cord are arranged in a pattern from cephalad to caudad. Motor nuclei form longitudinal columns crossing several segmental levels, innervating the peripheral musculature in a predictable manner.

Fig. 33.7 shows the segmental anatomy, based on Zancolli’s clinical experience with tetraplegic patients. When a spinal cord injury occurs, the motor nuclei cephalad to the injury will be functional; the motor nuclei at or caudad to the level of the injury will be nonfunctional.

In addition to hand surgery reconstruction, noteworthy research on and clinical treatment with a neuroprosthesis was performed from the 1980s to the 2000s. The use of electrical current for muscle stimulation in patients with spinal cord injury led Keith and associates at Case Western University to develop a fully implantable functional neuromuscular stimulation neuroprosthetic system. This technology takes advantage of paralyzed muscles that are no longer under cortical control (due to spinal cord injury) but have intact spinal reflex arcs (infralesional segment). These muscles can be stimulated to contract with weak electrical currents. The stimulation is controlled by normal muscle signals, nerve signals, or volitional movements above the level of spinal cord injury (supralesional segment), typically from the contralateral shoulder or neck.

The neuroprosthesis is indicated for tetraplegic patients for whom standard surgical procedures or orthotic devices cannot provide useful improved function. Most commonly, the neuroprosthesis is implanted in patients in groups 0, 1, 2, and 3. More than 250 adults and children have received the neuroprosthesis system worldwide. As of 2008, the company that manufactured the device discontinued its production. The second- generation 12- channel devices with telemetry and myoelectric control are only available under research protocols; however, they have shown promise in achieving independent two- handed use.

### Indications and Contraindications for Operative Intervention

The goal of tetraplegic hand reconstructive surgery is to increase the patient’s independence. Candidates for surgery include patients (1) whose recovery from injury has stabilized, (2) have an unchanging upper extremity motor examination, and (3) whose functional impairments can be improved by surgical reconstruction. Ideally, the patient is free from contractures and pain with spasticity well controlled. The patient must have the ability to comply with the postoperative rehabilitation regimen, have appropriate postoperative support services, and be highly motivated to improve function. It is also critical that the patient be medically stable, including bowel and bladder function and blood pressure control, and be without urinary tract infection or decubitus ulcers.

Two factors have had a uniformly adverse effect on the results of surgical treatment: spasticity and psychological problems. Spasticity that is uncontrollable is a strong contraindication to surgery. Freehafer and colleagues and Moberg stated that some spasticity might be helpful, but judging the degree of spasticity that is compatible with good results is very difficult. In fact, Moberg noted that even unappreciated spasticity leads to poor results. Psychological impairment, unrealistic expectations, and insufficient motivation to complete the operative and postoperative protocols will also result in disappointing results. The surgeon, patient, and therapist must be able to communicate effectively and share *realistic* expectations of the benefits of surgery. Patient- reported outcome measures, such as the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), are particularly useful. The COPM assesses an individual’s perceived occupational performance in the areas of self- care, productivity, and leisure. Allowing a prospective patient to visit a previous patient with a similar ICSHT classification that has undergone surgical reconstruction is particularly useful.

## TYPES OF OPERATIONS FOR TETRAPLEGIA

Four types of surgical procedures are most commonly performed: tendon transfer, tenodesis, arthrodesis, and nerve transfer (discussed later).

### Tendon Transfer

Tendon transfers are preferred over tenodesis or arthrodesis because these restore active control and strength. However, the primary problem after spinal cord injury is a paucity of adequate donor muscles available for transfer. It is critical to ensure that sacrifice of a potential donor muscle for transfer will not create another functional deficit. The available muscles are prioritized first for wrist extension, then for pinch, then for grasp, and finally for release functions. Tendon transfer principles, including donor availability, donor strength, amplitude of excursion, synergism, passive mobility of recipient joints, and condition of the soft tissue bed, are prerequisites. When tendon transfer options are exhausted, additional hand functions may be achieved through tenodesis and/or arthrodesis.

### Tenodesis

*Tenodesis* is defined as the movement of one joint produced by the motion of an adjacent (usually proximal) joint. For instance, passive extension and flexion of the wrist prompts digital flexion and extension, respectively. Although the finger and thumb muscles are paralyzed, there is sufficient remaining myostatic tension to induce reciprocal action of the digital flexor and extensor tendons that cross the wrist joint. Spinal cord injury patients learn to use the tenodesis effect naturally and commonly have functional but very weak pinch or grasp owing to the tenodesis effect alone. The tenodesis effect can be enhanced surgically without the need to transfer an active muscle. Passive tenodesis procedures anchor the paralyzed tendon proximal to the wrist joint to decrease effective tendon excursion and thus increase digital movement with active wrist movement. Common tenodesis techniques include anchoring the finger flexors and extensors for grasp and release, respectively.

### Arthrodesis

Thumb CMC joint arthrodesis is useful to stabilize this multiarticular joint for pinch. The index finger proximal IP (PIP) joint can undergo arthrodesis to stabilize the finger as a firm post for pinch. The wrist is never fused because the tenodesis effect would be lost. Surgical techniques for arthrodesis are no different in the paralytic hand. When possible, rigid fixation is preferred to allow earlier weight bearing and expedient mobilization.

### Nerve Transfer

Nerve transfers have supplemented and even supplanted the use of tendon transfers in some clinical scenarios. The ICSHT is applicable to nerve transfer with important considerations. Nerve transfers will be discussed in detail following the section on tendon transfers. Tendon transfer reconstruction is based on the patient’s international classification group (see Table 33.3). Elbow procedures are discussed first, followed by procedures for grasp, pinch, and release.

## SPECIFIC OPERATIONS FOR TETRAPLEGIA

Tendon transfer procedures will be discussed first, followed by nerve transfer procedures. Discussion of procedures will begin with the elbow and proceed in a distal direction. ### Elbow Extension Tendon Transfer

Most persons with cervical spine injury lack triceps function and elbow extension. Restoration of elbow extension carries a high priority. Active elbow extension assists the patient in reaching objects above the shoulder level, improves driving ability, aids in wheelchair propulsion, permits pressure relief, and facilitates independent transfer. Additionally, active elbow extension provides an antagonist to elbow flexion, which facilitates improved function through tenodesis after hand reconstructions that use the BR as a tendon transfer. Pinch strength has been shown by Brys and Waters to improve from 0.15 to 3.9 pounds in wrist extension in patients after elbow extension tendon transfers that improve elbow extension strength. Two options exist for tendon transfer: deltoid to triceps transfer and biceps to triceps transfer.

The biceps and the deltoid are innervated from the spinal cord at a higher level than the triceps. The posterior deltoid to triceps transfer has been used extensively over the past 30 years, but more recently, the biceps to triceps transfer has been used more frequently.

### Biceps to Triceps Tendon Transfer (Video 33.1)

The biceps to triceps tendon transfer is our preferred method for establishing elbow extension (Fig. 33.8). The biceps is used as the donor muscle. To ensure that function will not be lost with transfer of the tendon, the strength of the brachialis as an elbow flexor and of the supinator as a forearm supinator must be verified before transfer. Manual muscle testing for elbow flexion is performed, attempting to isolate the brachialis by supinating the forearm, which relaxes the biceps. This can be verified by palpating the groove between the anterior tubular biceps and the flatter brachialis that resides posterior to differentially test strength. In reviewing the segmental innervations of the upper limb from the spinal cord (see Fig. 33.7), note that the biceps, brachialis, and supinator are all innervated at about the same level (C5- C6). If the patient has strong wrist extension, the spinal cord lesion should be below C6, sparing the biceps, brachialis, supinator, and wrist extensors, but above the innervation of the paralyzed triceps. Such findings on clinical examination verify that transfer of the biceps will not lead to a functional loss. Electromyography is not part of the routine evaluation. If there is any doubt about the strength of the brachialis or supinator, the biceps muscle can be injected with bupivacaine, which induces temporary paralysis. Subsequently, the strength of the brachialis and supinator can be assessed.

The biceps to triceps tendon transfer can be performed using a medial or a lateral routing technique. The lateral technique was first described by Friedenberg in 1954 and later reported by Zancolli. No loss of active elbow flexion was noted, although flexor strength was diminished by 24%. In 1988 Ejeskär reported his results using the lateral routing technique in five patients, including the first complication of radial nerve palsy. Injury to the radial nerve and medial routing is strongly preferred.

The procedure is performed with the patient supine, a roll under the operative shoulder blade, and the limb draped free with a sterile tourniquet high on the arm. The incision extends from the midhumerus on the medial side, transversely across the antecubital crease, and courses distally, centered over the biceps insertion on the radial tuberosity.The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is identified and protected as the biceps tendon is dissected free from its medial and lateral fascial attachments in the arm and then sharply released from its insertion on the bicipital tuberosity. The lacertus fibrosus is freed from the forearm fascia during the dissection and can be preserved as a second tail of the biceps tendon for subsequent weaving. The muscle is raised in a proximal direction, with care taken to preserve the musculocutaneous nerve running on its undersurface and along the brachialis muscle.

A second (posterior) incision is performed over the distal one- third of the triceps tendon, passing lateral to the olecranon to avoid subsequent olecranon pressure ulceration and allowing an adequate skin bridge with the medial incision. A subcutaneous tunnel is made around the medial elbow from the anterior wound to the posterior wound, creating a line of pull that is straight and free for medial rerouting of the biceps (Fig. 33.9A). The medial intermuscular septum may need to be partially resected, and care is taken to protect the neighboring ulnar nerve. The biceps tendon is passed from the anterior wound into the posterior wound. The tendon can be routed superficial or deep to the ulnar nerve. We prefer deep to avoid iatrogenic compression of the ulnar nerve, particularly if there are future advances in neuroregeneration (see Fig. 33.9B). A No. 5 nonabsorbable grasping suture is placed into the end of the biceps tendon.

Tendon rehabilitation requires supervised therapy for months. The biceps to triceps can be mobilized early (7 to 10 days) or delayed (3 weeks) depending on the patient’s age, patient’s compliance, and the strength of the tendon transfer repair. The rehabilitation is divided into three phases: early mobilization, mobilization, and strengthening/functional retraining. Mobilization is initiated in gravity- eliminated plane within a limited arc of flexion. Flexion is progressively increased 15 degrees per week as long as no extension lag develops. Strengthening and wheelchair propulsion are prohibited until 12 weeks from surgery. Transfers and weight shifts are initiated 16 weeks from surgery.

The strength of the biceps to triceps transfer continues to improve for at least a year, resulting in substantial improvements in performing ADLs. Antigravity elbow extension is obtainable in the vast majority of cases (Fig. 33.10; Video 33.2). Mulcahey and coworkers published a randomized prospective comparison of posterior deltoid to biceps transfer versus biceps to triceps with medial routing transfer. These authors reported that at 2 years, seven of eight arms treated with biceps to triceps transfer had antigravity use of the arm, whereas only one of eight arms treated with posterior deltoid to triceps transfer had antigravity use of the arm.

### Posterior Deltoid to Triceps Tendon Transfer

The posterior deltoid must have adequate strength to motor elbow extension. Shoulder extension and palpation best assesses posterior deltoid strength and turgor. The examination can be performed with the patient seated, although placing the patient prone provides a more reliable assessment. Similar to biceps to triceps transfer, a supple elbow with near complete range of motion is also necessary. Patients with an elbow contracture require resolution of the contracture prior to surgery.

The posterior deltoid to triceps transfer uses the posterior third and the posterior half of the middle third of the deltoid muscle as a donor to provide active elbow extension (Fig. 33.11). An interposition tendon graft is used, and the triceps tendon serves as the insertion.

A distal incision is made over the distal third of the triceps tendon. The triceps’ tendon is identified. The ulnar nerve is isolated and retracted out of harm’s way. A subcutaneous tunnel is then created between these two incisions using sequential hemostats (see Fig. 33.12D). An allograft is favored to bridge the distance between the deltoid muscle and triceps’ tendon. The allograft is thawed and the proximal end is sutured to the posterior deltoid and underlying periosteum (see Fig. 33.12E). The remaining tendon is then passed through the subcutaneous tunnel to the distal incision (see Fig. 33.12F). The elbow is placed in full extension and the allograft woven into the triceps tendon and/or bone for additional augmentation (see Fig. 33.12G).

Several alternatives exist for the interposition graft. Moberg originally used toe extensors from the second, third, and fourth toes to allow at least three weaves at each attachment site. Alternative graft materials have included fascia lata, tibialis anterior, and extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) Alternatively, Castro- Sierra and Lopez- Pitaused the central one- third of the triceps. In this method, a 1- cm strip from the central one- third of the triceps is harvested from its insertion in a retrograde manner (proximally based), mobilizing it sufficiently to graft back to the distal end of the posterior deltoid. Reflecting the central third of the triceps with a bone block and internally fixing this to a bone block from the deltoid insertion has also been described.

After the interpositional graft has been placed, the transfer is sewn into place and tensioned with the shoulder in 30 to 40 degrees of abduction and no forward flexion so that the elbow can flex to 60 degrees with moderate tension. Most commonly, a well- padded long- arm cast is applied to hold the elbow in about 10 degrees of flexion. Forward flexion and shoulder adduction are avoided during the 6 weeks of cast immobilization. Gentle active exercises are performed to slowly gain elbow flexion for the next 3 months with a rate of 5 to 10 degrees of increased flexion per week.

Posterior deltoid to triceps transfers are compromised by the prolonged shoulder and elbow immobilization required postoperatively, as well as tendon graft elongation over time. Elongation of the tendon graft leads to decreased strength over time. Fridén and colleaguesused intraoperative metal markers at the ends of the tendon grafts and measured an average of 2.3 cm of elongation after 2 years. They changed their postoperative regimen to include longer elbow extension immobilization and increased shoulder abduction. Follow- up of five patients using this regimen using the same markers averaged 0.8 cm.

## PROCEDURES FOR PRODUCING FOREARM PRONATION

The forearm is the “forgotten joint” with regards to movement and balance. The forearm is articulated at both its proximal and distal ends (proximal and distal radioulnar joint joints). The strong interosseous ligament tightly links the radius and ulna and participates in load transfer. Forearm rotation is balanced by two muscles that supinate (biceps and supinator) and two muscles that pronate (PT and quadratus). Supination is primarily controlled by the C5 and C6 nerve roots (biceps and supinator muscles). Pronation is principally motored by the C7 (PT) and C8 (pronator quadratus) nerve roots. Midcervical tetraplegia frequently results in preservation of the supinators and loss of the pronators. The resultant imbalance will result in considerable deformity over time. The deformity is initially supple, but over time, the contracture(s) becomes fixed (Fig. 33.13).

![[Fig.33.13.png]]
> **Fig. 33.13** Spinal cord injury at C5-C6 with bilateral fixed supination forearm contractures. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospitals for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

Pronation is important to patients who have only active wrist extension (groups 2 and 3). These patients use the automatic or tenodesis effect for grasp, but if the hand cannot be pronated, gravity cannot be used to provide a tenodesis effect for digital extension and release. Those patients using a tenodesis brace need pronation for the same reason.

Zancolli produced pronation by converting the biceps into a forearm pronator. He rerouted the tendon around the radius, converting the biceps from a supinator to a pronator.

### Biceps Rerouting Procedure

The biceps is rerouted from a supinator moment arm that wraps counterclockwise around the proximal radius to a pronator moment arm by wrapping the tendon clockwise around the proximal radius (Fig. 33.14). This procedure can be done in isolation or in combination with one of the hand reconstructive procedures listed below. Indications for surgery are passive mobility of the forearm into pronation, without active pronation, as well as a biceps tendon of grade 5 strength that is a deforming force as a supinator leading to dysfunctional supination posturing. The biceps needs to be available for transfer, but not for transfer to the triceps to provide elbow extension strength.

## OPERATIONS FOR GROUP 0 PATIENTS

By definition, patients in this group have no muscle groups below the elbow with grade 4 strength to provide restoration of pinch function. Elbow extension transfers should be performed as described earlier. Some group 0 patients may have both a weak BR and a weak ECRL. Assessing the strength of the BR muscle is difficult and requires an experienced examiner. Examination is best performed by having the patient flex the elbow against resistance with the forearm in neutral position while palpitating the BR muscle belly and assessing its strength and turgor (Fig. 33.16). Transfer of the BR to the radial wrist extensors may provide sufficient strength to extend the wrist against resistance. This tendon transfer technique is described later for group 1 patients. Because the muscle’s strength is no better than grade 3, the patient needs good elbow extension to serve as an antagonist to the BR transfer to enhance wrist extension function. If sufficient wrist extensor strength is obtained, a wrist- driven hinged tenodesis splint can be used (Fig. 33.17), or a passive pinch tenodesis reconstruction can be considered.

### Passive Key Pinch Reconstruction

In ADLs, lateral key pinch is most useful. Although pulp- to- pulp pinch and opposition pinch have been attempted, restoration of these pinch patterns has largely been abandoned because opposition pinch is beyond the scope of restoration in high- level injuries.The KISS engineering principle (keep it simple, stupid) is directly applicable to upper extremity reconstruction in persons with tetraplegia. The surgeon should perform well- indicated and established tendon transfers to restore the basic functions of wrist extension, lateral pinch, grasp, and digital opening. Complex and complicated tendon transfers that attempt to restore multiple or advanced functions should be avoided. For lateral pinch, skeletal control of three segmental joints (CMC, MCP, and IP) must be combined with tenodesis vectors that appropriately position the thumb for effective pinch and release.

Because no additional muscles are available in this group (after BR to ECRB transfer), tenodesis of the FPL will produce a weak pinch that is motored by active wrist extension (from the BR to ECRB transfer). Tenodesis of the EPL is used for release of pinch, activated through passive wrist flexion. Alternatives to passive key pinch tenodesis reconstruction include a tenodesis splint (see Fig. 33.17) or functional electrical stimulation.

### Split Flexor Pollicis Longus Transfer

A midaxial incision is made along the radial side of the thumb. The dorsal flap is elevated the EPL tendon identified. The volar flap is elevated with the neurovascular bundle and the FPL isolated. The FPL is split in the midline (Fig. 33.22A). The radial half is traced to its insertion into the distal phalanx and cut (see Fig. 33.22B). This radial half is passed in a dorsal direction over the IP joint axis (see Fig. 33.22C). The tendon is then passed through the EPL and back to itself. Tension is set so that the IP joint rests in slight flexion. A small nonabsorbable suture is used to secure the repair making sure the suture captures the FPL and the EPL tendons. A 0.45 Kirschner is driven from the thumb tip across the IP joint for stabilization (see Fig. 33.22D). The pin is cut short and a pin cap applied. ### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GROUP 1 PATIENTS

For group 1 patients, a modified Moberg procedure is performed with the following elements:
- Stabilization of the thumb IP joint by split FPL transfer
- FPL tenodesis to the distal radius
- If necessary, stabilization of the MCP joint for excessive flexion via EPB/EPL tenodesis to the dorsal first metacarpal or for hyperextension via volar capsulodesis or MCP arthrodesis.Regarding the thumb (CMC) joint, this universal joint allows multiplanar motion. Without the support of the thenar muscles, however, this articulation can be unstable. The thumb may “miss” the index finger during attempted lateral pinch (Fig. 33.23). The pros and cons of CMC arthrodesis need to be contemplated. Stabilization of the CMC joint provides a foundation for stronger lateral pinch (Fig. 33.24). The position of arthrodesis, however, varies from the able- bodied person (45 degrees of palmar abduction). Persons with tetraplegia usually lack intrinsic function and MCP joint flexion. The lack of index MCP joint flexion alters the recommended position for CMC arthrodesis. Considerable less palmar abduction is necessary (about 20 degrees) for alignment between the thumb and radial side of the index proximal IP joint. This position narrows the thumb index web space and hampers acquisition of large objects such as bottles or cans. Generally, in patients with ligamentous laxity and very unstable CMC joints, arthrodesis is performed (discussed later). In patients without ligamentous laxity and some CMC joint stability, initial arthrodesis is not recommended. The patients are counseled about the possibility of additional surgery if adequate alignment cannot be achieved. Persons with spinal cord injury often rapidly extend their wrist such that the thumb flicks into the appropriate position for lateral pinch.

## DIFFERENTIATING GROUP 2 AND 3 PATIENTS

Patients in groups 2 and 3 have strong BR and wrist extension. Differentiating the strength of the ECRL (group 2) and the strength of the ECRB (group 3) is difficult. If the patient has only a strong ECRL, the wrist will posture in marked radial deviation. If the patient has strong ECRL and ECRB, there will be less radial deviation. The ECRB inserts on the radial side of the long metacarpal, however, and some radial deviation will still be present unless the ECU is working to balance the wrist. In general, if the examiner can “break” the active resisted wrist extension and push the wrist into flexion, only the ECRL is present or both the ECRL and ECRB are present, but neither is available for transfer. If the ECRB has less than grade 4 strength and the ECRL is transferred to the finger flexors, the result is devastating with functional loss of wrist extension during fist formation.

Methods have been described to test ECRL versus ECRB strength. Mohammed and coworkers described the Bean sign (see Fig. 33.1):

The exploration is performed under local anesthesia with sedation. A transverse incision is performed over the second extensor compartment. The wrist tendons are isolated and the ECRL is cut, leaving a remnant of distal tendon. The patient is instructed to extend the wrist fully and the strength is assessed. ## OPERATIONS FOR GROUP 2 PATIENTS (ICSHT MOTOR GROUP 2)

There are a variety of surgical of options for groups 2 patients. The BR is always available for transfer and has been used in a variety of ways. Street and Stambaugh recognized the value of side pinch or “key grip” in 1959 and, in an attempt to increase the strength of pinch, transferred the BR to the FPL. House and coworkers transferred the BR to the FPL for a one- stage active key pinch reconstruction, and House’s group and Zancolli preferred to fuse the thumb CMC joint in 30 degrees of abduction to improve positioning.

Moberg used the BR to improve the release phase of the grasprelease sequence. He placed traction on the EPL, EPB, and abductor pollicis longus (APL) tendons at surgery. He transferred the BR to the tendon that provided the best abduction. He performed this operation as a second- stage procedure, following construction of a passive key pinch.

### Brachioradialis to Flexor Pollicis Longus Tendon Transfer

A longitudinal radial incision is made from the styloid to the proximal third of the forearm. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous and radial sensory nerves are protected. The BR insertion into the radial styloid is isolated. The tendon is released and the BR dissected from its investing fascia. The dissection proceeds into the proximal one-third of the forearm to increase its excursion to 3 cm or more (Fig. 33.25A). The radial sensory nerve and radial artery must be protected throughout the dissection. The volar portion of the skin flap is mobilized. The fascia is incised along the FCR tendon. The underlying median nerve and FDS tendons are mobilized. The deep FPL is isolated (see Fig. 33.25B). The radial artery and its venae commitantes are elevated from the distal radius. The BR tendon is passed deep to the radial artery and woven through the FPL tendon using a Pulvertaft weave (see Fig. 33.25C). Tension is adjusted until there is firm lateral pinch during wrist extension and thumb opening during wrist flexion (see Fig. 33.25D).

Tendon rehabilitation following BR to ECRB transfer can be mobilized early (7 to 10 days) or delayed (3 weeks) depending on the patient’s age and cooperation along with the strength of the tendon transfer repair. The rehabilitation is divided into three phases: early mobilization, mobilization, and strengthening/functional retraining. Regarding the split FPL transfer, the Kirschner wire is removed 3 to 4 weeks after surgery.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GROUP 2 PATIENTS

For group 2 patients we prefer to transfer the BR to the FPL for active pinch and perform a split FPL tendon transfer to stabilize the thumb IP joint. We also perform a biceps to triceps tendon transfer to provide active elbow extension and to act as an antagonist to the BR tendon transfer. We prefer early mobilization in compliant patients with adequate tendon girth to achieve a strong repair. See Case Study 33.1.

## OPERATIONS FOR GROUP 3 PATIENTS (ICSHT MOTOR GROUP 3)

### Zancolli’s Staged Reconstruction for Group 3 Patients

For group 3 patients, Zancolli recommended a two- stage reconstruction. The first stage consists of (1) extensor tenodesis of the thumb and fingers, (2) intrinsic tenodesis using the “lasso” procedure to reduce clawing, and (3) thumb IP joint fusion. The second stage includes (1) ECRL to FDP tendon transfer, (2) BR to FPL tendon transfer, (3) a volar thumb MCP capsulodesis if the thumb MCP joint is hyperextended, and (4) a dorsal tenodesis of the EPB tendon if the thumb is in excessive flexion.

#### Stage 1: Extensor Tenodesis

An 8- to 10- cm longitudinal incision is made on the radial side of the forearm to expose the dorsum of the radius proximal to the extensor retinaculum. The common extensor tendons are divided just distal to the musculotendinous junction. Suturing the distal stumps together before severing the tendons preserves tendon balance. A bone window large enough to accept the distal stumps of the common extensor tendons is made on the dorsum of the radius. Stout nonabsorbable are passed through the tendon ends and brought through two drill holes made proximal to the bone window. Tension on the sutures draws the tendon stumps snugly into the bone window. The sutures are tied to each other. The tension is set so that the finger MCP joints are extended to 0 degrees with the wrist in slight palmar flexion. Next, a strip of the APL tendon is isolated and cut proximal. The tendon is passed through the tunnel of the EPL, around the Lister tubercle, and into the window on the dorsum of the radius. The APL and the EPL are sutured to the bone, which produces thumb extension. Arthrodesis of the thumb IP joint is performed in slight flexion to prevent hyperflexion of the joint and to facilitate side pinch. Additionally, a lasso is performed, as described later in the intrinsic rebalancing section.

#### Stage 2: Extensor Carpi Radialis Longus to Flexor Digitorum Profundus Transfer

When scar resolution is complete and the tenodesis is functional, the second- stage operation is performed. The same radial lateral incision used in stage 1 is reopened to expose the ECRL and FDP tendons at their musculotendinous junction in the wrist crease. The ECRL is detached as distal as possible, delivered proximal to the retinaculum, and dissected in a proximal direction to improve its excursion and line of pull. The ECRL tendon is passed into the volar forearm, woven through each of the profundus tendons, and sutured to produce balance among all the fingers. Preliminary tension on the fingers should be observed during passive wrist flexion and extension with the elbow in varying degrees of flexion. Final tension should be set so that the fingers and thumb come together in a lateral pinch with the wrist in approximately 40 degrees of dorsiflexion and the elbow at 90 degrees.

The tension is set loosely enough to allow easy release of the fingers during passive wrist flexion and so that the digital tips touch the palm with the wrist in full passive extension.

Next, the BR is transferred to the FPL as described earlier. In addition, a split FPL tendon transfer is performed.

### House’s One- Stage Key Pinch Reconstruction for Patients in Groups 2 and 3

For group 2 or 3 patients, House recommended a one- stage key pinch reconstruction. The following are components of the reconstruction:
- Thumb CMC joint arthrodesis
- BR to FPL tendon transfer (as discussed previously)
- Split FPL tendon transfer (as discussed previously)
- EPL tenodesis

#### Carpometacarpal Fusion

A 3- to 4- cm dorsal incision over the CMC joint between the APL and abductor pollicis brevis (APB) is performed. After exposure of the joint, a Kirschner wire is placed from the thumb metacarpal to the index metacarpal to preposition the thumb in the desired key pinch position: about 20 degrees of extension, 30 degrees of palmar abduction, and 10 degrees of pronation. The pulp of the thumb should be able to pinch against the radial side of the index finger at the proximal IP joint level. A thin slice of articular cartilage is resected from the base of the thumb metacarpal and the distal trapezium with a small oscillating saw. The Kirschner wire is withdrawn from the index metacarpal, the joint surfaces are firmly opposed, and the Kirschner wire is advanced back into the index metacarpal, securing the thumb position before final internal fixation. After appropriate positioning adjustments are made, final fixation is secured with a mini–fragment blade plate (Fig. 33.26).

The proximal portion of the EPL tendon is exposed and divided at the level of the musculotendinous junction. The EPL is left in the third dorsal compartment. ### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GROUP 3 PATIENTS

In patients that are ICSHT motor group 3, strong wrist extension is present, and therefore one of the wrist extensor tendons is expendable. We prefer to transfer the ECRL to the FDP tendons for grasp. Similar to group 2 patients, we also transfer the BR to the FPL for active pinch and perform a split FPL tendon transfer to stabilize the thumb IP joint. At the same setting, a biceps to triceps tendon transfer is performed to provide active elbow extension and to act as an antagonist to the BR tendon transfer. We prefer early mobilization in compliant patients with adequate tendon girth to achieve a strong repair.

As discussed earlier, synchronous digital opening is difficult to achieve, as MCP joint extension is mainly an extrinsic function (EDC, extensor indicis proprius, and extensor digiti quinti) and IP joint extension is primarily an intrinsic function (interossei and lumbricals). The only way the extrinsic system can elicit IP joint extension is by limiting MCP joint extension (e.g., Zancolli tenodesis of the FDS or House intrinsic tenodesis), which is discussed later. In group 3 patients, we tend to avoid setting the ECRL to FDP tendon transfer tight, as opening and object acquisition will be limited. Following pinch and grip reconstruction, the status of the hand can be reassessed. A misaligned thumb can be corrected by CMC arthrodesis, and limited digital opening can be corrected by extensor tenodesis ± intrinsic reconstruction.

## OPERATIONS FOR PATIENTS IN GROUPS 4 AND 5 (ICSHT GROUPS 4 AND 5)

These groups of patients are less common compared with ICSHT groups 2 and 3. These patients have grade 4 or greater strength in the BR, ECRL, ECRB, PT (group 4), and FCR (group 5). The remaining muscles below the elbow are less than grade 4 strength. These groups of patients uniformly have strong triceps and do not require tendon transfer for elbow extension.

In group 4 patients, the PT is available for tendon transfer. The PT provides strong action for manual wheelchair propulsion; therefore it should be used with caution. We agree with House and Van Heest that transferring the PT does produce a functional loss of pronation. Zancolli transferred the PT to the FCR to further enhance the strength of the wrist- activated tenodesis of digital extension. House described transferring the PT to the FPL as part of a two- stage reconstruction.

In group 5 patients, the FCR produces wrist flexion strength that improves wrist extension–driven tenodesis function of the fingers. We concur with House and Van Heest to leave the FCR in place to preserve maximal wrist control.

In groups 4 and 5, the BR, ECRL, and PT muscles are available for transfer. The ECRB and the FCR are preserved to retain good wrist control. Because there are more muscles available in patients in groups 4 and 5, the surgeon can choose a two- stage reconstruction of grasp (flexor phase) and release (extensor phase), using the method described by either House or Zancolli. At the first stage (extensor phase), the thumb is positioned and tenodesis of the extensor tendons is performed. At the second stage (flexor phase), the thumb and finger flexor tendons are activated for grasp and the finger intrinsics are activated for balance.

### Alternative Techniques for Patients in Groups 4 and 5 (ICSHT Groups 4 and 5)

Alternative techniques for reconstruction of groups 4 and 5 have been described by Fridén and colleagues. They have used a single- stage grip, pinch, and release *a* dvanced *b* alanced *c* ombined *d* igital *e* xtensor *f* lexor *g* rip (ABCDEFG) procedure, or “alphabet reconstruction.” They also describe using this technique for group 3 patients with verified grade 5 strength of both the ECRL and ECRB. If used for higher- level patients (group 5 or above), not all seven elements of the reconstruction would be necessary.

#### ABCDEFG Procedure (Alphabet Reconstruction)

This procedure has seven components, all of which may be used for each patient or some of which can be chosen based on the specific passive mobility or tenodesis pattern. The seven elements of the reconstruction include:

1. BR to FPL tendon transfer to provide pinch
2. ECRL to FDP tendon transfer to provide grasp
3. EPL tenodesis for passive thumb extension
4. ECU tenodesis to provide balanced wrist extension (without excessive radial deviation)
5. Split FPL transfer to prevent the Froment sign
6. Thumb CMC fusion to preposition the thumb in key pinch position
7. Intrinsic tenodesis to provide passive PIP extension

The technique for each of the elements of this procedure is described in this chapter, except for the ECU tenodesis, which is not described as part of other reconstructive plans.

#### Extensor Carpi Ulnaris Tenodesis

As part of the tetraplegic hand reconstruction, excessive radial deviation can be corrected by the ECU tenodesis procedure.

A curved incision is made at the wrist over the sixth compartment. The extensor retinaculum is opened over the ECU tendon, which is elevated with a tendon hook. The tendon is shortened approximately 2 cm by folding the tendon and securing it with two size 2- 0 double mattress nonabsorbable sutures, and then sutured onto the joint capsule of the ulnar head with two 4- 0 absorbable sutures. Passive position of the wrist decreases radial deviation but maintains flexion and extension.

Fridén and Reinholdt present 18 patients treated with a tetraplegia hand reconstruction that included ECU tenodesis and compared this with a historical control group of 33 patients treated without ECU tenodesis. The ECU tenodesis group had less radial deviation deformity and had 6.1 kg of grip strength compared with 2.1 kg of grip strength in the control group. No differences in pinch strength were noted.

Fridén and colleagues reported on 14 patients with 16 hands treated with the alphabet reconstruction and rehabilitation and compared this group with a historical control group of 15 patients with 18 hands treated with two- stage reconstruction. All patients were in ICSHT group 4. In the alphabet group, grip strength was 6.5 kg, compared with 1.6 kg in the control group. Pinch strength was similar in the two groups. The opening of the first web space was 5.5 cm in the alphabet group, compared with 3 cm in the control group. Complications included one tenolysis, one ruptured intrinsic tenodesis that required another repair, and one subsequent tendon transfer for digital opening. Four patients could not make a full fist with a 1- to 2- cm gap between fingertip and distal palmar crease.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GROUPS 4 AND 5

We prefer to preserve the PT and FCR unless there are extenuating circumstances, such as injury to the BR muscle or incomplete injuries with irregular muscle preservation. We prefer to stick to the KISS principle and restore active pinch and grasp. The patient will glean the greatest functional benefit from pinching and holding objects. Subsequently we will reassess the situation. If digital opening for object acquisition is problematic, secondary reconstruction of digital extension will be considered using the above described techniques. In addition, these groups of patients are prime candidates for nerve transfers to restore digital extension (discussed later). We have found the adductionopponensplasty transfer too complicated for the surgeon and patient resulting in inconsistent outcomes.

## OPERATIONS FOR PATIENTS IN GROUPS 6 AND 7

Patients in group 6 have at least grade 4 strength of BR, ECRL, ECRB, PT, FCR, and EDC. Patients in group 7 have at least grade 4 strength of the EPL for thumb extension. In these two groups, restoration of extension is not required, and reconstruction involves a pinch (BR to FPL tendon transfer) and grasp (ECRL to FDP tendon transfer) reconstruction. An intrinsic reconstruction is added as needed.

For group 6 patients, the finger extensors are strong but EPL function is either absent or too weak to extend the thumb adequately. The EPL can be transferred to the digital extensors to improve thumb extension. This can be done in conjunction with the flexor transfers or staged, based on the surgeon’s and therapist’s preference.

For group 7 patients, both finger and thumb extensors are strong, so reconstruction involves a pinch (BR to FPL tendon transfer) and grasp (ECRL to FDP tendon transfer) reconstruction. Thumb control can be augmented through either CMC fusion or opponensplasty. Factors include the thumb’s stability, tendons available for transfer, patient’s needs, and surgeon preference. Intrinsic reconstruction can also be done to augment digital grasp in patients with severe clawing and difficulty with object acquisition. Intrinsic muscle palsy with functioning extrinsic extensors and flexors leads to digital “roll- up,” which makes effective grasp difficult. Digital roll- up occurs when the distal IP (DIP) joint flexes before the PIP, and the MCP joint fails to flex. Such hand positioning makes it difficult to grasp large objects.

### Intrinsic Reconstruction: The Lasso Procedure

Intrinsic reconstruction can be performed using a tendon graft as discussed previously for the House’s two- stage grasp, pinch, and release procedure (see Fig. 33.28) or the lasso procedure (Fig. 33.30). The Zancolli lasso procedure helps initiate MCP flexion as the first stage of digital grasp, followed by PIP and finally DIP flexion, to enable grasping of large or cylindrical objects. Because the FDS is nonfunctional, the procedure is a static tenodesis that increases with progressive wrist extension. Transfer of the BR or PT to the FDS can activate the transfers and increase grip strength.

Data from House JH, Shannon MA: Restoration of strong grasp and lateral pinch in tetraplegia: a comparison of two methods of thumb control in each patient, J Hand Surg Am 10[1]:22–29, 1985.)

A zigzag incision is designed across the distal palmar crease and the flexor tendon sheath. Skin flaps are elevated and the neurovascular bundles are protected. The FDS tendon is identified via a window in the midportion of the second annular pulley.

The proximal and distal limits of the A1 pulley are identified at the base of each digit. Traction is applied to the A1 pulley using forceps to be certain that a transfer around the A1 pulley will pull the MCP joint into flexion. If the MCP joint does not flex, the proximal portion of the A2 pulley should be included. The flexor tendon sheath is opened at the level that ensures MCP joint flexion. Both slips of the FDS are divided distal to the A2 pulley, near the middle phalanx insertion, leaving a small FDS tail to prevent swan neck deformity. The tendon is transected and pulled through the window. The tendon is then passed over the proximal half of the second and entire first annular pulley. The transected end of the FDS is sewn back to itself proximal to the first annular pulley (see Fig. 33.28). This technique creates a passive tenodesis to MCP joint extension that increases with progressive wrist extension. The tensions are adjusted such that the digits are aligned for lateral pinch during wrist extension and extended for release during wrist flexion. The patient is immobilized with the MCP joints in 30 to 40 degrees of flexion with the PIP joint free. Early mobilization of the FDP tendons is necessary to prevent adhesions within the flexor sheath. After 4 weeks the same position is held with a dorsal forearm- based resting splint. Active range of motion and light ADLs are initiated. After an additional 4 weeks, the splint is worn only at night and strengthening exercises are added to the regimen.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: GROUP 6 AND 7 PATIENTS

The ECRL is transferred to the FDP for active finger flexion as described earlier, and the BR or PT is transferred to the FPL for active pinch. Our preference is to transfer the BR to the FPL for active pinch, preserving the full forearm pronation. Secondary surgeries to improve thumb control and intrinsic reconstructions are added as needed.

## OPERATIONS FOR GROUP 8 PATIENTS (ICSHT GROUP 8)

Group 8 patients are uncommon and many have incomplete injuries, which yield different needs and surgical expectations. There is grade 4 muscles strength in the BR, ECRL, ECRB, PT, FCR, EDC, and EPL as well as and partial finger flexion (FDP). These patients require active pinch reconstruction via BR to FPL tendon transfer. Their grip strength may need to be augmented for grasp. Surgical options include suturing the FDP of all four digits to one another in the distal forearm or performing a standard ECRL to FDP tendon transfer. In addition, intrinsic reconstruction and opponensplasty may be required.

## OPERATIONS FOR GROUP 9 PATIENTS (ICSHT GROUP 9)

Patients in group 9 lack only intrinsic function. Similar to group 8 patients, many have incomplete injuries, which generates different needs and surgical expectations. This is the least common group of patients with tetraplegia, and they are treated similar to patients with combined low median and ulnar peripheral nerve palsy. These patients benefit most from opponensplasty, adductorplasty, and intrinsic reconstructions, tailored to the patient. The Zancolli lasso operation is generally more effective because the superficialis muscles are under voluntary control in group 9 patients.

## NERVE TRANSFERS IN TETRAPLEGIA

Nerve transfers have supplemented even supplanted the use of tendon transfers in many clinical scenarios. One entity that has benefited tremendously from nerve transfers has been cervical spinal cord injury (SCI) or tetraplegia. Historically, the most common method of upper limb reanimation following SCI has been via tendon transfers.Nerve transfers are a more recent advancement and offer several potential advantages. First, a number of additional donor muscle/nerve groups become available that may allow for a more robust reconstruction. Second, the original muscles are recovered resulting in more normal upper limb biomechanics compared with tendon transfers. Third, most of the nerve transfer operations are in close proximity and thus require minimal immobilization following surgery. Often patients can resume their typical self- care without considerably more assistance in the immediate postoperative period. Fourth, fewer stages of surgery are required as nerve transfers for digital extension can be performed at the same time as nerve transfers for flexion.

Utilizing nerve transfers to restore upper extremity function following SCI is not a novel concept. Early attempts were made by French hand surgeons in the 1960s, and variations on these concepts were attempted by a Polish group in the 1980s and again in the early 2000s. What has changed since these early attempts is the refinement of our understanding of nerve transfers and the microanatomy of the peripheral nerves. With a detailed understanding of the microanatomy of the brachial plexus and peripheral nerves along with their branching patterns, the surgeon can select redundant donors and target specific recipients. This technique avoids or minimizes a donor deficit and specifically targets critical nerve branches that provide needed function.

### Pathogenesis of the Injured Spinal Cord

We previously discussed the differences between the supralesional segment, the injured metamere, and the infralesional segment (see Fig. 33.2).

![[Fig.33.2.png]]
> **Fig. 33.2** A, Traumatic spinal cord injury produces three different patterns of disruption. B, Spinal lesions above the level of the injury maintain intact control of the peripheral nervous system (C) at the site of actual injury, which experiences both direct tissue disruption and alteration of in-and outgoing connections, and (D) the levels below the site of injury that maintain normal anatomic configuration but no longer receive descending electrical inputs. (Redrawn from Brown JM. The reconstructive neurosurgery of spinal cord injury. In: Dimitrijevic MR, Kakulas BA, McKay WB, Vrbova G, eds. Restorative Neurology of Spinal Cord Injury. New York: Oxford University Press; 2012, pp 134–168.)

The supralesional segment refers to the region of the spinal cord cephalad to the site of injury, which retains normal function. The injured metamere is the region of the cord that suffered the direct trauma. Within the injured metamere the anterior horn cells are damaged and similar to a peripheral nerve injury, the associated muscles can only be effectively recovered in the acute period following the injury. Surgery should be undertaken early to optimize the results of the reconstruction. The more time that transpires without innervation in these affected muscles, the less effectively they can recover. Denervated muscles will readily accept a nerve transfer within the first 9 to 12 months. After a year from injury, the results are compromised. After 2 years, useful recovery is not expected. Thus earlier surgical intervention is recommended. The infralesional segment refers to the spinal cord below the region of trauma. This consists of normal spinal cord and normal associated peripheral nerves that have been disconnected from descending cortical control. The axons that innervate these muscles will maintain their integrity. These paralyzed muscles with retained innervation may be candidates for late nerve transfer years after injury. The ability to stimulate the muscle and the presence of adequate motor action potentials in the specific muscles being targeted are predictors of a successful nerve transfer.

Acute complete injuries are the most straightforward to address. Similar to tendon transfer, a donor nerve must be available and expendable. The donor nerve’s function should be redundant to another preserved muscle to avoid functional loss. The donor nerve should also be in proximity to its intended target.

Based upon the available evidence regarding spontaneous recover discussed earlier, we typically defer nerve surgery until 6 to 9 months following injury. At that time, patients who fail to demonstrate improvement in ASIA grades or recover of additional segment levels distal to their site of injury are encouraged to proceed with surgery.

### Selecting Nerve Sources for Transfer

Available nerves for transfer are determined based upon ICSHT with a few minor modifications. This system was developed to help surgeons define a treatment paradigm based upon available muscle- tendon units for transfer. The ICSHT is applicable to nerve transfer with important muscle/nerve additions and considerations. For example, the teres minor and supinator muscles are innervated by the C5 nerve root. These muscles are not useful tendon transfers but do provide options for nerve transfers.

### Goals of Surgery

#### Principles and Perioperative Considerations

In persons with tetraplegia, the ultimate results of hand reanimation must achieve greater function compared with their current status. Some patients are quite agile using a tenodesis grasp. They can pick up a cell phone, wrap their fingers around a utensil to write, and pick up a cup between two hands to drink. All these activities are accomplished without any active finger motion. We perform the GRASSP (Graded Redefined Assessment of Strength, Sensation and Prehension) testto assess hand function to determine baseline function and compare to the functional gains obtained after surgery.

The remaining tone in an upper motor neuron paralyzed hand can also be helpful in performance of daily activities. For example, retained finger flexor tone augments tenodesis grasp. Nerve transfer surgery cuts the recipient motor nerve and eliminates spastic flexor tone creating floppy fingers awaiting reinnervation. This loss of spasticity may temporarily downgrade the patient’s tenodesis grasp and interfere with ADLs until effective reinnervation is achieved. The surgeon must discuss this sequence of events prior to surgery as some patients will elect not to proceed for fear of losing function.

Reconstruction of the hand chronically compromised by SCI requires additional considerations. Any joint contracture must be resolved prior to nerve transfer. Therapy and/or surgical release is performed prior to nerve transfer surgery. Effective reinnervation will not be effective without adequate passive mobility.

#### Functional Goals of Surgery

The primary goal is effective prehension via pinch between the thumb and proximal phalanx of the index finger. This pinch must be strong enough to hold a utensil, pick up a cell phone, and self- catheterize. This is best coupled with active finger extension that provides an adequate aperture to allow object acquisition. A secondary goal is grasp with the remaining fingers. Similarly, an adequate aperture for object acquisition to surround a cup must be achieved. Grasp should be sufficiently strong to securely hold a 12- oz can.

Nerve transfers alone are frequently unable to achieve an optimal result. Hand intrinsic muscles are not able to be targeted with nerve transfers. If these muscles have not maintained innervation, they will exhibit poor ability to stabilize the thumb for lateral pinch. Similarly, clawing of the fingers due to absent intrinsic muscles will often occur. Additional tenodesis procedures, tendon transfers, or arthrodesis may be necessary to maximize hand function. These procedures have been detailed previously in this chapter.

Thumb positioning for optimal pinch may require a thumb CMC fusion or an opponensplasty. Claw correction may require a tenodesis to position the MCP joints in slight flexion. This tenodesis may achieve active IP joint extension via nerve transfer driven extrinsic MCP joint extension (similar to the Bouvier maneuver). The concept of a staged operation with secondary tenodesis, tendon transfer, or joint arthrodesis following nerve transfer must be discussed with the patient.

### Specific Nerve Transfer Options

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ACUTE C5 PATIENT (ICSHT GROUP 1–2)

Patients must undergo surgery within the first year to reanimate recipient muscles corresponding to the injured metamere (identical to a peripheral nerve injury). Patients are monitored for 6 to 9 months to assess for spontaneous recovery. If the target muscles do not recover in a meaningful way, surgery is recommended. For the procedures described in this section, the target muscles are those of the injured metamere that do not stimulate.

In a C5 injury, the goals of nerve transfer surgery are active elbow extension, hand opening, pinch, and grasp. The following series of nerve transfers are performed: (1) teres minor branch of the axillary nerve to the radial nerve branch innervating the long head of the triceps (LHT), (2) supinator branches of the radial nerve to the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), (3) brachialis branch of the musculocutaneous nerve to the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN).

The goal of triceps reinnervation is regaining the ability to hold the arm antigravity when laying in the supine position. This active elbow extension augments reach, particularly for objects above the horizontal plane. This active elbow extension can also assist in active locking of the elbows to facilitate transfers in and out of their bed and wheelchair. This nerve transfer does not produce strong enough elbow extension to allow the patient to lift his or her body weight for transfers. This is a common goal of persons with SCI and must be clearly explained to the patient prior to surgery.

### Triceps Transfer

General anesthesia is used without muscle relaxants. The patient is placed in the supine position with the arm on a hand table. The incision is designed along the medial arm and across the axilla. In the arm, the brachial vein is visualized and protected. The tendon of the latissimus dorsi muscle is located and exposed. The tendon is retracted from the humerus and the axillary nerve is identified by palpation. The posterior circumflex humeral vessels are protected, and the axillary nerve is dissected. The axillary nerve has three branches. There is a large anterior branch, a smaller posterior branch, and a branch to the teres minor. The radial nerve is identified immediately adjacent to the axillary nerve, just posterior to the median and ulnar nerves (Fig. 33.31). The LHT motor branch is identified as the most proximal branch of the radial nerve. This recipient branch is dissected in a proximal direction and sectioned. If additional length is needed, it can typically be neurolyzed from the radial nerve proximally to gain a few additional centimeters. In cases where the nerve does not stimulate, the branch is also traced in a distal direction to confirm that it enters the triceps muscle. The teres minor motor branch is verified by stimulation and dissected in a distal direction. This branch is cut as distally as possible. The teres minor and triceps long head motor branch are coapted using a 9- 0 nylon suture and/or fibrin glue.

Theoretically, using the posterior branch of the axillary nerve may preclude posterior deltoid to triceps tendon transfer. Although, the biceps to triceps tendon transfer is the preferred tendon transfer to restore elbow extension.

There are a few postoperative restrictions. Patients transfers need to avoid any lifting within the axilla that could disrupt the nerve transfer. We avoid this method of transfer for 6 weeks. We also to avoid shoulder abduction beyond 45 degrees for 6 weeks.

### Achieving Finger Extension

Finger extension is accomplished by transferring the supinator nerves to the PIN (Fig. 33.33). The supinator nerves are typically available in patients with a C5 level injury (ICSHT 1 and below).

For the posterior approach, an incision is designed along the proximal third of the forearm. The BR and ECRL are separated to expose the underlying supinator muscle. The PIN is identified under the vascular leash of Henry. The superficial branch of the radial nerve and the ECRB branch are found immediately radial to the PIN. The PIN is traced to the supinator muscle. There are typically two or three supinator branches that reside adjacent to the main trunk of the PIN. The supinator branches are divided as distally as possible and the PIN is sectioned proximal. The supinator branches are coapted to the PIN nerve using 9- 0 nylon suture and/or fibrin glue (Video 33.4 ; Fig. 33.34). ### Achieving Finger Flexion

The brachialis branch of the musculocutaneous nerve is used as a donor to reinnervate the AIN to regain finger flexion. Previously, we performed direct repair of the brachialis branch to the AIN fascicle of the median nerve in the arm. The AIN- dominant fascicle is the arm, however, is not solely AIN innervated muscles and yields innervation of unintended muscles (e.g., PT and FCR) diluting the axons that arrive at the intended target. Thus, a very weak grasp is a common consequence of this technique (Fig. 33.35).

We now recommend a more extensile incision into the forearm. The AIN is identified and traced in a proximal direction ensuring reinnervation of the intended target. An incision is performed in the volar forearm, angled across the antecubital fossa, and then extended into the arm. The interval between the BR and the PT is developed, and the median nerve is identified. The median nerve is followed in a distal direction to identify its branches. The AIN is about one-third of the caliber of the main median trunk and is isolated along the radial side. It is critical to ensure by anatomic dissection that it is the correct branch. This AIN is carefully dissected from the rest of the median nerve in a proximal direction. At the level of the antecubital fossa, the AIN is intimately associated with the main median trunk and meticulous dissection is required. This AIN is followed as in a proximal direction as possible above the antecubital fossa, ensuring the branch is only the AIN.

Approximately 1 to 2 inches above the elbow, the intraneural branching within the median nerve becomes more formidable. At this level, the AIN is divided leaving a gap between the brachialis branch and the AIN.

The cut distal AIN is redirected in a proximal direction, superficial to the vessels. This maneuver lessens the gap between the brachialis branch and the AIN. Any remaining gap can be overcome by flexing the elbow or placing a small intervening nerve graft (see Fig. 33.35C).

The musculocutaneous nerve is isolated between the biceps and brachialis muscles. The biceps branch is proximal to the brachialis branch. If there is any question about the strength of the biceps (to avoid downgrading the patients elbow flexion strength), the biceps branch can be isolated and stimulated. The brachialis branch is isolated and confirmed by stimulation. The adjacent lateral antebrachial cutaneous (LABC) nerve will not stimulate. The brachialis branch is traced in a distal direction to gain length. There can be two or three brachialis muscle branches. We prefer to use all the branches to maximize the number of axons entering the recipient AIN branch. The individual branches are dissected in a proximal direction to the common brachialis motor branch.

The brachialis branches are coapted to the AIN or the intervening nerve graft using a 9- 0 nylon suture and/or fibrin glue. If elbow flexion is necessary for the coaptation, the arm was is held in 90 degrees of flexion during closure. After surgery, the arm is immobilized with the elbow at 90- degrees of flexion for 4 to 6 weeks and then progressively extended over the next 6 weeks.

#### Chronic C5 Patient (ICSHT Group 1–2)

In the chronic phase following SCI, if the triceps do not stimulate (preoperative or intraoperative), nerve transfer will fail. Tendon transfer is the preferred technique to restore elbow extension. The biceps to triceps transfer is the preferred technique and precludes the use of the brachialis branch of the musculocutaneous nerve for hand reanimation. An alternative option is to transfer the supinator branches to the AIN to restore grasp (instead of finger extension) (Video 33.5). A prerequisite is that the AIN targets must respond to stimulation. If wrist extension is absent, a functioning and strong BR tendon should be transferred to the ECRB.

On the other hand, if the triceps does respond to stimulation, its innervation is in the infralesional segment and nerve transfer can be considered. When all the potential targets stimulate in the chronic period, we currently undertake the reanimation in two nerve transfer stages (Fig. 33.36). First, the triceps transfer (as described above) and at the same time will connect the brachialis branch of the musculocutaneous nerve to a sural nerve graft that is tunneled into the volar forearm. This strategy avoids any immobilization related to the brachialis nerve transfer and keeps the target finger flexors intact until the axons are in proximity, to avoid denervation atrophy. We then allow the axons to grow through the sural graft (similar to a cross- face nerve transfer), for 9 to 12 months. The second stage is then undertaken at that time and involves supinator to PIN and the distal end of the sural graft to the AIN with axons readily available to the AIN at the time of the distal repair. These functions will then emerge at about the same time several months later.

#### C6 Patients (ICSHT Group 3)—Availability of ECRB

In international classification group 3, a second wrist extensor is available, making an additional tendon or nerve transfer possible. In classic tendon transfer tetraplegia surgery, an ECRL to FDP tendon transfer is performed yielding strong finger flexion. Some surgeons may simply preserve the wrist extensor tendon for future needs relying on passive tenodesis grasp. The ECRB nerve can be used as a donor ensuring the ECRL is intact. The ECRB nerve can be transferred to the AIN for active finger flexion. The remaining ECRL does produce more radial deviation, but this posture can be corrected by a distal lateral- lateral tenodesis of the ECRL tendon to the ECRB tendon, providing more neutral wrist extension.

#### C7 Patients (ICSHT Group 4–6)—Pronator Teres

This group of patients typically have an intact triceps muscle and strong elbow extension. Our goals remain the same, but we now have an additional nerve that can be transferred. The supinator to PIN remains the primary transfer for finger extension. The PT branch is available for finger flexion (Fig. 33.37). This nerve transfer has several advantages. First, the transfer provides a direct antagonist function to the supinator, which is being used for finger extension. This facilitates training for rehabilitation protocols. Second, there are typically at least two good branches. If the nerve is quite strong, the more distal branch can be used and is able to sufficiently reinnervate the AIN. Sacrificing one branch will preserve forearm pronation. In cases with weaker PT, we direct both branches into the AIN. While this will initially sacrifice pronation, the reinnervation of the AIN will include the PQ, restoring pronation with the recovery of grasp.

In chronic injuries where the AIN distribution remains responsive to stimulation (infralesional segment), we have selectively reinnervated the FPL branch of the AIN using the distal branch of the ECRB. Using just this branch of ECRB leaves the proximal >two- thirds of the ECRB intact, which will preserve adequate wrist extension. Additionally, the ECRL still remains intact. We prefer selectively reinnervated the FPL to gain independent control of the thumb versus all the digits. This transfer requires meticulous dissection. If independent pinch is not achieved, a BR to FPL tendon transfer can be performed as discussed previously.

### Additional Considerations

Motor reeducation is instituted early after nerve transfers. It is important to practice nerve activation while awaiting the arrival of new motor function. For example, following supinator to PIN transfer, we ask the patient to concentrate on active supination yielding finger extension. This exercise is performed numerous times daily awaiting nerve regeneration.

We have found that timing of recovery differs between acute and chronic injuries. With acute injuries, the time course of recovery tends to mirror that of nerve injuries. Early signs of recovery may be early as 4 months with the supinator to PIN transfer, 6 months with the direct brachialis to AIN transfer, and closer to 8 months for the triceps nerve transfer. In chronic injuries, even the supinator to PIN transfer may take longer and not demonstrate functional recovery until 10 to 12 months. The reasoning behind the different rates of recovery is unclear but may be related to cortical reactivation. For all of these injuries, the time between the first observable movement until full strength ranges from a few months to a full year.

Following recovery of extrinsic movement, there is a tendency to for the hand to develop a claw deformity. IP flexion contractures and MCP joint hyperextension can develop. Therapy and a lumbrical bar will prevent contracture formation. An additional procedure for static or active intrinsic reconstruction may be required.

### Outcomes

Measurement of outcomes in surgery for tetraplegia has traditionally focused on range of motion, pinch, and grip strength. The World Health Organization has provided a framework for evaluating the consequences of disease through the *International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health.* This framework has been evaluated for patients with SCI. The classification measures not only impairment but also activity limitation and restrictions. Impairment is measured by objective physician- determined parameters such as range of motion and grip pinch strength. Quality of life is further assessed by outcome questionnaires such as the Quadriplegia Index of Functionand the Tetraplegia Hand Activity Questionnaire. This broader view of outcome assessment incorporates not only traditional measurements of physical impairment but also quality- of- life issues.

Patients with specific, realistic, functional goals will realize the best self- perceived outcomes. The recognition that objective measurements and legacy measures may not correlate with improved function has resulted in a dramatic shift in outcome assessments. The initiative toward patient- or caregiver- reported outcomes (PROs) has resulted in the development of reliable subjective measures. For persons with SCI, Shriners Hospitals for Children PROs uses the computer adaptive test (CAT) platform. This methodology has been shown to correlate with legacy measures, possess minimal floor and ceiling effects, and be less burdensome compared with previous measures. CAT platforms will change the manner in which we assess outcome in the near future.

## CONCLUSION

The management the upper limb tetraplegia requires a multidisciplinary approach. The drastic change from an able- bodied person to a person with SCI is a “game changer.” Routine ADLs become challenging and frustrating. Independence is replaced by dependence. Personal thoughts about bladder and bowel management become open dialogue. Hand and upper extremity reconstruction is only one facet of the medical care and support services necessary for rehabilitation after SCI. Utilizing the ICSHT and following guidelines regarding the hierarchy of hand function, a surgical and rehabilitative plan can be devised. The most useful evaluation of the tetraplegic patient involves the creation of a working list of what the patient *has* (grade 4 strength or higher) and what the patient *needs* (grade 1 strength or lower). The surgeon must determine the muscles and nerves are available donors for transfer and the muscles and nerves that best match the patient’s needs. The success of tendon transfer depends on a working understanding of the principles of work capacity, amplitude, and direction of pull. The success of nerve transfers depends on meticulous surgical technique that redirect unaffected nerve branches from redundant nerve structures to distal target nerves below the site of SCI. Nerve transfers must carefully consider the spinal cord level affected, the type of injury (upper versus lower motor neuron) and time since injury. Effective execution of a well- designed surgical plan will result in a grateful patient that is more independent and better able to perform ADLs. A successful outcome is rewarding to the patient, surgeon, and therapist.
