Damage to one of the three main nerves of the upper extremity causes loss of function due to paralysis of muscles and loss of sensation. Tendon transfers can be used to re- create the functions of some of the paralyzed muscles but cannot restore normal strength and coordination. Loss of function due to loss of sensation cannot be restored by tendon transfers but is often well accommodated by the eyes. This accommodation is lost when the eyes cannot see the hand such as in darkness or when the hand is in a pocket or bag searching for an object. The loss of sensation after radial nerve injury is minimal and does not involve critical areas of the hand, whereas the loss of motor function is major and significantly impacts function. Thus tendon transfers to restore radial nerve motor function have the potential to greatly improve hand function and the patient’s well- being and ability to pursue a “normal” life. In contrast, the loss of sensation after a median nerve injury involves the most critical areas of the hand, and absent or poor sensation or dysesthesia will reduce the potential benefit of tendon transfers to restore lost motor function.

Every surgeon will have her or his own indications for tendon transfer surgery and favorite transfers, which are based on experience and the teaching of their mentors. This is as comparative studies of different transfers in specific situations are rare. Thus transfers favored by some surgeons will not be favored by others. Perhaps the only universally accepted “best” transfer to restore a specific function is transfer of pronator teres (PT) to extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) to restore wrist extension.

## PRINCIPLES OF TENDON TRANSFER SURGERY

The principles of tendon transfer surgery are frequently reiterated but often forgotten. They were established by surgeons such as Mayer and Bunnell and must be adhered to regardless of whether the transfers are to restore median, radial, or ulnar nerve motor function.

### Prevention and Correction of Contracture

A passive, fluid range of joint movement, which is sufficient to improve hand function, must be present before a tendon transfer can be performed. This is because no tendon transfer can move a stiff joint, and it is impossible for a joint to have a greater range of active motion postoperatively than its preoperative range of passive movement. Soft tissue and joint contractures are easier to prevent than to correct, so it is imperative that all joints are kept supple after any nerve injury, particularly those with surrounding soft tissue injuries.

### Tissue Equilibrium

The timing of tendon transfers is controversial, but all authors agree that no transfer should be done until the local tissues are in optimal condition. Steindler’s expression, “tissue equilibrium,” implies that soft tissue induration has resolved, the wounds have matured, the joints are supple, and the scars are as soft as they are likely to become. Performing tendon transfers, or any elective operation, before tissue equilibrium has been reached is to invite disaster. If scar tissue remains after maximum recovery has been achieved, then the surgeon must consider providing new skin coverage with flaps before transfer. Alternatively, she or he must use transfers that avoid the scarred areas. Tendon transfers work best when passed between the subcutaneous fat and deep fascial layer and are unlikely to work at all in a scar pathway. Brand emphasized the concept of gentle tunneling using a blunt- tipped instrument and probing natural tissue planes to find the path of least resistance. When performing tendon transfers, the surgeon should minimize the likelihood of new scar formation, and skin incisions should be planned so that tendon junctures do not lie directly underneath them.

### Adequate Strength

The muscle chosen as the donor for transfer must be sufficiently strong to perform its new function in its altered position. The relative strengths (and excursions) of the donor muscle, as well as the muscles whose function they will replace, should be as well matched as possible (Table 31.1). The work capacity of a muscle is related to its volume.

Transfer of a muscle to replace the function of another inevitably weakens it, typically by one grade on the rather imprecise Medical Research Council (MRC) grading system. Thus, muscles that have been previously denervated, but have regained function, should only be used if they have regained near- normal strength.

### Amplitude of Motion

The surgeon must have an appreciation of the amplitude of tendon excursion for each muscle. This is proportional to the length of its individual muscle fibers (see Table 31.1). Lieber and colleagues have assessed muscle characteristics with intraoperative laser diffraction; however, this technique is not widely used and is complex. Boyessuggested the use of the following values for practical purposes.

Wrist flexors and extensors have excursion amplitudes of approximately 33 mm, whereas the finger extensors and extensor pollicis longus (EPL) have 50 mm and the finger flexors have 70 mm excursions, respectively. Thus a wrist flexor cannot be expected to restore the full range of active motion provided by a finger extensor, the function of which it is replacing. However, the range of active finger motion provided by a wrist flexor can be augmented through the tenodesis effect of active flexion and extension of the wrist. Thus following radial nerve transfers, wrist extension reduces tension in a flexor carpi radialis (FCR) to extensor digitorum communis (EDC) transfer and allows finger flexors to produce greater finger flexion. In contrast, wrist flexion increases tension in this transfer and allows it to produce increased “active” finger extension. This tenodesis effect explains the adage that the wrist should never, if at all possible, be fused in a person requiring tendon transfers. Excursion amplitude can also be increased by extensive dissection of the muscle from its surrounding fascial attachments. This is particularly true of the brachioradialis (BR).

### Straight Line of Pull

The transfer must run in a straight line from its own origin to the insertion of the tendon it is to drive. If a tendon transfer does not run in a straight line, increased force needs to be expended to overcome friction with the surrounding soft tissues, and the transfer will try to migrate so that it does run in a straight line. In doing so, the transfer will lose tension and become lax and less efficient or totally ineffective. Sometimes it is not possible for the transfer to run in a straight line, for example, in an extensor indicis proprius (EIP) or flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) opponensplasty. In this situation a robust pulley is required, around which the transfer can change direction. The pulley must not be able to migrate over time because this would result in reduced tension and loss of, or restricted, function. The transfer must run in a straight line from its origin to the pulley and from the pulley to the insertion of the tendon it is to drive; in addition, the angular change in direction of the transfer at the pulley should be as small as possible to minimize friction and adhesion formation.

### One Tendon–One Function

A single transferred tendon cannot be expected to perform two dissimilar active actions. If a transfer has two insertions that perform dissimilar active functions, it will act predominantly on the tighter insertion.However, one transfer can be attached to multiple tendons that all have similar functions. Thus the FCR can be attached to the EDC tendons of all four fingers (and, if necessary, the EPL) to actively extend them simultaneously, but not individually. Also the extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) tendon can be attached to a lateral band of the extensor apparatus of each finger to restore intrinsic function in all four fingers. A tendon transfer can also restore one active function and provide an additional and different passive function. For example, an opponensplasty can be attached in series, first to the insertion of abductor pollicis brevis (APB) and then to extensor mechanism of the thumb: (1) The initial attachment is to restore active opposition, and (2) the second is to passively restrict flexion of the thumb interphalangeal (IP) joint, which is sometimes a problem in combined median and ulnar nerve palsies.

### Synergism

Synergy of activity of the donor and “recipient” muscles and/or tendons is generally considered advantageous, and synergistic transfers should be selected if at all possible. However, Omerand Brand both believe that any transfer can be reeducated to fulfill its new function, especially in children, provided the restored function is of benefit to the patient.

Wrist extension usually occurs with finger flexion, and both sets of muscles are activated concurrently by the brain when gripping. On this premise, it is argued that patients find it easiest to train a wrist flexor to restore finger extension. A possible exception to this rule is the use of the superficialis (sublimis) tendons, which have more independent cortical control than other muscles in the hand.

### Expendable Donor

Transfer of a musculotendinous unit must not result in unacceptable loss of function. Thus there should always be at least one other musculotendinous unit providing the same function as the one being transferred so that the original function of the transfer is not completely lost. One should not transfer both the ECRL and ECRB to restore new functions because active wrist extension would be lost. The same is true for FCR and flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU).

An additional well- known guideline is suggested, that is, the paralyzed “recipient” muscle/tendon, whose function is to be replaced by a transfer, should not be divided to allow the transfer to be attached end- to- end unless there is no prospect of spontaneous recovery of the muscle. If there is any prospect of recovery, then an end- to- side attachment is preferred.

## MEDIAN NERVE PALSY

The aim of reconstructive surgery in median nerve palsy is to restore lost motor function, in particular, thumb opposition, flexor pollicis longus (FPL), and index finger profundus function. Attempts to restore sensibility in the hand, specifically to the thumb tip, with neurovascular island skin flaps generally have been abandoned, but sensory nerve transfer is possible. The benefits of reconstructive surgery are, to a large extent, determined by the quality of hand sensation,contralateral hand function, and the patient’s motivation and ability to adapt to any sensory loss.

Median nerve injuries are classified as “high” or “low” depending on whether the lesion is proximal or distal to the origin of the anterior interosseous nerve in the proximal forearm. In low injuries, the thenar intrinsic muscles innervated by the median nerve, usually the APB, opponens pollicis, and superficial head of the flexor pollicis brevis (FPB), are paralyzed. In high injuries, the PT, the FCR, all the finger superficiales, the index and middle finger profundi, the FPL, and the pronator quadratus muscles are also paralyzed.

### Low Median Nerve Palsy

Polio was the predominant cause of thenar muscle intrinsic paralysis in the first half of the 20th century. Because this disease causes a pure motor deficit, it provides an ideal indication for opponensplasty. The two world wars also produced a large number of median nerve injuries that were frequently complex and occurred in conjunction with other nerve and severe soft tissue injuries. The present indications for restoration of thumb opposition include compressive and traumatic disruptions of the median nerve; leprosy; neurologic diseases (e.g., Charcot- Marie- Tooth disease, monomelic amyotrophy), spinal muscular atrophy, and syringomyelia; and congenital absence of the thenar muscles.

#### Biomechanics of Thumb Opposition

Thumb opposition is a complex movement requiring trapeziometacarpal joint abduction, flexion, and pronation. Retroposition, the opposite movement, is a combination of trapeziometacarpal joint adduction, extension, and supination. Axial thumb rotation during opposition and retroposition, usually 90 degrees of pronation and 60 degrees of supination, respectively, occurs on the spheroid area of the saddleshaped trapezial articular surface. Pronation is probably an obligatory passive movement that occurs during circumduction toward the palm as a result of the force- couple produced by thumb intrinsic muscle contraction and passive tension within the dorsal trapeziometacarpal ligament. Retroposition probably also occurs passively during thumb extension as a result of the force- couple produced by contraction of the EPL and the extensor pollicis brevis (EPB) and the abductor pollicis longus (APL) muscles and passive tension within the radiopalmar (anterior oblique) ligament. Although the force- couples may be the prime mechanism of axial rotation, other mechanisms must exist inasmuch as thumb pronation is preserved after trapeziectomy.

The prime muscle of thumb opposition is the APB, although both the opponens pollicis and FPB also produce some opposition. The adductor pollicis (AP) and the two extrinsic thumb extensors cause thumb retroposition, whereas the FPL can act as a muscle of either opposition or retroposition, depending on the position of the thumb.

The classic description of thumb intrinsic muscle innervation is that the APB, the opponens pollicis, and the FPB are supplied by the motor branch of the median nerve, whereas the AP is supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve. However, there is considerable diversity in the pattern of innervation, and clinical studies suggest that all the thenar muscles are innervated, in part at least, by either the ulnar (2%) or the median (2%) nerve in a few instances. Furthermore, the FPB remains functional in 73% of complete median and 58% of complete ulnar nerve injuries, thus demonstrating that this muscle frequently has a dual median and ulnar innervation. Anatomic dissection suggests that the median nerve supplies the APB, the opponens pollicis, and the superficial head of the FPB in 63% of hands, but only the APB and the opponens pollicis in 30% of hands.

Zancolli and Cozzi believe that the superficial head of the FPB has dual median and ulnar innervation in 30% of hands, whereas its deep head has dual innervation in 79% of cases but is exclusively supplied by the ulnar nerve in 19%. They also believe that the oblique head of the AP has dual ulnar and median innervation in 35% of cases, whereas its transverse head is practically always (96%) innervated by the ulnar nerve. Although some of these findings are contradictory, the variability of thenar muscle innervation explains why thumb abduction and opposition are frequently retained after complete median nerve injuries.

#### The Deficit and the Deformity

Thumb abduction and opposition are frequently retained after isolated median nerve injury as a result of preserved ulnar nerve function. Jensen believed that opponensplasty was required in only 14% of median nerve injuries, and it is my experience that reasonable opposition is commonly retained in complete median nerve transections. Furthermore, I find that satisfactory thumb opposition is usually retained in severe carpal tunnel syndrome, even in the presence of significant thenar wasting. Foucher and colleagues found only 7% of the patients with severe carpal tunnel syndrome had absent or inadequate thumb opposition. All the thenar intrinsic muscles are paralyzed after combined median and ulnar nerve injuries; in this situation, thumb opposition is absent and the patient is only able to flex the thumb across the palm by using the FPL. In combined ulnar and high median nerve injuries, resting thumb posture tends to be extended and supinated by the unopposed extrinsic extensor muscles.

#### Tendon Transfers to Restore Thumb Opposition

**History.** Steindler is credited with performing the first opponensplasty in 1917. He attached a radial slip of the FPL tendon onto the dorsum at the base of the thumb’s proximal phalanx.Shortly afterward, Cook used the extensor digiti minimi (EDM) for an opponensplasty and Ney attached either the palmaris longus (PL) or FCR to the EPB tendon, which was rerouted through the carpal tunnel. Huber in 1921 and Nicolaysen in 1922 described ADM opponensplasty.

Bunnell in 1924 and Camitz in 1929 both reported that the PL tendon could be satisfactorily elongated with a strip of palmar aponeurosis so that it could be used in an opponensplasty without the need for a tendon graft. To maximize thumb opposition, Bunnell recommended passing the transferred tendon through a pulley on the ulnar border of the wrist so that it ran subcutaneously across the palm to its thumb insertion. Thompson used the ulnar border of the palmar aponeurosis as a pulley for Royle’s superficialis transfer. A limitation of both the palmaris and superficialis transfers is that they are useful only for low median nerve palsies. Aguirre and Caplan, in 1956, first described EIP opponensplasty, which can be used for both high and low median nerve palsies and does not require manufacture of a pulley or a tendon graft.

**Patient counseling.** Although the indication for an opponensplasty is loss of opposition, the need for surgery is loss of function due to loss of opposition. If loss of opposition is unilateral, especially if it affects the nondominant hand, then the consequent loss of function may be minimal and insufficient to warrant surgery. Additionally, if there is also loss of or absent sensation in the median nerve territory, this may reduce any potential benefit of an opponensplasty because the loss of function is primarily due to the loss of sensation. Careful patient counseling about the possible functional benefit, the rehabilitation process, and the likely outcome of surgery (this may be somewhat less than the published success rates for restoration of opposition movement) is mandatory. The surgeon must have a clear picture of the functional disability and be confident that this is due to the loss of opposition and not to other factors (e.g., sensory impairment or joint stiffness).

**General principles of tendon transfer with reference to oppon-ensplasty** Prevention and preoperative treatment of contractures. With median nerve palsy and complete intrinsic paralysis, the thumb may adopt a supinated and adducted position, and a first web space contracture can develop. This is readily prevented by passive thumb abduction and opposition exercises, supplemented by abduction splints as needed. Temporary internal splintage of the thumb metacarpal in abduction with a Kirschner wire may be indicated when there is an associated acute severe thenar or first web space soft tissue injury and thus a high risk of first web space contracture.

Established soft tissue contractures must be corrected either before or during opponensplasty because no opponensplasty has sufficient strength to improve a preoperative passive loss of opposition. Failure to detect and correct contractures preoperatively is a common cause of failure. First web space contractures are generally due to contractures of the skin and deep fascia on its extensor surface and usually can be readily detected. However, contracture of the dorsal capsule of the trapeziometacarpal joint, which restricts opposition but permits abduction, is detected only by careful clinical examination. If supervised physiotherapy and splintage fail to correct a contracture, surgical release is indicated.

In the absence of any injury to the thenar muscles or the base of the thumb, first web contractures usually can be released satisfactorily through a dorsal web space incision. The fascia over the AP and the first dorsal interosseous muscles is released and the dorsal web space skin widened with a skin graft or flap. Isolated capsular contractures of the trapeziometacarpal joint should be released through a small incision over the base of this joint. In severe contractures, both rotational osteotomy at the base of the thumb metacarpal and trapeziectomy have been recommended. An osteotomy modifies rather than improves the range of thumb movement. For a more comprehensive discussion of thumb web contractures, see Chapter 48.

Selection of motor for transfer. The normal function of the muscle selected for opponensplasty must be expendable, and it should have strength and potential for excursion similar to that of the APB and opponens pollicis muscles, the functions of which it is to replace. The ideal opponensplasty motor should have a tension fraction similar to that of the combined APB and opponens pollicis tension (1.1 + 1.9 = 3.0) and a muscle- fiber length that is at least as long as that of the APB (see Table 31.1). The greater the potential for excursion of the transfer, the greater the margin of error when setting the transfer’s tension.

It is also advantageous to select a motor with a tendon that is long enough to reach the thumb metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, thus avoiding the need for a tendon graft. This makes the operation less complicated and reduces the likelihood of troublesome adhesions. Finally, rehabilitation is simpler if the selected motor acts synergistically with the APB.

Pulley design. Because true thumb opposition is restored best by transfers that run subcutaneously across the palm, parallel to the APB muscle, all extrinsic opponensplasties should pass around a stout fixed pulley in the region of the pisiform on the ulnar border of the wrist. It is important that a pulley is strong and generates minimal soft tissue resistance. Opponens transfers from the forearm extensor compartment generally use the distal ulna as the pulley, and most of these transfers are simply passed through a wide subcutaneous tunnel on the ulnar border of the distal forearm. Alternatively, some extensor muscle opponensplasties pass through and use a window in the interosseous membrane as the pulley, in which case it is important to make a very large window.

The Camitz procedure does not classically employ a pulley and thus is less effective in providing true opposition. If a forearm flexor compartment muscle is used for opponensplasty, a pulley has to be manufactured on the ulnar border of the wrist. Numerous designs have been described, and these are discussed later in the chapter in the Superficialis Transfers section.

Opponensplasty insertions. Many insertions have been described (Fig. 31.1), and these can be broadly divided into single- and dualinsertion techniques. Dual insertion aims to allow the tendon transfer to perform two functions, usually active opposition and either passive stabilization of the MCP joint or restriction of IP joint flexion, both of which are beneficial in combined median and ulnar nerve palsies. Bunnell recommended (and others used) a drill hole in the dorsoulnar aspect at the base of the thumb proximal phalanx as the opponensplasty insertion point to produce thumb pronation as well as abduction. However, pronation occurs passively when the thumb is abducted and flexed, and biomechanical studies suggest that attaching the opponensplasty to the APB insertion on the radial aspect of the thumb MCP joint produces at least as good an opposition as the Bunnell and other commonly used insertions. Thus, the APB insertion is now widely used in isolated median nerve palsies.

Results. The majority of papers on opponensplasty report a high percentage of excellent and good results and attribute most failures to persistent contractures or technical problems. There is no universally accepted method of classifying the results of opponensplasty, but recent papers are probably more stringent than earlier articles in their assessment of outcomes. Unfortunately, it is impossible to compare the results and complication rates of different opponensplasties because of multiple variables, including: (1) the underlying disease process (e.g., polio, leprosy, nerve injury), (2) the neurologic deficit (e.g., low median, high median, or combined median and ulnar), (3) the sensory defect, and (4) the potential for spontaneous recovery. For example, if transfers are performed early when there is still potential for recovery, restoration of excellent opposition may be incorrectly attributed to the opponensplasty instead of nerve regeneration. Sixty- six percent of the patients regain or never lose good opposition after median nerve repair, and loss of thenar muscle function frequently recovers after carpal tunnel release.

Many papers classify an excellent result as one in which physically powerful opposition with full mobility is restored. A fair result is one with either full but weak opposition or limited but strong opposition.Sundararaj and Mani graded their results in leprosy according to the range of opposition and the position of the thumb IP joint (Table 31.2). In contrast, Foucher and colleagues objectively measured active thumb abduction, opposition, and rotation occurring on movement of the thumb from full active retroposition to opposition.

Superficialis opponensplasties. There is no universally accepted “best technique” of superficialis transfer, and a wide variety of different harvest techniques, pulley constructions, and transfer insertions are used, as described in the following subsections. The ring finger superficialis is widely used as the opponensplasty motor. However, because this may weaken power grip, some surgeons prefer to use the middle finger superficialis if it is available. Potential transfer insertions have been discussed earlier in this chapter.

Superficialis tendon harvest. Royle and Thompson both divided the ring finger superficialis tendon at its insertion onto the middle phalanx through a transverse insertion within the basal finger crease. However, it has subsequently been recognized that several superficialis harvest techniques, including division at its insertion, can cause donor digit proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint flexion contractures or, especially in the supple Asian hand, swan neck and other deformities.

North and Littler suggested that division of the superficialis tendon at its insertion destroys the vincula and disrupts the blood supply to the profundus tendon; furthermore, surgical trauma may create scar tissue within the flexor sheath and PIP joint capsule, thus causing adhesions and/or contracture. They recommended division of the superficialis tendon proximal to its bifurcation through a window between the A1 and A2 pulleys with the finger fully flexed. This avoids injury to the flexor sheath at the level of the PIP joint and leaves behind a 3- cm length of superficialis tendon that glides freely within the flexor sheath. With this technique, 8 of 16 patients experienced no problems in the donor digit and the remainder only lost an average of eight degrees extension of the PIP joint. None experienced any major disability or deformity. Other surgeons have recommended that the distal tags of the donor superficialis tendon be left long and sutured across the palmar plate to reinforce it and prevent hyperextension.

In a study series of 116 superficialis opponensplasties for leprosy, the ring finger superficialis tendon was harvested through either a midlateral approach or a Bruner incision on the flexor surface of the finger. Extension lag at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint and fixed- flexion deformities at the PIP joint occurred in 44% and 8% of cases, respectively, when the lateral approach was used but in only 8% and 0% of cases, respectively, when the Bruner incision was used. These authors believed that the high complication rates associated with the lateral approach were due to scar tissue and adhesion formation around the lateral bands of the extensor hood, which are retracted during this exposure. However, in another series of 100 leprosy patients with ulnar, median, or combined nerve palsies, no correlation was found between harvest technique and donor digit morbidity and deformity.

Swan neck deformities and DIP joint extension lags occurred in 15% and 29% of the donor fingers, respectively. The DIP joint extension lags were usually less than 20 degrees, unless associated with a swan neck deformity, in which case they could be as much as 80 degrees. In addition, 26% of the cases in this study developed a fixed- flexion deformity of the PIP joint (50 to 70 degrees), and PIP joint flexion was reduced in 16%. These authors concluded that postoperative deformity in superficialis donor digits is multifactorial and depends on the preoperative condition of the PIP joint, the method of superficialis harvest, the surgeon’s skill, the postoperative rehabilitation regime, the expertise of the therapist, and the patient’s motivation.

Many surgeons believe that division of the superficialis tendon at its insertion increases the risk of swan neck deformity. Furthermore, this technique usually requires an additional palmar incision to split the distal superficialis tendon and free it from the profundus tendon. Because sufficient superficialis tendon length for opponensplasty is provided by harvesting the superficialis tendon through an incision in the distal aspect of the palm, there seems little point in risking a more distal division.

The pulley. Passing the superficialis tendon around the FCU tendon in the distal forearm was a popular and simple pulley design, but this technique has fallen out of favor because the pulley is not fixed and migrates proximally. Thus the transfer gradually adopts an increasingly longitudinal line of action and becomes less efficient with time.

Bunnell described several pulley designs for use with superficialis and other forearm flexor opponensplasties. These included looping a free tendon graft around the FCU tendon and using a strip of the FCU tendon based on its attachment to the pisiform. Although the use of a distally based strip of FCU as a pulley was popular, its raw surface may encourage adhesion formation, and radial migration of this pulley has been observed over a period of years. To prevent this migration, the distally based slip of FCU tendon can be attached to the ECU tendon. The angle between the distal edge of the flexor retinaculum and the ulnar border of the palmar aponeurosis is used effectively as a pulley in the Thompson- Royle transfer, and good opposition is also restored when a window in the flexor retinaculum is used as the pulley. Guyon canal can also be used as a pulley, and although radial migration may occur, this does not necessarily reduce the effectiveness of the opponensplasty.

Biomechanical studies, including those done by Cooney et al.and Lee et al., have shown that the function of an opponensplasty can be modified to an individual patient’s requirements by altering the position of the pulley. Use of the FCU tendon or an FCU loop maximizes palmar abduction, whereas use of a Guyon canal, or the angle created by the distal edge of the flexor retinaculum and the ulnar border of the palmar aponeurosis, produces the closest approximation of the thumb tip to the little finger. In addition, placing the pulley so that the transfer’s line of action passes toward the pisiform produces maximal thumb abduction and opposition but only a small amount of flexion at the thumb MCP joint, whereas placing it so that the transfer’s line of pull passes distal to the pisiform produces more thumb MCP joint flexion and less abduction. Thus, a distally placed pulley may benefit patients with combined ulnar and median nerve palsies and paralyzed FPB muscles.

Surgical techniques. *Royle- Thompson opponensplasty.* With this technique, a 3- cm longitudinal incision is made at the base of the palm on the radial border of the hypothenar eminence. The ulnar border of the palmar aponeurosis is exposed and retracted radially, and the ring finger superficialis tendon is identified as it emerges from the carpal tunnel proximal to the superficial palmar arch. This tendon is then divided distally through a transverse incision at the base of the digit and delivered into the palmar wound so that it passes ulnar to the palmar aponeurosis. A third incision is made over the dorsum of the thumb MCP joint, and a wide subcutaneous tunnel is created between this and the palmar incision. The superficialis tendon is passed through this tunnel, with the angle created by the distal edge of the flexor retinaculum and the ulnar border of the palmar aponeurosis acting as the pulley. The transfer is then attached to the thumb by one of the many insertion techniques. Although Thompson originally used a dual insertion that stabilized the thumb MCP joint, he subsequently used the APB insertion.

The tourniquet is released and hemostasis is obtained before the incisions in the palm and at the base of the ring finger are closed. The superficialis transfer attachment to the thumb is then sutured while the thumb is held in full opposition. Because the ring finger superficialis tendon has a significant potential for excursion and crosses the wrist joint, adjusting the tension is not as critical as with other transfers, and there is a considerable margin for error. However, the transfer’s tension should be adjusted so that it is tight when the thumb is fully opposed and the wrist is in neutral. Postoperatively, Thompson held the thumb in opposition for 4 to 6 weeks with spiral adhesive strapping that allowed some active thumb movement; in addition, Rath advocated early mobilization of this transfer. However, most surgeons immobilize the thumb in full opposition with a cast for several weeks.

Some surgeons believe that this opponensplasty does not produce as wide a thumb abduction as some other transfers because it runs along the path of the superficial head of the FPB and not that of the APB muscle. However, the results were excellent or good in 8 of Thompson’s 10 original opponensplasties, and a subsequent report described excellent or good results in 78% of 94 opponensplasties.

*Bunnell’s opponensplasty.* Bunnell’s precept for restoration of full thumb opposition was that the transfer should run in line with the fibers of the APB to an insertion on the dorsoulnar aspect of the thumb proximal phalanx. Because his transfer passes distal to the axis of rotation of the MCP joint, it also flexes that joint.

The ring finger superficialis tendon is divided at the base of this digit by using one of the previously described techniques. An incision is then made over the ulnar neurovascular bundle, just proximal to the wrist crease, and the ring finger superficialis tendon is identified. The distal portion of the FCU tendon is then exposed and cut halfway across, 4 cm proximal to its insertion on the pisiform. The tendon is then split into two equal halves up to its pisiform insertion, thus creating a distally based strip of tendon tissue. The free end of this tendon slip is sutured back onto its base at the pisiform to create a fixed pulley through which the superficialis tendon can pass easily. Care should be taken to not make this loop too tight. An incision is then made on the dorsum of the thumb, and a wide subcutaneous tunnel is created across the palm between the two incisions. A hole large enough to accommodate the superficialis tendon is then drilled from the dorsoulnar cortex to the radial cortex of the base of the proximal phalanx. The ring finger superficialis is delivered into the wrist incision and passed through the pulley and then along the subcutaneous tunnel into the dorsal thumb incision.

Next, the tourniquet is released, hemostasis is achieved, and the skin incisions at the base of the ring finger and at the wrist are closed. The superficialis tendon is then passed superficial to the EPL tendon across the dorsum of the thumb MCP joint. It is then passed through the drilled hole in the base of the proximal phalanx in a dorsoulnarto- radiopalmar direction. Once the tendon has been passed through the drill hole, the transfer’s tension is set so that the thumb lies in full opposition with the wrist in neutral. The transfer is then sutured back onto itself or to the radial border periosteum. Alternatively, it can be held with a pull- out suture tied over a dental roll or attached to the proximal phalanx with suture anchors.

Jensen used the Bunnell opponensplasty and reported good opposition in 22 of his 27 superficialis transfers.

EIP opponensplasty. The EIP opponensplasty was favored by Burkhalter, who wrote earlier editions of this chapter, and is popular in high median nerve palsy and other instances when the ring and middle finger FDS tendons are unavailable. It has become increasingly preferable to the superficialis transfer in low median nerve palsies because it does not weaken grip and causes little, if any, functional disability.

Technique. A short incision is made over the index finger MCP joint, and the EIP tendon is identified lying deep and ulnar to the index EDC. It can be divided immediately proximal to the extensor hood, but the tendon is then only just long enough to reach the APB insertion on the thumb. As a result some surgeons, myself included, recommend that a 1- to 2- cm contiguous slip of the extensor hood is harvested with the tendon to lengthen the transfer (Fig. 31.3A).This makes the attachment of the transfer to the ADM insertion much easier but requires careful repair of the extensor hood, as otherwise its radial and ulnar halves may subluxate palmarly and cause an extensor lag. An incision is then made on the dorsoulnar aspect of the distal forearm and the deep fascia on the ulnar border of the extensor compartment is widely released to expose the finger extensor tendons proximal to the extensor retinaculum. The EIP muscle and tendon lie deep and ulnar to the EDC tendons and are identified by pulling on the distal end of the EIP tendon on the back of the hand (see Fig. 31.3B). The whole EIP tendon is pulled down from the back of the hand into the forearm wound, which is sometimes only possible after soft tissue connections between the extensor indicis and index communis tendons have been divided through a small incision on the back of the hand. The EIP muscle is then freed of soft tissue attachments in the distal third of the forearm (see Fig. 31.3C).

Results. Burkhalter and colleagues reported the results of extensor indicis opponensplasty in 65 trauma cases, which included 32 combined ulnar and median nerve, 13 high median nerve, and 2 brachial plexus injuries. Of the patients, 86% achieved excellent results, with powerful opposition to within 20 degrees of the plane of the palm of the hand. Perceived additional benefits of this transfer included ease of surgery (performed satisfactorily by residents) and rehabilitation (its excursion is naturally augmented by a wrist tenodesis effect). A disadvantage is that extensor indicis muscle- fiber length is shorter than that of the APB and the ring finger superficialis, so thumb extension may be restricted.

Anderson and associates used this transfer in 12 high and 28 low median nerve palsies caused by leprosy, trauma, and other conditions. In contrast to Burkhalter’s description, they attached the transfer to the thumb with the wrist in neutral and the thumb fully abducted and extended. Excellent or good results were seen in 88% of the cases monitored for more than a year. These investigators subsequently compared these results with those achieved by superficialis transfer and concluded that the EIP transfer should only be used in supple hands.

ADM (Huber) opponensplasty. The ADM opponensplasty (Fig. 31.4), described independently by Huber and Nicolaysen,was popularized by Littler and Cooley, who considered the ADM a close substitute for the APB. This transfer may improve the hand’s appearance by increasing the bulk of the thenar eminence.

The ADM muscle is then turned through 180 degrees on its long axis to reduce the tension on its neurovascular bundle (as if turning the page of a book), passed through the subcutaneous tunnel, and attached to the APB insertion. Because the ADM muscle has only sufficient length to just reach the APB insertion, it is invariably attached under adequate tension. Results. Littler and Cooley performed four Huber transfers, one of which failed and became fibrotic, probably as a result of vascular insufficiency. They considered this transfer difficult and recommended that it be performed only when simpler opponensplasties are contraindicated. Furthermore, primate studies have highlighted this transfer’s precarious blood supply, which is significantly reduced by dividing its pisiform insertion. In light of these concerns, some surgeons retain the pisiform origin and lengthen the transfer with a short tendon graft when necessary. Others, in complete contrast, lengthen the transfer by completely releasing the origin of ADM, which they leave to reattach itself by scar tissue formation.

Wissinger and Singsen preserved the pisiform origin in 15 Huber transfers and reported one failure as a result of transfer fibrosis.Twelve of their cases achieved excellent results with no donor site morbidity. Some surgeons use the Huber transfer as the opponensplasty of choice in pediatric patients with congenital thenar muscle deficiencies (see Chapter 37). One consideration in this scenario is that an FDS transfer allows augmentation of the deficient UCL in affected children, whereas a Huber does not have sufficient length to achieve this.

Palmaris longus opponensplasty (Camitz). The Camitz PL opponensplasty (see Fig. 31.5) is a simple transfer that is usually performed for loss of abduction and opposition occurring as a complication of severe carpal tunnel syndrome. The procedure restores palmar abduction rather than opposition, but it can be performed under regional anesthesia at the same time as carpal tunnel release. It is reasonable to perform a Camitz procedure at the same time as carpal tunnel release for patients with severe thenar wasting and loss of abduction and opposition that is causing functional disability, even though thumb opposition and abduction may recover after carpal tunnel release, over a period of months (Fig. 31.6). This transfer is not recommended for traumatic median nerve injuries at the wrist because the PL, which lies directly over the median nerve, and the overlying skin are usually scarred and damaged. However, its use has been utilized in this situation in young children.

Technique. Preoperatively, the presence of the PL tendon is confirmed by opposing the thumb to the little finger with the wrist flexed. A longitudinal skin incision is made, starting in the forearm, 2 cm proximal to the distal wrist crease, zigzagged across the transverse wrist crease (see Figs. 31.5 and 31.6A) and running distally to the proximal palmar crease in line with the ring finger. Care needs to be taken to identify and avoid injury to the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, which lies just radial to the PL tendon. The PL tendon is freed from the forearm to the palm, where a 1- cm- wide strip of palmar aponeurosis is dissected out in continuity with the tendon. The carpal tunnel is decompressed, and a second incision is made over the dorsoradial aspect of the thumb MCP joint. A wide subcutaneous tunnel is then developed from the distal forearm to the incision over the thumb MCP joint, and the PL tendon and its aponeurotic extension are passed through this. It is necessary to ensure a straight line of pull of the PL from its origin on the medial epicondyle to the APB insertion, and this will require separation of the subcutaneous tissues from the deep fascia on the radial aspect of the incision in the distal forearm and also more proximal division of the deep fascia covering the PL in the distal forearm.

The palmar skin incision is then closed and the tendon transfer is usually attached to the APB insertion, with or without another attachment to the dorsal MCP joint capsule, which may increase opposition. Foucher and coworkers favor attachment to either the EPB tendon or the dorsal capsule of the MCP joint in an effort to restore opposition as well as abduction. Whichever distal attachment is used, it is sutured with the thumb in full opposition, the MCP joint extended, and the wrist in neutral. The thumb incision is then closed, and a light cast, holding the wrist in neutral and the thumb opposed, is worn for 4 weeks. A night splint may be worn for a week or two longer.

Results. In a series of 73 Camitz procedures performed for severe carpal tunnel syndrome by Foucher and coworkers, 50% of the patients regained good opposition within 1 year and 91% regained good thumb abduction and satisfactory opposition by 16 to 102 months.Attaching the PL tendon to the dorsal capsule of the MCP joint or the EPB tendon restricted MCP joint movement slightly (15 to 25 degrees) in 20% of the patients in this series. Braun successfully used the Camitz procedure to reestablish thumb abduction in 28 cases, whereas Terrono and colleagues improved thumb function in 27 of 29 cases and believed that formal physiotherapy was unnecessary.

What we call the Camitz transfer was actually first described by Bunnell, who also suggested passing the PL tendon through a pulley close to the pisiform to create a more oblique line of pull and restore opposition. Passage of the transfer through a window in the flexor retinaculum has also been suggested. If the superficial head of the FPB muscle is innervated by the ulnar nerve, this may provide pronation and thus allow use of a standard Camitz procedure to restore good opposition.

**Other opponensplasties for special situations when none of the “big four” are appropriate** ECU opponensplasty. The ECU opponensplasty described by Phalen and Miller avoids the need for a tendon graft by utilizing the EPB tendon and its insertion.

Technique. A short incision is made on the dorsoradial border of the distal forearm, and the EPB tendon is divided at its musculotendinous junction, just proximal to the first extensor compartment. An incision is then made on the extensor surface of the thumb MCP joint, and the EPB tendon is delivered into this wound while preserving its insertion on the proximal phalanx. Next, an “L”- shaped incision is made on the ulnar border of the wrist, its short arm passing transversely along the flexor wrist crease toward the PL. A wide subcutaneous tunnel running from the thumb MCP joint to the pisiform area is then created across the palm. The EPB tendon is passed proximally through this tunnel, and the ECU tendon is then divided close to its insertion on the base of the fifth metacarpal. It is then withdrawn back under the extensor retinaculum and freed of soft tissue attachments throughout the distal third of the forearm. Care should be taken to identify and preserve the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve during this dissection. The ECU tendon is then passed around the ulnar border of the distal forearm (deep to the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve and superficial to FCU and the main ulnar neurovascular bundle) and sutured to the EPB tendon while the thumb is opposed to the base of the middle finger with its MCP and IP joints extended. Postoperatively, the thumb is immobilized in opposition with the wrist in neutral for 3 to 4 weeks.

Results. Wood and Adams performed this procedure on 12 patients’ complex cases with few available motors. In four instances they observed radial deviation wrist deformities that caused severe grip weakness. This may have occurred as a result of FCU paresis or paralysis, but an anomalous ECRL insertion onto the radial, rather than the dorsal, border of the index metacarpal was also implicated. In two patients the radial deviation deformity was corrected by transferring the ECRL tendon to the distal ECU stump.

There are three other problems with this transfer. First, the EPB tendon is sometimes nonexistent. Second, an MCP joint flexion deformity may occur if the EPB tendon is dissected out from the extensor hood right up to its insertion on the proximal phalanx. In contrast, MCP joint hyperextension may occur if it is not dissected out sufficiently distally. Third, a tendinous slip sometimes connects the EPB and EPL tendons; if present, this must be divided as the transfer may otherwise cause unwanted extension of the IP joint.

ECRL opponensplasty. The tendon of the ECRL muscle needs to be lengthened, and this can be done with a standard tendon graft.Alternatively, the ECRL (or ECRB) tendon may be detached distally at its insertion onto the index metacarpal, brought out into a wound in the dorsal forearm, and then carefully split in half longitudinally, such that one strip remains attached to the ECRL muscle and the other half is free. The free strip of tendon is then sutured to the distal end of attached strip to lengthen the transfer. The elongated transfer is passed through a wide subcutaneous tunnel around the ulna, just proximal to the pisiform, and inserted into the APB attachment and EPL.

Kaplan and coworkers described an alternative technique of elongating the ECRL tendon with the EPL tendon.

Technique. A skin incision is made over the dorsum of the distal radius, and the EPL tendon is divided at its musculotendinous junction. The ECRL tendon is then divided close to its insertion and freed of soft tissue attachments in the distal forearm. Next, the EPL tendon is delivered into a second incision on the extensor surface of the thumb MCP joint, and a third skin incision is made on the border of the wrist at the level of the ulnar styloid process. Wide subcutaneous tunnels are then created across the palm and around the ulnar border of the wrist/distal forearm. The EPL tendon is passed through the palmar tunnel to the wrist’s ulnar border, which may require a fourth incision in the midpalm. It is then sutured to the ECRL tendon with the wrist flexed and the thumb abducted and opposed. Postoperatively, the hand is immobilized with the wrist flexed and the thumb opposed in a belowelbow cast for 3 weeks.

Results. Kaplan and coworkers reported this transfer in four patients with isolated median or combined median and ulnar nerve injuries. All the four thumbs regained abduction and opposition, and thumb IP joint flexion and extension were preserved. Henderson also used the ECRL in 5 patients, all of whom regained satisfactory opposition. Baek and colleagues achieved excellent results in 10 of 11 cases and preferred it to the EIP opponensplasty as it provided stronger opposition. None of these authors report the impact of the loss of normal EPL function, which would be expected to restrict thumb extension and retroposition.

EDM opponensplasty. The EDM can be used for an opponensplasty, particularly when the EIP is not available, usually because it has been used, or will be used, for another transfer. It is important for the surgeon to have an alternate transfer in mind in the event that the EDM is determined to be insufficient or absent.

Technique. An incision is made over the little finger MCP joint and the EDM tendons (i.e., usually two tendons) are identified on the ulnar side of the little finger’s common extensor tendon. These tendons and, in continuity with them distally, a central section of the extensor hood over the proximal portion of the proximal phalanx are freed distally and withdrawn proximally, back under the extensor retinaculum into an incision on the dorsoulnar aspect of the distal forearm. The tendons are then freed of soft tissue attachments in the distal third of the forearm, and the little finger extensor hood is carefully repaired with interrupted, nonabsorbable sutures. The transfer is then passed subcutaneously around the ulnar border of the wrist and across the palm, using the technique described for EIP transfer. In the original description this transfer was attached to the APB, the extensor expansion, and the EPL tendon over the base of the proximal phalanx. Postoperatively, the thumb is immobilized in opposition with the wrist in neutral for 3 weeks.

Results. Schneider reported good opposition in 8 of 10 cases, most of which were combined median and ulnar nerve injuries. However, patients should be counseled that an extension lag of the little finger may occur, despite careful repair of the extensor hood after harvest of the elongated tendon (necessary to ensure adequate length). It may be advisable to perform preoperative ultrasound to assess the integrity of the EDC contribution to the small digit, which is not uncommonly deficient.

**Tendon transfers (“compromise opponensplasty”) for severe nerve deﬁcits.** In a complete or nearly complete intrinsic- minus thumb, such as occurs in combined median and ulnar nerve palsies, the FPL and EPL muscles both adduct and supinate the thumb. Furthermore, the combined overactivity of these two extrinsic muscles causes the IP joint to hyperflex and the MCP joint to extend (Fig. 31.7). The hyperflexed IP joint prevents the thumb pulp from meeting the index finger properly in either pulp or key pinch, and the fingers tend to strike the nail or the dorsum of the thumb, which is disabling and causes thumb supination (Fig. 31.8). However, a patient usually can hold objects between the adjacent sides of the thumb and hand (lateral squeeze) and often prefers this “pinch” to that provided by an opponensplasty that the patient therefore does not use (see Fig. 31.2). It is thus an attractive concept, which stems from the days of poliomyelitis, to use one of these extrinsic thumb muscles for an opponensplasty.

In progressive severe paralysis, a combination of two tendon transfers—one to provide opposition and the other to restore FPB function—is a luxury, and the hand may not have sufficient function to benefit from true opposition. In such instances, restoration of short flexor action alone often provides adequate thumb function. This can be produced by a single- tendon transfer in conjunction with either thumb joint fusion or an IP joint tenodesis.

FPL opponensplasty. An FPL transfer only provides limited pronation and is used for patients with severe hand paralysis for whom pinch of the thumb pulp to the sides of the index or long fingers is more important than pinch to the pulp. The desired end result is a single- axis thumb that can clear the fingers and oppose the fingertips and their sides with considerable power.

Technique (Fig. 31.9). If a tenodesis of the IP joint is planned, the FPL is exposed distally through an incision starting on the dorsum of the IP joint and running palmarly down the radial side of the proximal phalanx. The FPL tendon is then divided 1 cm proximal to the IP joint and the periosteum is stripped from the palmar surfaces of the neck and distal shaft of the proximal phalanx. The distal stump of the FPL is then firmly tenodesed to the proximal phalanx with the IP joint in 15- degree flexion.

Sometimes this is possible only after adhesions between the tendon and the radial palmar bursa have been divided at the level of the A1 pulley. The FPL is then passed around a pulley on the ulnar border of the hand; any of the superficialis transfer pulleys described earlier in this chapter can be used.

Mangus used the FCU tendon as his pulley, presumably with the FPL crossing the distal forearm in the plane between the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) and FDS tendons. After passing the FPL under, around, and back over the FCU tendon alone, it is passed across the front of the wrist and palm in a wide subcutaneous tunnel to an incision on the radial aspect of the thumb MCP point.

If the Thompson pulley is chosen, a palmar incision is made in line with the ulnar border of the ring finger. Next, the FPL tendon is passed through the carpal tunnel, between the superficialis and profundus flexor tendons, and is delivered into the palmar incision. An incision is then made over the dorsoradial aspect of the thumb MCP joint, and the FPL tendon is passed through a wide subcutaneous tunnel into this incision. After the tourniquet has been released and hemostasis obtained, all incisions, except the last one, are closed.

The FPL tendon is usually attached to the superficial head of the FPB rather than the APB. This allows the transfer to stabilize the MCP joint in slight flexion as well as abduct and, to a lesser extent, pronate the thumb. Furthermore, if the APB is used as the insertion, thumb opposition and flexion will be much weaker. Because the FPL muscle has a large potential excursion, setting the tension in this transfer is relatively easy and the thumb should rest in almost full opposition at the end of the procedure.

The hand is immobilized postoperatively with the wrist in 40 degrees of flexion and the thumb in full opposition for 3 weeks. Rehabilitation is then begun by using active wrist extension to bring the thumb into opposition. This is followed by exercises to increase the strength of the transfer.

Results. Burkhalter believed that this transfer had distinct advantages for the completely intrinsic- minus thumb, in which case short flexor action is more important than opposition. He found that EPL overactivity tended to disappear, and his patients quickly realized that they had a functional short flexor muscle and no longer needed to use the EPL as a secondary thumb adductor. After this transfer, the patient uses the EPL to move the thumb away from the fingers and the FPL opponensplasty for prehension activity.

Alternative FPL opponensplasties. Makin transferred the FPL “in continuity,” without dividing the tendon or its insertion, by passing it through an oblique osteotomy in the proximal phalanx so that it spirals around the proximal phalanx and the MCP joint (Fig. 31.10). Oberlin and Alnot used a similar technique in 11 intrinsic- minus thumbs and reported very good results in 8 of the 9 cases reviewed. Rather than passing the FPL tendon through an osteotomy, they passed it through either the IP or the MCP joint, which was then fused. They stressed that the transfer must pass over the extensor surface of the MCP joint and not over the dorsum of the proximal phalanx, which can cause a flexion deformity of the MCP joint (Fig. 31.11). These authors recommended this transfer for completely intrinsic- minus thumbs in high nerve injuries but favored EIP opponensplasty and MCP joint arthrodesis if there were no fixed deformity of the IP joint. Neither of these alternative FPL opponensplasty techniques can reroute the FPL tendon in the forearm, wrist, or palm, such that both are more abductorplasties than opponensplasties.

EPL opponensplasty. In some cases of peripheral nerve disease or spastic paralysis, when the only functioning thumb extrinsic or intrinsic motors are the EPL, EPB, and APL, an IP joint flexion contracture may occur even though the FPL is weak or totally paralyzed. This is due to a tenodesis effect of the FPL, which occurs as the functioning thumb muscles extend and supinate the trapeziometacarpal and MCP joints. In this situation, the extension and supination deformity at the base of the thumb may be corrected by an intermetacarpal bone block (i.e., static correction) or an EPL opponensplasty (Fig. 31.12) if this muscle is under voluntary control. If a spastic EPL is transferred, the thumb will lie in front of (and get in the way of) the fingers because the APL on its own is not sufficiently strong to hold the thumb out of the palm.

Results. Riley and colleagues used this transfer in 11 patients with progressive neurologic disease (e.g., syringomyelia and Charcot- MarieTooth) or traumatic high median or combined median and ulnar nerve intrinsic paralyses. Alternative EPL opponensplasties. Moutet and associates and Mennen have used EPL opponensplasties more routinely, both for isolated and combined median nerve injuries. However, instead of transferring the EPL tendon around the ulnar border of the wrist, they both passed it through a window in the interosseous membrane. Moutet and associates then passed it around either the FCR (isolated median nerve injury) or the FCU (combined median and ulnar nerve injury) tendon, and both sutured the transfer back onto the divided distal end of the EPL. Neither fused the MCP joint.

Mennen’s technique of EPL opponensplasty. The EPL tendon is exposed through an incision on the extensor surface of the MCP joint and divided 1 cm proximal to this joint. This tendon is then delivered into a second incision on the extensor surface of the forearm. The tendon and its muscle are freed of soft tissue attachments throughout the distal half of the forearm. An incision is made on the flexor surface of the forearm 3 cm proximal to the wrist crease and just radial to the ulnar artery. The interosseous membrane is then exposed between the ulnar neurovascular bundle and the finger flexor tendons, and a window is made that is large enough to easily accommodate the distal portion of the EPL muscle.

A subcutaneous tunnel is then created between the incisions on the anterior of the forearm and back of the thumb, and the EPL tendon is passed through the interosseous membrane and subcutaneously along the thenar eminence to the extensor surface of the thumb MCP joint. Here it is passed under the EPB tendon and reattached to its own distal stump with a 1- cm overlap. The thumb is immobilized in a short- arm thumb spica cast for 4 weeks, and a dynamic opposition splint is then used for another 4 weeks.

*Results.* By using this technique, Mennen regained normal opposition between the thumb and little finger pulp in 26 of 35 cases and also found that normal power of extension of the thumb’s IP joint was retained. In the series by Moutet and associates, 14 of 16 patients recovered functional opposition, and the results remained satisfactory 13 years later.

Adductor pollicis and FPB opponensplasties. DeVeechi transferred the AP to the insertion of the superficial head of the FPB. This transfer requires an extensive palmar incision that extends from the adductor insertion across the palm toward the middle finger metacarpal and then proximally toward the base of the thumb. The carpal tunnel is opened, and the median nerve and its branches are protected. The adductor tendon is then divided at its insertion and freed from the FPB. It is passed superficial to the index finger flexor tendons, the index/middle common digital neurovascular bundle, and the FPL tendon and attached to the superficial head of the FPB. This transfer restores FPB function and provides some thumb pronation but does not produce true opposition.

Orticochea transferred the ulnar- innervated deep head of the FPB around the extensor surface of the thumb and attached it to the aponeurosis of the adductor tendon.

Postoperative management of opponensplasty. The thumb is usually immobilized in opposition for 3 weeks after opponensplasty. However, early controlled mobilization is possible with a robust attachment of the transfer to the APB insertion and MCP capsule.The wrist should also be immobilized if the tendon transfer crosses the flexor surface of this joint. Transfers of muscles with relatively short excursions (e.g., the EIP) should be relaxed by immobilizing the wrist in 30 degrees of flexion and the thumb in full opposition. However, after transfer of muscles with larger excursions (e.g., the superficialis) the wrist can be immobilized in neutral with the thumb in full opposition. If the transfer is attached to either the extensor mechanism or the APB insertion, the IP joint of the thumb probably should also be immobilized in full extension.

Some find it useful to maintain thumb opposition postoperatively by using cyanoacrylate glue to stick the skin of the thumb tip to the ring or little finger at the end of the operation, before the transfer’s tension is set. With desquamation, the thumb is spontaneously released from the finger after 2 to 3 weeks (see Fig. 31.3H). After 3 weeks, all splintage usually can be discarded and most patients quickly regain thumb control. The emphasis should be on early restoration of wrist movement, which will alternately relax and tighten the opponensplasty.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: LOW MEDIAN NERVE PALSY

My enthusiasm for reconstructive surgery in median nerve palsy is tempered by the belief that sensation is the prime determinant of hand function in this situation. I thus believe that isolated injuries to the motor branch of the median nerve and focal pure motor deficits caused by nonprogressive or slowly progressive neurologic disease are the best indications for opponensplasty. The more severe the sensory deficit, the less likely the patient is to benefit from reconstructive surgery.

Therefore I believe that opponensplasty is rarely indicated for combined complete median and ulnar nerve injuries in adults because permanent sensory deficit is often severe even after careful nerve repair. In my practice, few patients require or request an opponensplasty after isolated traumatic division of the median nerve; in fact many retain adequate opposition, presumably because of anomalous thenar muscle innervation.

In combined nerve deficits it is imperative that both the patient and the surgeon consider whether the loss of opposition, taken in isolation from the rest of the neurologic deficit, is causing sufficient disability to justify further surgery and rehabilitation. This is especially the case in times of job insecurity and financial uncertainty, when time away from work may result in job loss. Many of my patients, especially those with unilateral loss of opposition, adapt well and minimize their disability so that an opponensplasty is not indicated.

My favored transfers are the Camitz procedure and the EIP transfer. I used to favor the Royle- Thompson superficialis transfer but have converted to the EIP transfer over time. I use the Camitz procedure for patients with loss of opposition secondary to carpal tunnel syndrome and perform this transfer at the same time as carpal tunnel release, even though I believe that thenar muscle function will recover in a considerable number of cases. I do this because the procedure has low morbidity and complication rates and requires little rehabilitation. Also, the recovery of thenar muscle function is unpredictable and may not occur for over a year.

The Camitz procedure does require general or regional anesthesia, whereas most of my carpal tunnel releases are done under local infiltration anesthesia. Restoration of thumb abduction in these patients may produce immediate improvement in hand function if median nerve sensation has not been completely lost and there is a reasonable prospect for sensory recovery.

In other instances, I now favor the EIP transfer, which rarely causes any donor site morbidity, provided that the index finger extensor hood is not damaged. Furthermore, this transfer uses a natural pulley (i.e., ulnar border of the wrist) that is resilient and cannot migrate. In isolated median nerve transfers, this transfer is inserted into the APB tendon. I have not experienced significant loss of thumb extension after this transfer and have found rehabilitation and reeducation straightforward.

With more complex upper limb paralyses, it is important to assess the disability carefully, ascertain which musculotendinous units are available for transfer, and consider the probable effects of loss of the transfer’s normal function before making any decision.

#### Expectations and Patient Counseling for Low Median Nerve Palsy

What I tell the patient about the expected outcome depends on (1) whether the underlying neurologic pathology is progressive (progressive neurologic disease) or static (nerve injury after regeneration period), (2) the functional disability attributable to the isolated loss of opposition, and (3) the functional disability attributable to other problems in the hand, such as sensory loss, and loss of other motor functions. This author would advise a motivated patient with an otherwise normal hand (e.g., isolated injury to the motor branch of the median nerve) that restoration of opposition with either an EIP, FDS, or ADM transfer should restore useful opposition and improve hand function considerably in 7 or 8 of every 10 cases. I tell patients with loss of opposition due to severe carpal tunnel syndrome that they will regain thumb abduction in a similar number of instances but that the functional benefit of this depends to a great extent on the recovery of lost sensation in the thumb and index and middle fingers after the carpal tunnel release.

For more complex cases, such as loss of opposition after combined median and ulnar nerve injuries, I tell patients that opposition can be restored in 7 or 8 of every 10 cases; however, I stress that their loss of opposition is (usually) only causing a small amount of their total hand disability and that they may not gain any significant functional benefit from a successful opponensplasty. In my opinion, before recommending surgery, there has to be convincing evidence that the loss of opposition, rather than loss of sensation or other motor deficits, is causing loss of function.

### High Median Nerve Palsy

In high median nerve paralysis, all the flexor compartment forearm muscles, apart from the ulnar- innervated FCU and the profundi to the little and ring fingers, are paralyzed. However, the working part of the profundus muscle often provides a full, although weak, range of middle finger flexion.

The primary aim of tendon transfers in high median nerve palsy is to restore flexion of the index finger and thumb, as well as opposition. Only the BR, ECRL, and ECU are available and suitable for transfer to the extrinsic flexors, and there are concerns with the use of ECU (see page 1200). Thus the BR is generally used to restore FPL function and, when needed, the ECRL to restore index finger profundus function. As these muscles have less potential excursion than the index profundus and FPL (see Table 31.1), satisfactory results can be obtained only if the patient can amplify the transfer’s excursion by actively flexing and extending the wrist; full finger and thumb flexion and extension are then achieved by the tenodesis effect of extending and flexing the wrist, respectively. If there is any hope of subsequent motor recovery, these transfers should be performed in an end- to- side fashion.

However, in most cases, adequate index flexion can be attained using side- to- side transfer to the functioning ring and little profundus tendons, obviating the need for ECRL transfer.

#### Timing and Selection of Extrinsic and Intrinsic Transfers

The selection and timing of tendon transfers for both intrinsic and extrinsic paralysis in high median nerve lesions depend on the prognosis for the median nerve injury, as well as the surgeon’s assessment and the patient’s perception of the functional disability.

In patients with median nerve palsy, the sensory loss is probably the most important single disability and, if permanent, dramatically reduces the functional benefit of tendon transfers, particularly for restoration of opposition. Although some surgeons believe that severe sensory loss, which is likely to be permanent in an adult, is a strong contraindication to tendon transfers, others argue that eyesight can provide sensory input and some hand function remains. Therefore they believe that a mobile, opposable thumb is probably more useful than one that cannot reach the remaining areas of the hand with normal sensation.

The functional deficit associated with a high median lesion is so profound that some surgeons advocate early end- to- side tendon transfers for selected patients to restore either opposition alone or opposition and extrinsic muscle function. These early transfers restore motor function during nerve regeneration and act as an internal splint, which may prevent contractures and eliminate the need for cumbersome external splints that may severely limit patients’ ability to use the hand. If patients ultimately regain some function in the muscles involved, the transfers act in synergy and provide additional power, whereas if no recovery occurs, the transfers act as a permanent substitute.

#### Extrinsic Transfers

Good extrinsic muscle function is usually restored after repair of a high median nerve laceration, in which case transfers to restore flexion of the thumb and index finger are not required. However, these should be considered if there is no evidence of extrinsic muscle recovery several months after the expected time of onset of the recovery after the nerve repair. This will depend on the distance of the median nerve injury from the paralyzed muscle. For practical purposes, extrinsic tendon transfers are indicated only when a high median nerve injury is not repaired or is grafted. If one decides not to perform early extrinsic tendon transfers, it is essential that mobility of the thumb and index finger are maintained until recovery occurs.

#### Restoration of Thumb Opposition

As previously mentioned, many patients retain thumb opposition after median nerve injury and do not require an opposition transfer. Burkhalter, who had a wealth of expertise and wrote this chapter in early editions, believed that those who lost opposition should undergo an opposition transfer soon after neurorrhaphy or grafting and before sensory recovery. His rationale was that the likelihood of recovery of thumb intrinsic muscle function after high median nerve injury is low.

Another reason to pursue early opponensplasty is to allow the patient to pick up objects with the forearm in any position of rotation. Patients who have lost thumb opposition and abduction use the APL and the extrinsic extensor muscles to position their thumbs when picking up an article from a table. Although the thumb–index web is open, the thumb is supinated and extended because all the muscles are supinators. With the thumb in this position, the patient can pick up objects from a flat surface only by fully pronating the hand, either by pronating the forearm or internally rotating the shoulder (see Fig. 31.2). Thus the patient cannot see the palm of the hand and cannot use sight as a substitute for the median nerve sensory loss, which renders motor function of little use to the person. If a patient is able to use a paralyzed hand to pick up articles and transfer objects from hand to hand in a position other than full pronation, the FPB is probably still functioning and an opponensplasty is not needed.

#### Restoration of Opposition in High Median Nerve Palsy

In high median nerve palsy, the EPL, EIP, and EDM are most readily available for opposition transfer. They position the thumb well, do not create significant donor morbidity, and do not require lengthening with a tendon graft. Although strong opposition can be restored with the FCU, which may be elongated with a paralyzed superficialis tendon, this is not advised because it eliminates the only functioning wrist flexor. Moreover, strong thumb opposition is not required as long as there are functioning intrinsic muscles capable of acting as a short flexor or short flexor substitute.

#### Brachioradialis- to- FPL Transfer

To achieve 30- mm excursion, the BR muscle must be freed of all soft tissue and fascial attachments throughout the distal two- thirds of the forearm. This involves dissection well proximal to the musculotendinous junction because the peripheral fibers of this muscle are inserted onto the deep fascia of the forearm. The transfer’s tension is set so that all three thumb joints can be fully passively extended when the wrist is flexed 30 degrees. Excessive tension must be avoided as otherwise flexion contractures can develop and cause considerable disability. Because the BR originates above it, the elbow is placed in approximately 45 degrees of flexion during tensioning. Although this degree of tension does not allow full IP joint flexion, it stabilizes it, improves thumb strength, and prevents a disabling flexion contracture from developing (Fig. 31.14). Most precision pinch activities are performed with the thumb IP joint fully extended.

#### ECRL- to- Index Profundus Transfer

An ECRL- to- index profundus transfer (see Fig. 31.14) is performed only in patients who need additional strength on the radial side of the hand and are unlikely to obtain significant reinnervation after nerve repair. Usually, index finger range of flexion, but not strength, is restored by side- to- side suturing of the index and the conjoint middle, ring, and little fingers’ profundus tendons in the distal forearm. If an ECRL transfer is indicated, its tension is adjusted so that the finger is virtually fully extended when the wrist is flexed 30 degrees and is fully flexed when the wrist is extended 30 to 45 degrees. If this transfer is placed in excessive tension, flexion contractures can result and cause considerable disability.

#### Complications

Swan neck deformities can develop in patients with hyperextensible PIP joints after high median paralysis due to unopposed intrinsic action on the PIP joint with paralysis of the FDS and median lumbricals. The deformity develops gradually as the PIP palmar plates stretch and may cause disability with locking of these joints in extension.

#### High Median Nerve Palsy Summary

In a high median nerve injury, the surgeon should consider performing an opposition transfer if forearm pronation is used as a substitute motion. If the nerve has been repaired, extrinsic transfers are seldom required because sufficient extrinsic muscle function is usually regained. If the patient needs a nerve graft, especially if this is done late or under unfavorable conditions (e.g., a long graft or a poor bed), useful extrinsic functional recovery is unlikely, and one should consider performing early extrinsic end- to- side transfers and/or a side- to- side transfer of the index and conjoint profundus tendons. All these intrinsic and extrinsic transfers can be performed at the same time.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: HIGH MEDIAN NERVE PALSY

Again, my enthusiasm for reconstructive surgery is tempered by the belief that sensation is the prime determinant of hand function and because I believe that sensory recovery after a high median nerve repair in adults is always poor and is even worse if a nerve graft is required. (See Chapter 30 for options for distal sensory nerve transfers.) Furthermore, I believe that nerve repair hardly ever restores lost opposition, although useful reinnervation of the forearm flexor muscles undoubtedly occurs. For these reasons, I do not favor early extrinsic tendon transfers and only restore opposition if, after maximal recovery has occurred, both the patient and the surgeon believe that this will improve hand function. Most patients will adapt and increasingly use their normal arm (even if this is the nondominant arm) for precision function; I thus rarely attempt to restore opposition when there is a combined median sensory and motor deficit. If an opposition transfer is indicated, I favor EIP transfer.

I use the BR transfer for restoration of thumb flexion, especially for conditions in which there is relative sensory sparing, and I use an endto- side attachment unless there is no prospect of later reinnervation. I do not think an ECRL transfer to restore index finger flexion is needed often and prefer to join the index and the common (i.e., middle, ring, and little) profundi tendons in a side- to- side fashion. Although this does not restore power to the index finger, I believe that the condition of the hand is usually too poor to benefit from an ECRL transfer, especially if there is a marked sensory deficit.

#### Expectations and Patient Counseling for High Median Nerve Palsy

It is imperative to stress to adult patients with high median nerve damage that they have suffered a severe injury and will never regain normal hand sensation, strength, or function. They should be aware that the aim of all surgery is to create the best possible “helper” hand, although this will always have rudimentary function compared with their normal contralateral one. The author thus does not enthusiastically recommend opponensplasty in the presence of severe sensory loss in the median nerve distribution because most patients will use their other hand for fine- pinching tasks, even after a “successful” opponensplasty. In contrast, the author does recommend tendon transfers for persistent loss of the thumb and finger extrinsic flexor function. However, it is important to stress that restored thumb and finger flexion will be weak, although with sensory input provided by the eyes, it should allow rudimentary use of the hand to hold and steady light objects.

#### High Median Nerve Palsy Summary

Tendon transfers (Table 31.4) can restore selected motor functions as a result of high and low median nerve palsy, but their ability to improve function is restricted by the severity of any associated sensory loss. Numerous opposition transfers have been described, and almost every possible motor has been used. Many pulleys are available, and there are numerous methods of distal attachment. The main reasons for failure of opposition transfers are persistent thumb contractures (recognized or unrecognized) and selection of a less than adequate motor. Early opposition transfer has been recommended for patients who use forearm pronation as a substitute for the paralyzed APB. Extrinsic tendon transfers for high median nerve palsy are usually required only when the injury is bridged with a nerve graft or left untreated.

## ULNAR NERVE PALSY

Ulnar nerve injuries also are classified as “high” or “low.” Low injuries occur distal to the origins of the motor branches to the FCU and ring and little finger FDP muscles. Strength of the extrinsic hand muscles is unaffected, but sensation is lost on the ulnar border of the hand and in the ring and little fingers, and the ulnar innervated intrinsic muscles are paralyzed. This results in weakness of thumb pinch, claw deformity, loss of the fingers’ normal pattern of flexion, and significant loss of hand dexterity and strength. High injuries occur above the origin of the motor branches to the FCU and ring and little finger FDP muscles. In this situation, loss of active ring and little DIP joint and weakness of wrist flexion compound these findings; although paradoxically, the claw deformity tends to be less severe.

The ulnar nerve usually innervates the hypothenar muscles (i.e., ADM, flexor digiti minimi, and opponens digiti minimi), all the interossei muscles of the fingers, the lumbricals for the ring and little fingers, and the AP muscle. In addition, the FPB muscle frequently (79%) has a dual median and ulnar innervation (see page 1192).

Ulnar nerve palsy is common with leprosy in areas where this disease is endemic, but in most parts of the world, it commonly occurs after a traumatic injury to the nerve or with severe compression of the nerve in the cubital tunnel (see Chapters 28 and 30). Less frequently the ulnar nerve may be compressed in the Guyon canal at the wrist, which may result in loss of intrinsic muscle function without loss of sensation. Injury to the medial cord of the brachial plexus, compression of T1 nerve root, and neurologic diseases such as syringomyelia, hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy ([HMSN]—Charcot- MarieTooth disease), poliomyelitis, and motor neuron disease may mimic ulnar nerve palsy.

### Patterns of Paralysis

The motor branch of the ulnar nerve can be lacerated very distally in the Guyon canal or as it traverses the palm of the hand, after it has given off its branches to the hypothenar muscles. This results in paralysis of the AP muscle and the interossei for the index, middle, and ring fingers while the hypothenar muscles retain normal function and sensation in the ring and little fingers is preserved. The motor branch of the ulnar nerve can also be damaged or compressed in the distal portion of the Guyon canal such that ring- and little- finger sensation is normal but there is a loss of function in all the ulnar innervated intrinsic muscles, including those of the hypothenar eminence.

Ulnar nerve injury or compression in the proximal portion of the Guyon canal causes loss of sensation on the palmar aspects of the hypothenar eminence and ring and little fingers as well as loss of function in all of the ulnar- innervated intrinsic muscles. However, sensation is preserved on the dorsoulnar aspect of the hand as this is provided by the dorsal branch of the ulnar nerve that arises in the distal forearm and perforates the deep fascia 6 to 8 cm proximal to the wrist. Only ulnar nerve injuries in or above the cubital tunnel cause loss of function in the FCU and the ring and little finger FDP muscles. The characteristic deformity of ulnar nerve palsy is clawing of the ulnar two digits because their lumbrical and interosseous muscles are innervated by the ulnar nerve, whereas the lumbricals of the index and middle fingers are supplied by the median nerve and continue to function. A low ulnar nerve palsy demonstrates more severe clawing of the ring and small digits than a high palsy because of unopposed IP flexion of the FDP.

### Anomalous Innervation Patterns

Anomalous innervation of the muscles of the hand and wrist is not uncommon and may cause errors in diagnosis and management. Innervation of the four FDP muscles may vary from all ulnar to all median, or each can have a dual innervation. The ring finger lumbrical has dual innervation in 50% of upper extremities, and in such a hand a complete ulnar nerve palsy only results in clawing of the little finger. The first dorsal interosseous is innervated completely or partially by the median nerve in 10% of hands and by the radial nerve in 1% of hands.The dorsoulnar border of the hand can be supplied by the superficial branch of the radial nerve, and this may lead to confusion concerning the level of an ulnar nerve lesion.

The Martin- Gruber—1763 and 1870, respectively—anastomosis is a motor nerve interconnection between the median (or anterior interosseous) and ulnar nerves in the proximal forearm that is found in 17% of people. It has four variations: 60% are type I, with motor branches from the median nerve traveling with the ulnar nerve to innervate some “median” intrinsic muscles; 35% are type II, with motor branches from the median nerve innervating some “ulnar” intrinsic muscles (most commonly the first dorsal interosseous); 3% are type III, with motor fibers from the ulnar nerve traveling with the median nerve and innervating some “median” intrinsic muscles; and 1% are type IV, with motor fibers from the ulnar nerve traveling with the median nerve to innervate some “ulnar” intrinsic muscles.

The 1897 Riche- Cannieu anomalous connection occurs between the motor branches of the ulnar and median nerves in the hand. Its presence can result in median nerve innervation of all of the lumbricals, such that finger clawing does not occur after a complete ulnar nerve injury. It may also explain the continued function of the median nerve–innervated thenar muscles after a complete division of the median nerve.

### Clinical Signs of Ulnar Nerve Palsy

There are many clinical signs and specific tests for loss of ulnar nerve motor function. Clawing, with hyperextension at MCP joint and flexion at IP joints, is the characteristic resting posture of the ring and little fingers (Duchenne sign). The Bouvier maneuver is used to test the integrity of the central slip and the lateral bands of the extensor expansion. Hyperextension of the clawed finger MCP joints is blocked and they are held in slight flexion. Attempted extension of the fingers will then result in full active extension of the PIP and DIP joints if the normal anatomy of the extensor expansion is preserved.

A sensitive way of detecting mild, as well as severe, loss of ulnar nerve function is to place the hand flat on a tabletop with the fingers abducted. The patient is then asked to abduct the middle finger from side to side (Pitres- Testut sign); not only can the strength of radial and ulnar abduction be assessed (tests second and third dorsal interosseous muscle strength) but also, if the patient is asked to move the middle finger from side to side as fast as possible, the coordination and ease of this maneuver can be compared with the normal hand to reveal subtle losses of motor function. Another sign of loss of ulnar nerve function is the inability to cross the middle finger dorsally over the index finger, or the index over the middle finger (a test of the first palmar interosseous and second dorsal interosseous muscles).

The Froment sign, marked thumb IP joint flexion when pinching sheets of paper between the thumb and index finger, indicates paralysis of the AD and first dorsal interosseous muscles with replacement of their pinch function by FPL. The Wartenberg sign is the inability to adduct the extended little finger to touch the extended ring finger. This is due to the EDM abducting the little finger (despite paralysis of the hypothenar muscles), unopposed by the third palmar interosseous, which is also paralyzed.

### Restoration of Function in Ulnar Nerve Palsy

The main aims of reconstructive surgery in “low” ulnar nerve palsy are to improve thumb pinch, correct finger clawing, and restore the normal pattern of finger flexion. “High” ulnar nerve surgery also aims to restore ring and little finger distal IP joint flexion. Attempts to restore sensibility to the ring and little fingers are possible but not normally performed, as the loss of sensation in ulnar nerve palsy is not as disabling as in median nerve palsy. Surgical procedures also have been devised to restore the concavity of the transverse metacarpal arch and correct little- finger abduction deformity; however, the former is rarely performed now.

### The Ulnar Claw Hand and Its Management

#### Low Ulnar Nerve Palsy

Clawing of the fingers in ulnar nerve palsy is due to the combined paralysis of the interosseous muscles of all the fingers and the lumbricals of the ring and little fingers. These intrinsic muscles flex the MCP and extend the PIP and DIP joints of the fingers; loss of their resting tone results in the “intrinsic minus” posture of hyperextension at the MCP joints and flexion at the IP joints. Clawing due to isolated ulnar nerve disease is most marked in the ring and little fingers because their interossei and lumbricals are all paralyzed; it occurs, however, in all four fingers in 63% of cases as a result of leprosy, even though the index and middle finger lumbricals are innervated by the median nerve.Clawing is more apparent during use of the hand than when it is at rest.

Marked clawing of the fingers occurs most commonly in those with mobile, lax finger joints and is less common in those with stiffer fingers (e.g., Caucasian manual laborers).

In addition to the static- claw deformity, loss of intrinsic muscle function can affect the normal dynamics of finger movement. In a normal hand, the MCP joints flex before the IP joints when gripping an object, such that the object is drawn into the palm of the hand. In ulnar paralysis, the DIP joints flex first, followed by the PIP joints and then the MCP joints, such that the object is not guided into the palm but instead is pushed out of the palm by the fingertips.

When assessing an ulnar claw hand, it is important to ascertain whether all the finger joints have full ranges of active and passive movement and whether correction of the hyperextension at the MCP joints results in full extension of the IP joints (the Bouvier maneuver). If this is the case, then operations that address the MCP joint hyperextension (i.e., capsulodesis or tenodesis of the MCP joint or Zancollitype tendon transfers) should completely correct the deformity. If the PIP joints remain flexed when the MCP joint hyperextension is corrected, but can be passively fully extended, then a static procedure will not correct IP joint flexion; thus a tendon transfer that inserts on the lateral bands of the fingers is needed to provide an extension force at the PIP joints as well as a flexion force at the MCP joints. However, such a transfer may not be successful in claw hands that have been present for a long time. This is because the dorsal transverse retinacular fibers of the extensor mechanism, which normally prevent palmar migration of the lateral bands, may have stretched, allowing the lateral bands to migrate palmar to the axis of rotation of the PIP joint. The lateral bands then become flexors, rather than extensors of these joints. In this scenario a secondary hyperextension deformity of the DIP joint may occur, as with a Boutonnière deformity. In this situation the lateral bands need to be repositioned so they pass dorsal to the axis of rotation of the PIP joint. Failure to do this before performing a tendon transfer into the lateral bands will exacerbate rather than improve the deformity. Also, fixed- flexion deformities of the IP joints, if present, need to be corrected before tendon transfer.

#### Outcome of Surgery Assessments

Assessment of the outcome of procedures for ulnar nerve palsy should be done at no less than 6 months postoperatively. Even then the comparison of the reported outcomes of various procedures to correct clawing of the fingers is difficult due to the heterogenicity of patient profiles in studies. Factors that will affect the outcome include the etiology of the ulnar nerve palsy (i.e., a static or progressive disorder), the level of the lesion (i.e., high or low), the presence of an isolated ulnar or combined median and ulnar nerve palsy, the presence or absence of joint contractures, and differences in patients’ overall joint mobility.

Careful review of the operative techniques used by various authors demonstrates that few surgeons use exactly the same surgical techniques and rehabilitation programs (e.g., one, two, or four tendons may be used for the FDS transfer, with the number varying from patient to patient in some studies). Finally, the movement and function being restored are complex, and different authors have used a variety of techniques for assessing the outcomes of their procedures. To my knowledge only one study has assessed the outcomes of these operations with a validated hand function assessment (e.g., Jebsen functional examination or the Purdue pegboard), and none has used a patient- reported outcome questionnaire such as Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) (Table 31.5).

#### Surgical Techniques for Correction of the Ulnar Claw Hand

Many techniques have been described to correct clawing of the fingers, and these can be divided into static and dynamic procedures that are discussed in the following subsections. If there are significant fixed- flexion deformities of the IP joints, then these need to be corrected with therapy, splinting, or surgical release before any static or dynamic procedure.

**Static techniques.** These prevent hyperextension of the finger MCP joints by either shortening their palmar capsules or creating “checkrein” ligaments/tenodeses. Although bone blocks to prevent MCP joint hyperextension, proximal flexor sheath release to cause bowstringing of the extrinsic finger flexors, and excision of an ellipse of skin and fascia from the palm of the hand to shorten the length of the palm (i.e., fasciodermadesis) have been described, these procedures are now rarely used.

To reiterate, static procedures will only correct clawing of the fingers satisfactorily if passive flexion of their MCP joints results in extension of their PIP joints (the Bouvier maneuver). If there are extension lags of the PIP joints in this position, then the use of a static procedure is contraindicated.

Static procedures avoid the need for tendon transfers, thus sparing potential motor tendons for other transfers. However, they do not restore the normal pattern of flexion of the fingers because IP flexion will continue to occur prior to MCP flexion. In addition, there is apprehension that they will stretch out over time, leading to recurrence of the deformity. This author’s sense is that attenuation is less likely to occur with tenodeses using tendon grafts rather than palmar capsular reefing. Most experienced surgeons would favor a dynamic tendon transfer to a static procedure if a motor is available and there are no other contraindications.

Palmar capsulodesis of the MCP joint (Zancolli). Zancolli described using longitudinal incisions over the A1 pulley of each affected finger; however, a transverse incision in the distal palmar crease improves exposure when two or more fingers require treatment. The A1 pulley of each finger is completely divided, as for a trigger finger release, and the flexor tendons are retracted to one side to expose the palmar plate of the MCP joint. Two parallel longitudinal incisions are made on the ulnar and radial edges of the palmar plate, and its insertion onto the metacarpal neck is released. This creates a distally based capsular flap with its base attached to the base of the proximal phalanx.

The MCP joint is hyperextended to improve the exposure, and a small transverse bone tunnel is created in the metacarpal neck with a fine awl or Kirschner wire. The metacarpal neck is then roughened with a curette or bur to create a bleeding bed for adherence of the capsular imbrication. Zancolli then passed a monofilament wire through the tunnel and the flap of the palmar plate, with sufficient tension, to maintain the MCP joint in 20 degrees of flexion. He then firmly twisted the ends of the wire and cut them to lie flat on the metacarpal. However, a small bone anchor with an attached 3- 0 braided nonabsorbable suture is also ideal for attaching the capsular flaps onto metacarpal necks.

The capsuloplasties are performed sequentially from the index to the little MCP joint. Postoperatively the hand is splinted using a palmar plaster slab with the wrist in neutral, the MCP joints in 20 degrees of flexion, and the IP joints free. Immobilization is maintained for 5 weeks and the hand is then mobilized.

Omer modified Zancolli’s technique by cutting away a triangular portion of the deep transverse metacarpal ligament (DTML) on each side of the palmar plate flap (Fig. 31.16), which is probably unnecessary. He also recommended excision of a 1.5- cm- wide ellipse of palmar skin to prevent stretching of the volar plate and immobilizing the hand with the MCP joints flexed for 6 weeks.

Results. Brown reported the outcome of this procedure in 44 cases, most of which occurred during the Vietnam conflict. All had at least 15 degrees of hyperextension of the MCP joints and 15- degree extension losses at the PIP joints; those with irreparable nerve injuries underwent early surgery as soon as other wounds permitted. He modified Zancolli’s technique by advancing the capsular flap further, such that it created 60 degrees of fixed- flexion deformity at the MCP joint, and sutured it to the periosteum of the metacarpal and dorsal wall of the flexor sheath; this most likely was not as robust an attachment as that used by Zancolli.

Most hands underwent ring and little finger capsulodeses, and all were followed up for 12 to 32 months. Five patients required early revision surgery for recurrence of clawing, and in another 18 cases the claw deformity gradually recurred, reaching the original preoperative severity in 9 cases. However, no progression of the recurrent claw deformity was seen after 12 months. Ten hands were left with fixed- flexion deformities of more than 30 degrees of the MCP joints and 9 hands developed fixed- flexion deformities of the PIP joints as a result of adhesions between the flexor tendons and the capsulodesis; these required treatment with a tenolysis. Overall Brown felt the surgeries had improved function and achieved their aim in 21 of the 44 hands. He considered that some of the complications he encountered probably were attributable to associated tendon injuries in severe forearm wounds.

In contrast, Leddy et al. performed the procedure on 56 fingers of 24 hands using Zancolli’s transosseous wiring technique to secure the capsular flap to the metacarpal. They attached the capsulodesis to hold the MCP in as little flexion as possible; sufficient to just produce full extension of the PIP joint, the amount being determined preoperatively. They also released the proximal 2 to 3 cm of the finger flexor sheath to the middle third of the proximal phalanx to allow the flexor tendons to bowstring over the MCP joints and reported correction of clawing in 53 of the 56 fingers, with no recurrence of clawing after 3 months.

There is thus controversy regarding the outcome of this procedure that is most commonly used now to address idiopathic mobile camptodactyly of the little finger. However, a troublesome flexion contracture of the MCP joint can result if the palmar plate is advanced too far proximally on the metacarpal, or if the pulley release is continued beyond the A1 pulley, and recurrence of clawing can occur even if the capsular flap is firmly anchored to the metacarpal. Anderson considered that this and other static procedures were not ideal for those engaged in heavy manual labor in factories, on farms, or in fields.

Riordan static tenodesis. With the Riordan technique, the ECRL and ECU tendons are split longitudinally into two equal halves throughout their lengths. One- half of each is divided transversely at the musculotendinous junction in the forearm but left attached to their insertions on the index and little finger metacarpal bases. The other, “intact” half is left to continue to actively extend the wrist. The divided halves of each tendon are turned distally on their intact insertions, and each is split in half longitudinally to produce a total of four slips.

The radial lateral band of the extensor apparatus is exposed through an incision on the side of the proximal phalanx of each finger and a tendon slip is passed through the interosseous space and lumbrical canal (i.e., palmar to the DTML) to emerge in the finger incision where it is sutured to the radial lateral band. The tenodesis is sutured with the wrist in 30 degrees of extension, the MCP joints in 80 degrees of flexion, and the IP joints extended; the hand is immobilized in this position for 3 weeks. The hand is then mobilized with the wrist splinted in extension for a further 2 weeks. The ECU can be used in isolation if only the ring and little fingers are involved.

Riordan recommended placing the transfer in too much tension, rather than too little, because he considered that excessive tension can be corrected by passive stretching if necessary. He reserved this procedure for cases in which a suitable motor was not available for a dynamic transfer.

Parkes static tenodesis. Two free tendon grafts (usually PL and a plantaris tendon) are needed if all four fingers require correction and each is divided into two strips. The radial side of the extensor expansion of each finger is exposed through a midlateral incision that curves slightly dorsally at the level of the PIP joint. Parkes advised scarifying the superficial surfaces of the extensor expansion at the proposed site of graft attachment. A palmar incision is made in the shape of a “7” with its transverse limb located at the distal palmar crease, and the palmar aponeurosis is reflected with the skin to expose the lumbrical muscles (Fig. 31.17A). The lumbrical sheath of each finger is opened, and a strip of tendon graft is passed down each into the finger incisions.

Disadvantages. Theoretically this wrist- powered tenodesis is effective only when the patient flexes the wrist to tighten the graft and produce active MCP flexion with IP joint extension. However, Riordan thought that it was effectively a tenodesis with little if any gliding of the grafts with wrist motion.

**Dynamic tendon transfers.** Many transfers have been described to correct finger clawing, and they use a variety of muscles as motors, both wrist and finger flexors and extensors, and a number of various insertions. Table 31.1 shows the resting muscle lengths and tension fractions of these muscles, as well as those of the interossei and lumbricals with functions that they will in part replace. Whichever muscle is selected as the motor for transfer, its tendon must pass palmar to the axis of rotation of the finger MCP joints to achieve flexion; this is accomplished by passing the transfer palmar to the transverse metacarpal ligament. All tendon transfers for correction of clawing cross the wrist joint, and their action can be amplified by the tenodesis effect of wrist motion. Wrist flexion will tighten transfers that cross the dorsal surface of this joint, and wrist extension will tighten those that run palmar to it.

Insertions for dynamic transfers. Several possible insertions are available, and the choice will fine- tune the function of the transfer. Insertion of the transfer to the proximal phalanx or onto the A1 pulley of the flexor sheath will provide pure flexion of the MCP joints. Insertion onto the A2a or entire A2 pulley will produce a greater moment arm and thus, theoretically, stronger flexion of the MCP joints. Insertion onto the radial lateral bands of the middle, ring, and little fingers and radial or ulnar lateral band of the index finger will produce MCP flexion (and some abduction/adduction) as well as extension of the IP joints. Finally, suturing onto the insertions of the dorsal interosseous muscles at the bases of the proximal phalanges will provide MCP flexion and abduction/adduction. However, as highlighted by Anderson, this insertion has a much smaller flexor moment at the MCP joint than either a lateral band or A1 pulley insertion. It thus provides weaker

MCP flexion than the other insertions and is only useful in mobile claw deformities in supple hands that do not require a great MCP flexion force to correct. In such hands, it probably has a lower risk of producing “overcorrection” (intrinsic- plus) deformities than other insertions.

Superficialis tendon transfer techniques and modifications. Stiles and Forrester- Brown are credited with the first description of a dynamic tendon transfer to correct finger clawing. They transferred one slip of each superficialis tendon into the corresponding extensor digitorum tendon over the proximal phalanx. Bunnell modified their transfer by rerouting both slips of all the superficialis tendons through the lumbrical canals and anchoring them to both the radial and ulnar lateral bands of the extensor mechanism (i.e., Stiles- Bunnell procedure). Bunnell’s aim was to correct the claw deformity and also restore MCP abduction and adduction, but the Stiles- Bunnell operation was beset with problems resulting from its complexity, adhesion formation, and the conversion of the prime flexors of the PIP joints (FDS) into powerful extensors of the IP joints. It often resulted in the development of overcorrection and intrinsic- plus deformities in the fingers after 1 or 2 years.

Modified Stiles- Bunnell procedure (Littler’s transfer). Littler modified the Stiles- Bunnell procedure by only using one (usually the middle finger) superficialis tendon, and this has become the standard technique for correction of finger clawing. The FDS of the middle finger is divided at the IP joint level, leaving a short length of one of its two distal slips attached to the middle phalanx. This is used to tenodese the PIP joint and prevent hyperextension. The main FDS tendon is withdrawn into a palmar incision and split lengthwise into four equal slips. One slip is passed through the lumbrical canal of each finger, palmar to the DTML, and is sutured onto the radial lateral band of the extensor apparatus (Fig. 31.19A). Each slip is sutured with the wrist in 30 degrees of palmar flexion, the MCP joints in 80 to 90 degrees of flexion, and the IP joints in full extension. The use of the ring, but not the middle, finger superficialis tendon for this procedure is contraindicated in high ulnar nerve palsy or when the profundus of the ring finger is weak.

*Modifications of the superficialis transfer.* Littler’s transfer, the modified Stiles- Bunnell procedure, has itself been modified by many surgeons to subtly alter its function and reduce the risk of potential complications. The modifications are primarily related to its insertion.

For the same reason, Riordan recommended attachment of the tendon slips to the A1 pulley if the PIP joints are lax and hyperextend.

Shah modified the superficialis transfer and only addressed clawing of the ring and little fingers. In each of these digits he exposed the A1 pulley, opened the flexor sheath distal to this, and divided the superficialis tendon as far distally as possible. He then pulled the cut end of the tendon out of the window in the flexor sheath, pulled it down over the outer surface of the intact A1 pulley, and sutured it onto itself with the finger MCP joint flexed to 90 degrees. He performed the surgery under local anesthetic and reported good results in 12 of 15 cases due to leprosy. Hastings and McCollam performed the same procedure, though not under local anesthetic, whenever the claw correction surgery was restricted to the ring and/or little finger and the little finger had an adequate FDS.

*Results.* Superficialis transfers do not increase grip strength and risk donor- site morbidity, such as the development of swan neck and other deformities, as described earlier.

Brandsma and Ottenhoff- De Jonge reported full correction of clawing in 16 and improved clawing in 43 of 76 hands with leprosy that underwent either middle or ring finger FDS transfer to the affected fingers. They used an insertion into either a lateral band or the A1 pulley.

Hastings and McCollam assessed 3- year outcomes of FDS transfers using Zancolli’s lasso insertion onto the A1 pulley in 23 digits of 12 hands, predominantly involving the ring and little fingers. These procedures were performed for traumatic ulnar nerve injuries with no evidence of functional intrinsic recovery, and, whenever possible, utilized the FDS tendon of an involved finger as the donor for that digit. Thus in most hands both the ring FDS tendon was lassoed around the ring finger A1 pulley and the little FDS tendon was lassoed around the little finger A1 pulley. Correction of the claw deformity was achieved in 19 of the 23 fingers, with 2 of the failures due to preoperative fixedflexion contractures of the PIP joints. Although grip strength was not improved and functional disability persisted, most patients felt that the procedure had subjectively improved their dexterity. Four fingers developed minor swan neck deformities, and these authors recommend the procedure for supple, ligamentously lax individuals who do not require increased power.

Anderson inserted the four slips of the middle finger FDS into the A1 and A2a pulleys of each finger in 20 hands affected with leprosy, all of which had undergone hand therapy preoperatively to address joint contractures. He obtained excellent and good claw corrections in most of the fingers with an average postoperative MCP joint flexion contracture of 10 degrees. He considered that, due to strong flexion moment, the procedure may not be ideally suited for patients with hypermobile joints.

Oberlin also utilized the A1 and A2a pulley as his insertion and, like Hastings and McCollam, used one FDS for each finger. He achieved satisfactory results in a group of 19 patients with leprosy who had no access to therapy, such that many underwent surgery with uncorrected fixed PIP joint contractures. Overcorrection (i.e., swan neck deformity) was noted in two hyperlax fingers.

Extensor indicis proprius and extensor digiti minimi transfers. Fowler’s technique uses small longitudinal or curved incisions on the dorsal aspects of the index and little MCP joints, through which the EIP and EDM tendons are exposed and sectioned. A dorsal transverse incision is then made just distal to the wrist, and the two tendons are retrieved into this and each is split lengthwise into two sections. The four slips are passed through the interosseous spaces, palmar to the DTML, and inserted onto the radial aspect of the extensor apparatus of each finger.

This procedure may produce excessive tension in the extensor apparatus and lead to intrinsic- plus deformities. It may also cause reversal of the normal metacarpal arch and, occasionally, extensor weakness in the little finger.

Riordan found the transfer technically difficult and wondered whether it acted primarily as a tenodesis. He felt that the length of the transfer was insufficient so that it had to be inserted in excessive tension that could result in an intrinsic- plus deformity. He therefore modified Fowler’s technique by splitting the EIP into two slips and passing these through the third and fourth interosseous spaces, palmar to the transverse metacarpal ligament, before inserting them onto the radial lateral bands of the ring and little fingers. The transfer of the relatively long EIP tendon to the relatively short ring and little fingers allowed it to be sutured in the correct tension. If there was a combined median and ulnar nerve palsy causing clawing of all four fingers, Riordan harvested a tendon graft (i.e., PL), split it longitudinally in two, and passed the slips through the first and second interosseous spaces to insertions on the radial lateral bands of the index and middle fingers. He then sutured the proximal ends of the grafts to the EIP, which then replaced the intrinsic function in all four fingers.

Anderson addresses the problem of insufficient length of the EIP and EDM transfers by harvesting 1- cm- long strips of the extensor hood apparatus in continuity with their tendons rather than dividing the tendons at the MCP joint level. Careful closure of the extensor hood is critical to prevent postoperative extensor tendon subluxation.

Transfers using wrist flexor and extensor muscles. BR and wrist extensor and flexor muscles have been used as motors for transfers to correct clawing. Such transfers, by increasing the force of MCP flexion with a motor that does not usually contribute to finger flexion, theoretically should also improve hand strength. All these transfers require lengthening with free grafts to provide sufficient length to reach the insertion site, unless it is possible to split the transfer’s motor tendon and use half of it as the graft.

Dorsal route transfer of ECRB/ECRL. This transfer was described by Brand. The insertion of ECRL or brevis tendon is divided through a short transverse incision at the level of the distal end of the radius and withdrawn into a second transverse incision 8 cm proximally in the distal forearm (Fig. 31.20). The plantaris (or the PL if stout) tendon is harvested and attached to the distal end of the motor’s tendon. Brand advised a “wraparound” technique of anastomosis so that its surface is covered with epitenon and there are no exposed cut ends of either the graft or the extensor tendon. This was to reduce adhesion formation (see Fig. 31.20B). The plantaris tendon is split into four tails and, using tendon- passing forceps, these are passed into the original incision over the distal radius.

This is simpler to perform if the wrist and all the fingers are fixed in this position on a frame during the stitching. This position is then maintained for 3 weeks in a light plaster cast.

Brand stressed that it was important not to allow the fingers to rest in the claw position during reeducation and rehabilitation, and he splinted each finger in extension. This encourages free MCP joint movement with resisted active extension of the wrist.

*Results.* Brand assessed the outcome of the ECRB procedure in 246 hands affected by leprosy, of which 183 had a combined high ulnar and low medium nerve paralysis. At an average follow- up of 2 years, he observed excellent or good results in 71% of cases. He found reeducation following this transfer difficult, although he judged that it worked better than the Littler (modified Stiles- Bunnell) transfer. He was also concerned that (1) wrist extension slackened the graft, such that the patient had to learn to avoid wrist extension while activating the ECRB transfer and that (2) adhesions would form where the tendon grafts (extending the transfer) passed through the intermetacarpal spaces. For these reasons Brand devised a flexor route transfer for ECRL. Riordan also had trouble retraining patients because the wrist extensor normally relaxes when the fingers are extended, but he still considered that this transfer produced good results.

Burkhalter and Strait modification. These authors modified the Brand extensor route ECRL transfer by passing it to only the ring and little fingers and inserting the tendon slips into the bases of their proximal phalanges (see Fig. 31.19B). This was because of concern that insertion into the lateral bands might cause hyperextension of the IP joints. The ECRL is lengthened with a split- ECRL tendon graft and passed through the third and fourth intermetacarpal spaces, ensuring that (1) the anastomosis between the ECRL and the graft is situated well proximal to the intermetacarpal spaces and (2) sufficient intermetacarpal fascia is removed to minimize the risk of troublesome adhesions. At the finger, each tendon slip is passed through a transverse drill hole in the proximal phalanx and held with a pull- out suture technique.

Shortening of the ECRL tendon ensures the tendon junction lies proximal to the carpal tunnel, and not within it. C, Each of the two tails of the plantaris is split longitudinally to create four equally sized tails. These are all passed from the flexor compartment forearm incision, through the carpal tunnel, and into an incision in the palm of the hand. D, Incisions are made on the dorsal surfaces of the proximal phalanges of each finger and a Carroll tendon passer (or equivalent) is used to pass the plantaris tails palmar to the transverse metacarpal ligaments and through the lumbrical canals to the radial aspects of the extensor hoods of the little, middle, and ring fingers and ulnar aspect of the extensor hood of the index finger. The forearm and dorsal wrist wounds are closed. The tails of the graft are now ready to be attached to the extensor mechanisms of the fingers with the wrist and MCP joints flexed and the IP joints extended.

The ECRL tendon is lengthened with a plantaris or PL tendon graft, taking care to ensure that the anastomosis is covered with a layer of epitenon (i.e., wraparound technique) and lies proximal to, and will never enter, the carpal tunnel. The tendon graft is split into four tails. An incision is made in the midpalmar region, along the thenar crease, and the palmar fascia is separated to expose the superficial palmar arch. A long tendon retriever is introduced into the palmar wound and passed ulnar to the median nerve, dorsal to the superficial palmar arch and through the carpal tunnel into the incision on the palmar aspect of the forearm. The four tails of the tendon graft are passed into the palmar wound (see Fig. 31.21B), then incisions are made on the dorsoradial aspects of the bases of the middle, ring, and little fingers and dorsoulnar aspect of the index finger.

Each tail of the tendon graft is individually passed with tendon retrieving forceps from the palm, through the lumbrical canal, and into the dorsoradial/dorsoulnar finger wounds. The wounds on the wrist, forearm, and palm are closed before tensioning the graft. With the wrist in 45 degrees of flexion, the MCP joints in 70 degrees of flexion, and the IP joints straight, the graft slips are sutured to the lateral bands of the extensor expansions (i.e., radial lateral band for the middle, ring, and little fingers and ulnar lateral band for the index finger), taking care that the four fingers are set in the same tension. This is simpler to perform if the wrist and all the fingers are fixed in this position on a frame during the stitching. The tourniquet is released and hemostasis is secured before skin closure. The hand is placed in a well- padded plaster cast with the wrist in neutral position, the MCP joints in 80 degrees of flexion, and the IP joints straight.

*Results.* Brand did not report his results for this transfer but found reeducation/rehabilitation easier than after the dorsal ECRB transfer, probably because the transfer tightens with wrist extension (passes palmar to the wrist joint). He noted that the transfer sometimes restricted wrist extension and accepted the theoretical danger of injuring/compressing the median nerve in the carpal tunnel. Malaviyaobserved loss of median nerve function in 1 of his 12 ECRL transfers through the carpal tunnel, but others have found that median nerve dysfunction is not increased by passing transfers through the carpal tunnel. Malaviya also observed that the ECRL tendon, if bulky, can be split longitudinally into two halves; one can be freed at the musculocutaneous junction and used instead of PL or plantaris as a tendon graft to lengthen the transfer.

Palande evaluated the outcome of Brand’s flexor route transfer in 32 hands affected by leprosy at a follow- up of 2 years using his method for assessing outcome. Overall, approximately 90% of hands achieved fair- to- good results in terms of appearance, ability to attain the intrinsic- plus position, and extent of finger flexion, although only 28% achieved satisfactory restoration of the transverse metacarpal arch. Even though 18% of the 128 fingers developed hyperextension deformities (mobile) of their PIP joints, the procedure and its rehabilitation increased grip strength by 25%.

*Disadvantages.* The need for a free tendon or fascia lata graft to lengthen the wrist extensor motor unit can result in adhesions, and the multiple steps of the procedure require increased operative time.

Flexor carpi radialis transfer. Riordan devised the FCR transfer for use in patients with longstanding claw hands who habitually flexed the wrist to attempt to extend their clawed fingers (tenodesis maneuver). The intention was to simultaneously correct swan neck and wrist flexion deformities.

The FCR tendon is divided at the wrist and elongated with a BR, plantaris, or PL tendon graft that is split into four longitudinal strips.

These are passed subcutaneously around the radial metaphysis to the extensor side of the forearm, then through the intermetacarpal spaces and lumbrical canals to their attachments on the radial lateral bands of the involved fingers.

*Disadvantage.* This operation removes the only wrist flexor in a high ulnar palsy, so in this situation may be contraindicated.

Palmaris longus four- tail transfer. The PL tendon is divided at its insertion into the palmar fascia and withdrawn proximally into the forearm. It is lengthened with a tendon graft and tunneled subcutaneously (palmar to the palmar aponeurosis) into a midpalmar incision. The rest of the procedure is as described for the Brand’s ECRL flexor route transfer, but some prefer to insert the transfer into the interosseous tendons rather than the lateral bands of the extensor expansion. Although a relatively weak donor, this procedure is useful for younger patients with supple hands who have a well- developed, strong PL. Taylor et al. found no difference in the outcome of this transfer and an ECRL transfer routed through the carpal tunnel (both inserted into the lateral bands). They advocate its use in all patients with a welldeveloped PL tendon and a hypermobile or mobile hand on the basis that there was likely to be less donor morbidity. Grip strength was not assessed in this study, but manual labor workers reported satisfactory outcomes.

#### Postoperative Management of Tendon Transfers for Claw Finger Correction

It is standard to immobilize the hand after transfer for 3 to 4 weeks with the wrist close to neutral, the MCP joints fully flexed, and the IP joints extended (i.e., intrinsic- plus position). Therapy designed to incorporate the transfer into hand function is then commenced, avoiding heavy gripping and lifting for 12 weeks. However, Rath mobilized his four- tailed FDS to A1 pulley transfers on postoperative day 2 and reported more rapid rehabilitation. He used Pulvertaft weaves to strengthen the Zancolli loop insertions of the tendon slips on the A1 pulleys and observed no cases of transfer dehiscence.

### Restoration of Thumb–Index Key Pinch and Tip Pinch

Thumb adduction force in a normal hand is primarily provided by AP, with the first dorsal interosseous acting as a force- couple to prevent distraction and dorsal subluxation of the trapeziometacarpal (TM) joint.However, contraction of the radial head of the first dorsal interosseous can contribute significantly to the force of thumb adduction when the index finger is stabilized. Loss of AP, FPB, and first dorsal interosseous muscle function results in weakness of TM joint adduction and thumb MCP flexion, such that the thumb has weak key and tip pinch. Biomechanical studies suggest that a 75% to 80% loss of power pinch occurs in patients with ulnar nerve palsy, and this is explained by the combined tension capability of the AP and FPB of 4.3%, which is nearly double that of the FPL (2.7%), as well as their large moment at the TM joint.

In ulnar nerve palsy the EPL and the FPL muscles are the only remaining adductors of the thumb, and both only provide weak adduction. Their actions in the absence of the ulnar intrinsic muscles contribute to collapse of the thumb into a “Z”- deformity if the joints are hypermobile, characterized by hyperextension of the MCP and flexion of the IP joint. Thumb pinch can only occur with flexion of the IP joint (i.e., Froment sign), such that the ability to roll small objects between the thumb and index fingertips is diminished.

The aims of reconstructive surgery to the thumb in ulnar nerve palsy are restoration of strong adduction of the first metacarpal, active flexion of the hyperextended MCP joint, and active extension of the hyperflexed IP joint in order to regain power and precision function. Although the thumb is of prime importance for pinch, the index finger MCP joint needs to be stabilized so that this finger does not drift ulnarward away from the thumb.

#### Dynamic Procedures to Restore Thumb Adduction

**ECRB as motor (Smith).** A dorsal transverse wrist incision is made and the ECRB is divided at its insertion on the middle finger metacarpal base. The proximal end of this tendon is retrieved into a second transverse incision just proximal to the extensor retinaculum. A third transverse incision is made between the dorsal aspects of the index and middle finger metacarpals, over the proximal end of the second intermetacarpal space, and the dorsal interosseous fascia is incised and a small window is created in the intermetacarpal space by excising some of the paralyzed interosseous muscles. A longitudinal incision is then made on the ulnar side of the MCP joint of the thumb and a curved hemostat is tunneled from this incision, deep (dorsal) to the transverse head of AP, through the window in the index/middle interosseous space and into the dorsal hand wound.

A tendon graft, usually PL, is passed through this tunnel to the thumb and is sutured to the tendon of the AP. The proximal end of the tendon graft is then passed subcutaneously into the most proximaldistal forearm incision, where it is sutured to the ECRB tendon. The tension is set such that the thumb lies just palmar to the index finger when the wrist is in neutral. With the wrist extended, the thumb should then fall into abduction, while with the wrist in flexion the thumb should lie firmly against the palm. Postoperatively the hand is immobilized with the thumb in a neutral position and the wrist in 40 degrees of extension. After 3 weeks the plaster cast is exchanged for a protective splint and the patient is encouraged to perform active rangeof- motion exercises.

Omer thought that ECRB was the best transfer for restoration of adduction of the thumb but favored passing the transfer through the middle/ring rather than the index/middle intermetacarpal space, then superficial to the transverse head of AP and dorsal to the flexor tendons. Insertion of the transfer onto the strong fascia over the abductor tubercle of the thumb (Fig. 31.22) may improve pronation for pinch.

**FDS as motor (Edgerton and Brand).** The ring finger FDS is released in the finger and withdrawn into a palmar incision, as described by Edgerton and Brand. It is then passed around a natural pulley, either a natural window in the palmar fascia overlying the neck of the middle finger metacarpal (Fig. 31.23) or the free distal edge of the vertical septa running from the palmar fascia onto the middle finger metacarpal. The use of a pulley prevents the otherwise inevitable gradual migration of the transfer radialward that will render the procedure ineffective. The transfer then runs across the palm parallel to the transverse head of AP to the thumb. It runs superficially to the palmar fascia if the former pulley is used and deep to it, the neurovascular bundles, and the finger flexor tendons if the latter is used. If there is an isolated ulnar nerve palsy, the tendon is inserted onto the adductor tubercle, whereas if there is a combined ulnar and median nerve palsy, it is inserted into the radial expansion of the extensor apparatus of the thumb to provide additional pronation.

**Extensor indicis as a motor (Brown).** A longitudinal incision is made over the metacarpal neck and MCP joint of the index finger and the EIP is detached from the extensor hood over the midportion of the proximal phalanx, as described by Brown. The incision is retracted ulnarly to expose the middle/ring intermetacarpal space, and a curved hemostat is used to pass the transfer through this space into a small incision, made onto the tip of the hemostat, in the palm at about the level of the proximal palmar crease. The transfer is then passed along the transverse muscle belly of the AP, deep to the finger flexor tendons and neurovascular bundles, and is sutured to the AP insertion. Proper tension is achieved by suturing the transfer in moderate tension with the wrist in neutral and the thumb and index metacarpals parallel to each other. Brand considered the EDC (index) too weak to provide adequate thumb adduction strength.

#### Combination of EIP and EDC (Little) Tendon Transfers for Pinch

The aim of this transfer is to improve both thumb adduction and abduction of the index finger to strengthen pinch. The ulnar slip of the EDC (Little) is detached at the MCP level of the little finger and retracted into an incision at the level of the wrist, just distal to the extensor retinaculum. It is then passed superficially across the dorsum of the hand to the adductor tubercle of the thumb. The EIP is divided at the dorsal hood of the MCP joint and also retracted into an incision at the level of the wrist, just distal to the extensor retinaculum. It is then passed around the thumb extensor tendons, looped around the first dorsal interosseous, and attached to the radial aspect of the base of the index proximal phalanx, thus making the EIP an abductor of the index finger. In a small series study of 6 patients, pinch improved to 30% to 50% of normal and index abduction to 30% to 50% of normal after 48 months.

#### Index Abduction Techniques

**Accessory slip of APL transfer.** Neviaser et al. described this technique as an alternative to the use of an EIP transfer, which they and others felt was unsatisfactory. The APL is exposed distal to the first dorsal compartment, and one of its slips, which inserts onto and extends the thumb metacarpal base, is identified and preserved (Fig. 31.24). Another accessory slip is detached, and a subcutaneous tunnel is created from the radial styloid to an incision on the radial side of the proximal phalanx of the index finger. A PL or plantaris- free graft is attached to the insertion of the first dorsal interosseous, passed through the subcutaneous tunnel, and interwoven into the abductor pollicis slip with the index finger and wrist in neutral position. Although this transfer does not appreciably increase the force of tip pinch, it is thought to provide the index finger with better stability and versatility than simply bracing it against the other fingers.

Graham and Riordan transferred the ring finger FDS to the first dorsal interosseous to produce strong abduction of the index finger. The technique is contraindicated in high ulnar nerve palsy secondary to FDP weakness.

#### Arthrodesis of Thumb MCP and IP Joints to Restore Pinch

In an intrinsic- minus thumb, the loss of AP and FPB function results in the extension or hyperextension of the MCP joint and flexion of the IP joint during pinch (i.e., Froment sign) because the extrinsic thumb muscles cannot control these two joints independently. Fixed deformities of the MCP (hyperextension) and IP (flexion) joints can develop, but even if none is present, the position of the thumb results in a poor pinch function. Pinch function and strength can be improved by fusion of either the thumb MCP or IP joint because the FPL and EPL will then have better control of movement at the remaining joint.

Brown demonstrated this by observing that splinting of an ulnarpalsied thumb IP joint increases pinch strength by 1 to 2 kg. It is important to realize that a fixed deformity of the remaining joint is a contraindication for arthrodesis of the other. Most surgeons with extensive experience in treating ulnar nerve palsy prefer to use tendon transfers to restore AP function and thus improve thumb pinch.

**Arthrodesis of the MCP joint.** This procedure is often preferred to IP joint fusion because the retained IP joint motion better preserves the ability to roll small objects between the thumb and fingers during pinch. Anderson considers that MCP arthrodesis is indicated when there is a severe MCP hyperextension contracture or marked hyperextension of this joint during key pinch with pain and instability. It is also indicated when MCP hyperextension develops after a tendon transfer to restore thumb adduction. The MCP joint should be fused in no more than 15 degrees of flexion; otherwise, a “crank handle” action may occur during pinch, resulting in external rotation of the thumb so that only end- to- side pinch, and not tipto- tip pinch, is possible with the index finger (see Fig. 31.8). Brand placed the MCP joint in 15 degrees of flexion, 5 degrees of abduction, and 15 degrees of pronation. Any of a number of techniques can be used to fuse the MCP joint; these are described in Chapter 11.

**Arthrodesis of the IP joint.** Tubiana preferred IP joint arthrodesis to fusion of the MCP joint; however, Brown observed that, although thumb pinch strength improved, thumb dexterity was sometimes lost, so he reserved this procedure for patients with IP joint instability. There are a large quantity of techniques for IP joint fusion; some of them are described in Chapter 11.

**Split FPL tenodesis (Fig. 31.25).** Mohammed et al. devised an alternative to IP fusion to stabilize the thumb IP joint, prevent hyperflexion, and retain a small (30- degree) arc of IP movement in tetraplegia. The FPL is exposed at the level of the proximal phalanx through a palmar longitudinal incision. The radial half of this tendon is divided at its insertion on the distal phalanx and split away from the intact ulnar half to the level of the base of the proximal phalanx. The EPL is exposed through a dorsal longitudinal incision over the proximal phalanx, and the radial slip of the FPL is passed subcutaneously around the radial border of this bone. A small incision is then made over the FPL tendon at the wrist. The radial slip is then sutured to the EPL close to its insertion so that the thumb IP joint is stabilized in 20- degree to 30- degree flexion when the FPL tendon is pulled on at the wrist. After wound closure the thumb is immobilized in 20- degree to 30- degree flexion and may be temporarily fixed in this position by transfixion of the IP joint with a Kirschner wire for 4 weeks. The hand and thumb are immobilized in a cast for 4 weeks, after which the thumb IP joint may be protected in a small plastic orthosis for a further 2 weeks. Following this tenodesis, the FPL provides balanced flexion and extension forces to the IP joint that is thus stabilized and cannot hyperflex.

### Restoration of the Transverse Metacarpal Arch

The intrinsic muscles, through a combination of adduction, abduction, and flexion at the MCP joints, produce digital rotation. This rotation occurs during precision grip, tool handling, and cupping of the hand to hold water or grasp large objects. In ulnar nerve palsy, the ability of the hand to make a cup or contour itself around an object is lost, such that simple acts (e.g., opening the lid of a jar or turning a valve) become clumsy. Several tendon transfers, which partially restore the normal curve of the transverse metacarpal arch, have been described.

#### Bunnell’s “Tendon T” Operation

An FDS is detached from a finger, retrieved proximally in the palm, and attached to the middle of a free tendon graft that is passed dorsal to the flexor tendons, as described for Bunnell’s operation. One end of the tendon graft is inserted into the radial aspect of the base of the thumb’s proximal phalanx and the other is inserted into the ulnar aspect of the neck of the little finger metacarpal to form a “T.” Contraction of the superficialis produces adduction and flexion of the thumb and the little finger, so the T is converted into a Y with restoration of the metacarpal arch. Brown considered this transfer inadequate in both strength and excursion for restoration of thumb pinch. Littler modified the procedure by detaching and splitting an FDS tendon and inserting one slip into the adductor tubercle of the thumb and the other into the ulnar aspect of the base of the proximal phalanx of the little finger, thereby avoiding the need for a free tendon graft.

#### EDM Transfer (Ranney)

The EDM is divided in a step- cut manner at its normal insertion, and the remaining strip is sutured to the EDC of the little finger—that is, the Ranney transfer. The EDM tendon is withdrawn into a dorsal incision in the forearm and is tunneled into the flexor compartment of it between the APL and the FCR. The EDM tendon is then passed subcutaneously to the neck of the fifth metacarpal where it is attached. Omer reported that a similar effect can be produced by looping the FDS of the little finger around the DTML of the fourth intermetacarpal space.

#### Palande’s Modification of Brand’s Flexor Route Transfer of ECRL

Brand’s flexor ECRL transfer for the correction of finger clawing is modified by creating five, instead of four, tails to the tendon/fascia lata graft, as modified by Palande. Instead of attaching the tails to the lateral bands of the fingers, a transverse incision is made in the distal palmar crease to expose the hypothenar, interosseous, and lumbrical muscles. The thick fascia overlying the interossei tendons in each of the three interdigital spaces is excised, and a nylon suture is passed around each pair (one palmar, one dorsal) of interossei tendons that are tied together. The five slips of the tendon graft are tunneled dorsally, the lateral four along the lumbricals, and the medial one in a similar plane. With the wrist in neutral, the MCP joints in 55 degrees of flexion, the IP joints extended, and the little finger CMC joint flexed, the medial tail is sutured to the hypothenar muscle- tendon junction. After that, the middle three are looped around and sutured to each of the tied pairs of interossei tendons, and the lateral tail is sutured to the first dorsal interosseous muscle/tendon.

Palande found that this procedure produced equivalent results to the original Brand procedure for the correction of finger clawing and had a reduced rate of hyperextension deformity at the PIP joints.In addition, it produced good restoration of the transverse metacarpal arch in 15 of 16 hands affected by leprosy and increased grip strength from an average of 12 to 15 kg.

### Correction of Little Finger Abduction Deformity

The EDM, as well as the ADM, can abduct the little finger through its indirect insertion into the abductor tubercle on the proximal phalanx. The third palmar interosseous counters this effect in normal hands but, along with ADM, is paralyzed in ulnar nerve palsy, leaving the EDM unopposed (i.e., Wartenberg sign).

#### Split EDM Transfer

The ulnar half of the EDM tendon is detached from the extensor hood of the little finger at the MCP joint and retrieved through an incision just distal to the extensor retinaculum. A palmar incision that extends obliquely from the distal–palmar crease to the proximal–digital crease is made to expose the DTML and the flexor sheath of the little finger (Fig. 31.27). The EDM tendon is passed through the fourth intermetacarpal space into the palm. If the little finger is not in a claw position, the tendon slip is sutured onto the insertion of its MCP radial collateral ligament on the proximal phalanx. If the little finger is clawed as well as abducted, the tendon slip is inserted onto a radially based flap of the flexor tendon sheath just distal to the A1 pulley. For tensioning, the wrist is held in neutral and the MCP joint is in 20 degrees of flexion. The ring and little finger MCP joints are splinted in flexion for 4 weeks with the wrist extended, but the IP joints are left free, and motion is encouraged to prevent adhesion formation around the flexor tendons.

A proximally based 3- to 4- cm strip of tendon is created from the central third of the ring finger extensor tendon just proximal to the MCP joint using the Voche and Merle procedure. The ulnar and radial portions of the ring finger extensor tendon are then sutured together, and the central tendon strip is passed palmar to the DTML and sutured either to the insertion of the radial collateral ligament of the MCP joint on the proximal phalanx of the little finger or to the radial aspect of the extensor hood of this digit. It is imperative that the tendon slip has sufficient length because a short slip will invariably loosen the extensor mechanism of the ring finger. The transfer is tensioned such that the little finger is not abducted when the fingers are extended, and it has 20 degrees less flexion than the ring finger when the finger MCP joints are flexed. The hand is immobilized in a splint that holds the wrist in extension and the ring and little fingers’ MCP joints flexed with their IP joints extended.

### High Ulnar Nerve Palsy

#### Restoration of Ring and Little Finger Flexion and Strength

In a high ulnar nerve palsy the FDP muscles for the ring and little fingers are paralyzed so that there is no active flexion at their DIP joints. The patient is thus reliant on FDS function, which may be rudimentary or absent in the little finger, for MCP and PIP flexion.

**Little and ring ﬁnger FDP suture to the middle ﬁnger FDP.** The profundus tendons of the ring and little fingers can be sutured to the profundus tendon of the middle finger in the forearm, leaving the index profundus free for individual function (Fig. 31.28). This will not increase overall grip strength of the hand, and an alternative option is tenodeses of the ring and little finger DIP joints using their paralyzed FDP tendons. Transfer of the FCR to the FCU can also be performed to restore strong flexion and ulnar deviation of the wrist.

Brand did not consider that loss of the FCU or the ring and little fingers’ FDP caused a significant functional problem and advised against tendon transfers to restore these functions. He usually treated isolated high ulnar nerve lesions in the same manner as low lesions, with the exception that an FDS tendon must *never* be harvested for use in tendon transfer from a finger with weak or absent profundus function.

Anderson, who has vast experience with the management of leprosy, deems it unwise to correct intrinsic muscle paralysis before restoration of adequate extrinsic flexion power; this is because the patient will find it difficult to close the hand fully and will continue to have a weak grip despite claw correction. He recommends a side- to- side suture of the middle FDP to the ring and little FDP in the distal forearm, but he warns the patient that this operation probably will exaggerate the claw deformity. The hand is immobilized for 3 weeks, after which muscle strengthening exercises are performed for 4 weeks. Care should be taken to prevent IP joint contractures before performing transfers for claw deformity.

#### Restoration of Sensibility

Loss of sensibility on the ulnar border of the hand and loss of proprioception in the little finger can produce significant functional limitations. For patients with leprosy, successful medical treatment does not restore sensation and insensate digits continue to be a problem for the rest of their lives. Repeated ulceration at the tips of the digits can lead to absorption and shortening despite the success of tendon transfers to correct the claw deformity.

**Digital nerve transfer.** Stocks et al. transferred the functioning median- supplied ulnar digital nerve of the middle finger to the nonfunctioning ulnar digital nerve of the little finger and reported that 85% of their 13 patients regained sensibility levels of S3+ or S4. Others (e.g., Ozkan et al.) also have observed success with this technique that may be beneficial to patients who present late after ulnar nerve injuries and those with trophic changes; however, clinically evident tissue loss precludes attempts at reinnervation (see also distal nerve transfers in Chapters 28 and 30).

Anderson transferred a neurovascular cutaneous island flap from the ulnar side of the pulp of the middle finger to the pulp of the little finger in patients with chronic ulnar nerve injury due to trauma or burns.

### Additional Issues

#### Wasted Intermetacarpal Spaces

Severe intermetacarpal atrophy can be quite disfiguring and may represent a barrier to social reintegration with leprosy. Dermal grafts can mask interosseous wasting and are most successfully performed between the thumb and index metacarpals. The patient must be informed that this “cosmetic” procedure is not always successful in the long term because the transplanted tissue may become atrophic and resorb with time.

#### Early Tendon Transfers (Internal Splints) for Nerve Injuries

Burkhalter introduced the concept of early tendon transfers after nerve repair to prevent the development of deformities and contractures, improve coordination of residual muscle- tendon units, and stimulate sensory reeducation during nerve recovery. The transfers function as internal splints for the paralyzed muscles and, in the event of a failure of useful nerve regeneration, provide a permanent solution.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ISOLATED ULNAR NERVE PALSY

The hand is a most complex structure, with 38 muscles working in a coordinated fashion to control its motion. Transfer of two or three tendons cannot be expected to restore the fine function of the many intrinsic muscles whose function is lost with a complete ulnar nerve lesion. It thus must be stressed to the patient that the aim of surgery for ulnar nerve palsy is to improve, rather than restore, normal function. Improvement in function by tendon transfer, tenodesis, joint capsule reefing, or joint fusion must produce a benefit that outweighs (1) the permanent loss of any donor muscle’s function or loss of joint mobility, (2) the inconvenience and loss of income due to the temporary loss of hand function due to the surgery and postoperative rehabilitation with splintage, and (3) the risk of complications and failure.

The loss of sensation is not as devastating in ulnar nerve palsy as in median nerve palsy, and the loss of motor function in a low lesion primarily involves fine dexterity and grip strength. However, much function is retained, and this led one of my late senior orthopedic colleagues to comment: “The ulnar nerve is a luxury to the manual worker.” Although I would not endorse this sweeping statement, it contains some truth with regard to my practice in the United Kingdom, where injury at the wrist is the most common cause of ulnar nerve palsy.

Ulnar nerve injury at the wrist in the United Kingdom often occurs in men with thickset hands and MCP joints that do not naturally hyperextend, such that an unsightly claw deformity does not develop. Such patients often adapt to the loss of ulnar nerve function and the consequent grip and pinch weakness, altered rhythm of finger flexion, and loss of dexterity by favoring their other hand for tasks they have difficulty performing with the injured one. Many decline reconstructive surgery when the option is discussed openly and honestly and it is stressed (1) that its aim is to improve specific functions rather than restore normal function and (2) that surgery and postoperative management will necessitate considerable time off work. This is because some are afraid of losing their employment if they take a prolonged absence from work and many, while awaiting ulnar nerve recovery after primary repair of the nerve injury, realize that they are capable, with adaption, of their normal activities and work even if there is little or no recovery of nerve function. With other patients, discussion of their objective for having surgery reveals that improved hand strength is the primary aim.

It is my belief, perhaps wrongly, that none of the tendon transfers used to restore finger intrinsic muscle function and correct finger clawing provides significant improvements in hand grip strength. I wonder whether some of the strength improvement reported after such surgery might be a result of intensive rehabilitation effort after the surgery rather than the surgery itself. Therefore I do not encourage surgery to restore finger intrinsic function as a means to improve grip strength. I also feel that transfers or fusions to improve thumb strength are unlikely to restore more than 50% of normal thumb strength, and I am thus cautious when discussing these procedures.

It must be stressed that my views are based on my clinical practice, and that the patients I see probably have completely different requirements and aims to those with leprosy who may have supple hands with PIP joints that naturally hyperextend. In such patients, severe claw deformities frequently occur, and this may not only cause significant loss of function but also carry a social stigma. For some patients the hand disability may be both of a physical and a mental nature as observed by Brand, who stressed the importance of the surgeon looking and examining patients’ hands with respect in order to build a bond of trust between the surgeon and the patient.

Although restoration of function is my main objective for surgery for finger clawing, I suspect that for many patients the esthetic appearance is often of great concern and that this is sometimes concealed from me. Esthetic improvement may be an acceptable indication for surgery for patients with severe claw deformity who are conscious of its appearance, provided they are fully aware of the risk of failure and complications and have the necessary commitment to the surgery and its rehabilitation process.

For correction of clawing and improvement in the pattern of movement of the fingers, I favor, when available, using the ring finger FDS as the motor and split its tendon into two slips that are attached around the A1 pulleys of the ring and little fingers. Rightly or wrongly I only perform transfers to the fingers that are clawed at rest, thus do not usually address the index and middle fingers. I find this the most straightforward transfer for correction of clawing and improving the dynamics of finger motion, but I have encountered some troublesome deformities in the donor ring finger. Hyperextension deformity is probably rarer in stiffer Caucasian hands than supple Asian hands, and I harvest the FDS through a Bruner incision at the level of the PIP joint, which may reduce the risk of donor complications. I have found insertion onto the lateral bands complicated and favor insertion onto the A1 pulleys, especially as my patients almost invariably have PIP joints that extend fully when MCP joint hyperextension is blocked. For further detail on the specific indications and surgical technical points regarding the subtle differences between available transfers and tendon insertions, I refer the reader to the excellent works of Brand, Burkhalter, Omer, and others that are included in the reference list for this chapter.

For the thumb, I think that many patients do not need an adductorplasty because, though reduced, they still have sufficient pinch strength for their everyday activities. If, however, a modest increase in thumb pinch strength is the aim of surgery, I would utilize the ECRB transfer, as modified by Omer, and stress to the patient that this probably will only restore approximately 50% of normal thumb strength. In other cases, I favor fusion of the MCP joint to fusion of the IP joint to allow pinch between the thumb pulp and the fingers; however, this is only offered if the “tip” thumb pinch is causing functional disability. I believe that fusion of the IP joint reduces the patient’s ability to roll objects between the thumb and fingers much more than fusion of the MCP joint. I have not used the split FPL tenodesis in this situation, but Scott Wolfe, the editor of this section of this book, finds that its use in combination with an ECRB adductor- plasty provides strong lateral pinch and a much more functional hand for patients with C8/T1 brachial plexus injuries.

I have no experience of tendon transfers to restore the concave transverse metacarpal arch nor do my patients find small finger abduction sufficiently troublesome to require tendon transfer.

#### Patient Counseling

Before embarking on surgery for ulnar, as well as median, nerve palsy, I firmly believe that the potential benefits and drawbacks of the proposed surgery should be honestly discussed with the patient, who should be given time to reflect and recognize that surgery cannot restore normality. Both the surgeon and the patient must clearly understand the functional aims of the patient, and the surgeon should perform the simplest procedure possible to fulfill these aims. Many doyens of tendon transfer surgery have stressed the need for simple surgery, and I firmly believe that the less complicated the surgery, the simpler the subsequent rehabilitation and the greater the chance of a satisfied patient. Complexity invites failure.

## COMBINED MEDIAN AND ULNAR NERVE PALSIES

Combined “low” injuries to the median and ulnar nerves are severe injuries that result in total loss of sensation to the palmar surface of the hand and complete loss of intrinsic muscle function; there is no chance of preservation of any intrinsic function through anomalous nerve interconnections (e.g., the Martin- Gruber anastomosis). The situation is even worse in combined “high” injuries because of the additional loss of all extrinsic wrist, thumb, and finger flexor function. In addition, such injuries, if due to trauma, are frequently accompanied by severe associated soft tissue, bony, vascular, and skin coverage issues or infection, which further complicates management and requires an individualized approach. The management of these combined palsies is complex. Omer, in an earlier edition of this book, described possible procedures to improve hand function in these cases; they are summarized in Tables 31.7 and 31.8.

## RADIAL NERVE PALSY

Loss of radial nerve function in the hand creates a profound disability. This is predominantly a result of loss of active wrist extension and the consequent awkward flexed position of the wrist that does not allow a powerful grip. The loss of finger and thumb extension also causes difficulty with grasping objects and restricts interosseous muscle function with a loss of active finger abduction. Tendon transfers to restore function in radial nerve palsy are the best and the most predictable in the upper extremity; however, as pointed out by Riordan: “There is very little hope that errors in technique can be overcome by local adaptation. The success or failure of an operation depends on the technical competence of the operator and his painstaking after- care.” Riordan also noted that “there is usually only one chance to obtain good restoration of function in such a paralyzed hand.”

Most injuries of the radial nerve occur distal to the branches to the triceps in the upper arm, and loss of triceps function in higher radial nerve palsies is usually compensated for by gravity passively extending the elbow unless the arm is held above shoulder height. For this reason, transfers to restore triceps function are not included in this chapter (see Chapters 32 and 33).

Neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage- Turner syndrome) can cause an isolated palsy of the posterior interosseous branch of the radial nerve. Other causes include inflammatory arthritis, iatrogenic injury, spaceoccupying lesions (ganglion/lipoma), and maybe posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) compression at the arcade of Fröhse or supinator. With this condition, wrist extension, including the inevitable associated radial deviation, is retained as the radial nerve innervates the ECRL before it divides into its two terminal branches: the posterior interosseous (motor) and superficial (sensory) branches. Additionally, the ECRB receives its innervation in most limbs (58%) from the superficial radial nerve rather than from the PIN.

### ANATOMY

The radial nerve arises from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus and passes dorsally through the inferior triangular space (bordered by the shaft of the humerus, the triceps muscle, and the teres major) before passing obliquely around the shaft of the humerus, following the spiral groove. Here it is at risk of injury from humeral shaft fractures. It then passes distally between the brachialis and BR muscles into the cubital fossa where it splits into its superficial branch (purely sensory) and the deep motor branch. The deep motor branch (PIN) passes between the superficial and deep heads of supinator and splays out into multiple thin branches as it emerges from the supinator about 8 cm distal to the elbow joint. Spinner has likened this to the cauda equina, and repair of the individual branches is difficult. Difficulty in repairing untidy injuries at this level often has an important influence on the timing of tendon transfers. Abrams and associates produced the best reference on the innervation order and location of motor branches of the radial nerve (Box 31.1). Although they found the order of innervation variable, knowledge of the general patterns and variations is important as it allows the surgeon to follow recovery after radial nerve injuries.

A surgeon who plans to perform transfers for radial nerve palsy must have a comprehensive three- dimensional understanding of the anatomy of the flexor and extensor muscles of the forearm. This is a complex area of anatomy that is difficult to master, so continual review is mandatory.

### Requirements for a Patient With Radial Nerve Palsy

A patient with an irreparable radial nerve palsy needs to be provided with (1) wrist extension, (2) finger (MCP joint) extension, and (3) a combination of thumb extension and abduction. The motors available for transfer for a patient with isolated radial nerve palsy include all the extrinsic muscles innervated by the median and ulnar nerves. This provides the surgeon with an almost limitless number of possible combinations for transfers. However, one or two combinations of transfers are used by the majority of surgeons in most instances. Unless a patient has a painful neuroma, the sensory part of the radial nerve usually can be ignored because loss of sensibility in its territory (a variably sized area of skin on the dorsoradial aspect of the hand) does not cause functional disability unless associated with neuropathic pain.

### Nonoperative Treatment

Nonoperative management of the hand is important while waiting for (1) recovery of radial nerve function from either a presumed axonotmetic nerve injury or after nerve repair or (2) the hand and arm to recover from associated soft tissue injuries after an irreparable radial nerve injury. The most important aspects of nonoperative management are maintenance of full passive range of motion in all joints of the wrist and hand and prevention of contractures, including contracture of the thumb–index web. For most patients, the constant supervision of a therapist is not required, but the patient must be taught very soon after the original nerve injury how to perform an appropriate exercise program to keep the joints supple. It is the patient’s responsibility to do the exercises, and the role of the therapist at this point is to teach the patient and to monitor progress to ensure that the exercise program is performed correctly.

Many types of splints have been described for patients with radial nerve palsy. Some incorporate some form of dynamic outriggers to extend the fingers and thumb with elastic traction while allowing full active flexion. These can make dressing awkward and require careful selection of clothing. In my opinion, few, if any, patients need or want such elaborate splinting that can interfere with, rather than improve, function. Patients’ individual needs should dictate the type of splint, but most find an “off- the- shelf” inconspicuous $10 cock- up wrist splint, which stabilizes the wrist and restores adequate, and often remarkably good, function without interfering with clothing. Such a splint often substantially increases grip strength.

A person who does data entry might find this more manageable with a dynamic finger and thumb extension splint but not with a standard wrist splint (Fig. 31.29), whereas an insurance salesperson who is more concerned about appearance would probably be content with a small, inconspicuous volar cock- up wrist splint. If a wrist splint is worn only during the day, then Brand recommended that a splint to hold the wrist and fingers in extension is worn at night. He was concerned that the disturbed balance of the wrist may result in loss of fiber length of the flexor muscles, making it more difficult to achieve normal balance after the final nerve recovery or tendon transfer operation.

### Early Transfers (Internal Splint)

Burkhalter believed that the greatest functional loss after radial nerve injury is weakness of power grip as a result of loss of wrist extension. Consequently, he was a strong advocate of early PT- to- ECRB transfer. This eliminates the need for an external splint and significantly improves power grip and hand function. He suggested that the transfer be done at the same time as radial nerve repair or as soon as possible thereafter. The tendon juncture is done end- to- side so that the ECRB remains in continuity and can function in its own right as a wrist extensor, should successful reinnervation occur. This author has no experience with “early” transfer and would be cautious of its use, except in cases in which there is little or no hope of useful recovery of radial nerve function and “tissue equilibrium” in the operative field, and the patient is enthusiastic for the procedure. It may have more value in practices that encounter a different spectrum of radial nerve palsies.

### Operative Treatment

#### Nerve Repair and Graft Versus Tendon Transfers

The first decision to make for a patient who has an established radial nerve palsy is whether to attempt a late repair of the nerve or to restore lost function with tendon transfers. The time since injury is a critical factor, but late repair of the radial nerve can produce reasonably good results at least in part because the nerve is almost entirely motor and the paralyzed muscles are often reasonably close to the site of injury. However, if the prognosis for recovery of function after nerve repair is poor, it would seem prudent to proceed directly to tendon transfers and avoid subjecting the patient to an additional 4 to 6 months of waiting for function to return before resorting to tendon transfers.

When attempting late repair, the surgeon must be prepared to do nerve grafts if the gap is too great to overcome by direct epineurial repair without tension. Several authors have shown reasonable results with belated grafting of the radial nerve. Samardzić and colleaguesgrafted gaps of 2 to 10 cm, but Kallio and coworkers concluded that results were better if the grafts were <5 cm (see Chapter 30).

#### Timing of Tendon Transfers

Brown advised early transfers to restore wrist, finger, and thumb extension when there was a poor prognosis for repair of the radial nerve injury. He advised ignoring the nerve and proceeding directly to the tendon transfers if there was a nerve defect of more than 4 cm, a large wound or extensive scarring, or skin loss over the nerve. However, if a good repair of the nerve is achieved, most would wait several months (at least 5 or 6 months after injury in the middle third of the upper arm) to allow nerve regeneration to occur. They would only proceed to tendon transfers if it was clear that inadequate muscle reinnervation had occurred by both clinical and electrodiagnostic criteria.

Bevin suggested a policy of never repairing the radial nerve and proceeding directly to tendon transfers. This reduced the period of disability after injury to 8 weeks (7.5 months after nerve repair); however, most would agree that the results of radial nerve repair are sufficiently good to warrant routine repair in most cases, especially for younger patients.

There does not seem to be any time limit as to how long a delay can be tolerated before transfers are done after nerve injury. Brodman reported successful transfers 24 years after radial nerve injury, despite what he described as “gelatinous degeneration” (i.e., translucent appearance) of the paralyzed tendons at the time of operation.

#### Historical Review

World War I and World War II enabled a few individuals to accumulate a lifetime of experience in a very short period, and most of the articles that established the fundamentals of transfers for radial nerve palsy were published in the immediate postwar years.

Jones is credited with being the major innovator of radial nerve transfers, and the “classic” Jones transfer has been quoted and misquoted many times, partly because he described at least two slightly different combinations of transfers (Box 31.2). Although Jones transfers became popular, the only part of them that has been universally accepted is the use of the PT to provide active wrist extension. A few authors have favored wrist arthrodesis, but most believe that it is important to maintain wrist motion. The only current controversy regarding the pronator transfer centers on the optimal insertion of the PT.

Major disagreement arises concerning the optimal method of restoring finger extension and thumb extension and abduction. Jones used both the FCU and FCR, and in 1922 Starr was the first to transfer the PL and the FCR, leaving the FCU intact. Zachary convincingly illustrated the benefit of leaving one wrist flexor intact. In 1996, Gaur and Swarup used the FCR and the FCU but reaffirmed the principle that improved functional results were achieved if the FCU was spared to maintain flexion and ulnar deviation of the wrist. However, use of both the FCU and FCR can improve hand function in exceptional cases with restricted potential donors for transfer. Scuderi refined the PL to rerouted EPL transfer, emphasizing the important principle that function is better when the transfer is to only one recipient tendon.The results of these and other studies gradually evolved into what has been referred to by some as the “standard” set of tendon transfers for radial nerve palsy:
-  PT to ECRB
-  FCU to EDC—index finger (II), small finger (V)
-  PL to rerouted EPL

In France, a slightly modified version of this set (i.e., FCU to EDC and EPL; PL to EPB and APL) is known as the Merle d’Aubigné procedure.

There is still disagreement on the best combination of transfers. In 1960 Boyes advocated using the Starr FCR transfer instead of the FCU to restore finger extension. His reasoning was that the FCU is a more important wrist flexor than the FCR because the normal axis of wrist motion is from dorsal- radial to volar- ulnar. Support for this concept was provided by Brand’s biomechanical and clinical studies that led him to believe that the FCU should not be used as a tendon transfer for two reasons: (1) the FCU is too strong and its excursion is too short for transfer to the finger extensors, and (2) its function as the prime ulnar stabilizer of the wrist is too important to sacrifice. Despite these theoretical concerns, studies have shown no functional loss of power grip with the FCU set.

Another reason for not using the FCU (or, for that matter, the FCR) set of transfers is that it cannot provide simultaneous wrist and finger extension. Because the amplitude of the wrist flexors is only 33 mm and the amplitude of the finger extensors is 50 mm, full active extension of the fingers with an FCU or FCR transfer can be achieved only by simultaneous flexion of the wrist, relying on the tenodesis effect of the transfer (Fig. 31.30). Boyes therefore suggested that the superficialis (sublimis) muscles, with their greater excursion (70 mm), would be better motors for finger extensors and devised a combination of transfers that also provided more independent control of the thumb and index finger:
-  PT to ECRL and ECRB
-  FCR to EPB and APL
-  FDS long finger (III) to EDC (via interosseous membrane)
-  FDS ring finger (IV) to EPL and EIP (via interosseous mem-

For a patient with PIN palsy, the PT transfer is unnecessary to restore wrist extension. In addition, an isolated PIN palsy provides perhaps the most compelling indication for the FCR transfer; static radial deviation postural deformity may occur when the only remaining ulnar deviator is transferred.

**Flexor carpi ulnaris transfer.** The first incision in FCU transfer (Fig. 31.31) is directed longitudinally over the FCU in the distal half of the forearm. Its distal end is “J”- shaped, with the transverse extension being long enough to reach the PL tendon. The FCU tendon is transected just proximal to the pisiform and freed up as far proximally as the incision allows. Separation of this muscle from its particularly dense fascial attachments may require the surgeon to extend the first incision more proximally. The muscle belly of the FCU is very long, extending usually to within a few centimeters of the insertion of the tendon on the pisiform. Many facilitate the transfer of this tendon by excising the muscle belly from the distal half of the tendon, which also makes the muscle less prominent as it passes around the ulnar border of the forearm.

The second incision begins 4 cm below the medial epicondyle and angles across the dorsum of the proximal forearm, aimed directly toward the Lister tubercle. The deep fascia overlying the FCU muscle belly is excised, and the remaining fascial attachments to the muscle are incised. It is imperative that the FCU be completely freed up so that its entire muscle belly and tendon can be transferred into the proximal wound to redirect the muscle. The limiting factor in the dissection is the innervation of the FCU, which enters the muscle in its proximal 4 cm, so the dissection must not extend this far proximally.

The third incision begins on the palmar- radial aspect of the midforearm, passes dorsally around the radial border of the forearm in the region of the insertion of the PT, and then angles back radially on the back on the dorsum of the distal forearm toward the Lister tubercle. The tendon of the PT is identified in the palmar aspect of the wound and followed to its insertion on the radius. It is vital that the PT tendon be elongated by freeing up its insertion with an attached long strip of periosteum. This ensures that it has sufficient length to create a strong tendon juncture (Fig. 31.32). Tubiana and colleagues emphasized that the PT muscle- tendon unit must be freed up proximally to divide adhesions to improve subsequent excursion. The PT muscle and tendon are passed subcutaneously around the radial border of the forearm, superficial to the BR and ECRL, to be inserted into the ECRB just distal to its musculotendinous junction.

A variation in technique recommended by Moberg and Nachemson is to open the dorsal retinaculum “to prevent ischemic necrosis of the tendons secondary to postoperative edema.” This author is unaware of this being a common problem, but their modification does provide a straighter line of pull.

At this point, I release the tourniquet, establish hemostasis, and close the first and second incisions. Setting the proper tension in the transfers is a tricky task, but it is crucial to the outcome of the operation. It is difficult to describe precisely how to adjust tension in tendon transfers, and a certain amount of experience is essential to be able to “feel” the proper tension. Generally, one should err on the side of suturing extensor tendon transfers too tight rather than too loose because the extensors tend to stretch over time.

The tendons must be tight enough to provide full extension of the wrist, fingers, and thumb, yet not so tight as to limit flexion of the digits. Green sutured the PT to ECRB ( *not* including the ECRL) just distal to the musculotendinous junction. The long tongue of periosteum at the end of the PT tendon is woven through the tendon of the ECRB as a Pulvertaft weave and secured with multiple 3- 0 or 4- 0 nonabsorbable sutures. Even if a tongue of periosteum has been harvested with the PT insertion, this does not always provide a secure tendon juncture, in which case it can be reinforced with a short strip of free tendon graft taken from the ECRL. I believe the periosteal strip aids weaving of PT into ECRB but has insufficient strength for the pull of the transfer, such that it is imperative that tendon (not just periosteum) be woven into the tendon. If this cannot be achieved, then I believe a graft is mandatory. The transfer is sutured with the PT in maximum tension and the wrist in at least 45 degrees of extension.

The FCU transfer is then sutured. Omer weaved the FCU tendon end- to- side through the EDC tendons at a 45- degree angle just proximal to the dorsal retinaculum (see Fig. 31.33). Moberg and Nachemson suggested that better results can be achieved if 4 to 5 cm of inactivated EDC muscle tendon is resected just proximal to the intended site of suture, allowing an end–end juncture and a more direct line of pull. Most authors do not include the EDM in the transfer so as not to create excessive tension in the small finger.

The tension must be tested by passively moving the wrist to show the synergistic action of the new transfer. With the wrist in extension, it should be possible to easily flex the fingers completely into the palm, and with the wrist in flexion, the MCP joints should pull into full extension but not hyperextend. The third incision is closed while an assistant stabilizes the position of the wrist and hand to protect the transfers. Closing of all three wounds with subcuticular sutures avoids unattractive crosshatches in the scars.

Postoperative management. In the operating room, a long arm splint is applied that immobilizes the forearm in 15 to 30 degrees of pronation, the wrist in approximately 45 degrees of extension, the MCP joints in slight (10–15 degrees) flexion, and the thumb in maximum extension and abduction. The PIP joints of the fingers are left free. Because limited elbow motion does not cause undue tension on the suture lines, a single sugar tong splint is a satisfactory alternative to a long arm splint. The splint and sutures are removed at 10 to 14 days, and a further long-arm cast is applied in the same position as noted previously. The cast is removed 4 weeks postoperatively and a short arm splint is applied to hold the wrist, fingers, and thumb in extension. This should be worn for 2 weeks, though it is removed intermittently for exercise.

A planned exercise program, begun at week 4, under the guidance of an experienced hand therapist, is very beneficial. A wellmotivated patient should have good control of function by 3 months, although many patients need 6 months to reach maximum recovery.

Potential problemsExcessive radial deviation after FCU harvest. Removing the FCU (an important wrist flexor and the only remaining ulnar deviator after radial nerve injury) from the wrist may contribute to radial deviation of the hand. This condition is likely to be aggravated further if the PT is inserted into the ECRL, which is a strong radial deviator. Even if the transfer is into the more centrally located ECRB, there may be some radial deviation due to the insertion of this tendon or intercommunication between it and the ECRL. Radial deviation is particularly severe in patients with PIN palsy who have a normally functioning, strong ECRL; removal of the FCU of these patients can seriously aggravate the radial deviation problem. Several solutions to the problem have been suggested.

*Avoidance.* Green considered significant preoperative radial deviation or a patient with PIN palsy to be a contraindication to FCU transfer.

*Alter the insertion.* If the FCU transfer must be used, it is possible to reduce the tendency to radial deviation by altering the insertions of the radial wrist extensors at the time of PT transfer. Such surgery increases the complexity of the procedure, increases the number of tendon junctures, and carries the risk of increased morbidity and failure, especially when performed only occasionally. The “simplest” way to do this is to resect the distal 2 to 3 cm of the ECRL tendon and suture the tendon more proximally into the adjacent ECRB, eliminating any possibility of pull through the ECRL insertion.

A more radical approach is to transfer the distal end of the ECRB into the tendon of the ECU or to cut the ECU at its musculotendinous junction, transpose the proximal end across the forearm, and include it in the PT- to- ECRB transfer. Brand did not favor this approach because the ECU has a very small moment for extension, and such an altered insertion would limit the total extension moment of the PT. He preferred to attach the PT to the ECRL and ECRB together proximally and then detach the insertion of ECRL and reinsert it into the base of the fourth metacarpal. Tubiana and colleagues also preferred to centralize the insertion of the ECRL.In addition to the different moment arms of ECRL and ECRB for wrist extension and radial deviation, they were also concerned about adhesions between the two tendons. They sutured the PT to the ECRB only, but also rerouted the insertion of the ECRL. The ECRL tendon is divided at its insertion into the base of the second metacarpal, freed up proximally to its musculotendinous junction, rerouted beneath the dorsal retinaculum in the fourth (EDC) compartment, and fixed to the base of the third and fourth metacarpals with sutures and staples.

Absence of the palmaris longus. In this situation, several authors have suggested including the EPL into the FCU- to- EDC transfer, although this approach significantly limits thumb abduction. Bevin suggested that including all of the thumb extrinsics (i.e., EPL, EPB, and APL) in the FCU- to- EDC transfer seriously violates the “one tendon–one function” principle. However, patients with this and similar transfers retained some independent thumb and finger function, such that they could actively flex their finger MCP joints while holding the thumb in extension and radial abduction. This apparent example of independent dual actions of a transfer may be due to greater tension in the contracting FDP and finger intrinsic muscles than in the relaxed FPL tendon altering the line of pull of the transfer.

Milford advocated the use of the BR, which is possible only in PIN palsy. If the BR is used, its muscle belly must be extensively freed up to augment its excursion. Beasley commented that the BR is difficult to reeducate during the rehabilitation program. Tsuge and Adachi substituted the FDS III or IV for an absent PL.

**Superﬁcialis transfer (Boyes).** A long incision is made on the palmar- radial aspect of the midforearm, and the tendons of the PT, ECRL, and ECRB are exposed using the procedure described by Boyes. The PT is harvested and transferred to the ECRB as discussed earlier (see page 1229). The superficialis (sublimis) tendons of the long and ring fingers are exposed through a transverse incision in the distal palm or through separate transverse incisions at the base of each finger. The tendons are divided proximal to the chiasma, freed up, and delivered into the forearm wound. Two 1 cm × 2 cm windows are created in the interosseous membrane, just proximal to the pronator quadratus, one on each side of the anterior interosseous artery. Care should be taken to protect the anterior and posterior interosseous vessels.

Numerous authors, including Adams and Wood and Beasley recommend that the muscle bellies of the transferred muscles be passed through the interosseous space to minimize adhesions; this may necessitate larger openings in the interosseous membrane. Others prefer to route the superficialis tendons around the radial and ulnar borders of the forearm in an effort to avoid tendon adherence, although ulnar nerve compression can occur when the FDS is transferred ulnarly. Passing the transfer deep to the ulnar neurovascular bundle and immediately superficial to the periosteum of the ulna avoids this complication.

A J- shaped incision is made on the dorsum of the distal forearm: The transverse limb runs from the radial styloid to the ulnar styloid, and the longitudinal limb extends proximally along the ulna. The FDS III is routed between the FDP and FPL, and the FDS IV is routed to the ulnar side of the profundus muscle mass. Both are then passed through the openings in the interosseous membrane into the dorsum of the forearm. Care must be taken to avoid kinking of the median nerve on a band of fascia on the superficialis muscle belly. The FDS III is interwoven, “end- to- side,” into the tendons of the EIP and EPL, and the FDS IV is interwoven, again “end- to- side,” into the EDC. The EDM is not included. The suture lines are placed proximal to the dorsal retinaculum, which should be excised over the proximal parts of the third and fourth compartments if there is danger of impingement by the tendon junctures.

The tension is set with an assistant clenching the patient’s fingers and thumb into a fist and bringing the wrist into 20 degrees of extension. This position is maintained until all the transferred tendons are attached to their new insertions under “considerable tension.”

Finally, the FCR tendon is exposed through a transverse incision at the base of the thumb, divided and freed up sufficiently to allow it to pass in a straight line to APL where it is woven into, and sutured to, the multiple slips of this tendon. The original description of Boyes included an additional insertion into the EPB, but this contravenes the “one tendon–one function” rule.

It is important to deflate the tourniquet before wound closure because of possible damage to the interosseous vessels and potential serious bleeding.

**Single- tendon transfer.** Gousheh and Aratesh described an isolated transfer of the FCU for radial nerve palsy to the EDC, EIP, and EPL, without an additional PL- to- ECRB transfer as a simple alternative to multiple tendon transfers in 108 patients with radial nerve palsy. The authors cited the simplicity of the procedure, reduced operating time, and quite adequate hand function despite incomplete restoration of wrist extension. More recently Sharma and coworkers reported the use of a single- split FCU transfer in 25 cases, of which 13 were effectively posterior interosseous palsies with preserved wrist extension, but 12 had higher injuries and had lost wrist extension in addition to finger and thumb extension. They divided the FCU at its insertion on the pisiform bone and split the tendon and muscle into two equal halves down to 6 cm distal to the medial epicondyle. The radial portion was joined to the EPL (rerouted) and the ulnar half to the EDC, EIP, and EDM. They reported 68% achieved an excellent or good result, with no poor results (Bincaz score) but the range of extension was only 0 degrees to 29 degrees in all the 12 cases with paralysis of all their wrist extensors. The strength of active extension was not reported, and it is probable that better extension would have been achieved with the addition of a PL- to- ECRB transfer for wrist extension.

**Outcome of radial transfers.** A retrospective assessment of 22 isolated radial nerve transfers at a mean follow- up of 6.3 years demonstrated that the majority of patients were capable of working, though 6 or 7 were unable to resume their previous intense manual employment. Weakness of power grip and wrist strength were noted, but it was felt that 21 of the 22 had achieved improved function. Altintas et al. assessed 77 patients 60 months after transfers using the FCR muscle to restore finger extension in 19 cases and the FCU muscle in 56 cases; 44- degree wrist extension and finger extension to 5 degrees were regained. Power grip was reduced to 49% and pinch grip was reduced to 28%. The mean DASH score was 15 ± 9, indicating good function, and 89% of patients remained employed.

Moussavi et al. compared the use of FCR, FCU, or FDS III + IV for restoration of finger extension in 41 Iranian patients with isolated radial nerve palsies. They found no difference in the outcomes of these three procedures, except that all three patients who were able to extend their fingers and wrist at the same time had undergone FDS transfers. They reported mean DASH scores of 30 to 38 for the three treatment groups and that 30 of their 41 patients were able to return to work without difficulty.

Latheef et al. studied the outcomes of 11 FCU “triple” transfers (PTECRB; FCU-EDC; PL- EPL) at follow- up of more than 6 months.Compared with the contralateral side there was 58% grip strength, 42% wrist extension strength, and 43% and 51% wrist extension and flexion, respectively. The reported mean DASH score was 3.5, and no patient had changed hand dominance or changed employment.

Dabas et al. assessed the objective outcomes of 10 patients who underwent an early transfer of PT- to- ECRB as treatment of a high radial nerve palsy. At 6- month follow- up there were median increases of 48% in power grip, 162% in tip pinch, 90% in key pinch, and 98% in palmar pinch from the preoperative values; however, decreased palmar flexion was seen in four patients. There was one unsatisfactory result. The authors advised early tendon transfer to quickly improve hand function and avoid the need for splintage while awaiting reinnervation of wrist extensors.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: RADIAL NERVE TENDON TRANSFERS

Most of my patients find that a simple off- the- shelf cock- up splint, which stabilizes the wrist in neutral but does not provide finger or thumb extension, improves hand function considerably while awaiting radial nerve recovery or tendon transfers. I thus do not routinely provide more complex splints.

I very much recommend tendon transfers for patients with no prospect of recovery of radial nerve function because I believe this surgery markedly improves hand function in most instances, predominantly by restoring strong active wrist extension. I explain the need for 6- week splintage of the arm but advise that hand function is very much improved in most cases within 12 weeks of surgery. I also advise tendon transfers for patients with PIN palsies with no prospect of recovery, provided they feel the loss of function due to loss of thumb and finger extension warrants surgery; a 6- week period of splintage of the forearm, wrist, and hand; and a 12- week recovery period. In my experience most, but not all, decide on surgery, although the elderly with a posterior interosseous palsy due to a deep giant lipoma frequently decline.

My favored transfers for restoration of function in radial palsy are PT- to- ECRB, FCR- to- EDC, and PL- to- EPL. I use FCR- to- EDC and PL- to- EPL for PIN palsies. For both I use a single longitudinal incision on the radial border of the forearm (Fig. 31.36). This allows exposure of both the flexor and extensor compartments of the forearm, harvest of the three donors, and creation of all three junctures. I believe the PTto- ECRB transfer is the most critical because restoration of wrist extension alone dramatically improves hand function. Some of my patients with longstanding PIN palsies, who all have retained wrist extension, do not consider the loss of finger and thumb extension a sufficient problem to justify the risks and inconvenience of tendon transfer surgery to restore these functions.

I intentionally apply too much tension because my transfers always end up looser than anticipated. In addition, the procedure is much easier if plentiful assistants’ hands are available.

In contrast, my experience is that all patients with a radial nerve palsy wish to undergo transfers in the hope (and expectation) of improved hand function. I almost always create an end- to- side PT–ECRB juncture, in case of a late recovery of radial nerve function, and I agree with Green that there is often “not quite enough” PT tendon to weave into the ECRB, even if the tendon is elongated with periosteum (which I always do). I thus also have no hesitation about reinforcing this juncture with a tendon graft (i.e., a thin strip of ECRL), which is weaved into the PT and ECRB tendons on either side of the juncture.

I used to find setting the correct tension in all four EDC tendons difficult but now use a technique that overcomes this problem. I pass a sling around these tendons (and usually the EDM tendon). My assistant then pulls powerfully on the sling to draw the distal portions of these tendons directly into the line of the planned FCR–EDC transfer. This should fully extend all four finger MCP joints. My assistant then holds this position while I pass a sturdy vicryl tie around all five tendons, just distal to the sling, and tie it as tightly as possible; this entraps the five tendons and prevents further relative movement of one against the other. I then use a robust vicryl suture to firmly tie the four EDC and the EDM tendons together, distal to the “cerclage tie.” Having done this, I perform an end- to- side transfer and weave the FCR into the EDC mass with the fingers extended and the wrist in 30 degrees extension. Alternatively, after insertion of the vicryl sutures to join the EDC tendons, one can divide them just proximal to the cerclage tie and then create an end- to- end juncture. I realize that this technique may appear unsophisticated, but it works in my hands, possibly because this transfer to restore finger extension is predominantly driven by the tenodesis effect of the wrist, such that soft tissue adhesions proximal to the wrist do not impede its function.

PL is harvested through the same incision and the EPL is readily mobilized and released from the third extensor compartment. I join the two, end- to- side, with a Pulvertaft weave, although others prefer an end- to- end juncture created by simply spiraling these two thin tendons around each other and then suturing them together with three or four “in- and- out” sutures. I use a direct line of pull with no pulley. I have contemplated using the ulna as a pulley to pass the PL subcutaneously around the forearm’s ulnar border to re- create the normal line of pull of the EPL. All this surgery can be done through the single radial border incision described earlier.

If there is no prospect of recovery of radial nerve function, the EPB and APL tendons can be divided and removed from the first extensor compartment, which is then used as a pulley for the PL–EPL transfer. The proximally divided EPL is passed through it and then joined to the PL. Absence of PL is frustrating, so I either incorporate EPL into the FCR–EDC juncture or do not reconstruct EPL function because many patients manage satisfactorily with only passive extension of the thumb. My policy may be borne out of ignorance, but I wonder if transfer of the ring finger FDS tendon to EPL produces an overall gain in function to the patient.

I have very little experience with use of the FCU–EDC transfer, which in my view has no obvious benefit, practical or theoretical, over the FCR–EDC transfer. I also have only rarely used the Boyes FDS–EDC/EPL transfers. Even though aware of its theoretical benefits (i.e., may allow combined active wrist and finger extension), most patients are satisfied with the outcome of FCR–EDC transfers. I wonder whether, though unproven, FDS harvest may reduce hand strength and dexterity and whether this would outweigh any benefit from improved finger extension.

### Radial Nerve Palsy Associated With Fractures of the Humerus

The most common radial nerve palsy encountered by most hand surgeons occurs after fracture of the shaft of the humerus. About 12% of these fractures are complicated by radial nerve injury, and there is at least one published article supporting virtually any plan of management, but common sense must prevail, and patient preference must be considered.

There are basically three ways in which radial nerve palsy seen with a humeral shaft fracture can be managed, as described in the following subsections.

#### Early Exploration of the Nerve

Historically, some authors have advocated that all radial nerve injuries associated with humeral shaft fractures should be treated with early exploration. The perceived benefits are listed next:

1. Assessment of the nerve (intact, contused, entrapped in the frac ture site, impaled on a fragment, or lacerated) with early treatment, when necessary, and provision of a prognosis and likely requirement of tendon transfers.

2. Stabilization of the fracture by internal fixation may protect the radial nerve from further damage.

3. Early operation is technically easier and safer.

The main issue with this strategy is whether early exploration will identify a significant number of patients with a “surgically correctable” lesion of the radial nerve.

When considering the reported rates of radial nerve damage, one must reflect on the source of the clinical material. Studies that have advocated early exploration often have based their conclusions on a series of patients treated in referral centers where the percentage of complicated problems and failures from other treatment facilities is unusually high. Conversely, studies from major trauma centers, where a consecutive series of patients with humeral shaft fractures complicated by radial nerve palsy are studied, find that most regain radial nerve function spontaneously and agree that nonoperative management is the treatment of choice.

Foster and coworkers found nerve laceration or entrapment in 9 of 14 open humeral shaft fractures they treated operatively. However, Kettelkamp and Alexander found *no* surgically correctable lesions in any of the 16 radial nerves they explored. In addition, Sonneveld and colleagues explored 14 cases and found only one “injured” (i.e., contused and not divided) nerve, and Dabezies and coworkers encountered only one lacerated nerve in 15 patients. No nerves were entrapped, and all intact nerves recovered.

The proximal spike of the Holstein- Lewis spiral fracture of the distal humerus may injure the radial nerve as it occurs within the area where the nerve is closely apposed to the bone and tethered by the lateral intermuscular septum. Holstein and Lewis advised early operative intervention for patients with this fracture and an accompanying radial nerve palsy; however, others have reported that early operative treatment is unnecessary, including Szalay and Rockwood. They studied 15 patients with this combination of injuries, and all 11 who were treated without exploration attained complete nerve recovery. The nerve was found to be in continuity in the 4 patients in whom exploration was carried out, and all regained normal radial nerve function.

It used to be believed that a secondary radial nerve palsy (not present initially and present after fracture reduction) was an absolute indication for immediate nerve exploration. However, there is convincing evidence for this, and secondary radial nerve paralyses can be treated nonoperatively with a good expectation for full recovery in most cases.

#### Nerve Exploration for Failure to Improve Spontaneously

Exploration of the radial nerve at 6 to 8 weeks after onset, if there is no evidence of recovery, has been advocated, but now seems illogical as Mast et al. reported that less than half of the nerves that recovered spontaneously did so in less than 12 weeks. Amillo and associates recommended surgical exploration at 3 months if there was no clinical or electrophysiologic signs of nerve recovery. However, it is well documented that the initial signs of motor recovery may not appear until 4 or 5 months after a radial nerve palsy associated with a fractured humerus. Korompilias et al. explored 12 radial nerves showing no sign of recovery after 16 to 18 weeks and found two complete transections. The other 10 nerves were macroscopically intact and recovered fully after 20 to 24 weeks (postinjury).

The work of Seddon regarding nerve regeneration after axonotmesis suggests nerve- fiber regeneration occurs at the rate of approximately 1 mm/day following a latent period of 30 days. On this basis the maximum length of time that may be required for the first signs of motor recovery to occur can be estimated by measuring the distance on the radiograph from the fracture site to the point of innervation of the BR muscle, which is approximately 2 cm above the lateral epicondyle. In most midshaft humerus fractures, this distance is 90 to 120 mm (Fig. 31.37), indicating regeneration times of between 17 and 21 weeks.

Evidence of reinnervation on electromyography may precede the clinical appearance of motor function by approximately 4 weeks.Therefore, if the decision is to treat the fracture nonoperatively, it is logical to wait 4 to 5 months before proceeding with exploration if there is no return of nerve function. The major advantages of this plan of management are (1) unnecessary operative intervention is avoided in most patients, (2) most of these patients achieve full recovery of the radial nerve without surgical treatment, and (3) the humerus fracture usually heals.

A question that must be raised is whether the delay in nerve repair for the very few patients in whom neurorrhaphy becomes necessary is excessive and reduces the chance for good functional recovery. Historically, delays of 12 months or longer were not considered likely to jeopardize functional motor return after nerve repair, but this view is now challenged, and 3- month or shorter windows of opportunity for optimum nerve repair have been suggested.

In the past, most humeral shaft fractures were treated nonoperatively, but operative fixation is preferred now for a significant number of these injuries. If the surgeon feels that the fracture characteristics make operative fixation preferable, then the nonfunctioning radial nerve should be explored at the same time. In addition, there is currently the dilemma on how to manage a radial nerve palsy, which develops after operative fixation of a shaft fracture of the humerus, using an operative field that puts the radial nerve at risk of damage from a retractor, scalpel blade, power drill, tap, or plate. The decision here will depend on many factors, including (1) the expertise of the surgeon who performed the fracture fixation, (2) the operation notes, (3) recollections (if any) of the condition of the radial nerve at the end of the surgery, (4) whether the radial nerve was seen during the procedure, and (5) patient preference for expectant treatment or reoperation.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: RADIAL NERVE PALSY AFTER HUMERAL SHAFT FRACTURE

My policy is to advise expectant treatment to most patients with radial nerve palsy associated with humeral shaft fractures that have been managed nonoperatively. I only consider exploration of the nerve after a realistic waiting period, estimated on the basis of Seddon’s work (see Fig. 31.37). If, however, the fracture requires plate fixation, the radial nerve should be identified and examined during the procedure. My biggest dilemma is the patient who presents with a radial nerve palsy after plate fixation of the humerus. In this scenario I review the operation notes to see whether the radial nerve was identified and protected throughout the procedure and also discuss the procedure with the surgeon who fixed the fracture.

This helps a surgeon estimate the likelihood of the nerve being intact and capable of spontaneous recovery with an injury in continuity or divided/crushed (by a plate) with no prospect of recovery. I then discuss the options of immediate reexploration, or expectant treatment for at least 12 weeks, with the patient, and together we devise an agreed- on treatment plan that suits the patient’s circumstances and psyche and reflects his or her preferences.

I agree with David Green that it is illogical to explore the radial nerve at 6 to 12 weeks. If the decision has been made to await spontaneous recovery of function, then sufficient time must be allowed, and <12 weeks is insufficient. It should be realized that radial nerve transfers will cause some donor morbidity, but this is usually offset by the benefit of the restored wrist, finger, and thumb extension. Should radial nerve function recover after the transfer of PT and other motors, then it is not feasible to “undo” the now unnecessary transfers.

#### Nerve Transfer as an Alternative to Tendon Transfer

Direct transfer of functioning, intact nerves or nerve fascicles to the motor nerves of denervated muscles is used to restore function in patients with brachial plexus palsy and proximal nerve injuries. The same concept has been increasingly reported for restoration of wrist, finger, and thumb extension in radial nerve palsy; thumb and index IP flexion and forearm pronation after median nerve palsy; and intrinsic muscle function after high ulnar nerve palsy (see Chapters 28, 30, and 34). The technique is based on one of the principles of tendon transfer: that the loss or weakening of the normal function of the donor muscle(s) is not critical and that its loss can be accommodated by other functioning musculotendinous units. Nerve transfer is not an option if (1) the paralyzed musculotendinous unit whose function is to be restored was damaged in the injury that caused the nerve palsy, (2) the musculotendinous unit is tethered by scar tissue, or (3) a prolonged time interval between nerve injury and nerve transfer has resulted in the inability of the recipient muscle to function after reinnervation (approximately 10 months). The potential benefits of nerve transfer over tendon transfers are that it avoids the needs for prolonged splintage to protect transfers or multiple skin scars to fashion the musculotendinous transfers. However, the recovery of function is protracted in comparison to tendon transfer.

**Restoration of radial nerve function.** Restoration of function is achieved by exposing (1) the median nerve and its branches for FDS and FCR/PL, (2) the radial nerve and its branch to the ECRB, and (3) the PIN. The FDS branch is transferred to the nerve to ECRB, and the FCR/PL branch to the PIN.

Technique. Through a single volar- radial incision in the proximal forearm, the radial nerve, the PIN, and the median nerve are exposed. The “donor” median nerve branches to the FDS, FCR, and PL visualized and confirmed with a nerve stimulator. The “recipient” radial nerve branch to the ECRB, and the PIN, is also identified. The donor nerves are transected as distally as possible and the recipient motor branches are transected sufficiently proximal to allow a tension- free coaptation.

Outcome. Restoration of extension of the wrist and fingers with grade 4 power was reported in the index case, with the first evidence of reinnervation occurring at 6 months. Since then, over a dozen creative distal nerve transfers have been proposed and implemented successfully (see Chapter 30). With similar techniques, restoration of median nerve extrinsic muscle function can be achieved by transfer of (1) the branches of the radial nerve to ECRB to the branch of the median nerve to PT and (2) the branch of the radial nerve to supinator to the anterior interosseous nerve. In one report, reinnervation of PT was demonstrated after approximately 4 months and FPL and index FDP at 8 months, maturing to grade 4 strength by 18 months. Large studies are required to evaluate and compare the outcomes of this treatment and standard radial nerve tendon transfers.
