## RADIAL LONGITUDINAL DEFICIENCY (Case Study 38.1)

### Preoperative Evaluation

Radial longitudinal deficiency (RLD) is a spectrum of malformations affecting the structures of the radial side of the forearm (radius, radial carpus, and thumb), including hypoplasia of the bones and joints, muscles and tendons, ligaments, nerves, and blood vessels. In the Oberg, Manske, Tonkin classification of congenital hand differences, RLD is categorized as a malformation of the radial- ulnar axis. RLD is an uncommon condition, although it is the most common type of congenital longitudinal deficiency. A population- based study in Finland found RLD in nearly 1:5000 live births.

Children with RLD should undergo thorough and serial examinations of both upper extremities. RLD is frequently bilateral and asymmetric, and manifestations may be subtle. Because the radius and many organ systems develop at the same time, a systemic workup is also required. RLD is commonly associated with several congenital syndromes, such that only a third of patients with RLD present with an isolated upper extremity malformation (Table 38.1). Therefore, a complete physical examination must be performed, and consultation with a clinical geneticist is recommended. In addition, radiographic and serologic tests are required as described below.

Although children with bilateral and severe RLD have functional impairment as a result of thumb dysfunction, wrist instability, and short upper extremities, there is no difference in long- term activity and participation levels between those with mild versus severe RLD.For some children with RLD, independent performance of activities of daily living, such as fastening buttons and zippers or accomplishing personal hygiene, may be difficult, prompting consideration for surgery.

Radiographs tend show the degree of involvement of the radius, thumb, and carpus dependent on the degree of ossification and/or previous surgical intervention. Classification of the RLD is based on radiographic studies. Spine x- rays, renal ultrasound, and echocardiography are required to assess for associated anomalies.

Persons with RLD have a high incidence of medical and musculoskeletal anomalies, which escalate with increasing severity of the deficiency. In addition to genetic counseling, testing for Fanconi anemia is recommended. Although there are several known genes responsible for Fanconi anemia, the gold standard for testing remains the chemical chromosomal breakage test. We recommend testing for Fanconi anemia for all children with deficiencies of the thumb and radial border of the forearm. Additional findings, such as abnormal skin pigmentation *(café- au- lait spots)* , kidney abnormalities, growth retardation, and microcephaly, add to the suspicion of Fanconi anemia. Failure to ascertain the diagnosis of Fanconi anemia will have lifelong ramifications for the child, family, and physician. Early testing allows Fanconi anemia to be diagnosed before the onset of bone marrow failure. Timely diagnosis allows for years of preparation to search for an appropriate bone marrow match that may prevent the child from dying from aplastic anemia. Innovative techniques, such as preimplantation genetic determination, allow the parents to have another child with matching blood characteristics and without Fanconi anemia. At birth, the cord blood can be harvested and used as a donor for the affected Fanconi anemia child. When hand surgeons and pediatricians fail to consider the diagnosis of Fanconi anemia in children with RLD, the child and the family are left to suffer the consequences of a painful terminal illness that could otherwise have been treated. For this reason, the association of Fanconi anemia and RLD is often tested on orthopedic in- training and board examinations.

RLD is associated with other upper extremity anomalies, including humeral hypoplasia, proximal radioulnar synostosis, congenital radial head dislocation, and finger stiffness. Less common associations include metacarpal synostoses and syndactyly. Infrequent findings are contralateral radial polydactyly and phocomelia.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

Bayne and Klug classified RLD into four types based on the radiographic severity of the radial deficiency (Table 38.2). James and associates modified this classification to include patients with deficiency of the thumb or carpus in the presence of a radius of normal length (Fig. 38.1). This classification is used in combination with the modified Blauth scheme for classifying thumb hypoplasia (Fig. 38.2).

### Types of Operations

Indications for treatment vary with the severity of the deformity and the age of the patient. Children with type 0 or 1 or mild type 2 radial deficiencies may require only stretching and splinting. When considerable radial deviation is present, tendon transfers and soft tissue releases may be indicated. Severe type 2 radial deficiency is difficult to treat. Lengthening of the radius may be possible, or centralization of the carpus on the end of the ulna may be performed to achieve stability and correct the radial deviation. A well- aligned forearm improves the likelihood that the child will use a reconstructed thumb.

We have previously recommended bony centralization, including (if necessary) carpal resection and ulnar epiphyseal shaving, to correct the wrist instability and radial deviation in types 3 and 4 RLD. Although bony centralization is still performed by some surgeons, others prefer not to resect the carpus or shave the distal ulna because of the high rate of recurrent deformity and complications such as wrist stiffness and forearm growth impairment secondary to iatrogenic damage to the ulnar physis.

The best results of centralization are obtained when aberrant radial wrist extensors can be transferred to help maintain the new position and when the centralization is done before 1 year of age (as long as general anesthesia is not precluded by other manifestations of the child’s syndrome such as cardiac problems or thrombocytopenia). Before centralization, serial casting and/or application of a distraction device facilitates stretching of the taut radial soft tissue structures. This preliminary stretching diminishes the soft tissue tension and the likelihood that bony resection will be required during centralization.Preliminary external distraction is most helpful for older children with untreated type 3 or 4 radial deficiency. A bilobed skin flap or dorsal rotation flap may redistribute excess tissue from the ulnar to the radial side of the wrist.

With unilateral type 3 or 4 involvement, the affected forearm is usually only half as long as the unaffected forearm. This deficit in length has been surgically addressed by ulnar lengthening via distraction osteogenesis. Lengthening can be performed in older children after centralization. However, lengthening procedures may deter the growth of the ulna, such that repeated lengthenings will be required to maintain the relative gain in length.

Vilkki described the combination of soft tissue distraction and microvascular epiphyseal transfer for the treatment of type 4 radial deficiency (Fig. 38.3). Historically, attempts to treat radial deficiency by surgical transfer of nonvascularized growing bone were unsuccessful because the graft failed to grow. Although Vilkki’s early and midterm results are promising, the technique is technically demanding and the outcome at skeletal maturity is unknown.

#### Preliminary Soft Tissue Distraction

Unilateral and circumferential fixators have been used successfully to distract the radial soft tissues before centralization (Fig. 38.4). The fixator is applied across the wrist via an open technique for pin placement to protect the anomalous anatomy and gradually distracted until the wrist can be centralized without residual radial deviation after the external fixator is loosened or removed (Fig. 38.5).

#### Centralization

Although the wrist and forearm are profoundly abnormal in a radially deficient limb, the anomalies are predictable. All the radial forearm structures are deficient to varying degrees. The radial wrist extensors and extrinsic thumb motors are usually absent or aberrant. The radial nerve is generally absent below the elbow. The median nerve is always present and is frequently the most prominent structure on the radial side of the wrist. The radial artery is usually absent. Occasionally, a child with RLD has an elbow extension contracture. In this case centralization is contraindicated, as wrist radial deviation is needed for the child’s hand to reach the face.

The incision for centralization begins at the level of the distal end of the ulna just radial to the midline, extends toward the ulna in a transverse direction, and then forms an ellipse to excise excess skin and subcutaneous tissue. Alternately, excess tissue from the ulnar aspect of the wrist is rotated to the radial side. The dorsoulnar sensory nerve is preserved, and the extensor retinaculum is exposed. The extensor carpi ulnaris is isolated at its insertion into the base of the fifth metacarpal. The extensor retinaculum is raised as a radially based flap and the finger extensor tendons are retracted radially. The radial wrist extensors are generally absent or adherent to the dorsal capsule and devoid of proximal musculature. Their remnants are isolated and elevated with a piece of dorsal capsule.

The wrist capsule is incised in a transverse direction over the ulnocarpal joint to expose the distal end of the ulna (Fig. 38.6). The carpal bones are often palmar and radial to the distal ulna. The ulna is freed from its soft tissue attachments distal to the physis, and the carpal bones are detached from the palmar capsule. The carpus is then reduced onto the distal ulna. If reduction cannot be achieved, the radial structures are examined for any persistent taut tissue. In severe cases without preliminary distraction, adequate reduction cannot be achieved and alternative measures are necessary. Surgical options include carpectomy or limited shaving of the distal ulnar epiphysis while avoiding injury to the growth plate or the application of an external fixator, postoperative distraction, and delayed formal centralization.

The reduction is maintained by placing a Kirschner wire through the carpus and third metacarpal into the ulnar shaft. If the ulnar angulation is greater than 30 degrees, a closing wedge osteotomy is performed at the apex of the deformity and secured with the same Kirschner wire. In severe deformity, separate Kirschner wires are often necessary. The wrist capsule is imbricated, and, if present, the radial wrist extensors are transferred into the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon. The extensor carpi ulnaris is tightened by detaching and advancing the tendon or imbrication to increase soft tissue tension on the ulnar side. The retinacular flap is passed underneath the finger extensors to reinforce the wrist capsule. Excess skin and subcutaneous tissue are excised, and the wound is closed with absorbable suture. A long- arm bulky compressive dressing is applied and covered with a long- arm fiberglass splint or cast.

#### Soft Tissue Release and Balancing

The complications of formal centralizations, particularly when the carpus cannot be passively reduced onto the end of the ulna, have shifted many surgeons toward performing a soft tissue release and balancing procedure instead of a formal centralization (Fig. 38.7). The major difference between these two operations is that in the soft tissue rebalancing, the joint capsule is not opened. Carpectomy and shaving of the distal ulnar epiphysis are also not performed. Tendon releases and transfers are performed in the same manner as for a formal centralization, and the hand and carpus are then pinned in the best position possible, accepting some translocation and/or deviation.

#### Ulnar Lengthening

This procedure is rarely indicated. When lengthening is performed, the principles of distraction lengthening are followed. A uniplane or multiplane fixator is used, depending on any concomitant angular bony correction needed. For application of a circumferential frame, two half- pins and one transfixion wire are used proximal to the osteotomy. One half- pin and two transfixion wires (one in the ulna and one in the metacarpals) are placed distal to the osteotomy.

For a uniplane fixator, two pins each are placed proximal and distal to the osteotomy. We prefer a uniplane fixator with a hinge at the midpoint to allow correction of the radial bow at the time of fixator application. Any residual radial deviation is corrected at the time of fixator application or during the distraction. Acute bony correction can be achieved with a closing wedge osteotomy followed by distraction osteogenesis. Distraction is initiated approximately 1 week after application of the fixator and is continued at 1 mm/day divided into two to three increments. Radiographic progress of bone regeneration is monitored weekly. The rate of lengthening is slowed if regeneration is lagging. The child and family are taught lengthening techniques and exercises to maintain elbow and digit motion. The ulna can usually be lengthened by 30% to 50%. The fixator is maintained during consolidation of the bony regenerate, which generally takes twice as long as the lengthening phase. The fixator is not removed until a total of three cortices are visualized on the anteroposterior and lateral x- rays. Occasionally, supplemental bone grafting is necessary to augment union.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: RADIAL LONGITUDINAL DEFICIENCY

Range- of- motion exercises for soft tissue stretching are started shortly after birth. The parents are instructed in stretching exercises to be performed with each diaper change or feeding. Once the child’s arm is large enough for splinting, generally around 3 months of age, a customfabricated long- arm Orthoplast splint (North Coast Medical, Gilroy, CA) is made for nighttime use in addition to the daytime stretches (Fig. 38.8). The parents are instructed to continue stretching the wrist several times each day for at least the child’s first year. In certain children, neither pollicization nor centralization is indicated (Video 38.1 ). These children typically have a mobile and dexterous small finger along with a stiff index finger. Pinch and grasp are accomplished using the ulnar side of the hand (ulnar prehension), and straightening the wrist would put the ulnar digits in an unfavorable position for approaching and interacting with objects. This shift in position may be detrimental to the child’s overall function and independence.

![[Fig.38.8.png]]
> **Fig. 38.8** An example of a custom thermoplastic splint for nighttime stretching.

Soft- tissue balancing and formal centralization have overlapping indications, and to date neither has proven superior in long- term function or esthetics. If the child is a candidate for centralization and is safe to undergo general anesthesia around age 1 year, surgical correction is planned. If the wrist can be passively reduced prior to surgery, formal centralization is performed. If not reducible, the parents are offered a choice between a soft tissue release and balancing procedure versus preliminary distraction followed by centralization. In the case of formal centralization, prolonged pin fixation followed by long- term splinting is necessary to minimize recurrence. Ulnar lengthening is reserved for mature older children or teenagers. Lengthening requires realistic expectations and emotional stamina on the part of the patient and family. Preoperative counseling and psychological assessment are helpful. The support of a limb- lengthening team, including a nurse, social worker, and occupational therapist, is also essential.

### Expected Outcomes

In the treatment of RLD, the surgeon and family must choose between a wrist that is flexible and deviated or a wrist that is stiff and straight. Long- term follow- up studies of bony centralization report problems with recurrence and stiffness, as well as significantly worse ulnar growth than would be expected from natural history (Fig. 38.9). Despite these difficulties, Kotwal and colleagues did report that patients with RLD who underwent centralization showed improvements in both subjective measurements (such as appearance) and objective measurements (such as alignment, grip strength, and finger and wrist range of motion) compared with patients who did not undergo surgery.

![[Fig.38.9.png]]
> **Fig. 38.9** A 3½- year-old child is shown 2 years following the centralization detailed in Fig. 38.6. A, Clinical appearance with mild recurrence. B, X-ray with hypertrophy of ulna. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

The numerous long- term complications of centralization have resulted in some experienced pediatric hand surgeons abandoning formal centralization in favor of soft tissue reconstruction, such as a bilobed skin flap combined with musculotendinous releases and tendon transfers. The soft tissue reconstruction has been shown to not limit ulnar growth.

### Postoperative Management and Expectations

After soft tissue balancing, the extremity is placed in a cast for at least 8 weeks. Subsequently, a long- arm splint, custom- fabricated to maintain the wrist position, is worn full time for 3 to 6 months and then at night until skeletal maturity is reached.

Complications of ulnar lengthening are common, especially pin tract infections, which usually resolve with local care and antibiotics. Finger stiffness may occur during the lengthening process and is treated by slowing the distraction rate and therapy. A second procedure to place bone graft at the osteotomy site is occasionally necessary to achieve union.

## ULNAR DEFICIENCY

### Preoperative Evaluation

Ulnar deficiency occurs approximately once in 25,000 live births and is 4 to 10 times less common than radial deficiency. Ulnar deficiency occurs sporadically and is not associated with systemic conditions as occurs in children with radial deficiency. Ulnar deficiency is associated with other musculoskeletal abnormalities that warrant careful physical examination supplemented by radiographs.

Unilateral ulnar deficiency is more common than bilateral deficiency. Children with ulnar deficiency have hypoplasia of the entire upper extremity. The elbow is malformed or fused (i.e., radiohumeral synostosis) in the majority of cases (Fig. 38.10). The ulna may be partially or completely absent, and a cartilaginous ulnar “anlage” may be present. The hand and carpus are always affected, and occasionally isolated hand involvement is seen. About 90% of hands are missing digits, 30% have syndactyly, and 70% have thumb abnormalities (Fig. 38.11).

The other musculoskeletal anomalies that may accompany ulnar deficiency include proximal femoral focal deficiency, fibular deficiency, phocomelia, and congenital scoliosis. Radiographs should be taken of both upper extremities, both lower extremities, and the spine. Ulnar deficiency is rarely associated with anomalies of other organ systems.

### Types of Operations

Surgery for patients with ulnar deficiency is mainly focused on the associated hand differences. Surgical indications for many of the hand anomalies associated with ulnar deficiency are well established. Hand function is improved by release of the syndactyly and reconstruction of the thumb and first web deficiencies. Procedures that may be indicated include deepening of the first web space, opponensplasty, rotational osteotomy of the thumb metacarpal, and pollicization.

In cases of radiohumeral synostosis, the forearm is hyperextended relative to the humerus such that the hand rests on the buttock or flank (Fig. 38.12). With this awkward positioning, children may be unable to reach their mouth or place their hand on top of their head. Over time, children may compensate for this position and obtain adequate function (Fig. 38.13). In recalcitrant cases, a flexion osteotomy of the distal humerus improves the position of the hand and the overall function of the limb (Fig. 38.14). Forearm rotational osteotomy is rarely necessary, even though the forearm is usually supinated. Rarely, the deformity may recur with growth, necessitating a repeat osteotomy at skeletal maturity.

The indications for surgical treatment of ulnar deviation of the wrist and instability of the forearm are less certain. Several authors have reported that ulnar deviation at the wrist is due to tethering by the ulnar anlage, a fibrocartilaginous structure occasionally found in the forearm of children with ulnar deficiency (Fig. 38.15), hypothesizing that the ulnar anlage fails to grow with the radius and causes increasing ulnar deviation. Proponents of this theory have advocated early excision of the ulnar anlage to prevent progressive deformity. Others, however, have shown that increasing ulnar deviation is uncommon, and therefore resection of the anlage should be reserved for documented progression. The indications for surgical treatment of the forearm are even less defined. Some authors advocate surgical construction of a one- bone forearm to enhance forearm stability; however, complete loss of forearm rotation may be detrimental to function. The surgeon must remember that despite substantial differences, these children are adaptive and ingenious, especially with parental support and therapy guidance.

#### Excision of the Ulnar Anlage

The ulnar remnant is approached via a longitudinal incision along the ulnar border of the forearm (Fig. 38.16). The ulnar extrinsic flexor muscles are often absent. The anlage will be adjacent to the ulnar neurovascular bundle, which must be protected. The anlage is dissected from its carpal attachment. The entire anlage need not be removed; removal of the distal third is adequate to release the tether. The subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed with absorbable suture and a long- arm cast is applied. The wrist is positioned as close to neutral as possible. Casting is maintained for 6 weeks and followed by a short- arm splint that is worn at night until skeletal maturity.

![[Fig.38.16.png]]

#### Osteotomy of the Humeroradial Synostosis

The distal end of the humerus is approached via a lateral incision. The course of the brachial artery should be marked preoperatively with a

Doppler (Fig. 38.17). Careful dissection is performed through the subcutaneous tissue, with care taken to visualize the radial nerve, which may be anomalous. The alignment of the humerus is marked by electrocautery or by inserting parallel Kirschner wires above and below the planned osteotomy site. A transverse osteotomy is performed, and the distal fragment is flexed and rotated until the hand can be placed in front of the trunk. Tight soft tissues may require bone shortening to prevent a traction injury to the neurovascular structures. The osteotomy is fixed with crossed Kirschner wires or a small plate. The subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed with absorbable suture. A well- fitted long- arm cast is applied and attached to the body with a Velpeau- type dressing.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ULNAR DEFICIENCY

Excision of the ulnar anlage is reserved for children with progressive ulnar deviation of the wrist. The anlage may be resected at the same time as hand reconstruction, usually at around 1 year of age. Humeral rotational osteotomy is reserved for children who are impaired by inability to reach their hand to the midline or to their face and head. Surgery is performed after children are old enough to evaluate their ability to perform activities of daily living. This age allows additional determination of the optimal limb position for function.

### Expected Outcomes

Children with unilateral ulnar deficiency generally function well, but those with radiohumeral synostosis, absent or stiff fingers, and/or bilateral deficiencies are more impaired.

## MADELUNG DEFORMITY

### Preoperative Evaluation

Madelung deformity consists of excessive radial and palmar angulation of the distal radius caused by a growth disturbance of the palmar and ulnar portion of the distal radial physis, although it may involve the entire radius. The exact cause of this growth disturbance is still unknown. It may be due to a bony lesion in the ulnar portion of the distal radial physis and/or an abnormal palmar ligament tethering the lunate to the radius proximal to the physis (Vickers ligament) (Fig. 38.19). Madelung deformity is usually bilateral, and the deformity typically becomes clinically apparent in early adolescence. The underlying cause of this deformity is often Leri- Weill dyschondrosteosis, a genetic condition with a dominant inheritance pattern and 50% penetrance that is due to a mutation in a short- stature homeobox- containing *(SHOX)* gene. Repetitive loading of the wrist in a growing child may also cause partial physeal arrest. The resultant appearance is similar to Madelung deformity.

![[Fig.38.19.png]]
> **Fig. 38.19** A 17-year-old female with Madelung deformity and prominent ulna. A, X-ray with increased radial inclination and ulnar-positive variance. Note radiolucency in the metaphysis of the radius consistent with the presence of a Vickers ligament. B, Operative findings with a markedly thickened Vickers ligament. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

The distal ulna of the affected wrist is dorsally prominent, and the distal radius appears to have a dorsal concavity in the lateral view (Fig. 38.20). Ulnar deviation and extension may be decreased. The forearm is shorter than normal. Despite the clinical appearance, there is typically minimal functional impairment. In advanced cases, however, radiocarpal alignment can be strikingly abnormal, leading to pain and functional limitations. Standard radiographic measurements have been established to characterize the severity of the deformity.

![[Fig.38.20.png]]
> **Fig. 38.20** Bilateral Madelung deformity in a 14-year-old. A, Right wrist with the hand palmar and pronated with respect to the long axis of the forearm. The distal ulna appears prominent dorsally. B, Left wrist with similar clinical features. ### Types of Operations

No treatment is necessary for painless deformities. However, physiolysis combined with release of the abnormal ligament should be considered for asymptomatic, skeletally immature patients.

A painful deformity with functional limitations is an indication for treatment. Physiolysis combined with release of the Vickers ligament may reduce the pain and gradually improve the deformity during growth. However, it is unclear whether improvement in radiographic measurements from physiolysis truly translates into functional improvement and avoidance of later osteotomies.Furthermore, most patients do not present for treatment until adolescence, when there is insufficient growth remaining for physiolysis to correct the deformity. In these instances, physiolysis and ligament resection may be combined with ulnar epiphyseodesis and/or a dome osteotomy of the radius. In a skeletally mature patient with wrist pain, there are multiple options for correction of the deformity and relief of pain, including (1) ligament resection and dome osteotomy, (2) radial closing wedge osteotomy and ulnar shortening,(3) radial opening wedge osteotomy, (4) radial osteotomy and distal ulnar resection, and (5) radial osteotomy and the Sauvé- Kapandji procedure.

For complex deformities in skeletally mature patients, threedimensional modeling is becoming increasingly popular to help plan correction. This technique is commercially available (Materialise®, Brussels, Belgium) and allows more precise correction than standard osteotomy techniques. A three- dimensional computed tomography (CT) scan is performed according to the company protocol. The forearm is reconstructed via virtual surgery between the surgeon and software engineer. Preoperative and postoperative bone models are manufactured along with cutting jigs. A manual is provided with stepby- step instructions on placement of the jigs, cutting of the bone, and subsequent bony fixation. This process is reserved for severe Madelung deformity.

#### Physiolysis

The radius is approached through a longitudinal palmar incision (Fig. 38.21). Exposure proceeds ulnar to the palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis tendons to protect the radial artery and median nerve. The distal edge of the pronator quadratus muscle is located. A flap of distal radial periosteum that includes the Vickers ligament is raised in a proximal to distal direction. The flap is elevated until the physis is identified, which appears narrow and wavy. The lunate is carefully protected. Any tethering fibrous tissue and bone about the physis are removed. A fat graft may be inserted into any physeal defect to inhibit bone formation. The fat graft is held in place by the surrounding soft tissue. The skin is closed with running absorbable suture, and the forearm is immobilized in a bulky compressive dressing with a removable wrist splint. A similar procedure can be done dorsally for reverse Madelung deformity.

![[Fig.38.21.png]]
> **Fig. 38.21** Early Madelung deformity treated by physiolysis and fat grafting. A, Palmar approach. B, Vickers ligament identified (in forceps). C, Ligament released. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

#### Physiolysis and Dome Osteotomy

A longitudinal palmar incision is made starting just proximal to the wrist crease along the flexor carpi radialis tendon (Fig. 38.22). The radial artery is protected, and the pronator quadratus is incised near its radial border while leaving a cuff of tissue for later repair. Ulnar reflection of the pronator quadratus reveals the Vickers ligament, which is released from the radial metaphysis and reflected distally. The metaphysis of the distal radius is exposed. A biplanar dome osteotomy is performed with curved osteotomes or a curved oscillating saw. The dome osteotomy can be convex like a smile or concave like a frown depending on surgeon preference. Since the deformity in Madelung is centered around the radiocarpal joint, greater correction can be achieved with a convex dome osteotomy, as this places the center of rotation of the dome closest to the center of the deformity. The distal radius fragment is manipulated from an anterior ulnar position to a dorsoulnar position. The dome shape of the osteotomy makes this complex threedimensional deformity easier to correct than with a simple wedge osteotomy. A Steinmann pin may be used as a joystick to position the distal fragment. The distal fragment is pinned to the proximal fragment after coverage of the lunate has been achieved. Definitive fixation can be accomplished by two parallel pins inserted from the radial styloid across the osteotomy site or a plate and screw construct contoured across the osteotomy. The palmar step- off of the proximal fragment is removed with a rongeur; the dorsal step- off will remodel after the osteotomy heals. If possible, the pronator is repaired. The subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed with absorbable suture. A long- arm splint or cast is applied. The pins are removed after 6 weeks, and the osteotomy is protected for an additional 4 to 6 weeks with a removable splint.

#### Dorsal Closing Wedge Osteotomy of the Distal Radius and Ulnar Shortening

The size and shape of the radial wedge osteotomy are planned according to the preoperative radiographs and amount of deformity. The goal is to restore palmar and radial tilt. The osteotomy is performed at the point of maximum angulation through either a dorsal or volar approach. Plate fixation is preferred. The ulna is shortened with standard techniques. The goal is to obtain a neutral or slight ulnar- minus variance. Dynamic compression plate fixation is preferred for the ulnar osteotomy.

#### Multiple Osteotomies Using Three- Dimensional Modeling

Three- dimensional imaging of the deformity is most easily achieved via CT scanning with three- dimensional reconstruction (Fig. 38.23). Because bilateral involvement is typical of the Madelung deformity, the use of the contralateral wrist as a model for comparison can be difficult. If the contralateral wrist is asymptomatic and not severely affected, it can be used as the comparison model. Other options for comparison models include generic bone models and age- matched patients. Using the appropriate computer software, the three- dimensional models of the affected bone are superimposed onto the goal model and osteotomies planned. From these data, physical bone models are fabricated using three- dimensional printing techniques, along with customized guides to assist with preplanned osteotomy and subsequent plate and screw fixation.

## TRANSVERSE DEFICIENCY

### Evaluation

Transverse deficiency, often inaccurately termed *congenital amputation,* occurs when the upper limb fails to form below a certain level. The most common level of failure of formation is at the proximal part of the forearm (Fig. 38.26), followed by transcarpal, distal forearm, and through- humerus (above- elbow) failure. This condition is almost always unilateral. Under the Swanson classification of congenital upper limb differences, this condition was referred to as “transverse failure of formation.” It is now more commonly referred to as transverse deficiency and categorized as a failure of axis formation or differentiation of the entire upper limb.

![[Fig.38.26.png]]
> **Fig. 38.26** A child with a right short, below-elbow transverse deficiency. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Northern California.)

Transverse deficiency is sporadic. The most prevalent theory of causation is vascular compromise of the developing limb bud or apical ectodermal ridge (see Chapter 35). Risk factors include maternal use of misoprostol (Cytotec), alcohol, tobacco, or cocaine, as well as low maternal intake of riboflavin, but for the majority of cases the cause is unknown.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

The diagnosis is readily apparent, although there is variability in the length of the residual limb. The most common transverse deficiency is at the level of the proximal forearm. The elbow generally has full flexion and extension. Forearm rotation is frequently restricted by proximal radioulnar abnormalities. Finger nubbins are usually present at the distal end of the limb, which emphasizes the mesodermal affect with preservation of the ectoderm. Finger nubbins may be evidence of an intrauterine attempt at limb regeneration. Elbow and forearm radiographs should be obtained.

### Types of Operations

Some parents want their infant’s nubbins removed, but older children with nubbins rarely request this procedure. Children with nubbins use them for sensory feedback and manipulation of small, light objects. Some surgeons remove these nubbins routinely, arguing that the resulting stump is more esthetically pleasing and easier psychologically for the family, but this is not our current practice. We believe removal of finger nubbins from the distal end of the amputation stump is not indicated unless they frequently become infected or the small nails become problematic.

There have been isolated reports of forearm lengthening for transverse below- elbow deficiency. However, this procedure has a high complication rate and has not improved function.

The Krukenberg procedure may be indicated in rare instances. The radius and ulna are separated and the muscles reattached such that the radius pinches against the ulna. This procedure is traditionally reserved for blind bilateral distal forearm amputees because it provides unilateral prehension with sensory feedback. However, it may occasionally be indicated for a sighted child with below- elbow deficiency and a long forearm. To date there are few published data regarding functional improvements after this procedure, and the appearance of the Krukenberg forearm is strikingly abnormal.

Great improvements have been made in hand transplantation, such that more than 100 hand transplants have been performed for adults with upper extremity amputations due to trauma. Only one bilateral hand transplant has been performed on a child with amputations due to sepsis and a previous kidney transplant necessitating immunosuppressive medications. Parents of children with congenital limb deficiency often inquire about this procedure. At this time, hand transplantation is contraindicated for children with transverse deficiency. They are unlikely to have formed muscles to move a transplanted hand, and the risks of prolonged immunosuppressive medications outweigh the potential benefits, as their level of functioning is extremely high.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: TRANSVERSE DEFICIENCY

Observation and emotional support are the mainstays of treatment. One of the greatest challenges in the treatment of children with transverse deficiency is helping parents adjust their often unrealistically high expectations of prosthetic technology. The technology has certainly advanced with myoelectric multiarticulating bionic hands. These prostheses have provided tremendous benefits to adults with traumatic bilateral limb amputations supplemented with targeted muscle reinnervation. The lack of sensibility combined with the ingenuity of young children, however, has prevented similar benefits in children. Good communication is important and should focus on numerous functions their child achieves with and without the prosthesis. Adaptive prosthesis for certain activities may be necessary as the child grows (Fig. 38.27). Surgery is rarely required.

## CONGENITAL DISLOCATION OF THE RADIAL HEAD

### Preoperative Evaluation

Congenital dislocation of the radial head (CDRH) is the most common congenital anomaly of the elbow and is usually bilateral. CDRH may be sporadic or familial. Although its cause remains unknown, a recent review highlighted five potential pathways to CDRH, including abnormalities of collagen formation, endochondral ossification, differential growth of the radius and ulna, and altered *HOX- D* expression.

The radial head may be dislocated in an anterior, posterior, or lateral direction. Almquist and coworkers reported that 47% of dislocations are anterior, 43% are posterior, and 10% are lateral. About 60% of congenital radial head dislocations are accompanied by other upper extremity congenital anomalies, whereas the remaining 40% are isolated findings. CDRH is associated with congenital radioulnar synostosis and numerous syndromes, including Klinefelter, Cornelia de Lange, Ehlers- Danlos, and nail- patella syndromes.

A delay in medical evaluation is common with CDRH. The lack of forearm rotation is unrecognized until the complexities of daily activities amplify the problem, such as catching a ball or eating soup. CDRH is often recognized after a perceived elbow injury and may be erroneously attributed to that injury. When unilateral, CDRH may be difficult to distinguish from chronic traumatic dislocation (Table 38.5). The initial complaint usually involves prominence and restricted elbow and forearm motion. Elbow “popping” or pain with activity is uncommon before adolescence. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be helpful in distinguishing CDRH from a traumatic dislocation. In CDRH, the radial head generally remains intracapsular, whereas in a traumatic radial head dislocation, the radial head usually tears through the elbow joint capsule. Additionally, the shapes of the cartilaginous radial head and capitellum can be assessed using MRI. In CDRH, the radial head is generally convex instead of concave, and the capitellum is hypoplastic and ovoid instead of convex.

### Types of Operations

Surgical intervention is seldom necessary in childhood. Most children are asymptomatic and have minimal functional limitations. Open reduction of a congenitally dislocated radial head with reconstruction of the annular ligament has not been consistently successful and is not recommended at this time.

#### Radial Head Resection

In adolescence or adulthood, a dislocated radial head may become painful because of degenerative changes at the point of contact between the radial head and the distal humerus. This is the best indication for resection of the radial head, although appearance and limited forearm rotation are relative indications. In a small series with an average 10year follow- up after surgery, Bengard and colleagues demonstrated that 25% of patients who underwent radial head excision for CDRH developed wrist pain requiring additional surgery. For this reason, we recommend that radial head excision be reserved for patients with substantial elbow pain, and that the operation be delayed until skeletal maturity whenever possible.

Excision of the radial head in posterior CDRH is performed using an approach through the interval between the extensor carpi ulnaris and the anconeus (Kocher approach) or directly over the prominent radial head (Kaplan approach). The capsule is incised with the forearm in maximum pronation. The capsular incision is not extended distal to the radial neck to avoid injuring the posterior interosseous nerve. Reverse retractors are placed around the radial neck. The radial head is excised with a saw perpendicular to the radial neck. The elbow is moved through a full range of motion to make sure that the proximal radius does not contact the distal humerus. Additional impingement requires further resection. The capsule is closed with nonabsorbable suture (Fig. 38.30). The subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed, and the arm is placed in a long- arm splint with the elbow at 90 degrees and the forearm in neutral rotation for 7 to 10 days.

## PROXIMAL RADIOULNAR SYNOSTOSIS

### Preoperative Evaluation

Congenital proximal radioulnar synostosis (PRUS) is an uncommon anomaly caused by failure of prenatal separation of the radius and ulna, which normally occurs late in the first trimester. PRUS is part of a spectrum of anomalies ranging from radial head abnormalities to complete synostosis and culminating in absence of the radial head. PRUS may be sporadic or inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. It is associated with other conditions in up to a third of affected children,including thumb hypoplasia, carpal coalition, symphalangism, and clubfoot. Associated syndromes include Apert syndrome, arthrogryposis, fetal alcohol syndrome, and Klinefelter syndrome.

Children with PRUS are usually seen initially between 2 and 6 years of age with painless absence of forearm rotation, the forearm fixed in pronation or supination (usually pronation), and a slight elbow flexion contracture (Fig. 38.31). Typically, a parent or teacher notices that the child has difficulty positioning the hand to catch a ball, drink from a cup, or accept small objects in an open palm or has trouble performing midline activities requiring the use of both hands (Fig. 38.32). The delay in diagnosis is attributed to lack of requirement for forearm rotation in infants and the use of compensatory shoulder and wrist motion. Forearm rotation must be assessed at the radial styloid and ulnar head to avoid measuring rotation through the radiocarpal, intercarpal, or carpometacarpal joints (Fig. 38.33). Bilateral involvement is seen in approximately 60% of children, and the forearm is fixed in more than 60 degrees of pronation in approximately 40% of patients. Children with bilateral involvement and forearms fixed in marked pronation have more functional limitations. In addition, PRUS is more often diagnosed during infancy in children whose arms are fixed in severe pronation and supination (Fig. 38.34).

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

PRUS is initially cartilaginous and eventually ossifies, at which point it becomes visible on radiographs. The radial head may be dislocated. The synostosis is nearly always proximal, and the length of the synostosis varies.

### Types of Operations

#### Resection of Synostosis

Surgery to restore forearm rotation by resection of the synostosis has traditionally been unsuccessful. A review of the literature showed that resection of the synostosis and mobilization of the forearm failed in 22 of 23 forearms. Interposition of a free vascularized fascial flap between the separated forearm bones may prevent recurrence of the synostosis, but the amount of forearm rotation gained is small. One contributing factor to the difficulty of simple resection of the synostosis is the underlying bony deformity present in most cases.

#### Derotation Osteotomy

Fixed extreme pronation (>60 degrees) that interferes with function is an indication for derotation osteotomy; unilateral PRUS does not usually cause sufficient functional impairment to warrant surgical repositioning. The timing of surgery is controversial. Recommendations vary from before school age to waiting for the child to choose an intended occupation. The child’s preoperative rotational position should be carefully measured and recorded. The optimal rotational position of the forearm is also controversial because the best position varies with the task. Preoperative evaluation by an occupational therapist can help determine the individual child’s functional deficits and optimal forearm position. Recommendations for surgical treatment of bilateral synostosis have classically been to place the dominant forearm in 10 to 20 degrees of pronation and the nondominant forearm in neutral rotation. The increasing use of technology requiring bilateral forearm pronation has led many surgeons to position both forearms in slight pronation. Supination tasks, such as eating, perineal care, and carrying, require careful consideration when determining the optimum position. For unilateral PRUS, the forearm is generally placed in 0 to 15 degrees of pronation. A study of the optimal rotational position for a one- bone forearm indicated that 30 degrees of pronation provides the best function for writing and working with small objects with the dominant arm. In cultures where supination is more impor tant for eating and hygiene, positioning the forearm in slight supination may be preferred.

Derotation osteotomy is performed either through the fusion mass or through the proximal ulna and distal radius, and small Steinmann pins or a plate with screws is used for fixation (Fig. 38.35 and Video 38.2). Additional options include gradual correction with the Ilizarov method for severe deformity, two- stage osteoclasis without internal fixation, and osteotomy with bone morselization and cast immobilization. If the desired amount of derotation is less than 30 degrees, an osteotomy at the distal radius alone may suffice, with one report in the literature demonstrating even larger amounts of correction with a radial osteotomy alone.

#### Radial Head Excision

For the patient with an anteriorly dislocated radial head (Cleary and Omer type 4) who develops an acute elbow flexion contracture, radial head excision may reduce pain and restore motion.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PROXIMAL RADIOULNAR SYNOSTOSIS

Surgery should be considered when the forearm is fixed in more than 60 degrees of pronation. Children compensate for lost forearm rotation by increased rotation through the radiocarpal and intercarpal joints. Hence, an accurate evaluation of forearm versus hand rotation is important. Derotational osteotomy is the preferred method of treating this condition. Prior to surgery, a preoperative evaluation by a pediatric occupational therapist can help determine the individual child’s functional deficits and optimal forearm position. The patient and parents need to realize that derotational osteotomy will only change the position of the synostosis, not increase forearm motion. This concept can be difficult for laypeople to understand.

### Postoperative Management and Expectations

Complication rates are high in reported series. Wrist and elbow pain has been reported in adolescents after surgical treatment, but there are few reports with long- term follow- up. The most serious complications are vascular compromise and compartment syndrome, which occur more frequently with changes of more than 85 degrees in rotational position. Immediate intervention is required to reduce the correction and release the forearm fascia. In Simmons and colleagues’ series of 22 patients, 4 required an emergency second operation to remanipulate the osteotomy because of poor vascularity of the forearm. Green and Mital reported one patient with a Volkmann contracture postoperatively. If excessive correction is needed, a two- stage operation or gradual correction via an external fixator is safer. Other complications include posterior interosseous nerve palsy (as a result of either intraoperative damage or traction from rotational change), loss of correction, and nonunion. Ogino and Hikino reported two radial nerve palsies occurring in 13 operations, with recovery at 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively. Simmons and associates reported one posterior interosseous nerve palsy that recovered at 6 weeks and three patients who lost correction of 60, 50, and 20 degrees. Green and Mital reported two patients who required repeat osteotomy.

### Expected Outcomes

Rotational osteotomy for PRUS is a reliable procedure for realigning the forearm. Most children and parents note improvement in hand positioning for activities of daily living and midline activities. Rotational malalignment does not recur.

## CONGENITAL PSEUDARTHROSIS OF THE ULNA OR RADIUS

### Preoperative Evaluation

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the ulna occurs when a segment of the ulna, usually in the distal or middle third, is replaced by fibrous tissue (Fig. 38.41). This condition is rare and generally associated with neurofibromatosis. Seventy percent of persons with ulnar pseudarthrosis have neurofibromatosis. Familial pseudarthrosis associated with neurofibromatosis has been reported but is unusual.

![[Fig.38.41.png]]
> **Fig. 38.41** Radiographs demonstrating congenital pseudarthrosis of the ulna. Note the large radiolucent defect, very little callus formation, and tapered bone ends. Radial head is dislocated.

Neurofibromatosis is an autosomal dominant trait with variable penetrance and a high rate of spontaneous mutation that occurs in approximately 1 in 3000 live births. Neurofibromatosis is the most common single- gene disorder. Congenital pseudarthrosis, most commonly in the tibia, develops in 5% to 10% of people with neurofibromatosis, but its cause is unknown. In the majority of cases, the pseudarthrosis contains fibrous tissue and not a neurofibroma. Pseudarthrosis may occasionally involve both the radius and ulna or the radius alone (Fig. 38.42).

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the ulna causes a progressive forearm deformity, diminished rotation, instability, and weakness. The deformity may be painful. The forearm is short, and the radius bears the entire load of the forearm, which leads to radial bowing and eventually to radial head dislocation. The pseudarthrosis is diagnosed on radiographs, which also reveal the extent of radial bowing and position of the radial head (see Fig. 38.42).

### Types of Operations

The goals of surgical treatment are to achieve bony union, stabilize the adjacent distal radioulnar and ulnocarpal joints, and allow continued skeletal growth. Similar to tibial pseudarthrosis, attempts to achieve union with nonvascularized bone grafting and immobilization usually fail. Historically, creation of a one- bone forearm was the only option available to restore forearm stability. External distraction techniques combined with compression across the pseudarthrosis may achieve short- term union in individuals with tibial pseudarthrosis.In the forearm, however, differential lengthening of the paired bones results in loss of forearm rotation.

Resection of the pseudarthrosis and transfer of a free vascularized fibular graft to the defect reliably results in union. Preliminary distraction histogenesis may be necessary to achieve adequate soft tissue length. Fibular transfers maintain length and may preserve some forearm rotation. A one- bone forearm may be necessary to restore forearm stability, especially when the pseudarthrosis affects both the radius and ulna (see Fig. 38.42). Although follow- up of a large number of patients to skeletal maturity is lacking, free vascularized fibular grafting is promising. In our series of five patients treated with this method, union occurred at an average of 10 months postoperatively, although one patient with a very distal pseudarthrosis of the ulna did require repeat surgery to achieve union. A recent systematic review pointed toward creation of a one- bone forearm as the most reliable technique for union, followed by vascularized fibular transfer.

The donor fibula is contralateral to the pseudarthrosis to facilitate intraoperative positioning. Radiographs of both forearms and the donor leg are obtained. Preoperative arteriograms of the donor and recipient sites are not essential, but a preoperative Allen test is necessary to plan end- to- end versus end- to- side anastomosis of the ulnar artery to vascularize the fibular graft. The amount of fibular graft needed after resection of the pseudarthrosis is estimated. A minimum of the middle third of the fibula must be harvested to protect the nutrient artery. At least the middle half of the fibula can be harvested without knee or ankle instability. Some reports indicate that the distal 6 to 8 cm of fibula should be preserved to maintain ankle stability, although other biomechanical studies indicate that only the distal 10% of the fibula is necessary to preserve ankle stability. We prefer to perform a concomitant distal tibiofibular synostosis (Langenskiöld procedure) in children up to skeletal maturity to decrease the risk of valgus deformity at the ankle.

Two surgical teams are used to decrease operative time. The patient is placed in a floppy lateral decubitus position with the affected arm positioned along an arm board. The pseudarthrosis is exposed along the forearm, and the exposed neurovascular structures are isolated (see Fig. 38.42C to E). The entire pseudarthrosis is resected back to healthy bone at each end. The size of the defect determines the length of fibula resected.

Internal fixation is used to secure the graft in place before vascular anastomosis. Both the recipient and donor bone are osteotomized in a step- cut or transverse fashion. The proximal osteotomy is always fixed with plate and screws. Frequently, the segment of distal metaphyseal bone that remains after resection of the pseudarthrosis is very short, thereby precluding plate fixation. Interfragmentary screw, Kirschner wire, or tension band fixation may be used to secure the graft distally. An end- to- end or end- to- side arterial anastomosis is performed between the peroneal and ulnar arteries. The peroneal veins are often larger than the venae comitantes and require anastomosis with subcutaneous veins about the forearm. Supplemental cancellous bone graft from the ends of the donor fibula or from the iliac crest can be placed at each end of the fibular graft to enhance union. The wound is closed with interrupted subcutaneous sutures and running absorbable skin sutures. A drain is placed if necessary. Long- arm and long- leg casts are applied.

In skeletally immature patients, Bae and colleagues proposed transferring the proximal fibular epiphysis with the vascularized fibular graft to allow subsequent skeletal growth. Their series consisted of two children 3 and 5 years old in whom preoperative radiographs demonstrated atrophy, narrowing, and sclerosis of the entire distal ulna with obliteration of the intramedullary space and a paucity of callus formation. In these cases, the entire distal ulna was removed. To harvest the proximal fibular epiphysis, the proximal tibiofibular joint is opened and the lateral collateral ligament, biceps femoris, and peroneal muscle attachments are released. The peroneal nerve is isolated and protected. The graft is taken with both the peroneal and anterior tibial vascular pedicles to perfuse both the proximal epiphysis and fibular diaphysis. This mandates vein graft reconstruction of the anterior tibial artery for two- vessel flow and adequate vascularity of the lower extremity. The triangular fibrocartilage and DRUJ capsules are repaired to the proximal fibular epiphysis. Follow- up of 33 months in one patient and 80 months in the other was reported to show skeletal growth matching radial growth. However, a recent report of two cases of epiphyseal transfer demonstrated premature physeal closure in both patients.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: CONGENITAL PSEUDARTHROSIS OF THE ULNA OR RADIUS

Debridement of the pseudarthrosis and placement of a vascularized fibular graft successfully restores ulnar length and stability in most cases. The authors recommend performing this operation in younger children, before the radial head dislocates. Surgical tibiofibular synostosis is especially important in younger children to prevent progressive ankle valgus deformity.

### Postoperative Management and Expectations

The patency of the anastomosis may be monitored at the surgeon’s discretion; monitoring options include harvesting of the fibula with a skin paddle or implantable flow monitors. Postoperatively, the patient is kept warm, calm (with sedation if necessary), and at bed rest for 72 hours. One baby aspirin (81 mg) per day is administered for 7 to 14 days. Caffeine and cigarette smoke are avoided.

Weight bearing on the affected leg is not allowed for 6 weeks. Radiographs of both extremities are obtained with casts off at 6 weeks. Casting is then continued until radiographic union is apparent at each site. The decision between long- arm and short- arm casting is made based on the age of the child, the location of the pseudarthrosis, and the strength of the internal fixation.

A vascularized fibular graft achieves a higher rate of union than any other operative treatment of ulnar pseudarthrosis other than the onebone forearm. Unlike creation of a one- bone forearm, vascularized fibular grafting preserves motion. This operation is best performed early to reduce the chance of progressive forearm deformity and radial head dislocation.

Suzuki and coauthors reported three patients with 5- to 8- year follow- up after vascularized fibular grafting for ulnar pseudarthrosis.Anterior bowing of the ulna and anterior dislocation of the radial head developed in two, and the other had considerable shortening of the ulna at the wrist. The authors noted that although free vascularized fibular grafting is the treatment of choice for congenital ulnar pseudarthrosis, patients must be monitored radiographically during growth. No published studies have reported follow- up to skeletal maturity.

Donor site morbidity is a frequent occurrence. Progressive ankle valgus deformity may develop in skeletally immature patients after fibular resection. This deformity can be prevented or at least minimized by preserving adequate distal fibular length, inserting a distal tibiofibular syndesmosis screw, creating a synostosis between the distal tibia and fibula (Langenskiöld procedure), or any combination of these measures. Distal tibiofibular synostosis is the preferred method of stabilization of the fibula to prevent ankle valgus deformity. Other donor site problems include objective motor weakness, pain, and sensory abnormalities.

## ELBOW AND FOREARM DEFORMITY CAUSED BY MULTIPLE HEREDITARY EXOSTOSES

### Preoperative Evaluation

Multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE) is a disorder of enchondral bone growth, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with high penetrance and variable expressivity. Cartilaginous exostoses, also called osteochondromas, grow from the physes of long bones and from the pelvis, ribs, scapula, and vertebrae. Many different names have been used to describe MHE, including multiple cartilaginous exostoses, diaphyseal aclasis, dyschondroplasia, hereditary deforming chondrodysplasia, and osteochondromatosis. MHE is frequently confused with multiple enchondromatosis (Ollier disease), an entirely different condition. Multiple exostoses can also occur in other conditions, such as metachondromatosis and Langer- Giedion syndrome. The prevalence of MHE is approximately 1 in 50,000. MHE has an equal prevalence in both sexes, although males tend to have more severe involvement (Fig. 38.44). The most common sites of involvement are the knee, humerus, hip, scapula, ribs, distal radius and ulna, ankle, elbow, hands, feet, and pelvis. Approximately half of all patients with MHE have forearm involvement.

![[Fig.38.44.png]]
> **Fig. 38.44** A 16-year-old boy with severe multiple hereditary exostoses. A, The upper and lower extremities are severely involved. B, Bowing of the right forearm. C, An x-ray shows ulnar shortening and radial bowing. The radiocapitellar joint is located. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

Genetic studies of families with this condition have mapped the chromosomal abnormality to at least three different genes.The genes are termed *EXT* genes and are located on different chromosomes. Because the *EXT* genes are tumor suppressor genes, MHE has been classified as a familial neoplastic syndrome.

A clinical “bump search” on all long bones is indicated if MHE is suspected but the diagnosis has yet to be established. The ribs should be included in the search. Shoulder, elbow, forearm, wrist, and digit range of motion should be measured. Serial measurements of forearm rotation are used to document progressive forearm deformity, and the length of the ulna can be measured clinically to derive the proportional ulnar length. Clement and Porter demonstrated that a shorter proportional ulnar length (the length of the ulna described as a percentage of the patient’s height) is a risk factor for eventual radial head dislocation. They described a cut- off of proportional ulnar length of 13% or less in males and 12% or less in females as the point at which forearm range of motion is likely to be below 100 degrees of rotation and thus limit activities of daily living. Radiographs should be obtained for any part of the extremity in which osteochondromas are suspected or that has a decreased range of motion. A thorough neurologic examination, including gait and reflexes, should be performed for all patients with MHE because intraspinal osteochondromas can lead to myelopathy. Any neurologic abnormalities should prompt further workup. Occult intraspinal exostoses (without any neurologic symptoms) have been reported, in some instances leading to paralysis after minor trauma. In our practice, all patients with MHE undergo a screening spine MRI once they are able to undergo imaging without sedation, generally around the age of 5 or 6 years.

Most exostoses are asymptomatic and do not require removal. Exostoses removed before skeletal maturity may recur. However, exostoses may cause local discomfort, nerve or tendon impingement, decreased range of motion, and longitudinal and angular growth abnormalities. Growth abnormalities may also occur in MHE in the absence of radiologically visible exostoses. Several reports of adults with untreated forearm deformities secondary to MHE indicate that they maintain function and are comfortable with their appearance.These reports question the role of aggressive surgical treatment to maintain or improve function. In evaluating the forearms of untreated adults with MHE, Noonan and colleagues demonstrated decreases in strength, motion, and function relative to controls. Seventy- seven percent of patients were free of pain, only 13% thought that they were limited in job performance, and just 4% had evidence of osteoarthritis.There were no radiographic measures that were associated with a poor outcome.

The exostosis may appear sessile (broad based) or pedunculated. More severe angular deformities appear to be associated with a predominance of sessile lesions. The sessile lesion can produce an extrinsic compression of the adjacent bone (Fig. 38.45). Exostoses possess a cartilage cap and are larger than their radiographic appearance. MRI is not routinely performed but will reveal the size of the cartilaginous cap. It is the imaging technique of choice to evaluate for suspected malignant transformation.

Certain factors associated with exostosis about the forearm yield loss of forearm rotation over time. A reduced proportional ulna length is associated with radial head dislocation and diminished forearm rotation. Radiographically, forearms with isolated osteochondromas of the distal ulna are more likely to develop radial head dislocation.Ulnar lengthening with angular correction in the radius and ulna has been shown to correct carpal slip and improve both forearm and elbow alignment. In addition, all 17 patients in that series maintained radiocapitellar joint congruency. The effect of early ulnar lengthening on long- term forearm rotation has yet to be determined.

Osteochondromas may cause local pain, visible deformity, and growth disturbance. Malignant transformation is uncommon. Local pain, caused by impingement of the osteochondroma on surrounding tissue, is a frequent indication for removal of the exostosis, which effectively relieves the pain. If forearm rotation is blocked by an exostosis, removal will often improve motion.

Forearm osteochondromas frequently cause a discrepancy in length of the radius and ulna. The forearm deformities caused by growth arrest secondary to MHE are complex. Even when osteochondromas of the distal ulna are not visible on radiography, distal ulnar growth arrest is common. The ulnar physis appears to be especially susceptible to growth impairment, possibly because of its relatively small crosssectional area. The resultant deformity is a length discrepancy between the two forearm bones. The radius becomes longer than the ulna and accepts the entire forearm load, which results in radial bowing, radial tilting, and dislocation of the radial head. The increased radial inclination and lack of ulnar support position the wrist in ulnar deviation and cause the carpus to “slip” toward the ulna. The length discrepancy causes diminished forearm rotation.

Early removal of osteochondroma to retard or prevent progressive growth disturbances is controversial. Hemiepiphyseal stapling of the radial side of the distal radius retards radial growth and allows correction of the radial articular angle and ulnar length discrepancy with growth. Stapling may be performed in conjunction with ulnar lengthening in a skeletally immature child. Ulnar lengthening may be performed in a single stage or by gradual distraction osteogenesis. Differential lengthening combined with angular correction can be used to reduce the radial head because restoration of normal forearm anatomy may result in spontaneous reduction of the radial head. In patients with an isolated distal ulnar lesion, freeing the soft tissue tether between the ulna and ulnar- sided carpus and distal radius may prevent dislocation of the radial head. The dislocated radial head may become painful in adolescence; resection relieves the pain and removes the associated prominence. Radial head resection may be indicated for pain, but it is delayed until skeletal maturity because removal of the radial head in a growing child may cause cubitus valgus or proximal radial overgrowth. Creation of a one- bone forearm may be used to salvage a severely disorganized forearm.

In adults, an indication for excision of MHE is suspected malignant transformation. Symptoms and signs of malignant transformation include local pain and growth of an osteochondroma after skeletal maturity. Radiographic changes include internal lytic areas, erosion or destruction of the adjacent bone, and the presence of a soft tissue mass containing irregular calcifications. MRI frequently reveals a large cartilaginous cap. Malignant transformation, however, is quite rare in the upper extremity.

Contraindications to removal of osteochondromas are relative. Simple procedures, such as removal of symptomatic osteochondromas and dislocated radial heads, may improve the appearance and relieve pain with a low complication rate. Complicated procedures, such as forearm lengthening, are more likely to be associated with complications. Ulnar lengthening may prevent radial head dislocation and loss of forearm rotation in selected patients.

Careful preoperative neurologic and range- of- motion examinations and contemporaneous radiographs are required before surgery.

The patient, parents, and surgeon should agree on exactly which osteochondromas will be removed because there are often several present.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

Masada and associates developed a classification scheme for forearm deformities based on location of the osteochondromas. This scheme is based on the location of the main forearm osteochondroma, the resultant deformity, and concomitant clinical problems. Type 1 is the most common form. The main osteochondroma is located in the distal ulna, which causes a short ulna and bowed radius (see Fig. 38.44C). The radial head remains located within the radiocapitellar joint. In type 2, the main osteochondroma is located in the distal ulna, which causes a short ulna, bowed radius, and dislocated radial head (Fig. 38.46). Type 2 is subdivided into type 2a, with an osteochondroma on the proximal radial metaphysis, and type 2b, with no proximal radial osteochondroma. In type 3, the main osteochondroma is located in the distal radial metaphysis and the radius is relatively short.

### Types of Operations

#### Removal of Osteochondroma

The osteochondroma is approached through a longitudinal incision centered over the tumor. The most readily available anatomic interval is used for the approach. Usually, the osteochondroma splits the surrounding structures and the approach is straightforward. Nerves or vessels (or both) may be wrapped around the pedicle of the tumor or flattened over its surface. These neurovascular structures are retracted, and the entire tumor, including the periosteum, is removed until a smooth bony contour remains. It is common for osteochondromas to be present on both the distal radius and ulna, such that both volar and dorsal approaches to the forearm may be required to fully remove the symptomatic tumors. In this case prophylactic fasciotomy is recommended, and patients are admitted overnight to closely monitor their swelling (Fig. 38.47).

#### Distal Radial Hemiepiphyseal Stapling

The radial aspect of the distal radial physis is approached through a transverse incision while taking care to avoid injuring the dorsoradial sensory nerve (Fig. 38.48). Under radiographic guidance, three extraperiosteal staples are placed across the distal radial physis. Staples are placed on either side of the first dorsal compartment; usually there is ample room for two staples dorsal to the compartment, and one staple palmar to the compartment.

#### Radial Head Excision (Posterolateral Dislocation)

See the section on resection of the radial head under “Congenital Dislocation of the Radial Head.” A substantial portion of the proximal radius may be dislocated in patients with MHE. In these cases, the posterior interosseous nerve must be visualized and protected to avoid injury.

#### Ulnar Distraction Lengthening

The ulna can be lengthened to maintain the relationship between the ulna and radius (Fig. 38.49). Lengthening can also maintain radial head alignment and/or promote radial head reduction. Pins for a uniplanar external fixation device are applied to the ulna, two pins proximal and two pins distal to the planned osteotomy site. The pins are placed perpendicular to the bone at each location, so that when the fixator is placed, the ulna will be straightened, eliminating any bowing of the ulna. Once the pins are in place, the site of ulnar osteotomy is isolated. The periosteum is incised carefully. Retractors are used to protect the soft tissues, and a transverse osteotomy is made using an oscillating saw. The external fixator is applied. While the wound is still open, the device is lengthened and then recompressed to ensure that there is no hindrance to lengthening. Patients return to the clinic in 5 to 7 days to start the lengthening process. Lengthening usually proceeds at 1 mm per day; patients return to the clinic weekly or send weekly x- rays during the lengthening phase. Lengthening is slowed or stopped temporarily if there are any concerns about inadequate bone formation, excessive digital stiffness, or neurovascular problems. If the bone regenerates rapidly, lengthening can be sped up to 1.5 or 2 mm per day. Once the lengthening phase has been completed, the fixator remains in place until bone regeneration is complete, generally twice the time of the lengthening phase.

#### Differential Forearm Lengthening

This complex operation is uncommon. See Dahl’s detailed description of planning osteotomies, designing fixation, ulnar lengthening and angular correction, and radial head reduction.

#### Ulnar Detethering

At times considerable deformity can be present as a result of an ulnar tether, which is most often the sequela of premature ulnar physeal closure. To release this tether an incision is made over the area of the distal ulna. The tether is transected from the ulna (through the epiphyseal or styloid area with the triangular fibrocartilage complex) to the distal fragment. The extensor carpi ulnaris subsheath is also released because this can be an additional deforming force. At this point a corrective radial osteotomy can be performed, depending on the deformity present and/or growth potential remaining in the radius.

#### Postoperative Management and Expectations

For removal of distal forearm osteochondromas and distal radial hemiepiphyseal stapling, use of a soft dressing and wrist splint for 2 to 3 weeks is adequate immobilization. Radiographs of the distal radius should be obtained twice a year after hemiepiphyseal stapling, and the staples should be removed when the desired amount of radial tilt (usually 20 degrees) is attained.

Osteochondromas tend to grow until skeletal maturity. Recurrence is uncommon when resection is performed near skeletal maturity. Radial head resection reliably relieves pain in persons with radial head dislocation. Differential forearm lengthening may improve the appearance and prevent radial head dislocation

Akita and coworkers reported the long- term results of surgery for forearm deformities in patients with MHE. Thirty- one forearms in 23 patients were monitored for an average of 13 years after surgery. The mean age at the time of surgery was 11 years. The most beneficial procedure was excision of the osteochondroma, which improved forearm pronation and appearance. Corrective osteotomy and lengthening of the ulna were not found to be beneficial. Ulnar lengthening was associated with complications, including nonunion, fracture of callus at the site of lengthening, and temporary radial nerve palsy. Although the radiographic parameters of ulnar variance, radial articular angle, and carpal slip were initially improved by the surgery, at final follow- up the deformities had progressed. In two patients with radial head dislocation before skeletal maturity, the radial head was reduced with corrective osteotomy of the radius, ulnar lengthening, and reconstruction of the annular ligament. At last follow- up, both had degenerative changes at the elbow and restricted forearm pronation. Two other patients with dislocation of the radial head at skeletal maturity were treated by radial head resection. Both had relief of pain and improvement in motion at follow- up. These authors concluded that forearm deformities are unrelated to function, and they no longer recommend corrective procedures for prevention of functional impairment.

Shin and colleagues reported outcomes after excision of the osteochondroma, ulnar lengthening, and the Sauvé- Kapandji procedure.Although excision improved the range of motion of the forearm, the Sauvé- Kapandji procedure also improved wrist stability, movement of the forearm, and radiographic appearance. The authors hypothesized that the Sauvé- Kapandji procedure reduces the tethering effect that the distal ulna has on the radial physis.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: ELBOW AND FOREARM DEFORMITY CAUSED BY MULTIPLE HEREDITARY EXOSTOSES

Hemiepiphyseal stapling, resection of the osteochondroma, and removal of the radial head are simple operations with good results. Forearm rotation should be measured twice a year in children with MHE and forearm involvement. Progressive loss of forearm rotation is considered an indication for removal of osteochondromas. Freeing the tether between the radius and ulna, either by soft tissue release of the distal ulna, by ulnar osteotomy and lengthening, or with the Sauvé- Kapandji procedure, may prevent dislocation of the radial head when there is a solitary distal ulnar osteochondroma and progressive bowing of the radius. Early detection of radial head subluxation can be remedied with correction of the ulna via distraction osteogenesis. All children with MHE should be screened for intraspinal osteochondromas with serial neurologic examinations. We also obtain a screening MRI of the spine once the child is old enough to undergo imaging without sedation.

CRITICAL POINTS: Multiple Hereditary Exostoses
- MHE is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with high penetrance. Cartilaginous exostoses (osteochondromas) grow from the physes of long bones and from the pelvis, ribs, scapula, and vertebrae.
- Most exostoses are asymptomatic and do not require removal. Early removal of osteochondromas with corrective osteotomies to retard or pre vent progressive growth disturbances is controversial.
- Large exostoses may cause local pain from irritation of surrounding tissues. Sim ple removal is recommended. The risk of recurrence is higher in younger children.
- Forearm deformities are common in MHE and are based on the location of the osteochondromas.
- Hemiepiphyseal stapling of the radial side of the distal radius retards radial growth and allows correction of the radial articular angle and ulnar length discrepancy with growth.
- Ulnar lengthening may be performed in a single stage or by using gradual distraction osteogenesis. Differential lengthening combined with angular correction can be used to prevent or reduce the radial head.
- Malignant transformation is uncommon.

## Case Studies

### CASE STUDY 38.1 Use of Precentralization Distraction in the Treatment of Radial Longitudinal Deficiency

A boy with left type 4 radial deficiency was initially seen at 2 months of age. Stretching and splinting were instituted when he was 1½ years old (eFig. 38.1), and radiographs were taken at that time (eFig. 38.2). Treatment began with preliminary distraction (eFig. 38.3). Next, centralization was performed (eFig. 38.4). The final result is shown in eFig. 38.5. Pollicization was then performed (eFig. 38.6). The 2-year outcome is shown in eFig. 38.7.
