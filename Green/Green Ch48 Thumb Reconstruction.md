The thumb is the most important digit, and its loss causes up to 40% impairment of the hand. There are many things that a surgeon should know about the beauty of this most crucial digit. However, as this book is eminently surgical, and my space is limited, the discussion of this chapter is orientated to surgery and reconstruction of the thumb. I have divided it into four parts: terminal defects, central defects, first web problems, and the management of devastating injuries. The aim is to give the reader an idea of how to approach an injured thumb.

The first consideration at the time of reconstruction is to set what we want to accomplish. The response is obvious, a normal thumb, and we should stress that there is nothing like the original thumb. Not surprisingly, Haas et al. evaluated long- term subjective and functional scores of thumb replantation and found that no reconstructive procedure could be compared with a successful replantation. They underlined that it is mandatory to attempt replantation in every amputation. However, reconstruction is many times necessary. Littler, while writing about the thumb reconstruction has said that “it is not on the length, strength or mobility of the thumb but on the strategic position of the thumb and the sensation of the fingertip where rests the power of the human hand.” This axiom is very true for proximal amputations, as we will discuss later, but without doubt the most important issue we should consider is to provide a thumb without pain. If the thumb is painful, the patient will not use it no matter how perfect it may appear. Similarly, the length is also important: a short thumb will have difficulties grabbing large objects and picking up smaller ones. When set by the side of the index, the ideal thumb is about 0.5 cm shorter than the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint of the index. Longer thumbs will look grotesque. By the same token, the surgeon should strive for a shorter thumb when the other digits are missing to make it possible to oppose with them.

Shorter thumbs are also preferred when the joints are stiff, as the neothumb will be more exposed to trauma when making a fist.

Before starting to manage any thumb injury, it is worth stressing the importance of not adding scars that may hinder the definitive reconstruction. If the surgeon is unsure about what to offer, the wisest thing is to clean the wound and debride devitalized tissues and send the patient to a specialized center. One should avoid any attempt at primary closure if this implies sacrificing important tissue, particularly functioning joints. If this is not feasible, a good alternative is to cover the stump with a pedicled groin flap or another flap as this will prevent the sacrifice of structures that may be crucial for a latter reconstruction and will give time for proper planning.

In the literature there are a myriad of procedures dealing with thumb reconstruction, but before a method is selected, the surgeon should consider the obvious: what the patient’s needs and wishes are, if the patient is fit for a long operative procedure, and if the surgeon is competent to provide what is needed. Some of the procedures discussed in this chapter rank highly in terms of complexity and are thus risky, while others are more accessible to all of us and therefore safe. Through the years I have seen that the main reason for failure is due to attempting to do too much, that is, searching for the ideal when you are not prepared to do so. So, if I can give a piece of advice, it would be this: do not set too high a goal for your reconstruction. A successful reconstruction will spur you on to go for more, and the contrary will occur if you fail. On the other hand, there will be surgeons who are so skilled that they will find even the more complex reconstructions presented here too easy. In my defense, this book is targeted at beginners and those with limited experience. Too much philosophy, and I have just said this was not my intention. Let’s get down to work!

## TERMINAL DEFECTS

There are many classifications available describing the different levels of amputation: Campbell- Reid, Lister, Hentz, and Merle are among the most popular. These classifications are very precise, but the problem is remembering which grade/number corresponds to which level. I must admit that despite being an expert in the matter, to remember them is beyond my mental capacity. Furthermore, none of them includes the important prognostic modifiers for the sake of not becoming overwhelmingly complicated, ending up being useless for correct surgical planning. Dr. Strickland in the first edition of this book used a simple classification, which I will endeavor to stick to (Fig. 48.1),albeit I should stress that it lacks significant prognostic value. Taking all this into account, what I find more useful is to define the exact anatomic location of the amputation. This requires little mental effort and is easily understood by all. However, length is just one important factor in choosing an option for reconstruction, and the surgeon should also consider whether there is thenar damage, the quality of the skin, whether the nerves are avulsed, and the functionality of the joints proximal to the amputation. All these facts together will determine the correct surgical choice.

### Partial or Total Amputations of the Distal Third

#### Soft Tissue Defects

Pulp injuries are extremely common. Preserving length is a priority but the surgeon should also strive to provide a pulp covered with sensitive skin, without scarring and with good cushioning. If the pulp is painful, the patient will skip using the thumb and opt for the homolateral index (as in a cigarette pinching) or simply use the other hand for any delicate activity (Fig. 48.2).

![[Fig.48.2.png]]
> **Fig. 48.2** Despite adequate lengthening after bone distraction, the patient skips her painful thumb when pinching. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Immediate coverage of the wound has the highest priority to prevent further tissue desiccation and length loss. In recent years we have witnessed considerable advances in flap design, owing to better understanding of the blood supply to the thumb. This has led to crossfinger flaps and many distant flaps being either reserved for extreme situations or plainly discarded, and thus they will not be discussed here. Below, we will deal with the most effective local flaps for these defects.

**Volar advancement ﬂap (Moberg).** Moberg described a flap based on both proper neurovascular bundles for coverage of palmar defects of the pulp. The flap permits coverage of moderate- sized defects, providing at the same time sensitive skin.

Elevation. The flap is elevated under tourniquet. Midaxial incisions are made over the radial and ulnar sides of the thumb and extended proximally from the lesion to the proximal thumb crease. Placement of the incisions across the dorsal apices of the flexion creases facilitates dissection dorsal to the neurovascular bundles. The palmar flap is carefully dissected from the underlying flexor tendon sheath; the flap should include subcutaneous tissue and both neurovascular bundles (Fig. 48.3). The mobility of the flap is tested by distal traction to determine whether any additional proximal undermining or releases are necessary. If tension is too great and good coverage of the pulp requires more than 45 degrees of flexion of the interphalangeal (IP) joint, transforming the flap into a true island or combing the advancement with a “V- Y” flap or Z- plasty in the lateral incisions can give some extra length.

![[Fig.48.3.png]]
> **Fig. 48.3** A to C, The flap is raised through two midaxial incisions. Both neurovascular bundles are included in the flap. Some flexion contracture is permitted temporarily for closure. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

It is said that defects up to 2 cm (about half the pulp length) can be covered with this flap; however, this is often at the price of causing a flexion contracture of the IP joint that can result in a painful thumb and a beaked nail deformity. Problems will be encountered when asking too much of the flap (Fig. 48.4).

**Dorsoulnar homodigital ﬂap.** Brunelli et al. studied the layout of the arteries on the dorsal surface of the thumb. Their constant disposition and anastomoses permitted raising distally based flaps safely from the dorsoulnar region of the metacarpophalangeal joint.

This flap is based on the ulnar dorsal digital artery of the thumb, which, as its radial counterpart, has a peculiar anatomy (Fig. 48.5). The artery originates from the palmar digital artery of the thumb at the head of the first metacarpal. The artery continues in the suprafascial layer distally, about 1 cm from the proximal phalanx midline, ending up as a contributor to the nail fold arterial arcade. During its short course, it has two constant anastomoses that allow this flap to rotate in two different spots. The first pivot point is located at the neck of the proximal phalanx (2.5 cm proximal to the cuticle); the second is located at the level of the nail fold arcade (0.9 to 1 cm proximal to the cuticle). While the first point of rotation permits the flap to reach dorsal thumb defects, the distal one allows the flap to cover the pulp and the nail.

![[Fig.48.5.png]]
> **Fig. 48.5** Arterial disposition of the dorsal arteries of the thumb. Note that the radial dorsal digital artery only reaches the nail fold arcade in 20% of the cases, while on the ulnar side, this connection is constant. (Copyright © 2020, Dr F. Piñal.)

Elevation. Understanding the anatomy is crucial for safe elevation. In all its path, the artery runs superficially within the subcutaneous tissue, adjacent to the fascia. The flap is outlined on the ulnar surface of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint of the thumb and elevated in a subfascial plane. If necessary, the branch of the radial nerve is identified and dissected proximally for later reconnection. The skin over the pedicle is elevated in a subdermal plane. Then, a generous subcutaneous pedicle about 1 cm wide, including one or two veins, is raised to protect the artery and permit safe outflow. The flap is rotated at the chosen pivot point, taking care to avoid any tension during the rotation process. As venous outflow may be a problem, tight skin closure over the pedicle or tunneling of the flap is currently not recommended. The ulnar dorsal digital nerve can be included to be coapted to one of the volar digital nerves if necessary.

Several variations of this flap have been presented, including an adipofascial extension to reduce donor site morbidity and the possibility of carrying bone, probably in doubtful conditions.

Irrespective of the fact that this flap represents a considerable advance in thumb coverage (Fig. 48.6), and it obviates the need for cross- finger flaps and all two- stage methods, it should, nevertheless, be used with caution. The surgeon should only raise the flap when the area of the cross anastomoses is intact, otherwise arterial failure will occur. Furthermore, the flap suffers from venous congestion if the skin over the pedicle is closed; skin- grafting is thus recommended.In addition, because the skin at the site of the flap is elevated and is quite loose—nature provided it to permit thumb abduction—the surgeon may be tempted to close primarily excessively large donor sites. This will cause iatrogenic first web contracture and reduced MCP joint motion. Skin grafting is again recommended if large flaps are raised.

A major drawback of this flap is the lack of sensory recovery, even when the dorsal branch of the radial nerve is connected to the ulnar digital nerve. For this reason, the flap is not recommended when there are defects involving more than 50% of the pulp. However, it may be indicated for larger defects in the elderly, where sensory recovery is frequently poor irrespective of the reconstructive option selected. For these reasons, this flap should probably only be utilized by those with experience in its application.

**Dorsoradial homodigital ﬂap.** A similar flap based on the radial dorsal digital artery was described by Moschella and Cordova to solve radial and dorsal thumb defects.

This flap’s artery originates from the radial artery itself at the level of the anatomic snuffbox and passes deep to the extensor pollicis brevis tendon. It travels over the proximal phalanx at about 1 cm from the midline in the suprafascial plane. The rotation point of this flap is always the middle of the proximal phalanx. The reason for this is that it has a different arterial communicating pattern than the ulnar counterpart. Indeed, it constantly communicates with the palmar vasculature at the level of the middle third of the proximal phalanx (see Fig. 48.5), but the communication with the distal arcade is present in only about 20%.

![[Fig.48.5.png]]
> **Fig. 48.5** Arterial disposition of the dorsal arteries of the thumb. Note that the radial dorsal digital artery only reaches the nail fold arcade in 20% of the cases, while on the ulnar side, this connection is constant. (Copyright © 2020, Dr F. Piñal.)

Elevation. The skin island to be transferred is drawn on the axis of the artery. The flap can be quite large (up to 3 × 4 cm) and still achieve primary closure, as there is no risk of contracture. As for the dorsoulnar flap, a sufficiently wide strip of subcutaneous tissue must be included in the pedicle. Likewise, visible larger veins should be preserved to secure adequate venous drainage of the flap. Tight closure over the pedicle is to be avoided and skin grafts should be used freely. One recommendation is to add a cutaneous tail to facilitate direct skin closure after rotation of the flap.

Although a branch of the radial nerve can be included and sutured, the originators did not recommend this flap for major pulp defects. Notwithstanding, the flap is a quick and safe solution for many lateral and dorsal defects of the thumb.

**First dorsal metacarpal ﬂap (kite ﬂap).** One of the most popularly employed procedures for thumb defects is the first dorsal metacarpal artery (FDMA) flap, also known as the “kite flap,” described by Foucher and Braun in 1978. This flap is an evolution of the innervated crossfinger flap described by Hilgenfeldt and refined by Holevich. The flap was devised for dorsal defects and as a vein carrier for thumb replantation. However, the fact that it can include a sensory branch from the radial nerve has made it useful for covering palmar defects, particularly in older people.

The arterial supply of the flap is based on the radial dorsal artery of the index. This branch originates from the FDMA, which takes off from the radial artery between the crossing of the extensor pollicis longus and its penetration into the apex of the first interosseus space.

The radial dorsal artery of the index is constant and travels on top of, or at times in, the aponeurosis of the first dorsal interosseous muscle. After providing a few branches, including one to the joint, the artery terminates supplying vascularity the dorsal skin of the proximal phalanx. There are a number of subcutaneous veins that can be included with the flap and constantly some sensory branches from the radial nerve that end in the proximal phalanx.

Elevation. A pattern matching the size of the defect is drawn on the dorsum of the index finger proximal phalanx. A lazy “S” incision is performed on a line drawn from the apex of the web to the radial side of the index finger (Fig. 48.7). The flaps are elevated deep to the dermis from side to side, thus keeping the vessels and nerves together in a subcutaneous pedicle. The skin on the dorsum of the proximal phalanx is incised and the flap elevated with care to leave the peritenon in place, otherwise an ungraftable bed will result. The subcutaneous pedicle is raised in a proximal direction to the pivot point in the apex of the web. During the dissection of the pedicle, no attempt must be made to skeletonize the artery, and in fact a strip of fascia of the dorsal interosseus should be included for its protection. Often the vessel can be seen in the undersurface after the flap is fully raised. Once the flap is fully mobilized, it is transposed to the thumb, customarily through a generous subcutaneous tunnel. The flap is sutured in position and the incisions are closed loosely on the dorsum of the hand. The tourniquet is now released. I prefer to assess the vascularity of the flap only after it has been sutured onto its final position. Doing otherwise will not rule out the possibility of later necrosis due to pedicle kinking by a fascial band or constriction in the tunnel. After hemostasis of the bed, a fullthickness skin graft is applied and dressed under a tie- over bolus on the proximal phalanx. I keep this dressing for 7 to 10 days unless drainage or inflammation appear.

The flap is particularly indicated for dorsal thumb defects. The use of the flap in volar defects is controversial. According to Foucher and Braun, thumb pulp defects “will not be well served” by the kite flap because of the problem of cortical reorientation. Trankle et al., however, found this not to be a problem in their series, and in fact in a later publication the authors consider this flap first choice for thumb pulp defects. Others have applied the kite flap for small defects in the palm or in the first web.

I do not consider this flap applicable for pulp defects, but probably it is a suitable indication in older people. The donor site can be (if large) a source of hypertrophic scarring and painful ulcerations. To prevent this from happening the flap should not trespass the MCP or PIP joint creases; furthermore, the peritenon should be left intact. The kite flap plays an indisputable starring role for dorsal thumb defects combined with venous outflow difficulties (Fig. 48.8).

**Heterodigital island ﬂap (Littler ﬂap).** Although in purity the kite flap could also be considered an heterodigital island flap, the name is reserved for the flap described by Littler.

The flap is taken from the ulnar aspect of the middle finger and transposed under a tunnel to the pulp area of the thumb, based on its neurovascular bundle, thus providing sensibility to the pulp. Classically, the flap was the last stage after a neo- thumb reconstruction with a tube flap, usually an osteocutaneous flap from the groin, to provide sensibility to the palmar aspect of the “thumb.” Hence, the technique of raising the extended flap is represented in the books for such an indication (Fig. 48.9).

![[Fig.48.9.png]]
> **Fig. 48.9** A, An extended island flap is designed on the ulnar side of the long finger with an identical defect pattern over the palmar aspect of the thumb. B, Mobilization of the island flap on its neurovascular pedicle, and preparation of the recipient bed on the thumb. C, Completed transfer of the neurovascular island flap with a full-thickness skin graft on the donor area of the long finger. Care must be taken to avoid a longitudinal scar along the palmar aspect of the donor finger. Note the area of undermining in the palm. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Elevation. Under tourniquet, the flap is outlined on the ulnar aspect of the middle finger, according to the defect’s needs. Small darts are created at the IP creases, and the flap may extend dorsally beyond the midlateral line of the finger. The neurovascular bundle to the web space is identified by means of a zigzag incision. No attempt is made to skeletonize the artery during the dissection, but on the contrary some fat should be preserved around the pedicle as the venous drainage occurs through minute veins around the artery. The digital artery to the radial side of the ring finger is identified and ligated. The digital nerve to the ring finger is carefully teased apart with microsurgical instruments, well into the midpalm. The artery and nerve to the ulnar side of the donor finger have now been isolated as far as the base of the flap. Incisions are now made around the entire periphery of the flap, until fully islanded in the neurovascular pedicle. During elevation of the flap, utmost care should be taken to preserve the sheath of the flexors and the peritenon of the extensor, as otherwise the bed may not be graftable. The recipient site is prepared to accept the flap that is passed through a generous tunnel. It is recommended to put a suture on the tip of the flap, passing it through the tunnel, and pulling from the other side, sliding the flap under the bridge of the palm. This is a risky maneuver and the author has witnessed the avulsion of the flap from its pedicle while entangled in the tunnel. To prevent this from happening, the tunnel should be made large and the flap, rather than being pulled from the thumb position, should be pushed, with a mosquito, into the thumb’s recipient site. Like any island flap, twisting of the pedicle or tension will not be tolerated. It is thus important to create some redundancy of the pedicle and a generous subcutaneous pocket to prevent tension and, consequently, ensure flap survival.

As for other flaps, the flap is loosely tagged in place, the wounds partly closed, and the tourniquet released. Any bleeders are coagulated, and the vascularity of the flap is assessed. Completion of flap suture and repair of the palmar and proximal digital wounds are carried out at this time. A full- thickness skin graft taken from the medial aspect of the forearm, arm, or groin is sutured onto the donor defect in a tie- over bolus manner. After bandaging, the flap is left exposed for monitoring, and the donor site is left untouched for 7 to 10 days.

The flap is safe and superb in many respects but also has problems and limitations. The most important drawback comes from the fact that this is a sensitive flap, but the nerve is the one of the middle finger. According to Littler, there was no need to connect any nerve, as the patient will learn to reorient the sensibility from the middle finger to the thumb. We, as several others, have found that patients may be permanently disoriented, and might not use the thumb due to this lack of proper sensibility. For this reason, provided there are nerves available, it is wiser to perform a neurorrhaphy between the recipient thumb nerves and the nerve of the flap. The most common untoward finding after using this flap is flexion contracture of the donor middle finger. This complication is more frequent when a large flap has been raised, the skin graft has not taken well, or poor aftercare. Foam- padded aluminum splints maintaining the PIP joint in extension during nighttime are mandatory to prevent this. Venous problems may occur with the Littler flap, particularly when a large flap has been raised and some fat around the pedicle is not preserved. To avert this, a dorsal vein can be included and connected in the recipient site, but this is not necessary for the standard size flap. If the pedicle is insufficiently dissected, the flap will only reach the thumb with difficulty, potentially causing a painful band across the palm. To prevent this from happening the surgeon has to release the neurovascular pedicle as needed for a tension- free transposition. When facing the problem secondarily, the treatment consists of severing the nerve at the base of the thumb and connecting it to the thumb’s digital nerve. The proximal stump of the middle digital nerve is transposed to the interosseus space. This problem will be exacerbated if the flap is raised from the ulnar side of the ring finger. This later version was recommended to bring sensibility when the median nerve was damaged (an unheard- of situation in the current days of microsurgery). This author has come across, while raising this flap, the setting where the common digital nerve is pierced by the common digital artery (known as Hartmann loop). In this particular scenario, the nerve, not the artery, should be divided and repaired during the dissection. Doing otherwise will endanger the venous outflow of the flap.

In summary, I find this flap indicated for the patient who has bone exposed and who does not want to have a more complex reconstruction (Fig. 48.10). To avoid cortical disorientation the nerve should be connected to the thumb ulnar digital nerve, and this takes only minimal additional time. If the surgery is planned correctly, the dorsal branch of the digital nerve can be preserved and the proximal stump of the ulnar digital nerve of the middle finger transposed to the intermetacarpal space away from trauma (see Fig. 48.10C). In my view, another residual indication may be to provide sensation after an osteoplastic reconstruction of a missing thumb. Chen and Noordhoff have raised bilobed flaps for covering large defects but at the price of an excessively high donor site morbidity.

#### Nail Defects

Not only is the nail an important cosmetic element but it also allows us to pick up small objects and offers stability to the pulp when pinching (see Chapter 9). Hence, loss of the nail itself may be seen by the patient as an important handicap. Microvascular reconstruction seems to be the only method that produces consistent results.

The problem of nail loss is compounded because it is often associated with bone loss. Hence, a length problem is added to the already functional and cosmetic issue. If the entire sterile matrix is lost the nail will grow short and will lack adherence. Any accidental snagging of the free edge will cause painful lifting of the nail plate. The patient may wish to have the nail removed completely to prevent this from happening. An alternative is to transfer the sterile matrix and the bone from the hallux as a free onycho- osteocutaneous flap. Remarkably, the matrix of the toe will “meld” with the remaining thumb nail (Fig. 48.11).

#### Total Distal Amputation

While there are a plethora of papers conceding that functional loss is negligible for amputation distal to the IP joint of the thumb, my experience is radically different. Patients whose thumb is amputated at the distal phalanx perform delicate tasks poorly. They also expressed a deep concern for the cosmetic outcome. Furthermore, the locking effect of the thumb distal phalanx (the so- called vice grip, as termed by Buncke and Valauri) is also lost. Several surgeons have published on the salutary effect of reconstruction of these defects with minimal donor site morbidity at the foot.

Notwithstanding that the technique of great toe dissection is discussed thoroughly in the previous chapter, there are some technical particularities worth emphasizing when dealing with minihallux flaps. The ipsilateral hallux is preferred as this permits the dominant artery and nerve to be oriented along the ulnar side of the thumb. Veins are dissected first, and this is greatly facilitated if there is some blood in the veins; hence, we elevate the tourniquet without applying an Esmarch bandage. The inclusion of the whole nail greatly facilitates harvesting because large dorsal veins forming proximal to the eponychial fold can be included. On the other hand, harvesting a partial nail obliges one to dissect the tiny veins of the lateral aspect of the pulp that are minute, fragile, and easily torn (Fig. 48.12). Regarding arterial inflow, in most cases I only take the peroneal (lateral) digital artery in the first web space. This speeds up harvesting at the price of a smaller vessel for anastomosis. Most importantly, it minimizes donor site morbidity. Utmost care is taken to identify, isolate, and ligate a constant branch proximal to the neck of the proximal phalanx. If this is missed or avulsed, the blood supply to the transfer may be endangered (see Fig. 48.12, *arrow* ).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: DISTAL AMPUTATIONS

In the most common type of distal amputation a part of the sterile matrix is still preserved. In these cases, efforts to preserve length and to provide sensitive, nonpainful cover is the goal. For small and moderate pulp defects, without fractures, the conservative approach of Mennen and Wiese is a simple and inexpensive method that can procure miraculous results (Fig. 48.14). The stump is covered with a semiocclusive transparent adhesive dressing after cleaning and debridement, which is changed weekly. The patient is allowed to use the thumb freely. The patient should be warned that the foul odor is normal and of the importance of keeping the dressing in place while the healing process takes place. The wound should heal in around 6 weeks, usually with minimal scarring. Complications are quite unusual.

When a fracture is exposed in the wound, I will choose a flap for coverage. I will also opt for a flap for slightly larger pulp defects (transverse and proximal oblique amputations). In these instances, the classical Atasoy and Shepard flaps, or modifications, and the Moberg flap all have a place. Choice is based more on preferences and the experience of the surgeon, since all flaps are equally effective.

Larger pulp defects (more than 50% of the pulp lost) may benefit from the Moberg flap, but the risk of flexion contracture is high. In my experience, only the Litter flap (“reconnecting” the nerve) is a reasonable alternative, but the potential damage done to the middle finger must be taken into account. My first choice in the fit individual (see below) is a peroneal hemipulp transfer (see Fig. 48.4). The procedure may at first glance seem excessive, but this alternative does not impair existing hand function, and with a busy microsurgical team the procedure can be performed without a lengthy operating time, with very low failure rates. The Litter flap is my first choice for patients not willing to undergo a complex microsurgical procedure.

The Brunelli and the Moschella flaps do not provide sensitive skin, and for this reason, except in older adults, they are not recommended for pulp loss. The dorsoradial flap is ideal for radial defects, while both are equally good for covering dorsal ones.

Amputations proximal to the sterile matrix will produce problems with nail adherence, which will cause painful lifting of the nail: this, in turn, will make the patient seek a definite solution. While total nail resection and a flap can be an alternative, partial toe is my preferred choice. Flap elevation can be accomplished in 30 to 40 minutes, and surprisingly the nail will grow uniformly (see Fig. 48.11).

In more proximal amputations, the classic teaching that patients compensate for the loss and do not have any functional impairment with amputation distal to the IP joint is, in my experience, a fallacy. These patients, in fact, perform poorly and are deeply concerned about the loss. For this reason, they are much better served by a customized hallux transfer, which yields high satisfaction and minimal donor site morbidity. In my practice, they are indicated not only for musicians and “delicate young ladies” but also for manual workers and for any patient willing to have a near perfect thumb and who understands the procedure. I do not make any distinction between people who smoke or drink, are overweight, have diabetes, or are older. My only proviso is that they can withstand the operation. Smokers are counseled on the negative effect of smoking as a trigger of spasm and vascular problems. They are advised to refrain from smoking in the immediate postoperative period, and usually they do well for a couple of weeks or less, but once they feel the critical period is over, they go back to their old ways. Despite this unselected population the probability of failure in my hands has been very low (3 cases in more than 500 toe- to- hand transfers performed by the author).

That being said, partial toe transfers are not for beginners. Apart from the need for familiarity with handling small vessels and anastomosis, to achieve good cosmetic results the dissection has to go to the tip of the hallux to reduce the bone in the sagittal and coronal planes, as the great toe is much larger than the thumb (Fig. 48.15).

As a final caveat, I strongly recommend early transfer for distal amputation cases, not only for the sake of preserving exposed tissue but also for smooth and straightforward surgery. Furthermore, early coverage of the stump—with the toe—allows one to preserve structures without the need for temporary flaps. Contrarily, if the toe transfer is delayed, subsequent scarring will make dissection more difficult, tissue will prove unyielding, and, what is worse, the local arteries will tend to go into unremitting spasm.

### Amputations in the Middle Third

Assuming that the thumb is covered by nonpainful sensitive skin, its length is the most crucial consideration: All efforts should be directed to restore as close to the normal length as possible (i.e., 0.5 cm proximal to the PIP joint of the index when set by the side). Length will directly affect the functional capabilities of the thumb to the point that at the metacarpal head thumb function will be totally absent (40% to 60% impairment of the hand).

There are several methods to reconstruct a thumb at this level, the most valuable are phalangization, bone lengthening, osteoplastic reconstruction, pollicization/on- top plasty, and toe transfer. The last has been discussed thoroughly in the previous chapter and will not be dealt with here. Others, such as the time- honored “cocked- hat” of Gillies, are out of the armamentarium in the current Internet era.

#### Phalangization

Phalangization consists of increasing the working length of the remaining thumb stump by deepening the first web. This is achieved by a combination of Z- plasty and the release of the fascia of the adductor and of the first dorsal interosseous (Fig. 48.16). The lengthening effect can be increased further by releasing the first dorsal interosseus completely from the first metacarpal and transferring the insertion of the adductor more proximally on the metacarpal. How to design the Z- plasty will be discussed in detail in the online section.

Currently, phalangization plays a minor role as a sole procedure and has been surpassed largely by other methods. Nevertheless, it still has a role in distal amputations (see Fig. 48.10) or combined with other lengthening methods, such as mini toe transfers or bone distraction.

#### Bone Distraction

Distraction of the first metacarpal was introduced by Ivan Matev in the late 1960s. Initially only the first metacarpal was lengthened, but refinements to the technique and devices permitted its application for phalanges also.

The procedure of distraction osteogenesis *(callotasis)* uses the principle of slow distraction through an area of healing fracture callus. It requires, as prerequisites, a thumb stump with good coverage and patient understanding of the importance of daily care and accepting that the whole process may take about 3 to 4 months. Matev claimed that patients with two- thirds of the metacarpal would be eligible for the operation. However, taking into account that only about 3 cm of length can be obtained, the very best result in that scenario would be a ridiculous stump. Therefore only when the whole metacarpal, or better, a part of the proximal phalanx is present, should this procedure be considered.

**Technique.** After installation of the device, a transverse osteotomy is performed in the metacarpal. Seven days later, lengthening is begun at a rate of 1 mm/day. Once the desired length is achieved, a number of options are available: The gap can be filled with an iliac crest bone graft, a bridging plate can be placed while the callus strengthens, or the device can be kept in place until bone forms in the gap (Fig. 48.17). If a remnant of the proximal phalanx exists, the MCP joint should be pinned in extension during the whole process, as otherwise a flexion contracture of the proximal phalanx will occur.

![[Fig.48.17.png]]

C, Gradual widening of the midmetacarpal gap by distraction of the external fixation device. D, Iliac bone grafting of the defect with Kirschner wire fixation is performed at a second-stage operation after the desired length has been achieved. Bone distraction may be better than nothing, but the procedure at best procures a poor thumb replica with no nail, no pulp, and usually a thumb that is still too short. Furthermore, pain when pinching may be an issue (see Fig. 48.2). Last but not least, the patient should understand the procedure and the need for their cooperation to avoid complications. Pain at the carpometacarpal joint, secondary to pressure on the joint during the distraction, may require the process to be abandoned. As a matter of fact, early degeneration of this joint may be an issue in younger patients (see Fig. 48.23C, later).

Despite these drawbacks, metacarpal lengthening has the advantage of retaining the native innervation and, apart from time, requires little effort on the patient’s part, except for a commitment to ensure pin care.

In summary, callotasis of the first metacarpal may be indicated in older individuals, as the procedure is easy and function may be improved. The paradox is that this age group, who may benefit the most, has the least bone- forming potential and the highest incidence of basal joint degeneration. Complications of bone healing and the need for additional grafting are thus the norm, prolonging an already protracted process. Furthermore, pain at the base of the thumb may force the procedure to be aborted.

#### Osteoplastic Reconstruction

Osteoplastic reconstruction was popular in the 20th century; however, today it is used less frequently. As primarily devised by Nicoladoni and others, it consisted of covering a piece of bone graft along with a tubed flap, typically from the abdomen. The main problems were bone resorption and lack of sensibility. Successive improvements were added by several pioneers, such as using a tricortical bone graft from the iliac crest rigidly fixed to decrease the degree of resorption, using the groin flap, and adding a neurovascular flap to provide sensibility. The procedure is summarized in Fig. 48.18, and the technical details of the operation (groin flap elevation) are discussed in Chapter 44.

> **Fig. 48.18** Osteoplastic reconstruction of the thumb. A, Thumb amputation through the metacarpophalangeal joint. B, Site of removal of extraperiosteal tricorticocancellous iliac crest bone graft. C, Preparation of the recipient site with insertion of the iliac crest graft into the first metacarpal, shown here with Kirschner wire fixation. D, Bone graft coverage with a tubed groin flap. E and F, An extended neurovascular island pedicle flap provided vascularized and innervated skin on the palmar aspect of the reconstructed thumb (see also Fig. 48.9). (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

The procedure has the undeniable advantage of not sacrificing another finger, but it still had several drawbacks, among which are a long process, the need for two operative procedures, poor cosmesis, and cortical confusion related to the Littler flap. Even classic authors recommended that osteoplastic reconstruction be abandoned, owing to the poor results and the need for several operations.

The operation has a limited place in the armamentarium particularly if one adds the improvements to be discussed below. In order to shorten the lengthy process, Biemer and Stock and Foucher et al.independently published the feasibility of reconstructing the frame of the thumb by means of distally based radial forearm osteocutaneous flap. The sensibility was provided by connecting the forearm nerves to the ones in the thumb or by a Littler flap. Despite the payback of a shorter procedure, the quality of the reconstruction was pretty much the same as the classic osteoplastic approach. Furthermore, bone resorption of this marginally vascularized bone included in the flap was still an issue.

Lin et al. proposed a 21st- century osteoplastic reconstruction. In their technique an osteocutaneous flap pedicled from the groin was followed in a second stage by a toe transfer, thus reducing the need of more soft tissue and bone from the foot. In this way, resorption of bone moved in the first stage was no longer an issue, as it was attached to vascularized bone on both ends after transfer of the toe. However, the reconstruction was still lengthy, as even when using an osteocutaneous flap, a period of 3 to 6 months was allowed for the flap to mature and for the bone to heal before the toe could be transferred.

#### Pollicization/On- Top Plasty

If the loss of the thumb was proximal to the metacarpal head, several authors have recommended transferring an intact finger to replace the thumb—the so- called pollicization. Littler introduced pollicization of the index as a neurovascular pedicle method of digital transposition in 1953. He stressed that Gosset, from France, was perhaps the first to use the neurovascular pedicle finger transfer concept in 1949. BuckGramcko refined the technique of pollicization in congenital thumb hypoplasia. The technical details of the operation can be found in Chapter 37; however, there are some differences in the present scenario worth emphasizing. First of all, after trauma, it is not uncommon for muscles and skin to be scarred and useless. Both need to be replaced at the same time or prior to the procedure, as we will discuss later. Furthermore, excessive tendon length, secondary to the reposition, will not adapt as in the child. Repair of the proximal stump of the flexor profundus of the index to the flexor pollicis longus stump in the forearm should be done as a routine. In this group of patients where the saddle joint is preserved, the length of finger transferred should match a normal thumb (Fig. 48.19).

![[Fig.48.19.png]]
> **Fig. 48.19** A, A zigzag incision has been made in the web. The amount of bone to be resected is crosshatched. B, The pollicization completed. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Hilgenfeldt, Gosset, and Brunelli recommended the ring or the middle finger as the donor for pollicization, arguing that removing the index will deprive the patient of performing fine pinch activities.The discussion is somewhat outdated as the middle finger rapidly assumes its neo- index role. Most importantly, pollicization of a ring or middle finger is much more difficult and may jeopardize the transplant vascularization due to crossover of vessels and tendons. Hence, at this point in time, there is no place for such a transfer. Similarly, most authors would agree that pollicization is not indicated when there is more than the thumb missing. However, when there is an index stump available, based on the same principles of neurovascular finger transfer, Kelleher et al. introduced the so- called on- top plasty. In this operation, a portion of the damaged index is used to lengthen the short thumb. As an aside the procedure serves to increase the first web span as well. Good results have been reported.

Pollicization and on- top plasty are not indicated in cases of concomitant palmar trauma with suspected neurovascular injuries. In these instances, the procedure may be too risky, as, even if patent, the arteries may be damaged during the dissection, and a disastrous outcome can be anticipated if microsurgery capabilities are not available. Admittedly, one could transfer the index based on a dorsal skin pedicle, much as Nicoladoni used to do in the late 18th century. However, the result would be no more than an insensitive post.

**On- top plasty of a middle ﬁnger stump.** While pollicizing an intact ring or middle finger is an unwise operation, the same cannot be said for a stump in the same location. Not only will this stump help rebuild the thumb, but a hindrance to hand function will be removed. Furthermore, the procedure adds length to the thumb and can also restore motion at the MCP joint if this is missing.

The technique of pollicization of any finger or on- top plasty follows very similar steps; however, while the neurovascular structures fall into place for the index (see Chapter 37), dangerous crossovers will occur when transferring any other stump. To prevent that from occurring several steps should be taken. The more complex central stump on- top plasty is described here, and slight modifications should be made for any other case (Fig. 48.20).

![[Fig.48.20.png]]
> **Fig. 48.20** A to F, Author’s recommended technique for pollicization of a middle finger stump. Note that the nerves of the middle finger are “reconnected” to the nerve of the thumb. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Technique. The skin incisions are V- shaped dorsally and in a zigzag manner volarly to avoid a retractile scar when closing the palmar creases. The stump is raised on both common digital arteries. Before severing the ulnar digital artery to the index, that artery should be clamped and the tourniquet released to make sure that the radial artery of the index is competent. If not, the middle finger segment should be based only on the third common digital artery. The dissection of the stump itself is carried out as a standard ray resection. The tendinous insertions of the second and third dorsal interosseous are included with the transplant. The bone is cut with an oscillating saw at the desired level. In order to avoid dangerous crossovers of neurovascular structures, the dominant flexor tendon on the stump (usually the sublimis) is divided and retrieved distally. Similarly, the stump of the extensor digitorum communis- 3 is divided. The venous drainage of the whole flap can be compromised if dependent on the vena comitantes only. It is thus mandatory to include a dorsal vein from the web to be reconnected at the recipient thumb site. Such veins are very large, can be anastomosed with loupes, and will prevent venous congestion of the flap. Once fully elevated on the volar neurovascular bundles, a generous subcutaneous tunnel is created to pass the stump across the palm. This can be quite difficult due to the septations attaching the skin to the underlying bony structure, and some would open the skin where the pedicles would run, elevate flaps on both sides, and loosely close at the end of the case. Adjustments in bony length should be made in such a manner that at least motion at the MCP is restored in the neo- thumb. Fixation is carried out with one of the available plates, 90- 90 wires, or cannulated screws. The stump of the sublimis is retrieved proximally with a tendon passer through the path of the flexor pollicis longus in the carpal canal and sutured to the stump of the flexor pollicis longus in the wrist. In this level of amputation some thenar muscles are still available: The adductor muscle is sutured to the ulnar interosseus and the abductor pollicis brevis- opponens to the radial one. If possible, the ulnar digital nerve of the stump is sutured to the ulnar digital nerve of the thumb if available, in order to prevent cortical confusion.Dorsally, the tendon is sutured to the extensor pollicis longus, and the vein anastomosed to a local vein.

I prefer to close the midpalmar space by transferring the index finger on top of the middle finger metacarpal. To do so, the index metacarpal is cut with an oscillating saw at the same level as the osteotomy of the third metacarpal. The proximal index metacarpal is resected, leaving the base intact so as not to disturb the insertions of the extensor carpi radialis longus and flexor carpi radialis. The index is rigidly fixed by means of a longitudinal and an oblique cannulated screw.Finally, the excess palmar skin is resected in such a way that a linear scar is avoided.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: AMPUTATIONS IN THE MIDDLE THIRD

As for any type of thumb loss, primary use of a damaged/amputated finger is the best option, and this includes the index or any spare tissue that may be replanted in the first ray. It is true that a finger placed in the thumb position will not provide the same pinching capabilities as the wider original thumb’s pulp, but it gets around the need for further surgery, and a high degree of satisfaction can be anticipated (Fig. 48.21).

When there is nothing to replant or in cases of late referrals, reconstruction is of the utmost necessity. All these classic methods have been surpassed by toe transfer, not to mention when the loss involves other parts of the hand. As I have already discussed in the previous section, I see very few reasons that a toe transfer would not be indicated provided the patient is fit and accepts the operation. In my opinion, the best result can be obtained by using the trimmed toe transfer as described by Wei (see Chapter 47). This has the advantage of replicating the native thumb size and maintains some IP joint movement. On the other hand, the second toe is a poor replica and being more slender provides a small surface for opposition. I should stress again the importance of early reconstruction, no later than the first week because, as discussed, it is technically much easier and shortens the time the patient is away from their duties. Alternatively, delaying the procedure gives the patient time to mourn the loss and thus appreciate the result achieved. However, this will unnecessarily prolong the time off work and will make the procedure more difficult. Furthermore, the surgeon would be obliged to stay out of the zone of injury, potentially increasing the tissue needs from the foot. By the same token, emergency toe transfers (on the day of injury) do not allow the patient to understand the procedure and the entailed risks, and thus I do not recommend this either.

Speaking highly of the virtues of the toe transfer, one has to appreciate that some patients may not be willing to accept the loss of a toe. In such instances, an alternative should be offered acknowledging its inherent limitations. Transfer of stumps or damaged fingers have a major role in my armamentarium. They can be used as a sole procedure or as part of a more complex reconstruction, that is, combined with a minihallux (see Fig. 48.49, later). Transfer of a stump provides a double benefit: removing a hindrance and minimizing the donor site morbidity. Contrarily, if the patient lacks more than a finger, I will not favor on- top plasty but multiple toe transfers (Fig. 48.22).

The indication for pollicization of the index finger has decreased, but it still represents a very good alternative with a high degree of patient satisfaction. The obvious drawback is the loss of a finger. Besides, in the study by Ishida et al., only when the thenar muscles were preserved was good function achieved, otherwise the neo- thumb “will function little more than as a post.” Primary pollicization using an injured digit can be considered when secondary reconstruction may deteriorate hand function or complicate the procedure. Severe contamination and vascular impairment of the palmar skin are definite contraindications if the surgeon is not capable of managing them. Infection may have catastrophic consequences, and the key to preventing this lies in an adequate debridement. At least one neurovascular bundle should be patent.

Finally, phalangization does not play any role on its own but can be used to complete other types of reconstruction. Understanding Z- plasty is vital (see the next section).

### Amputations in the Proximal Third

This group is the least common, and even in busy centers the experience is limited to a handful of cases. The methods do not differ much from what we have discussed in the previous section: osteoplastic reconstruction, pollicization, and toe transfer. However, their pertinent downsides and limitations are multiplied. First of all, not only does the thumb need to be reconstructed, but 5 cm of metacarpal are also requisite. Furthermore, most proximal amputations add three more problems apart from the lack of the thumb itself: the lack of adequate soft tissue cover, the absence of thenar muscles, and, in very proximal amputations, the loss of the carpometacarpal joint.

#### Building the Neo- Thumb

Osteoplastic reconstruction provides a thumb that is poor cosmetically and functionally unless it is topped with a toe. Besides, the reconstruction process is very long and may exhaust the most optimistic and cooperative individual. As a matter of fact, less than half of the patients completed the reconstruction in the study by Lin et al. This is a quite undesirable end result but perfectly understandable when the reconstruction process is long or protracted.

Pollicization can restore the thumb even if the carpometacarpal joint is lost, but the obvious price is the loss of a finger. In addition, the patient needs to learn how to use the neo- thumb, as the process of incorporation is not as quick as for a child. Furthermore, the performance of a pollicization is quite dependent on the remaining muscles, and the function achieved in proximal amputations with loss of thenar muscles “is little more than [that of] a post.”

Thus toes are probably the best option for proximal amputations. However, because the metatarsal has to be included the choice of toe is limited. Indeed, the hallux, which is ideal in other instances,is not recommended, as including the first metatarsal would lead to significant donor site morbidity. The second toe, on the other hand, has the advantage that it is not critical during the gait cycle, and this allows the entire metatarsophalangeal joint and metatarsal to be harvested with negligible donor site morbidity. Its drawbacks, however, should not be forgotten, and these include a poorer cosmetic appearance, the tendency to claw, a short nail, and the smaller surface available for opposition. Since 2004 we have circumvented the issue of harvesting the hallux, minimizing donor site morbidity, by the switching- two- toe transfer (STTT) (Fig. 48.24). This flap is a variation of the twistedtwo- toe flap of Foucher, permitting us to restore any length of thumb and obtaining a closer thumb replica without endangering the foot. The flap is composed of a trimmed- hallux (see Chapter 47) plus the second metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint and the second metatarsal bone.

![[Fig.48.24.png]]
> **Fig. 48.24** Author’s technique for reconstruction of proximal thumb amputations: the STTT procedure. (From del Piñal F. Extreme thumb losses: reconstructive strategies. Plast Reconstr Surg 144[3]:665–677, 2019. Copyright © 2018, Dr. F. Piñal.)

The hallux is based on the peroneal digital artery, whereas the second MTP joint and the second metatarsal bone are based on branches from the distal communicating artery, as well as the tibial digital artery of the second toe distally. The distal second toe circulation is thus based on the remaining fibular digital artery, which should be carefully preserved. Proximally, the dissection can stop at the first dorsal or plantar metatarsal artery, depending on dominance. The “thumb” is built on a side table by switching the proximal phalanx of the hallux on top of the base of the proximal phalanx of the second toe. Fixation is performed with 90- 90 wiring. The foot is closed by transferring the second toe on top of the hallux proximal phalanx. The dissection of the components of this STTT flap are discussed in the previous chapter, but there are some important technical considerations that should be stressed.

Most importantly, when building the neo- thumb, the second MTP joint should not be twisted into position, as recommended by Foucher, but switched across. In other words, the MTP joint should be transposed beneath the hallux rather than rotated 180 degrees around the hallux. Otherwise, the MTP joint would be reversed and this creates sidewise instability at the time of pinching. (Despite the fact that the literature recommends rotating the MTP joint 180 degrees to increase its arc of motion, this is a pertinacious mistake, as this will cause sidewise instability on the transferred joint.)

#### Coverage Issues

In proximal losses, soft tissue coverage is always an issue. In order to prevent desiccation and further losses of vital remaining structures, it is essential to provide healthy coverage. However, one should resist the temptation to use local and regional flaps that may endanger critical vascular structures and negate the use of definitive reconstructive options. Most authors favor providing cover with a groin flap, as this gives abundant tissue and some extra time for planning. Sabapathy et al. highlights the importance of planning the flap in such a way that the seam of the tube is palmar. In this manner, at the time of the second stage, the flap can be opened and defatted aggressively, knowing that inset of the flap will not be jeopardized. In turn, the vascularity of the flap will not be affected.

#### Short Muscles

Lin et al. proved the benefits of opponensplasty in proximal amputations to improve the quality of pinching in proximal thumb amputations. Although this approach may be valid in unsophisticated reconstructions, such as a metacarpal hand, it will not work if we are striving for a “normal” thumb. A normal thumb needs seven tendons to control the three thumb joints. If there are fewer, such as in paralytic conditions, collapse and Z- deformity will occur. There is no reason to think that a reconstructed thumb will behave differently. Thus at the time of reconstruction, the surgeon has to judiciously use arthrodesis and transfer of available motors to prevent a useless neo- thumb. General management is summarized in Fig. 48.25, but the potential combinations are many, as the scenarios rarely repeat themselves.

Notwithstanding that tendon transfers may be needed, we have found that the only way to achieve a normal- functioning thumb is by preserving the original intrinsic muscles. Staging reconstructions will render the muscles unsalvageable and will skyrocket the need for transferring tendons, which may not be available. This is, without doubt, the main advantage of immediate reconstruction and has not been sufficiently stressed in the literature—delaying the reconstruction will entail functional loss of the important short muscles.

#### Carpometacarpal Joint

Absence of the basal joint proves difficult to treat, with limited available reconstructive options. The workhorse is pollicization because this is the only option that will recreate a basal joint. The index MCP joint becomes the saddle joint of the new thumb. The index may be too long, and some authors have recommended resecting the distal phalanx or transferring the ring finger instead. However, removal of the distal phalanx deprives the neo- thumb of the most important nail and, as discussed previously, the ring finger will cause dangerous crossovers of tendons and muscles. The current body of knowledge prefers to pollicize the index. One should bear in mind when the decision is made for a pollicization that these patients will have also lost the thenar muscles and there is quite possibly extensive soft tissue loss.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: AMPUTATIONS OF THE PROXIMAL THIRD

In an emergency situation, the same applies as for more distal amputations: A primary pollicization of an injured finger or ectopic replant will yield a functioning thumb and a high degree of patient satisfaction. When this is not the case, and the patient is seen several days after the injury, the classic approach is to first provide soft tissues with a groin flap and in a second stage, reconstruct the thumb. However, in the process of waiting for the flap to mature, the thenar muscles become useless, the first metacarpal contracts in adduction, and the need for tendon transfers skyrockets. So, it is of utmost importance to abandon the two- stage approach and adopt a much more aggressive management. I, in fact, recommend carrying out all the reconstruction (cover, tendon transfers and thumb) *in a single stage and as early as possible* (Fig. 48.27).

If on the other hand the surgeon waits, all remnant muscle will irreversibly shorten and be rendered useless.

Loss of the thumb at the trapezium level is a colossal challenge for which there is no perfect solution. The most popular method is pollicization. Nevertheless, the performance of the neo- thumb— *and this cannot be otherwise* —is quite dependent on the remaining thenar muscles, and for this reason, at this level, it will behave “little more than a post.”

Surely, combining pollicization with our current understanding of early muscle preservation/tendon transfers, will improve the results.

Conceptually, I do not like the idea of removing healthy fingers to build a thumb. Apart from the obvious drawback of the loss of the index in a manual worker, in my environment this operation will inevitably lead to the worker’s retirement. I, unfortunately, do not have a miraculous alternative to offer either. While I have experience reconstructing isolated basal thumb joints by means of a vascularized second MTP joint with good results, I have not yet dared combine an STTT with a contralateral 2- MTP joint (for the saddle joint) plus a flap for cover, as this would entail three flaps with little margin for error (perhaps for the next edition). At this point, when the basal joint is missing, I prefer an STTT and to fuse the second metatarsal to the trapezoid. This approach reduces the number of tendons needed to control the thumb, that in most very proximal amputations are also very limited (Fig. 48.28).

## INTERCALATED DEFECTS

Traditionally, our attention has been focused on terminal defects of the thumb; however, intercalated injuries may cause as much damage as the loss distally. The reconstructive surgeon now has tools to replace any of the intermediate portions lost. The foot is the main donor site to replace like with like. Major defects of bone/nerve/soft tissue are managed in my practice by resorting, in most instances, to the second toe as the chief donor site. However, any area of the body that may fit into the defect and complete the jigsaw puzzle of any loss is most welcome.

### Partial Second Toe Elevation Technique

Despite the modifications needed according to the contents of any given flap, the technique of elevation is similar to all partial toe flaps.

All dissection is carried out with 3.5 loupes and the leg is exsanguinated by elevation. This is so because it is crucial when dissecting the veins that they contain some blood for identification. Vein harvesting is sometimes the most difficult part of the whole operation—they are very small and are easily damaged. I used to do the dissection of the veins going from the distal edge of the flap and then directed proximally. This is very time- consuming and the tiny veins are at risk. Now, I prefer to dissect the veins in the dorsum of the foot and track the vein and its branches distally, to the proximal edge of the flap. This is much faster and safer. In some cases, I have resorted to volar veins when they are dominant, as has recently being published. Contrarily, dissection of the arteries is much easier. The digital artery is located distally and traced proximally. Usually the digital artery is our donor artery, and the dissection stops at the toe web. Once the vessels are isolated, elevation of the flap itself is carried out expeditiously by following the periosteum and flexor sheath plane with a knife. The nerve is included or not, depending on whether it is needed (see also Fig. 48.41, later).

## THE WEB: FIRST WEB CONTRACTURE

The first web is a complex, multilayered anatomic region spanning the first and second metacarpals. It has a triangular shape, with its vertex located at the base of the first and second metacarpals; the skin web that joins the MCP joints of the index and thumb corresponds to its base. It is covered by glabrous skin on the palmar side and minimally hairy skin on the dorsum. It harbors the radial extension of the superficial palmar (natatory and transverse) and deep ligaments and the bellies of the adductor pollicis and first dorsal interosseous muscles with their investing fascia. The radial artery dives and divides into the base of the muscles from dorsal to palmar at its most proximal aspect.

First web contracture (FWC) is common after trauma to the hand, particularly in the radial part, and has a tremendously negative effect on thumb function, especially on pinch strength (because of the muscles) and on the ability to grasp large objects. Because of its triangular shape, even small limitations of aperture at its base have a major effect on the extremes of the triangle (the thumb and index pulps) (Fig. 48.32). Even in small defects, such as a retractile scar, abducting the thumb will cause pain and the aforementioned limitation. In severe forms, the thumb is incarcerated, causing a disability tantamount to a thumb absence.

![[Fig.48.32.png]]
> **Fig. 48.32** Note dramatic reduction in the ability to grab large objects caused by minimal retraction on the web. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Most of us have been educated as if FWC were an unavoidable bad companion (collateral damage) of some forms of trauma to the hand. The surgeon’s role was limited to releasing the contracture once established—quite destructive surgery. However, by understanding the pathophysiology and acting diligently, the surgeon can prevent progression to fixed contracture in most cases.

### Etiopathology

One should bear in mind that FWC is an ongoing process. Time elapsed after the injury worsens the prognosis regardless of the severity, not only because the deformity becomes irreversible but also because unaffected structures become involved. Three periods of evolution in first web contracture can be identified as follows: elastic, reversible, and fixed. The swelling fluid seen after any trauma is rich in proteins (from the lymph and the hematoma), and in the process of its resorption, collagen is deposited, which as it matures becomes rigid and unyielding. In the early stages, 4 to 6 weeks, it is possible to correct partially the deformity passively after some traction on the thumb by the examiner (elastic FWC). When the patient is seen later than 4 to 6 weeks, the surgeon will be unable to open fully the web by traction. Many of such cases can be corrected by appropriate orthotic treatment (reversible FWC). After 3 months, there is a phenomenon of progressive involvement of formerly healthy layers in such a way that initially the problem may originate in one single structure (e.g., deficient skin), but in time it becomes widespread on the web (affects muscle, fascia, and ligaments). Thus the surgeon will have to sever structures that were formerly healthy and functional in order to release the web (fixed contracture).

The etiology of FWC is varied and the management is different according to the type (Table 48.2).

#### Idiopathic

Even after minor trauma the surgeon may be surprised by a severe FWC in the midterm. Disregarding obvious reasons, such as splinting or a tightly binding dressing between the first and second metacarpal often seen in the past, other less apparent factors surreptitiously driving the first metacarpal toward the second are as follows:

1. Swelling. As the hand swells, the previously flattened skin of the hand will expand until nearly a balloon- shaped hand occurs. In this process, the first metacarpal moves toward the second to allow the skin envelope to deform as a sphere.

2. Antifunctional postures. Most patients after a hand or wrist injury carry their wrist in flexion as an antalgic position. While the wrist is in flexion, the extensor pollicis longus will passively drag the thumb into extension and most importantly into adduction- supination.

3. Functional positions. Even correct immobilization (i.e., the hand above the heart in a sling with the elbow flexed) can cause FWC. In this position if the forearm is in neutral rotation, the thumb is cranial and will spontaneously fall against the index by its own weight, thus closing the web. The situation is even worse if the forearm is in pronation as then the body squeezes the thumb against the hand.

4. Adductor forces. Because of this tendency of the thumb to adduct by swelling, functional and antifunctional positions are aggravated because abductor forces are much weaker than flexor- adductor ones.

Progressive night splint and education are the mainstays of treatment of the idiopathic form.

#### Cutaneous- Linear

It is axiomatic that any linear wound that crosses a flexion crease or a concavity will heal by forming a retracting band: The first web is no exception. Not only should attention be paid to the free edge, but transverse scars in the palm or the dorsum will be a source of a limiting contractile- painful scar (Fig. 48.33). The surgeon can prevent this progression by performing emergency Z- plasty.

#### Cutaneous- Major Defect

Any major skin loss at any part of the body will recruit skin from the neighborhood. At the dorsum of the hand the only mobile part is the first web, and the contracting forces will slowly drag the first metacarpal toward the second.

Therefore any moderate skin loss in the region should be replaced with a well- padded noncontracting flap as an emergency or early procedure. Skin grafts have no role in this scenario: Besides the requirement of complete take to avoid secondary retraction, it is difficult to control its inherent tendency to contract as time passes.

#### Deep Scar Variant

Major trauma or crush to the first web is generally accompanied by partial muscle destruction (first dorsal interosseous or adductor pollicis) and/or fractures or even concomitant bone loss. The skin envelope might or might not be deficient but this is irrelevant in this type of contracture. What really matters in this situation is the build- up of hemorrhagic fluid, edema, and contused- necrotic muscle that fill up the web space. This will evolve, producing a cicatricial- retracting magma in the depths of the web. After this type of trauma, neighboring structures not directly damaged by the injuring agent will be filled by edema fluid (for example the carpometacarpal ligaments and muscle fascia) and this will result in fibrosis and stiffness in a shortened position.

The critical step to halt the progression of this form of FWC is immediate radical debridement of all devitalized tissues. This will diminish the potential cicatricial mass, minimize the risk of infection, and limit inflammatory edema. This should be followed by rigid bony fixation, primary coverage with a nonretracting flap (if needed), and a program of night splinting (Fig. 48.34).

#### Ischemic Variant

Failure to release swelling muscles confined in closed osteofascial compartments invariably causes muscle death and, subsequently, contracture. At the radial part of the hand this will cause an adduction contracture (with antepulsion or retropulsion).

In contradiction to the small number of cases of acute compartment syndrome of the hand reported in the literature even in large trauma centers, we noticed a high number of patients who, after suffering a closed crush to the hand, developed acute compartment of the hand with minimal symptoms. *Silent* (untreated) compartment syndrome of the radial part of the hand are behind the scenes in many fixed FWC. By the same token, early recognition and treatment allows *restitutio ad integrum* (putting things back the way they were). For these reasons, the clinician must have a low- threshold policy to indicate to measure the intracompartmental pressure since elevated subfascial pressure may be present despite the absence of classic signs and symptoms(see also Chapter 51)

### Management

The adage “prevention is better than cure” holds true in FWC. This is so because most cases can be prevented by appropriate action of the surgeon. In some, patient education will be enough, while in others, splints or surgery may be needed to prevent a fixed contracture.

#### Splints

In the very early stages, instructing the patient to actively abduct the thumb while in the sling will prevent many of the idiopathic cases. If the patient comes some time after the trauma, and the thumb cannot be fully abducted, progressive casting plays a major role (reversible FWC). Treatment in this stage is based on the reversibility of collagen crosslinking by applying a light continuous force in the direction opposite to the deforming force. Several methods and splint designs have been presented in the literature. We prefer custom- made splints made with 8 to 12 layers of cast (Fig. 48.35). The splint is worn at night and held in position with a bandage, taking care to sit it deeply in the web to maximize its effect over the carpometacarpal joint rather than the MCP joint. The patient is seen every week to assess the degree of correction and new splints are made as required. In our experience, serial casting will correct most cases less than 3 months old, beyond that time the response is variable (fixed FWC), and surgery is often needed.

![[Fig.48.35.png]]
> **Fig. 48.35** Molding the splint to the web and wearing it at night is a key part of reverting/treating FWC. This needs to be changed every week or every other week until full correction is achieved. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Splinting will fail when the thumb or index are unstable. For such cases, we prefer to block the MCP joint in flexion with the Kirschner away from the web. Similarly, when the thumb can hyperextend because of volar plate laxity, splints will fail to control a palmar contracture. Finally, when the thumb has been amputated near its MCP joint, the lack of lever arm to apply a cast and the lack of use of the thumb causes rapid FWC development (see below).

A word needs to be said here on the use of cross Kirschner wiring and/or on the use of external or internal devices to maintain the first web during the immediate posttrauma period after major web injury.I have found it to be unnecessary, and perhaps deleterious, as Kirschner wire insertion will produce additional damage to the muscles and will hence promote contracture on its own. Its use, however, may be considered when splinting is not an option or fails to prevent the contracture.

#### Z- Plasty

An established retractile scar, if localized, will benefit from Z- plasty. Whether one can do a single “Z” or multiple ones, the size, design, and so on depend on the location of the scar and the quality of its neighboring tissue. The principles are easily understood, although considerable experience is needed to set the flaps in the proper location and with the correct angle, as otherwise either they will not “cross” or the intended lengthening effect will not be achieved. The principles of the Z- plasty are dealt with in Chapter 44. There are, however, some important issues that are specific to the first web:

- Flaps based on scarred tissue will not move easily or may necrose

after transposition. Thus, as a principle, Z- plasty should only be performed when the damage is linear, and if there is enough tissue to mobilize. The surgeon can modify the design to take this into account.

- The standard "simple" Z- plasty is composed of two flaps, each at a

60- degree angle to the linear scar. Transposition will yield a lengthening of 75%. In other words, the central linear scar will lengthen from 1 cm to 1.75 cm (if the length of the arms of the flap was 1 cm). Simple Z- plasties are easy to design, but as a rule they are inappropriate for a first web since for any benefit to be gained the flaps need to be large. If one opts for such a large flap, the dorsal skin will be transposed to the palm and similarly glabrous skin to the dorsum: Both are quite unesthetic. Furthermore, the curved shape of the web will be broken at the crossing point of the flaps, thus creating an undesirable ridge. Furthermore, 60- degree angle Z- plasty flaps are difficult to transpose at times. So, my preference is for smaller flaps in order to better distribute the changes. Smaller flaps provide less lengthening, but this can be solved by doing several smaller Z- plasties.

- Similarly, 45- degree angle Z- plasty flaps provide less lengthening

(50% of the central arm, i.e., 1:1.5), but they are much easier to transpose. In other words, depending on the local condition the surgeon can use smaller flaps with different angles and combinations in order to avoid using damaged tissue in the Z- plasty flap (Fig. 48.36).

#### Flaps for the Web

A flap may be needed to tackle the progression to a “fixed” contracture when there is a primary coverage defect or when releasing an already contracted web. Large dorsal rotational flaps, pedicled groin flaps, or cross- arm flaps have long been considered good alternatives. There are, however, certain disadvantages to these, which in my view have relegated them to a second line, namely increasing local morbidity in dorsal rotational flaps and the need for prolonged immobilization in the other two. Furthermore, dorsal flaps are unusable if there is local damage. Regional island flaps, such as the distally based radial forearm flap and distally based posterior interosseous flaps, are very popular and provide tissue from healthy areas. The radial forearm flap has the limitation of sacrificing a major forearm artery; the posterior interosseous flap donor site needs to be skin- grafted even for moderate defects (see Chapters 44 and 45). Tonkin and Stern have used the posterior interosseous flap as a free flap in these scenarios, thus improving the blood supply of the flap (as well as the pliability of the skin and the quality of the fat). However, the unsightly donor site is not solved by using it free. Scheker et al. popularized the use of the lateral arm flap for releasing contractures of the first web. In most cases the donor site can be closed primarily and the donor site is in the same limb. A problem shared by all mentioned skin flaps (pedicled or free) is that the color and texture of the skin is far from a perfect match and they tend to be chunky, particularly in the dorsum of the web where the pedicle is located. Careful tailoring of the flap at the time of transfer should prevent this from occurring, and defatting is commonly needed. As a rule, muscle free flaps are contraindicated to cover defects of the web, as in their healing process they retract, causing secondary web contracture. Similarly, skin grafts are to be avoided, as in this region will retract causing severe contractures and sometimes generate a worse problem than the one solved (Fig. 48.37).

### The Established Contracture

As stated at the beginning of this section, much of our interest, as surgeons, has been focused on the surgical treatment of fixed first web contracture. In related articles, systematic release of skin, fascia, muscles from their origins or insertions, and trapeziometacarpal ligaments and even resection of the trapezium itself are recommended for the sake of placing the thumb in an abducted position.Several types of flaps have been presented to cover the resulting defect. Regrettably, it has been my experience that although an operation can restore the span of the web, the function of the web (i.e., pinch strength, allowance of thumb pronation, pinch dexterity) will often never be restored.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THE ESTABLISHED CONTRACTURE

Proactivity to prevent the evolution to an established contracture is paramount. Night splinting and “preventive” flaps should be used freely when a contracture is anticipated. Otherwise, destructive surgery will be unavoidable (Box 48.1).

**BOX 48.1 Author's Key Remarks on FWC**

1. Prevention, prevention, and prevention.
2. Night-splinting is key to all pathologic forms.
3. Early surgery is recommended to prevent established forms.
4. In established forms, radical removal of scar tissue is critical.
5. Soft tissue defects are smaller than they look.
6. Any form of surgery should be followed by splinting.

#### Management of the Soft Tissue Defect

The reader may find it confounding that there are as many ways and flaps to solve FWC as there are papers on the subject, and I hope below to aid the decision- making process. In my view, local flaps have only a role in very small defects. Z- plasty works well in linear scarring or when the damage is localized. As stated above, my preference is for smaller flaps, which obviously provide less lengthening but results in a smoother web. Of all possibilities, the modified “jumping man” yields the most lengthening by combining the Z- plasty concept with a Y- V effect (the opposite to the V- Y effect) (Fig. 48.38). By the same token, a less ambitious 90- degree angle four- flap Z- plasty (split in 45- degree angle flaps) is easier to transpose compared with the original 120- degree angle flaps and still provides a good lengthening (1:2, compared with 1:2.5).

Large dorsal rotational flaps are “heroic” methods, but they further local morbidity and are unusable in the presence of local damage.Regional flaps, particularly the posterior interosseous flap are, in theory, a good choice, but I have found it difficult to achieve a good cosmetic result. Furthermore, for medium- sized defects, the donor site needs to be skin- grafted, which I find very objectionable, leading to patients’ complaints (at least in my office). The raising technique is discussed elsewhere (see Chapter 44). I should stress that this is a reverse flow flap: The arterial supply is not first angiosome quality, and the venous drainage takes place against the valves. This will entail that the tissue we are transferring is not of first quality and, even if undetected, will cause more scarring and fat necrosis. This statement has no scientific support, but the logical thinking of how other flaps behave in the body: The farther away from the first angiosome, the more fat necrosis. Scarring- fat necrosis in the hand translates into less fat pliability and poor gliding. In other words, coverage can be achieved with the said flaps but the final result may be hindered. Thus, if one wants to provide pristine quality tissue, which adds healthy tissue to the existing local damage, the choice would be a free flap. Free flaps are first angiosome and provide the type of tissue one wishes to replace. Furthermore, free flaps for the web, technically speaking, are straightforward to revascularize—in most cases the anatomic snuff- box where the radial artery is located is unharmed and thus the pedicle can be quite short. The surgeon will have plenty of healthy vessels to choose from. As my preference is to minimize donor site morbidity, I usually deal with small vessels: dorsal transverse carpal artery or FDMA can be easily mobilized and used for end- to- end anastomosis. Alternatively, if the surgeon has a large caliber arterial vessel, end- to- side anastomosis to the radial artery is also a good choice.

As a rule, there is a tendency to overestimate the size of the web defect and thus harvest a much larger flap than needed. The end result would be a chunky, unsightly reconstruction that would need secondary surgery. At the time of planning, one should remember that only the web itself needs pliable skin and that the defect may be only dorsal, only volar, or both dorsal and volar. In other words, there is no need to invade the palm when the defect is dorsal and vice versa: Many severe contractures can be solved with small flaps. Larger defects are to be expected only when the whole web (dorsum and palm) is involved (Fig. 48.39).

Regarding the selection of free flap, any which offers thin and pliable skin can be a good alternative. My preference is to replace like with like, and in this region only the webs of the foot and the neurocutaneous toe flap provide an unparalleled match. These flaps are unable to cover large defects, in which cases my choices are the scapular, the anterolateral thigh, the lateral arm, and the groin flap. The lateral arm flap will not have as much covering capacity as the anterolateral thigh, but it is quite conveniently located, and ideal if the patient does not want donor sites away from the limb. Both are discussed in Chapter 45. The free groin flap is an outstanding flap even for large defects in the web, as its dermis is very pliable, and the flap itself very thin, even in overweight individuals. Furthermore, its donor site is inconspicuous. Unfortunately, this flap has been much maligned owing to its anatomic variability. At this point of our development in ultramicrosurgery, it deserves a reconsideration.

**Web ﬂap.** The web flap yields an unparalleled cosmetic and functional result. Although all webs are transferable, as a rule, my first choice is the second web for several reasons discussed below. If the size of the defect is larger, then I will use the first web instead, but let’s stress again that there is a tendency to overestimate the defect: Too large a flap will yield a displeasing cosmetic result.

The flap is designed according to the defect: entire web, predominantly dorsal, palmar, or combinations. In general, the ipsilateral side is used for the first web, but either side can be used in most instances. Except for burns, as well as the skin, the fat occupying the web space should also be included to replace the volume lost in the web (Fig. 48.42). No preoperative vascular study in the hand or the foot is required as there are a number of variations and at surgery the dominant artery would be defined.

Elevation. The dissection is carried out under tourniquet with exsanguination by elevation only. The web flap is incised dorsally and a dorsal subcutaneous vein is identified and isolated. In most cases, a short length of vein is sufficient to anastomose to one of a number of dorsal veins available in the hand. The flap dissection is continued to the periosteum and the flap is dissected away from the metatarsals, MTP joints, and proximal phalanx, preserving the periosteum and peritoneum in situ. Otherwise, an ungraftable bed may result (see below).

While the fat is dissected in the proximal aspect of the web, the dominant artery comes to light. This is the most worrisome part of the operation, as for when dissecting the hallux or any toe, the surgeon may find the dorsal or plantar system dominant and has to make a choice. While when restoring the other webs (second to fourth) I prefer the plantar system for easier anastomosis to the palmar digital vessels, for the first web, it makes little difference whether to use the dorsal or plantar systems. Both reach the vessels in the web equally well. When the plantar system is used, the maximum pedicle length is 1.5 to 2 cm. The pedicle could be further dissected proximally by dividing the intermetatarsal ligament and placing a lamina spreader between the metatarsal heads to improve visualization. Nevertheless, the pedicle length obtained is rarely longer than 2.5 cm even with this technique. If because of local damage a long artery is needed, then the first dorsal metatarsal artery should be used.

Once the pedicle is isolated proximally, the skin of the dorsum aspect of the toes is incised and the flap dissected plantarly in the supraperiosteal- epiperitenum plane until the digital arteries are reached. In most cases, the digital nerves are dissected off the flap and left in situ. Once the nerves are out of danger and the arteries dissected to the tip of the flap, the plantar incision is made and the flap dissected off the flexor sheath. Finally, the digital arteries are tied distally. The tourniquet is now released to assess the blood supply to the flap. The flap is then transferred to the hand and anastomosed, preferably endto- end to a branch of the radial artery. The donor vein is sutured also end- to- end to a local vein. The technique of anastomosis and the postoperative management is the same as that presented in the Intercalated Defects section.

My general policy is to carry out the whole operation, including the anastomoses, under tourniquet control. This permits the surgery to be done much faster, and only a bit of experience is required to choose healthy recipients. A rule that never fails is to use only arteries surrounded by healthy fat. In this manner, the whole procedure can be performed in a tourniquet time.

The donor site, when the second or third web is harvested, is solved by a primary neo- syndactyly. The end result is very forgiving cosmetically speaking, and the patient returns to activities in a matter of days. Although creation of a neo- syndactyly poses no functional problem for the first web defect, it is quite obtrusive and we would not recommend it—skin graft is thus preferred. However, utmost care should be taken to achieve a primary take of the graft, as painful hyperkeratosis may occur. The surgeon should pay attention to achieve hemostasis, use of a medium- thickness skin- graft and a tie- over bolus dressing. I usually keep the dressing in place for 7 to 10 days to allow a full take. Adani et al. demonstrated the beneficial use of artificial dermis for donor sites, but this obliges one to perform a second operation for skin grafting. If attention is paid to detail, there should be no problem with the donor site. If I were ever to face the complication of a hypertrophic painful hyperkeratosis, I would solve this by replacing the area with one of the available super thin flaps.

In summary, the web flap, as well as its “little brother” the neurocutaneous flap, are extremely versatile. Depending on the requirements, either the nerves or the arteries can be included to provide vascularized nerve grafts or restore the arterial flow distally in flow- through configurations. Preoperative planning in complex cases is crucial (Fig. 48.43).

**Iliac ﬂap.** The iliac flap, a lateral extension of the groin flap, is a terrific yet forgotten flap, whose only sin is that of having been born too early. In those early days of free tissue transfer, its anatomic variability and the size of the nutrient artery (in the 1 mm range), together with the appearance of safer flaps (such as the latissimus dorsi) made both the groin flap and the iliac flap fall into disuse. However, things have changed a lot in the past 40 years and with them the advantages of the flap, namely thinness, pliability, and an inconspicuous donor site, all of which make the iliac flap deserve its rightful place in our armamentarium. In my practice, it is first choice not only for covering elbow and wrist fold defects but also for the dorsum of the hand and for large web defects (see Fig. 48.39).

Anatomy. The flap’s territory is slightly lateral to the groin flap, in the vicinity of the anterior superior iliac spine, which is the thinnest area of the abdomen. The anatomy can be confusing but is simple if one regards it with the perspective of competitive blood supply: the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA) creates arcades and the surgeon has to decide which vessel to choose depending on the dominance. There are also variations regarding the origin of the SCIA proper, well known since the very early days of microsurgery.More laterally, the SCIA divides into a superficial branch and a deep branch. The superficial branch goes directly to the skin and may play a major role in the blood supply of the iliac flap. The deep branch continues laterally deep on the groin. Close to the medial border of the sartorius fascia, the deep branch usually gives off a medial cutaneous perforator and progresses by perforating the medial edge of the sartorius fascia. On its way, it irrigates this muscle and ends up perforating the fascia laterally, sending a branch (lateral perforator) to the skin that in itself gives origin to another named flap: the superficial circumflex iliac perforator (SCIP) flap.

The iliac flap can be irrigated by the superficial branch of the SCIA, the medial perforating branch of the deep branch, the lateral perforating vessel of the deep branch, or combinations of all three (Fig. 48.44). Usually, the lateral branch can be discarded, as the other two are sufficient; this expedites the dissection to a great extent (see below). Furthermore, the lateral branch may perforate the fascia lateral to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, and its inclusion will require sacrifice of the latter, yielding an unacceptable donor- site morbidity. If none of them are clearly dominant, before sacrificing any of the branches the surgeon should make sure that the others are self- sufficient by alternate temporary clamping.

The venous drainage of the flap is dual: a superficial system (the superficial epigastric vein) that follows the path of the deep artery in a more superficial location and a deep system (vena comitantes) that accompanies the artery. The veins of the deep system are flimsy and about 1 to 1.2 mm in diameter, whereas the superficial ones are large (about 2 mm), and with a robust wall. Either can be absent or of a diameter useless for transfer. However, if it is of sufficient diameter, the deep system is preferred.

Elevation. The course of the SCIA is traced with a unidirectional Doppler probe, approximately 2 fingerbreadths below and parallel to the inguinal ligament. Quite often, the takeoff of the medial perforating branch can be heard as a louder sound at the medial border of the sartorius. The flap is centered on the course of the artery; as a rule, one half of the flap is located lateral to the anterosuperior iliac spine as in this area the flap is thinner. Classically, the iliac and groin flap were dissected medially and the pedicle followed laterally. However, the variability of the anatomy medially made the dissection time- consuming and fraught with difficulties, and in turn this made the flap become unpopular. The key to elevating this flap in an expeditious and safe manner (often less than 30 minutes) is by ignoring the medial anatomy and raising the flap in a lateral to medial direction. Once the flap is partially raised, the anatomy will reveal itself, and the surgeon has only to track the vessels medially to the femoral artery and vein, as needed.

Thus the flap is incised on its lateral half and dissected from lateral to medial in a suprafascial plane. The first part of the elevation proceeds rapidly with a cautery. On the lateral border of the sartorius, the lateral perforating branch is sought by dissecting with scissors. In most cases, the branch is discarded (unless abnormally sizable) and the dissection proceeds medially until the vessels are seen in the undersurface of the flap. The anatomy will reveal itself: the medial arterial inflow (the superficial branch and/or the medial perforating branch of the deep SCIA). Once the vessel(s) is/are identified, the surgeon has to track them only medially as needed to the femoral artery (Fig. 48.45).

The flap harvesting finishes by ligating the deep branch, distally to the take- off of the medial perforating branch, and dissecting the SCIA trunk proximally as necessary. When a long pedicle is required (6 to 8 cm can be obtained), the incision needs to be prolonged medially up to the femoral vessels. At this stage, a decision has to be made regarding the venous drainage. If the venae comitantes are of sufficient caliber, the remaining medial half of the flap is incised with impunity, as the pedicle, already fully dissected, can easily be protected on the undersurface of the flap. Otherwise, the flap’s medial corner is dissected until the superficial iliac vein is isolated and dissected for the required length. Approximately in half of my cases I use the venae comitantes only, and in the rest either the superficial only or both if the dominance is unclear. The flap is transferred to the hand and revascularized as explained for the other flaps.

Despite the fact that the lateral perforating artery can be used as a free flap, it is rarely the dominant vessel of the iliac flap. If this so happens, the dissection has to be modified: The lateral perforating branch has to be tracked under the sartorius fascia, to its parent deep branch, and finally to the SCIA proper. The dissection of this anatomic variant is more cumbersome.

An important consideration during this flap elevation (and by extension *any* flap for the hand) is that utmost care should be taken to reduce the amount of fat harvested. Despite this and the fact that this flap is already quite thin, further thinning medially is often required and may have to be quite radical in overweight patients. Spasm should be prevented while raising the iliac, the neurocutaneous, or the web flaps: Any small vessel free flap may fall easily into spasm. Reverting spasm takes time and may be exasperating; hence it is much better for the surgeon to take active measures to prevent it from occurring. Keeping the patient warm and well- perfused and avoiding vessel dryness while dissecting is crucial. Furthermore, traction of side branches, leaving a small branch untied, or any rough pulling on the vessel may cause long- lasting spasm. Reversion requires combination of rewarming the flap and the patient, increasing the blood pressure, and irrigating the pedicle with topical verapamil (a calcium blocker). If after 10 to 15 minutes no improvement in flap perfusion is seen, examination of the pedicle by searching for untied side branches or focal damage follows. The latter may require the resection of a segment of damaged artery and anastomosis in the middle of the pedicle. Finally, although as a principle only wellvascularized flaps should be transferred to the hand, we have been forced to do otherwise while still managing the ordeal successfully.

#### Fixed Contractures

I recommend releasing the severely contracted web in a stepwise manner to retain as much function as possible (Box 48.2). The web space is usually approached by dividing the skin dorsally or palmarly, but for cases where there is no true skin deficit, the approach is through the free edge of the web where the Z- plasty will be performed. During the procedure, the assistant maintains tension on the first metacarpal so the surgeon can locate with the scissor tips where the tightness is located, and only those fibers are selectively divided.

**BOX 48.2 Stepwise Release of Fixed FWC**

Release progressively the following:

- Incise skin/excise if scarred.
- Incise fascia of AP and FDIO.
- Excise fascia of AP and FDIO.
- Incise any retinacular ligament.
- Release transverse AP from third metacarpal.
- Release FDIO from first metacarpal.
- Release oblique bundle of AP.
- Incise the intermetacarpal ligament CMC (rarely).

Excise muscle (if replaced by scar).

Treat any OA of the CMC of the thumb.

Treat any joint instability.

Provide adequate cover.

Fill up the web space with healthy tissue.

The procedure is begun by incising the skin. This is followed by incision of the investing fascia of the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis. Often there are additional fibrotic fibers depending on the deep palmar and superficial retinacular tissue. If still contracted, an intrasubstance tenotomy of the fibers of the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis is carried out. Next, the proximal insertions of the adductor pollicis in the third metacarpal and the insertions of the first dorsal interosseous from the first metacarpal are released. At this point, in most if not all cases, the first web contractures should be fully corrected. Occasionally, the oblique bundle of the adductor pollicis may need to be released, too.

Severing the distal insertions of the adductor pollicis and first dorsal interosseous is a much more expeditious technique but will defunction the muscles. During the procedure, utmost care should be taken to preserve the princeps pollicis artery, which has a variable course; the ulnar digital nerve of the thumb; the radial digital nerve of the index; the flexor tendon of the thumb and index; and the motor branch of the ulnar nerve. Finally, in the depths of the web the radial artery should be preserved; however, the radial artery of the index often has to be ligated if the ulnar digital artery of the thumb is to be maintained.

The procedure is modified when the muscles are replaced by scar, in which case the muscle is to be radically excised. As discussed in several parts previously, the space is to be filled with vascularized tissue to prevent hematoma accumulation, thus starting the vicious circle again. Rarely, in my experience, is there a need to divide the intermetatarsal ligament of the first carpometacarpal joint. However, it is often necessary to concomitantly treat first carpometacarpal joint osteoarthritis, which is behind the scenes in some “frozen” first web contractures (see below).

#### Special Scenarios

If the surgeon is attentive and pertinacious with the use of splints, rarely will a first web contract, provided the web has good coverage tissue and the muscle compartments have been released. However, in the two instances below, splints will not work in preventing the contracture, and immediate surgical action is needed.

**Instability of the metacarpophalangeal joint.** Much the same as in the gamekeeper’s thumb, or rheumatoid thumb (type III) where the first metacarpal becomes adducted and the MCP joint becomes unstable dorsally or laterally (type IV) (see Chapter 55), if the patient has instability, due to degeneration or trauma, in any direction of the MCP joint, the splint will not work. In this instance the surgeon has to deal with it rapidly to prevent the deformity from becoming fixed. If acute trauma is the reason, normally blocking the MCP joint in 30 degrees of flexion for 6 weeks should be sufficient to treat the problem (see Fig. 48.41D). This should always be associated with splinting even with the Kirschner wire in place. In chronic cases, the web contracture will aggravate the deformity. Management will include not only the web but also the MCP joint (arthrodesis or Zancolli’s sesamoidesis) and the primary deformity, if any (trapeziectomy and ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition [LRTI]) (Fig. 48.47).

**Radial- sided amputations and FWC.** When the thumb has been amputated near its MCP joint, the lack of a lever arm to which to apply a cast and the nonuse of the thumb cause rapid first web contracture development. The problem is compounded in the typical radial- sided injury because not only will the thumb be missing but different degrees of index damage and first web contracture will coexist.

The classic approach consists of using the index to lengthen the thumb, as in an on- top plasty, as this will solve three problems in one- stage: The web will be wider, the hindrance of a short index will be eliminated, and the thumb will be lengthened. The price is obvious—a four- fingered hand. Less evident is that the neo- thumb would be slender, thus providing a somewhat less stable surface for pinching in a hand that is already missing elements. Furthermore, the neo- thumb will lack a nail, which is an important element for fine- pinching activities. Not personally being a terrible fan of “plain” on- top plasty, I can, however, see a good indication for the absolutely unsalvageable index in the lower demand individual. The technique on- top plasty has been discussed in the Terminal Defects section; nevertheless, I should add two words of caution here. The first, which applies to all cases when the index is to be removed, is that the surgeon should avoid releasing the web too much, as this will give a grotesque aspect to the hand, similar to a type III cleft- hand. The other is specific for cases secondary to crush or explosions: In both instances, the digital nerves and volar blood supply may be damaged, and dissection always proves to be more difficult. Lack of sensibility volarly is a red flag that the palmar arteries may also be damaged (or easily torn during dissection). The risk of complications is higher and the blood supply poorer.

To summarize, my preferences in three clearly different scenarios in radial- sided damage are as follows:

- Index preserved: My best option is to proceed to an early hallux

transfer where the splints can antagonize the tendency to web contracture.

- Index amputated distal to the PIP joint: My preferred alternative is

to provide a five- fingered hand by carrying out a hallux transfer to the thumb and a second toe, from the contralateral foot to the index position. This works true in my practice even in distal amputations (Fig. 48.48).

- Index amputated proximal to the PIP joint: In this instance, a sec

ond toe in the index position will not yield a normal moving finger, and as a rule, it is better to resect the second ray. To reconstruct the thumb a hallux is my first choice; however, the donor site morbidity can be minimized by combining the procedure with an ontop plasty on the index stump (or an *index in- the- middle- plasty* ).The idea is as follows: The ray resection would solve the hindrance of the index stump and provide space for the web, while minimizing the need for a hallux to be harvested. In most cases, only a *mini* hallux is needed. Technically, the portion of the index needed is pollicized in a standard manner. During the procedure, a 0.5 cm stump of the radial artery to the middle finger is preserved in order to anastomose the artery of the toe. If the patient is referred late, and the contracture is fixed already, it should be released at the same time. A program of splinting should be implemented afterward in all instances.

## DEVASTATING DEFECTS

There is a group of patients in whom injuries are so severe—metacarpal hands and beyond—that the surgeon may be uncertain how to proceed. One option, in an emergency, is to provide cover with abdominal flaps or to bury the hand in order to preserve as much tissue as possible. Conversely, the surgeon may just consider providing a stable stump, abandoning any reconstruction and thinking of a hand transplantation as the alternative. However, we should not forget that currently there may be more drawbacks than benefits to hand transplantation and that the patient may not want to undergo the risks associated with immunosuppression. Thus it is worth looking back at more classic alternatives. Along this line, preserving useless or discarding helpful elements may prompt unnecessary surgery or be a source of regret at a later date. Hence, although these types of injuries are better managed by highly qualified groups, general knowledge of what is to be done in an emergency, and what can be done later by the specialist, is paramount.

Similar to the case of searching for the *acceptable hand* in less severe injuries, a similar goal- driven policy should be in the surgeon’s mind (Fig. 48.50).

![[Fig.48.50.png]]
> **Fig. 48.50** Upgrading of a digitless hand (see text). (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

The variations on how to perform the reconstruction are countless, but the very first goal is to provide a pincer- hand. This is a hand composed of two rays with a cleft, with at least one of the rays moving, and with sensibility. The importance of having sensibility is worth stressing, as covering with large abdominal flaps will render the hand useless.

The next upgrade is known as a tripod- pinch hand. In this instance, the “thumb” opposes with two fixed elements. The stability provided by this type of hand is much increased compared with the pincer hand.

In the upper part of the spectrum, the surgeon can provide a better alternative that I have called *tripod- plus hand.* This conceptually is like the acceptable hand but with much fewer capabilities. The ability of the hand to provide stable grasping of large objects, as well as the esthetic aspect, will be much improved. Unfortunately, this alternative is rarely feasible unless some digits or parts of phalanges remain (Fig. 48.51).

![[Fig.48.51.png]]
> **Fig. 48.51** A, Reconstruction of a tripod-plus hand is ideal but only feasible when at least some phalanges remain. B and C, The result in this 44-year-old self-employed printer, of independent second and third toes and the contralateral hallux transfers, is shown at 1 year. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

Regarding the methods, all procedures that may have been discussed in this chapter or any chapter in this book can be useful providing the basic premise of giving sensibility is respected. Toe transplantation, bone lengthening, on- top plasty, and ray resection are among the most common procedures the surgeon can use to achieve the goal of a useful pincer- hand. The endeavor becomes easier when part of the metacarpals are present, even more so with phalanges. By the same token, it becomes impossible in very proximal forearm amputations where prosthesis or arm transplantation are the only alternatives. Furthermore, discussion with patients considering their wishes, social needs, and medical facts may help in any given choice.

The most important consideration at the time of choosing any method of reconstruction is the length of the remaining stump, so a length- based approach is presented.

### Metacarpals Present

As in any reconstruction the presence of the trapezium- metacarpal joint helps enormously in the decision—this joint will drive any element toward the ulnar post and will allow the “fingers” to pinch. Several authors have presented methods that are based on the same concept: to provide a pincer by means of combinations of phalangization, ray resection, and transposition. There are limitations to phalangization if the length of metacarpals available is limited. An unsatisfactory result will occur if the web is not deep enough, if strong motion in one of the rays is not present, and when there is not good sensibility on both sides of the pincer.

Some popular methods in the past are to be discouraged. Using nonvascularized bone grafts covered with pedicled flaps will not work due to lack of sensibility. Furthermore, bone resorption will create secondary problems of instability. Tubiana and Roux proposed resection of the second and fourth metacarpals, leaving a sort of trident- hand. The resulting hand is difficult to accept by today’s esthetic standards. Besides, it is doubtful that it provides any functional benefit compared with the classic phalangization or the procedures described below. Toe transfers for metacarpal hand are discussed in the previous chapter.

#### Phalangization of the Metacarpal Hand

The idea of the operation is to create a web and something in front to which the moving thumb could oppose. Its original inception involved removing the trapezoid, the base of the second metacarpal, and a part of the third metacarpal. This degree of resection weakens the adductor and thereby the pinch strength also. By the same token, removal of the trapezoid will not improve the motion of the thumb, and it adds morbidity; thus I do not recommend it. A more modern approach is to combine bone lengthening to increase the size of the “arms” of the pinch, rather than to (over)deepen the web, which will weaken the adductor muscle.

A zigzag incision is designed over the second metacarpal to the base of the thenar eminence. Flaps are planned to interdigitate on the ulnar side of the mobilized first metacarpal.

The radial digital nerve together with the common digital nerve to the long and index are either transected proximally near the origin in the median nerve or, preferably, translocated to the proximal aspect of the neo- web away from trauma. If present, the flexor and extensor tendons are divided proximally and allowed to retract.

Next, the second metacarpal, the trapezoid, and as much as half of the third metacarpal is resected. Resection of the last is limited if possible in order to preserve the maximum origin of the transverse bundle of the adductor muscle.

Following closure of the ulnar side on the mobilized first ray, it is necessary to elevate a dorsal rotation flap for the radial side of the cleft. The dorsal donor site will in turn receive a full- thickness skin- graft (Fig. 48.52).

![[Fig.48.52.png]]
> **Fig. 48.52** A to C, Modified phalangization of a metacarpal hand. Note that the base of the index is left in place in order to preserve the insertions of the FCR and the ECRL. Similarly, the trapezoid is not excised, as this will not modify the motion at the saddle joint, and may even lead to instability. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

The procedure procures a crude pincer, but the upgrading from the original situation can be quite rewarding for the patient. It is paramount that the first carpometacarpal joint is functioning and, if possible, the hypothenar muscles too for the fifth metacarpal to supinate, thus improving the pinch. The presence of phalanges in the fingers and thumb adds considerably to the function.

By the same token, phalangization procedures can be upgraded by bone “gymnastics” as Dr. Strickland wrote ironically in the first edition of this book, namely on- top plasties, one- stage lengthening, and bone distraction. Today the so- called death defying leaps are no longer such, and the procedures can be performed by any standard hand practitioner. Kelleher et al. and Michon and Dolich championed on- top plasties and metacarpal lengthening by bone interposition. Matev and Seitz refined distraction procedures. Any of the above, or all of them together, will effectively lengthen the arms of the pincer. It should be stressed that insufficient arm length/depth of the web is a major cause of an unfavorable result. In summary, phalangization and the like play a major role when the patient does not want or cannot sacrifice toes (Fig. 48.53).

### Carpal and More Proximal Amputations

When the level of amputation is at the proximal metacarpals, local alternatives such as transposing bone or lengthening will provide a minimal benefit. Similarly, attempts at clefting will yield poor results, except for the Krukenberg operation. However, this will require sacrifice of a considerable number of useful elements. The surgeon should, in this instance, consider adding elements from the toe. In the simplest approach, often used in the peromelic hand, two second toes facing each other will provide a useful pincer, although cosmetically the result will not be so pleasing. A better alternative, when the first carpometacarpal joint is functioning, is a nonvascularized phalanx in the first metacarpal and two (or one) second toes in the ulnar part of the hand. In this way, a tripod pinch will be produced. The family should be warned of unequal growth and the need for bone distraction: Three epiphyseal plates of the toe grow more than only one of the toe phalanx.

A more ambitious plan, which I prefer in adults, is to build the hand distally with the goal of a tripod pinch. The hand will look more like a hand, but the complexity will be much increased: There will be need for providing additional cover to wrap the metatarsals and tendon grafts to prevent zigzag collapse. The upgrading of these types of proximal amputations can be stunning, particularly when the carpometacarpal joint is preserved (Fig. 48.54).

If the patient is not interested in very complex alternatives, a single toe may be enough to provide a pincer. Furnas and Achauer presented, in a case of hand amputation, good functional result by transferring the great toe to the side of the radius. This permitted the patient to pinch small objects and also to grasp 3- cm- sized items. In their case, the patient had some carpals and metacarpals remaining (and hence wrist motion) into which the patient pinched. Nearly at the same time, Vilkki proposed in more proximal amputations to set the toe at the side of the radius. Others transferred toes to the forearm stump.

#### Furnas Operation

The toe is harvested in the standard manner, including the metatarsal. The volar radius is exposed and the area where the proximal metatarsal would meet the carpus is defined. At that point, and depending on the surgeon’s preference, a trough is created in which to slot the base of the metatarsal. In their case, a 30- degree angle oblique osteotomy was carried out in the metatarsal. In both instances, rigid fixation with compression screws was preferred. The flexor hallucis longus was sutured to the flexor carpi radialis and the extensor hallucis longus to the brachioradialis. The digital nerves and the deep peroneal nerve were sutured to the median nerve. The patient required a tenolysis at 6 months to achieve 30 degrees of active motion at the IP joint (Fig. 48.55).

![[Fig.48.55.png]]
> **Fig. 48.55** The Furnas and Achauer operation. (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

There are several issues to discuss in this breakthrough case. Above all, the inclusion of a part of the first metatarsal is to be discouraged. Although, the authors reported no foot complaints, we know today that if the great toe metatarsal is removed, most patients will have gait abnormalities and pain when walking. For this reason, the operation as devised cannot be recommended except for instances where the foot is going to be amputated, as in the case of cleft hand- cleft feet.

From a mechanical standpoint, it seems sounder to motorize digital extensors with wrist flexors and to reserve the brachioradialis for hallux flexion, as in this way the transfer will have a somewhat automatic function. Nonetheless, the major concern with the case is that if a claw is to be prevented, the long flexors and extensor should be motorized, as well as the lumbricals or interosseous (see next section). In Furnas and Achauer’s case, only a flexor and an extensor were repaired and this caused clawing (fixed IP contracture) of the neo- thumb. Fortunately, the wrist moved well and the patient had a good pinch ability even with the collapsed thumb. In summary, the use of the hallux is discouraged, but the concept was an important contribution to the literature.

#### Vilkki Operation

Concomitantly with the above advance, Simo Vilkki of Finland devised a procedure for blind bilateral amputees that consisted of creating a pincer between the radial side of the radius and a second toe. A part of the radius was removed to increase the pinch capability, and in scarred stumps a portion of the hallux hemipulp was included to bring sensibility to the opposite side of the toe (Fig. 48.56). Initially applied for patients with hand amputation at the radiocarpal level, later it was also expanded to more proximal amputations.

![[Fig.48.56.png]]
> **Fig. 48.56** A, Schematic rendering of the procedure. Note that a significant segment of radial metaphysis is resected to create space for the pincer (B). (Copyright © 2020, Dr. F. Piñal.)

The main advantage of this operation, compared with the Furnas’, is that the hallux is left in place. A lesser advantage is that the use of prosthesis is not excluded in nonblind patients. Cosmetic prosthesis for either social purposes or even functioning prosthesis can be fitted. Saying this, the greatest contribution was the management of tendons. Indeed, Vilkki, an eminent hand and microsurgeon, stressed the importance not only of restoring flexion and extension of the neothumb but also of proper short muscle balance to prevent clawing.

**Technique.** The best indication are adult patients with bilateral amputations and blindness, as the latter cannot be rehabilitated with prosthetic hands. However, any adult patient with uni- or bilateral hand amputation at wrist level or with two- thirds of the forearm remaining can benefit from this method. In small children, toe transfers should be done distally to the physeal plate. Otherwise with growth, the toe will end in the midforearm. The operation is best performed early after the amputation. Beyond 3 months, preoperative training of the future motors is encouraged for at least a month.

Preferably the ipsilateral toe is harvested. The toe is elevated in the standard manner, including the joint and the metacarpal. However, the long flexors and extensors are included, as well as the lumbrical tendon and interosseous tendinous expansion on each side. Additionally, if the opposing surface of the radius is severely scarred, the peroneal hemipulp of the hallux is included with the same pedicle.

A longitudinal incision is made on the distal radial aspect of the forearm, and large flaps with skin and subcutaneous tissue are elevated, exposing neurovascular structures. Musculotendinous units are selected only if they have appropriate excursion and lack of scarring. The median nerve with distal neuroma is identified. In severe avulsion injuries, this nerve may not be present. The dorsal sensory branches of the radial and ulnar nerves are usually in the neighborhood and can be used as sensory donor nerves if required. About half of the radius joint surface and 7 cm of radius shaft are removed (see Fig. 48.56B). A flap of periosteum dorsally and a muscle flap of the pronator quadratus are raised and used to protect the raw bony surface. A slot in the proximal radius is created in the position where the metatarsal will sit. Sufficient separation of the MTP joint from the radius is needed to allow room for the tendons to glide and to give adequate room for the pinch. Vilkki insisted on the need for seven tendon transfers, but taking into account what we have discussed previously, five tendons should be enough. For flexor and extensor longus, the flexor pollicis longus, and a wrist extensor are preferred. To motorize the ulnar interosseous, the abductor pollicis longus is chosen, and the flexor digitorum superficialis IV on the radial side to interosseous tendons, while the palmaris longus is reserved for the lumbrical. Nevertheless, at surgery, considerable improvisation is needed depending on the local conditions, and only muscles with sufficient excursion (3 to 5 cm) should be used. After anastomosis and neurorrhaphies, the skin flaps are closed. The island hemipulp, if needed, is sutured in an optimum location for the toe to oppose.

#### Toe- to- Forearm

Several authors have presented the feasibility of restoring a pincer by transferring single toes opposing in the ulna and double toes on top of the radius. Yu devised a metallic prosthetic device for allowing triple toe transfers whose midterm is more than doubtful.Similarly, attempts to place one finger in each bone stump are doomed to failure as extremes of pronosupination would de- oppose the toes.

By the same token, in proximal amputations the results were poor and it is not recommended, and it is generally agreed that a length of two- thirds of the forearm is needed in order to have sufficient excursion of the motor muscles. The technique is similar to that presented in the previous section.

#### Krukenberg Operation

Although apparently quite disruptive for the self- image, some patients seem to love the ability of pinching provided by the operation devised by the German surgeon Hermann Krukenberg during the First World War. The operation is perhaps the best option in proximal amputations in patients living in undeveloped countries or in any patients not willing to be fitted with prosthesis.

In civil life, most cases come from crush injuries, blasts, or electrical burns. The number of cases of bilateral and unilateral hand amputations by machete in some “modern” wars may be appalling. Bilateral amputees are set in a state of total dependence on others for survival. They can no longer feed themselves, dress, or go to the toilet without help. They find themselves a burden to a group already struggling for survival. After the surgery, improvement in function was outstanding, with all patients able to feed themselves and perform some basic life activities as early as 3 months.

A minimum of 10 cm of ulna and radius, measured from the olecranon, is needed. Similarly, the pronator teres and flexor carpi radialis should be functioning, as these muscles are the ones that will “adduct” the radius. Likewise, the brachioradialis and wrist extensors in the radial side should be working to abduct the radius. Good quality of skin is required at the future stumps and tension should be avoided to prevent extrusion of the bone stumps, and shortening the stumps up to 15 cm can be done if needed. The results are uniformly satisfying for the patient, and poor results are limited to too- short stumps or severe scarring in the surrounding tissues. The procedure can be combined with cosmetic prosthesis.

**Technique.** The operation is not very complicated and consists of splitting the ulna and radius and sharing muscles on both sides. U- shaped or inverted L incisions are made on the volar and dorsal sides of the forearm to create cutaneous flaps that will wrap around the inner aspect of the gently separated ulna and radius. During the elevation, utmost care should be taken to preserve the cutaneous nerves of the forearm, as they will be responsible for the sensibility in the pincer. The median nerve is included with the radius and the ulnar with the ulna, but in case of painful neuromas, they can be resected proximally up to the motor branch to the pronator or the flexor carpi ulnaris, respectively. Excess of muscles are excised to reduce the volume of the stumps, but the radius should include flexor carpi radialis and the pronator teres on the flexor surface, with the brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, and brevis attached on the extensor surface. The ulna holds attachments of the flexor carpi ulnaris on the flexor surface, with the extensor carpi ulnaris and extensor digiti quinti proprius attached to the extensor surface. The flexor and extensors of the digits can be excised. Good coverage should be provided to the opposing surfaces areas, and the “web,” but skin grafts can be used in the rest to avoid tension.

In the immediate postoperative period the patient is trained to use the pincer’s wrist extensor for opening and the wrist flexors for closing the pincer. Minimal time is required for patients to incorporate the pincer into their daily activities. In Irmay et al. study, at 3 months all patients could feed themselves and 75% of patients had regained some manual dexterity, regardless of their unilateral or bilateral amputation status (Fig. 48.58).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: DEVASTATING DEFECTS

Toe transfers, on- top plasty, resections, allotransplantation, and prosthesis are all possible options in devastating thumb losses. The choice of any procedure will depend on many factors, most importantly the length of the remaining stump. An algorithm is proposed in Fig. 48.59. Nevertheless, the choice is complex and discussion with patients considering their wishes, social needs, environment, and medical facts may help. It is important to reemphasize that without sensory capacity, the reconstructed extremity will be used simply to please the surgeon at the moment the picture is taken, but never used again.

In metacarpal or even carpal amputations, I am biased toward toe transfer, as this upgrades whatever is left after the amputation. I am also committed to obtaining a tripod or a tripod- plus hand. However, there are four considerations that may tilt the choice to a less ambitious program, namely, proximal avulsion of nerves, avulsion of tendons, lack of proximal bone stability, and major deep scarring in the forearm. Under the last item, I include those cases where the forearm muscles are rock hard on palpation, as those muscles will not be capable of moving any tendon. The effort will not be rewarded by a good function but a calamitous result.

Once the patient is cleared for the reconstruction, I should warn that the operation is far from a standard toe transfer, and major intraoperative challenges, even for an expert surgeon, are the rule. First, the need for additional soft tissue cover, requiring a concomitant free flap, is the norm. As can be imagined, this immensely complicates an already complicated operation. Distorted anatomy, massive scarring, missing tendinous elements, lack of excursion of those available, lack of gliding layers, and unstable stumps are but a few of the local problems. Thus a considerable understanding of classic hand surgery is required to provide a truly useful pincer. Furthermore, the microvascular part is full of traps. Usually, the arteries are at the stump site, but they are sitting in scarred beds and tend to spasm. A good tip is to use only arteries surrounded by healthy yellow fat and to carry out the anastomosis proximal to a pumping side branch. The veins on the other side are often scarred locally, and they are small. As the superficial system is often destroyed, I use the deep system in the snuffbox or the deep forearm veins, which are usually healthy, and this avoids the need for a preliminary covering flap from the groin. If one follows logical principles, amazing results can be achieved for helpless hands (Fig. 48.60).

In more proximal amputations, the choice of a toe transplanted onto the radius can provide a good pincer with sensibility. Amputations 8 to 10 cm proximal to the radiocarpal joint yield the poorest results because the ability of the local muscles will be much reduced. Efforts to provide a pincer by a similar Vilkki operation has yielded poor results. In normal- sighted patients, the fitting of a prosthetic hand after this neo- thumb–type reconstruction is possible. In fact, the new thumb can be used to steer the microswitches to activate the artificial limb functions even more precisely than a myoelectric steering. In bilateral amputees, particularly in harsh living conditions, the Krukenberg is a good option. The procedure should also be offered to any patient, even to unilateral amputees, as satisfaction has proven to be very high, and the fact that renowned surgeons advocate it deserves a place in our armamentarium. Finally, in all instances the patient may prefer a prosthesis (see Chapter 50) where major advances are taking place, not to say the open field of transplantation, once the rejection phenomena is overcome.

## CONCLUSION

The thumb is crucial to hand function. Whether the surgeon reconstructs the most distal tip injury or provides a pincer in a forearm amputation, the upgrading from the previous situation will be outstanding and patient satisfaction high. In this chapter, we have covered the current state of thumb reconstruction; however, as foreseen by Dr. Strickland in the first edition of this book, “Much of what has been written in this chapter may soon become outdated thanks to microsurgery.” I also foresee that most of the techniques of my chapter will be outdated once the rejection phenomenon is finally solved. In the meantime, this is the very best I can offer my patients.
