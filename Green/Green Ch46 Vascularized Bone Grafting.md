# Vascularized Bone Grafting

*Allen T. Bishop, Alexander Y. Shin*

---
Bone grafts have been used since the first decade of the 20th century to fuse joints and repair defects. The desirability of a living bone graft was recognized by several early investigators who used pedicled fibular grafts for tibial defects as early as 1905. The concept of pedicle grafting has been widely applied since that time, using rib, clavicular, iliac crest, scapular, radial, greater and lesser trochanter, medial femoral condylar, pisiform, second metacarpal, and humeral grafts.

The microsurgical transfer of autogenous living bone awaited the development of techniques and instruments for microvascular anastomosis. With appropriate microsurgical instruments and techniques, development of free vascularized bone flaps was rapid. This included the first free bone transfer of an osteocutaneous composite rib flap,followed with use of rib, fibula, and iliac crest transfers. The fibula remains the mainstay for most large extremity defect reconstructions. Many variations have been described that allow tailoring of the graft to meet specific needs, including reconstruction of combined bone and soft tissue loss in one setting. In recent years, smaller bone grafts, as well as periosteal and osteochondral free flaps, have provided additional choices better suited for some specific applications than the fibula, rib, or iliac crest.

## GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS IN VASCULARIZED BONE GRAFTING

### Pedicle Flap Versus Free Flap

Vascularized bone grafts allow living bone tissue to be transplanted to an adjacent or remote location and survive by maintenance or restoration of blood flow. Free vascularized bone grafts require microvascular anastomoses to a recipient site artery and vein, whereas pedicle grafts are moved with their blood supply undisturbed to an adjacent area. Pedicle flaps may have orthograde or retrograde flow through a distally based pedicle with satisfactory results.

### Biology of Conventional Bone Grafts

A bone graft obtained without a blood supply is termed a *conventional graft.* It may be obtained from the patient (autograft), another individual (allograft), or another species (xenograft). Cancellous conventional autografts are commonly used to encourage healing of fractures, arthrodeses, or allograft/host junctions in reconstructive surgery. Structural autografts are used to span significant defects in the axial or appendicular skeleton. Autografts are superior, in general, to allogeneic or xenogeneic material.

For most bone defects of less than 6 cm with a well- vascularized bed, adequate soft tissue cover, and absence of infection, a conventional cancellous or corticocancellous autograft is generally recommended. Cancellous bone has greater inductive capacity than cortical bone and should be used unless mechanical stability is required.

The process of bone graft incorporation is by *creeping substitution,* a process of gradual vascular ingrowth, resorption, and replacement of necrotic bone. Creeping substitution results in rapid revascularization of small cancellous grafts but is slow and incomplete in cortical bone. As much as 40% to 50% of lamellar bone remains necrotic, and the revascularization process that does occur causes significant mechanical weakening because of resorption of necrotic bone. Large structural allografts, like autografts, must also be replaced by living bone. Despite the reduction in immunogenicity resulting from cryopreservation, they invoke a local and systemic immune response that diminishes the stimulus of new bone formation and are replaced more slowly and less completely than similar grafts of autograft bone. This effect may be diminished by freezing, freeze drying, irradiating, or decalcifying the bone or eliminated with the use of immunosuppressive drugs. Conventional (nonvascularized) bone grafts of all types have substantial problems with fatigue fracture, even years after the surgical procedure. Successful grafting requires a well- vascularized bed, adequate immobilization, and protection from excessive stress by rigid internal fixation.

### Biology of Vascularized Bone Grafts

Unlike conventional bone grafts, vascularized bone remains alive and dynamic in its new site. Viable bone does not undergo creeping substitution; thus significant osteopenia does not occur. The result is improved strength, healing, and stress responsiveness compared with nonvascularized conventional bone grafts. The incidence of stress fracture is lower than in massive structural autografts or allografts. Further, union is more rapid, and bone hypertrophy in response to applied stress may occur with time. Bone healing is more likely in difficult circumstances, including scarred or irradiated beds, or in avascular bone. Vascularized grafts have superior material properties. These include strength, toughness, and an elastic modulus two to four times greater than these factors in conventional structural grafts.

The ability of vascularized grafts to adapt to applied stress by hypertrophy is a frequently observed phenomenon. Hypertrophy does not invariably occur, however. It is less likely when the graft is significantly stress- shielded by extensive internal fixation, or in the absence of weight bearing. Other important factors include age, extent of bone vascularity, and the source of the bone graft. The fibula is more likely to undergo hypertrophy than the iliac crest or rib.

### Other Advantages of Vascularized Bone Grafts

In addition to superior cell survival, maintained circulation, and useful mechanical properties, vascularized grafts have other significant advantages over conventional grafts. These include the possibilities of restoring longitudinal growth by inclusion of the growth plate, revascularizing adjacent necrotic bone, improving local blood flow in scarred soft tissue beds, and reconstructing composite tissue loss in one procedure by inclusion of skin, muscle, tendon, nerve, and other tissues with the bone graft.

## INDICATIONS FOR VASCULARIZED BONE GRAFTING

Based on the information reported above, it would seem that vascularized autografts would be ideal for grafting under most circumstances. Their use as free tissue transfers is technically demanding, however, and pedicle grafts are often more limited in location, dimension, pedicle length, and, hence, indications. Prolonged operative times and extensive dissection increase the risk of complications, and donor site morbidity may be significant. For bone defects under 6 to 8 cm with normal soft tissues, conventional techniques remain the method of choice under many circumstances.

### Segmental Bone Loss

Vascularized transfer is indicated in segmental bone defects larger than 6 to 8 cm that are due to tumor resection, traumatic bone loss, osteomyelitis, or infected nonunion.

### Biologic Failure

The use of vascularized grafts in smaller defects is reasonable in cases where “biologic failure” of bone healing is likely or has already occurred. Examples include persistent nonunion after conventional graft treatment, poorly vascularized bone and/or surrounding soft tissue, avascular necrosis of bone, and congenital pseudarthrosis. Other indications include composite tissue loss requiring composite bone/soft tissue flap reconstruction, joint arthrodesis in exceptional circumstances, and a need for longitudinal bone growth requiring physeal transfer.

### Avascular Necrosis of Bone

Vascularized grafts have been used to treat osteonecrosis in many areas, including the femoral head, scaphoid, talus, and lunate. Experimental models of avascular necrosis have demonstrated that vascularized bone or arteriovenous pedicle grafts can be used to repair necrotic bone by neovascularization and new bone formation extending from the vascularized graft into the damaged area such as an ischemic or avascular proximal scaphoid. An accurate appraisal of bone vascularity in the clinical setting is elusive. It is often inferred by radiographic density, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal changes, or histologic examination. MRI does not correlate with bone blood flow when measured quantitatively in experimental models, and histologic studies are subject to sampling bias. At present the gold standard still remains visualizing the absence of bone bleeding with the tourniquet released at surgery. Vascularized bone or corticoperiosteal flaps also optimally treat nonunions of long bones due to osteoradionecrosis. Congenital pseudoarthroses of the forearm healed more reliably with vascularized fibular transfer than with nonvascularized graft in a review of 94 published cases.

### Complex Tissue Loss

Many vascularized bone flaps may be harvested to include other tissues. Examples include osteomuscular or osteocutaneous fibular flaps, composite groin flaps including skin and/or muscle, and scapular flaps based on the thoracodorsal pedicle that may include skin and serratus anterior or latissimus dorsi muscle (Fig. 46.1). Flow- through fibular flaps permit simultaneous transfer of a second free flap, allowing simultaneous coverage of large soft tissue defects in a single stage. The unique ability of free tissue transfer to solve complex tissue defect problems with a single- stage reconstruction makes them highly desirable in these difficult circumstances.

### Physeal Arrest

Transfer of vascularized epiphyses may allow continued growth of a limb. This is currently practical only for fibular grafts with inclusion of the entire proximal fibula, or with toe flaps including bone or joint.

### Osteomyelitis

Infections represent a particularly difficult problem. Application of systemic or local antibiotics and aggressive debridement of necrotic bone and soft tissue must be carried out until the infection is deemed inactive or eradicated. Criteria commonly used to determine inactive sepsis include an absence of sinus tracts, negative bacterial cultures, a negative C- reactive protein level, and a sedimentation rate of less than 15 mm/hour, maintained for at least 1 month. When strict criteria for determining quiescence are used, recurrent infection will occur infrequently. The use of bone with blood supply serves to further improve local blood supply and likely assists with a successful outcome.

### Carpal Defects or Disorders

Vascularized bone or osteochondral flaps may be applied to defects of the carpus and hand to aid or accelerate fracture healing or replace deficient bone. However, they have most widely been indicated for revascularization of ischemic bone. The most common instances are scaphoid fractures resulting in proximal pole avascular necrosis, osteonecrosis of the lunate (Kienböck disease), or osteonecrosis of the scaphoid (Preiser disease). They are also indicated when previous conventional surgery has failed or is deemed likely to fail. Bone- only flaps must be used with some caution once subchondral fracture results in damage to the articular surface, or in very small or fragmented scaphoid proximal fragments. In such instances, partial replacement of both deficient bone and a damaged or nonfunctional articular surface has been recently demonstrated in scaphoid nonunions with small or fragmented proximal segments and in avascular necrosis of the lunate. In both circumstances, replacing the proximal half of the lunate or scaphoid to include the radial articular surface may prove to be a viable technique.

## VASCULARIZED BONE GRAFT SELECTION

Selection of the optimal donor for a vascularized bone graft depends on consideration of a number of factors, including whether material taken from the donor site will match not only the size and shape of the defect but also its material properties. Other considerations include the proximity to the donor area, adequacy of the vascular pedicle for microvascular anastomosis at the recipient site, and donor site morbidity. The most important source of vascularized grafts for large upper extremity bone defects (>6–8 cm) is the fibula. Historically, the iliac crest has been used as well, but its membranous nature, curved shape, and relatively weak mechanical properties make it a secondary consideration in most such instances. Smaller defects, particularly in the hand and wrist, continue to benefit from vascularized bone flaps, as well. Distal radius pedicle grafts taken from either the dorsal or palmar surface have been extensively used for carpal nonunion or avascular necrosis based on well- defined anatomy and flap design. In recent years, the medial femoral condyle (MFC) flap variants, including bone, periosteum, and articular cartilage, are finding increasing favor in difficult clinical situations. Similar grafts may also be obtained from the lateral femoral condyle.

Corticoperiosteal flaps are superior to other methods in treating failed reconstructions and radiation- induced pathologic fractures of the clavicle. The healing rate and time to union are significantly better in scaphoid nonunions with humpback deformity and dorsal intercalated segmental instability carpal collapse when treated with a large MFC graft. Previously unsalvageable lunates, with damaged proximal articular surfaces, may also be reconstructed with osteoarticular MFC segments. Additional options may include pedicled flaps from the pisiform, metacarpal head, proximal second metacarpal metaphysis, the distal lateral humerus, and ulnar or radial diaphysis. Free microsurgical transfer is also possible from the iliac crest, lateral femoral condyle based on a lateral superior genicular pedicle, and rib.

### Preoperative Planning

#### Recipient Site

Preoperative planning is vital for successful free tissue transfer. The bony, vascular, and soft tissue anatomy of a particular recipient site must be considered, along with factors related to the bone defect itself. For example, the length and location of the bone defect, infection status, amount of soft tissue loss or scarring, and condition of recipient vessels must be determined. The method of bone fixation should be planned in advance. Rigid fixation and/or the use of structural allograft or autograft bone improves stability and thus the rate and extent of union, particularly in long bone diaphyseal and metaphyseal defects. If osteomyelitis is present, extensive debridement of all devitalized bone and adequate preliminary soft tissue coverage are prerequisites before vascularized grafting is considered.

The adequacy of recipient vessels is a paramount consideration. Clinical examination of pulses and the Allen test may be all that is required in nontraumatic cases. In many instances of traumatic or postirradiation bone deficiency, the extent of soft tissue injury extends well proximal and distal to the level of bony abnormality. Within the reactive zone, a patent vessel may be prone to spasm, as well as friability due to surrounding scar or granulation tissue. This will render any anastomosis potentially flawed. In such situations, planning an anastomosis proximal or distal to the zone of injury is necessary. This may be performed with a sufficiently large flap or long pedicle, or at times by the use of vein grafts. Arteriography of the recipient site should be performed if there is any preoperative concern. This is most common in trauma indications, where vascular damage is likely. The probable need for vein grafting may be determined by preoperative arteriography and by consideration of known factors such as pedicle length and distance of bone from available recipient vessels.

Vein grafting may be done as conventional arterial and venous interposition grafts or by fabrication of a temporary arteriovenous fistula. In the latter circumstance, a reversed saphenous vein graft is anastomosed end- to- side to the recipient artery and end- to- end to the vein. End- to- side arterial anastomoses may have a higher patency rate in free tissue transfer and preserve distal circulation. The shunt is allowed to perfuse while the donor tissue is harvested.

The vascularized graft can be placed in either an orthograde or retrograde flow position, as dictated by the position of the best vascular anastomosis. A retrograde position has no effect on patency or rate of union. Bony stabilization is performed, followed by vascular anastomosis. Use of stable internal fixation provides results superior to those of minimal internal or external fixation. Plates spanning the entire reconstruction, or intramedullary fixation, are optimal. Stabilization may include the use of intercalary allografts. In the upper limb, this is most generally considered for humeral shaft and shoulder arthrodesis reconstructions. Addition of supplemental cancellous graft at junction sites increases the primary healing rate.

#### Donor Site

Selection of the best donor site is based on a consideration of the recipient site requirements and the characteristics of available donors. Larger defects in the upper limb are generally best reconstructed with fibular flaps, but smaller defects and bone with impaired vascularity or failure to heal with conventional methods may heal with smaller vascularized bone, corticoperiosteal, or osteocartilaginous flaps.

The donor site should be examined for evidence of previous injury or surgery, and an adequate history should be obtained to rule out systemic disease or local closed injury possibly affecting the surgical plan. A vascular examination should be performed, including peripheral arterial pulses. In the case of fibular harvest, vascular anomalies are rare, and adequate perfusion of the foot following harvest has been demonstrated to be nearly normal in most cases. We do not routinely perform angiography unless the examination suggests possible problems. Less invasive methods, such as computed tomography (CT) scanning or magnetic resonance angiography, are proving to be of value.

### Postoperative Monitoring

Thrombosis of the vascular pedicle in a vascularized bone graft results in conditions less favorable for vascular invasion and the usual process of bone graft incorporation than a conventional bone graft. Monitoring of graft viability in the immediate postoperative period is therefore desirable. A variety of methods have been used to assess viability on an intermittent basis for the purposes of planning subsequent treatment and anticipating outcome. These include selective angiography, monitoring of healing and hypertrophy with serial radiographs, bone biopsy or observation of bleeding on subsequent surgery, or early 99m technetium bone scans, generally in the first postoperative week, as well as use of Doppler ultrasound scanning or single photon emission CT scans.

Intermittent monitoring methods, however, will not detect acute thrombosis. Continuous monitoring is possible with the use of a surface or implantable laser Doppler velocimeter or a thermocouple probe, or, most practically, monitoring of a skin paddle serving as a “buoy flap.” Detection of thrombosis allows revision of the anastomosis and salvage of a significant number of failing free tissue transfers.

#### Buoy Flap

By far the most feasible and easily interpreted continual monitor is a fasciocutaneous buoy flap. It enables direct observation of the transferred tissue, allowing immediate return to the operating room if loosening of the dressing, altering of the limb position, or trial of anticoagulation does not reverse the observed changes in 1 to 2 hours. Cutaneous island monitoring is possible with most vascularized bone flaps, including the most common fibular, iliac crest, medial femoral condylar, and 1,2 intercompartmental supraretinacular artery (1,2 ICSRA) flaps.

#### Implanted Laser or Ultrasonic Probes

Although measurement of blood flow in an imbedded free flap is difficult, special probes have been used effectively for at least 48 hours postoperatively. In a study by Schuurman et al. of free fibular transfer, an occlusion rate of 10%, with 50% successful salvage was seen with use of a laser Doppler probe. A miniature Doppler ultrasonic probe is also available, designed to be secured to the outflow vein of the flap via a silicone rubber cuff. The device was successful in salvaging 20 instances of thrombosis or spasm in 16 of 135 consecutive free flaps.

#### Arteriogram

Arteriography allows assessment of bone viability at a single point in time. Angiography performed 6 to 8 weeks after surgery, if positive, is suggestive of likely clinical progress.

#### Bone Scan

Experimentally, positive bone scans correlate with vessel patency when done before the end of the first week, prior to the onset of creeping repair in both vascularized and nonvascularized autografts (Fig. 46.2). Use of dynamic imaging provides a more accurate, even semiquantitative, linear relationship between scan values and bone blood flow.

Bone scans obtained in the first 1 to 2 weeks following flap transfer have been demonstrably useful in clinical and experimental studies, correlating well with preserved circulation and clinical outcome. In these studies, patients with a “hot” postoperative radionuclide were more likely to have a successful clinical outcome than those with negative or equivocal scans. In one series, only one of seven patients with poor scans healed primarily, and two cases (almost 30%) resulted in frank failure, necessitating removal of the transferred bone.

## COMPLICATIONS OF VASCULARIZED BONE GRAFTS

### Impaired Vascularity

Vessel thrombosis occurs in a small percentage of free tissue transfers. If thrombosis occurs in a bone flap, it is likely that the surrounding cuff of necrotic soft tissue may impede neovascularization. Experimentally, the viability of thrombosed vascularized bone grafts is significantly less than that of conventional nonvascularized grafts, as quantified by measurements of bone blood flow, new bone formation, and osteocyte counts.

Thus, the process of creeping substitution of necrotic bone is likely less extensive in failed vascularized grafts than in conventional grafts.

### Delayed Union: Need for Secondary Bone Graft

Secondary bone grafting will be necessary in a substantial number of patients treated with structural vascularized bone grafts of the long bones. Primary healing occurs in roughly 68% of vascularized fibular grafts, with poorer results in some locations and with a diagnosis of osteomyelitis. Most that do not heal primarily will benefit from a secondary bone graft. Because the mean time to healing is 6 months for fibular grafts and 7 months for iliac crest grafts, timing of a secondary graft is generally somewhat longer, frequently at or before 12 months following the vascularized graft. A largely cancellous autograft at the ununited junction is usually sufficient. Problems associated with insufficient internal fixation may require revision at that time.

### Stress Fracture

Stress fracture of a structural graft spanning a segmental defect may occur in both the upper and lower extremities (Fig. 46.3). Minami reported an incidence of 12 fractures occurring in 10 of 53 vascularized fibular transplant patients (23%). Rigid stability provided by spanning plates or allograft replacement with intramedullary fixation on only fibular grafts results in a lower incidence of fracture. Healing of stress fractures may occur with immobilization, provided the graft has adequate vascularity. Rapid formation of callus at the fracture site and hypertrophy of the bone is observed thereafter, although displacement is common and requires stabilization.

The time of fracture occurrence may be significant because most occur within the first year after grafting. Fractures occurring in this time period generally have adequate circulation, as demonstrated by postoperative bone scanning, but have had insufficient time to undergo hypertrophy. Late fractures are uncommon and are associated with negative or equivocal bone scans. Most stress fractures occur through the transferred bone, rather than at its junctions.

When the fracture occurs in adults, union is often relatively easily obtained. Nondisplaced upper extremity fractures will heal in a cast alone, whereas displaced fractures require internal or external fixation, with or without bone grafting. A vascularized graft should be protected against fatigue fracture during the first year, allowing a gradual increase in mechanical loading, which enhances remodeling and hypertrophy.

## SPECIFIC VASCULARIZED GRAFTS: LONG BONE RECONSTRUCTION

### Vascularized Fibular Grafts

### ANATOMY

The fibula is the most used vascularized bone graft because its structure and shape are appropriate for diaphyseal locations. A long, straight segment of 26 to 30 cm in length may frequently be harvested and osteosynthesis securely obtained to the recipient bone.

The blood supply to the fibula, as in other long bones, is derived normally from a nutrient artery via radially oriented branches, which penetrate the cortex and anastomose with the periosteal vessels. The source of the nutrient blood supply is a branch of the peroneal artery, generally entering the bone in its middle third. The resulting blood flow is centrifugal from medulla to cortex. This arrangement is the norm for the fibula, which has a single nutrient vessel entering its middle third from the peroneal artery. Additional periosteal branches from the peroneal and anterior tibial arteries also supply the diaphysis.

The proximal epiphysis is supplied by an arcade of vessels, including the lateral inferior genicular vessels and a recurrent branch from the anterior tibial artery. Although some recommend use of the genicular blood supply, recent studies support the use of the anterior tibial vessels, which will also support the proximal two thirds of the diaphysis via the periosteal supply. Such a pedicle supplies adequate vascularity to provide either longitudinal growth in children or viable articular cartilage for joint reconstruction. Although the genicular pedicle will provide periosteal blood flow, the peroneal vessels must be separately identified and supply the fibular shaft.

Transfer of a fasciocutaneous skin paddle of up to 10 by 20 cm is made possible by a series of fasciocutaneous or myocutaneous perforators from the peroneal artery, which typically pierces the soleus muscle adjacent to the lateral intermuscular septum (Fig. 46.4). The location of the perforators may be determined in the operating room prior to skin incision by use of a Doppler ultrasound stethoscope. Osteomuscular flaps, including the flexor hallucis longus muscle and portions of the soleus or peroneal muscles, may also be raised using the same peroneal artery pedicle.

The patient is positioned supine, with a small sandbag beneath the ipsilateral buttock and another secured to the table near its foot to allow the patient’s foot to rest securely with the knee flexed. The entire leg is draped free. Next, a pneumatic tourniquet is inflated. The fibula is measured, and its middle third identified. An incision is made directly along the fibular longitudinal axis laterally, extending through skin and subcutaneous tissue (Fig. 46.5A). The anterior border of a skin flap is incised at this step. This exposes the broad tendon of the peroneus longus, which is easily identified. A fat stripe posterior to the tendon identifies the interval between the peroneus longus muscle and the soleus muscle. This interval is developed with blunt dissection, beginning in the middle third of the bone (see Fig. 46.5B). The cutaneous vascular perforators supplying the skin may be visualized at this point passing either through the lateral intermuscular septum or, more commonly, through the adjacent anterior centimeter of the soleus. As the soleus is reflected posteriorly, one can identify the flexor hallucis muscle belly covering the posterior surface of the bone and obscuring the peroneal vessels from view.

The flexor hallucis longus (FHL) muscle origin lies well distal to the fibular neck. Proximal to the muscle, the peroneal vessels are identified as they pass deep to the FHL origin (see Fig. 46.5C). The vessels should be mobilized carefully from the posterior surface of the fibula to prevent their inadvertent injury when the bone is divided.

Dissection is continued progressively proximally, elevating the soleus tendon of origin from its fibular attachment. Muscular branches of the peroneal vessels to the soleus will be visualized during the dissection and should be ligated and divided unless a composite osteomuscular flap with the soleus is desired. The soleus is completely detached from the proximal fibula.

Next, the lateral compartment muscles are elevated from the fibula. Proximally, this must be done subperiosteally to avoid injury to the peroneal nerve. The nerve must be visualized and protected at this proximal level, lying directly on the bone of the fibular neck and proximal diaphysis (see Fig. 46.5D). As the muscle is elevated in a distal direction, the superficial branch of the nerve lies further from the bone, and an extraperiosteal dissection plane will better preserve the bone blood supply. This dissection should leave small occasional wisps of muscle rather than a large muscular cuff on the bone surface because this will form a necrotic barrier to revascularization should the anastomosis fail (see Fig. 46.5E).

The anterior compartment is elevated similarly. Again, care is needed proximally to protect the deep branch of the peroneal nerve. The anterior tibial artery will be identified, as will the interosseous membrane at the conclusion of this dissection. Depending on ease of visibility, the interosseous membrane may be divided at this point (see Fig. 46.5F) or the bone divided proximally and distally first. Bone division is carried out with a Gigli saw, protecting neurovascular structures with careful circumferential retraction and blunt passage of the saw cable. The middle third of the bone, at a minimum, must be raised to preserve the nutrient artery supply. Harvesting more of the proximal third will improve visualization of the peroneal artery pedicle at the conclusion of the graft elevation. Although the entire proximal fibula may be harvested if articular surface or physis is required, 7 to 8 cm of the distal fibula should be preserved to prevent valgus instability of the ankle. In children, a distal tibiofibular syndesmotic fusion is necessary with an iliac crest interposition graft and screws to prevent ankle instability (Fig. 46.6). Fusion is not necessary in adults.

**Fibula with skin flap.** Osteocutaneous flaps are often advocated because the skin serves as a visible and immediate monitor of peroneal vascular patency. At times, the skin is needed to enable wound closure without tension and provide coverage for the underlying fibula and vascular reconstruction. Inclusion of skin is made possible by several fasciocutaneous perforators from the peroneal artery, which supplies the skin at intervals, particularly along the middle third of the bone. If a cutaneous flap is elevated with bone, the locations of the major fasciocutaneous perforators are identified with a Doppler ultrasound stethoscope prior to tourniquet elevation because their exact position and number vary considerably between individuals. They may be readily identified midlaterally by an audible pulse using the probe, near the soleus/peroneal junction (lateral intermuscular septum). Alternatively, the exact placement of the skin island can be determined by visualization of the vessels directly after the anterior incision is made. The dissection proceeds exactly as for a conventional fibular graft, except that the peroneus- soleus interval is left undisturbed in the middle and distal thirds until the peroneal vessels are exposed anteriorly (after fibular osteotomies, and division of the tibialis posterior). Isolation of the perforating vessels is performed by division of the posterior skin flap at this point, and their mobilization back to the peroneal vessels. To avoid problems with excessively tight skin closure and compartment syndrome, the donor site at the skin paddle area should be closed by a split- thickness skin graft, unless a very small buoy flap only is taken.

**Fibula with physis.** Inclusion of the proximal growth plate to allow for growth of the transferred bone is a reasonable procedure when no other options exist. In a leg with normal vascular anatomy, current opinion favors the use of the anterior tibial vascular pedicle, which will support the fibular head and neck with a nutrient vessel supply, and a periosteal supply to the diaphysis. The technique of harvest is well described by Innocenti. It is most used in the upper limb for restoration of growth following resection of the distal radius in children, but it is also useful with resection of the proximal humerus or distal ulna.

A longitudinal incision centered between the tibialis anterior and extensor digitorum longus is prolonged proximally, following the biceps femoris muscle. Dissection in the intermuscular plane between these two muscles, starting distally, exposes the tibialis anterior neurovascular bundle and preserves the delicate network of periosteal vessels supplying the diaphysis. During proximal dissection, many motor branches of the peroneal nerve cross the operating field, and they must be carefully preserved.

The common peroneal nerve is identified and protected at the level of the fibular neck. The origins of the extensor digitorum longus and peroneal muscles are incised and reflected laterally and inferiorly 2 cm distal to the proximal fibular tip, leaving a muscular cuff surrounding the fibular epiphysis. Once the vessels are isolated, the fibular head is released by division of the biceps femoris tendon and iliotibial band attachments and release of the lateral collateral ligament and tibiofibular joint capsule. The anterior tibial vessels may be ligated at their origin to complete the harvest.

**Fibula with muscle.** The fibula may be harvested with inclusion of the lateral portion of soleus and/or the flexor hallucis muscle. The FHL provides only enough muscle to cover the distal fibular segment. It is easily harvested by dividing its distal tendon rather than by separating the FHL from the fibula at its origin.

A larger muscle, sufficient to obliterate dead space or provide coverage, may be preferred. In this instance, use of the lateral portion of the soleus is appropriate. This is also easily accomplished by sparing the soleus muscular branch(es) visualized during exposure of the peroneal pedicle early in the dissection, as described previously. Once one or more of the muscular branches is identified and spared, the dissection proceeds unchanged until the end of the elevation, when the soleus is divided longitudinally in its midsubstance. The tibialis posterior artery and tibial nerve are carefully protected during this process.

**Double- barrel flap.** Strength can be improved particularly for femoral applications, or two segments can be separated sufficiently to allow simultaneous reconstruction of the radius and ulna, by use of a double- barrel graft. Following harvest, the free fibula graft is osteotomized at its midpoint, the surgeon having first gently elevated and separated the peroneal vessels from the bone at the osteotomy point. This produces two vascularized bone grafts that require only one set of anastomoses. This folded fibula provides twice the crosssectional area of a single fibula transfer when placed in a single bone. For forearm use, resection of a short intercalary segment of fibula will increase the separation of the proximal and distal segments of bone without tension on the spanning vascular bundle for reconstruction of both forearm bones. An effort should be made to ensure that one of the segments is vascularized by both a periosteal and an endosteal blood supply, including the nutrient vessel. In most cases, this will be the proximal segment; the distal segment will therefore be vascularized by periosteal vessels alone.

#### Fibular Graft Complications

Donor site complications are unusual and mostly transient. A review of 132 free fibular grafts from the Mayo Clinic demonstrated donor site problems in 10 patients (8%). These included transient peroneal nerve palsy (in 5 patients), FHL contracture (in 3 patients), stress fracture of the ipsilateral tibia (in 1 patient), and compartment syndrome (in 1 patient). Closure after harvest of an osteocutaneous fibula should include use of a split- thickness skin graft to minimize the risk of compartment syndrome postoperatively.

In children, valgus deformity of the ankle may occur unless a distal tibiofibular fusion is performed proximal to the physis (see Fig. 46.6). No similar sequelae have been seen at the knee when the fibular head is harvested for articular surface or physis.

### Iliac Crest Flap

Although widely used in past decades, the iliac crest flap has been less commonly used in recent years, particularly in the upper limb. This is due to the development of alternative sources of smaller bone grafts, such as the MFC, and the preferential use of fibular flaps in long bone reconstruction. Additional factors are its curved shape and relatively weak material properties compared with the fibula. The donor scar is well hidden, however, and it can include a very large cutaneous component, permitting simultaneous reconstruction of large soft tissue defects. Its principal advantages are its largely cancellous nature and the large amount of soft tissue that may be raised with bone as a combined osteomusculocutaneous flap. The advantages of the osteocutaneous groin flap include (1) its use in supplying vascularized bone to what is frequently a poor recipient bed for a bone graft, (2) its use in reconstruction of both soft tissue and bony defects simultaneously, and (3) the fact that it can be used in facilities without the capability for microvascular surgery when used as a pedicle flap for the upper extremity. As such, it remains a valuable reconstructive option in selected circumstances. Smaller grafts may certainly be taken. Excellent clinical outcomes have been reported for reconstruction of both scaphoid nonunions and Kienböck disease, for example.

### ANATOMY

The iliac crest receives a dual blood supply from the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA) and deep circumflex iliac artery (DCIA). Of the two, the DCIA system is most important. Musculocutaneous perforators penetrating the abdominal wall 1 cm proximal to the iliac crest provide its nutrition. The skin paddle is less reliable than a standard groin flap, and its size, when based on the DCIA, is quite variable, ranging from 7 by 10 to 15 by 30 cm. Enhanced skin survival may result from “supercharging” its blood supply using both SCIA and DCIA vessels. An osteomuscular flap may also be raised, including a portion of the adjacent internal oblique muscle or transversalis muscle based on the ascending branch of the DCIA.

The DCIA arises from the external iliac artery 1 cm proximal to the inguinal ligament. Several branches supply the internal oblique and iliacus muscles, as well as the lateral femoral circumflex vessels. The DCIA courses obliquely cephalad and laterally between extraperitoneal fat and the transversus muscle, until it reaches the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). At this level, it pierces the transversus muscle to run between it and the internal oblique muscle along the inner lip of the iliac crest adjacent to the iliacus muscle. At its origin, the artery has a mean diameter of 2.78 mm, and the vena comitans, 3.6 mm.

Although the entire crest may be harvested, the practical limit of length is 10 cm as a vascularized graft because of its curved shape. It is relatively less well suited for diaphyseal reconstruction than the fibula because remodeling for tolerance of weight bearing is prolonged. Further, osteosynthesis is difficult and weak.

#### Technique

The patient is positioned supine. The patient is draped to the midline anteriorly, costal margin proximally, and as far posteriorly as possible. The leg is draped free to facilitate hip flexion for wound closure. The skin flap is marked, its axis along the inner margin of the iliac crest, centered at a point 4 to 8 cm posterior to the ASIS. The pubic tubercle, ASIS, femoral pulse, and iliac crest are marked. The skin flap is incised along its medial margin, extending it medially along the inguinal ligament and obliquely inferiorly along the femoral pulse. Just proximal to the inguinal ligament, the external oblique is incised, exposing the inguinal canal contents. These are secured and retracted medially with an elastic cord. The floor of the canal (internal oblique and transversalis fascia) is incised to visualize the DCIA and accompanying veins. After identifying their origin, they are traced laterally toward the ASIS, and ligation of multiple small branches, as well as the large ascending branch, is performed. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve is identified and spared if it lies below the vessels. It will occasionally pass superficially and must then be divided for later repair. The external and internal oblique and transversus muscles are sectioned 2 cm medial to the iliac crest insertion to preserve the skin blood supply. At this point, the vessels are not visualized. Finally, the lateral skin incision is made and the tensor fascia femoris and gluteal vessels are dissected extraperiosteally from the outer table of the ilium. The ilium is osteotomized and the pedicle divided to complete the harvest. Closure is important to prevent herniation, first approximating the transversalis to the iliacus fascia and then the internal and external oblique to the gluteus and tensor fascia femoris. It may be necessary to attach the abdominal wall muscles to the bone via suture holes or bone anchors. Skin closure is facilitated by hip flexion.

#### Iliac Crest Complications

Complications related to the iliac crest include incisional hernias and partial loss of the cutaneous portion of the composite flaps. The surgical approach to the vascular pedicle is through the inguinal canal; this approach raises the risk of an abdominal hernia.

### Results: Large Segmental Bone Loss

#### Fibula Donor Results

The authors’ review of 478 vascularized fibular grafts performed for all indications demonstrated the rate of primary union healing (without secondary bone grafting) to be 68%, with ultimate healing after supplemental bone grafting to occur in 82% of cases. Healing, as well as hypertrophy, depends on a number of variables, including patient age, weight- bearing location, type of graft used, method of fixation, and vascular status of the graft. Hypertrophy, when it occurs, is seen by an average of 13.5 months postoperatively. It occurs primarily in young individuals (age <20 years) and is more common in the lower extremity when stresses of weight bearing are not shielded by rigid fixation spanning the fibular graft (Fig. 46.7). Fibulas are more likely to hypertrophy than other grafts. Improving the biomechanics of the reconstruction will improve results. Options include simultaneous use of a size- matched cryopreserved structural allograft with either an onlay or an intramedullary fibula or internal fixation spanning the entire reconstruction (plate or intramedullary rod).

#### Iliac Crest Donor Results

Vascularized iliac crest grafting is subject to higher rates of complications with lower overall success compared with the fibula. A summary of 97 flaps in the six reports cited reveals an average time to union of 7 months, with prolonged protected weight bearing (mean, 18 months). The overall frank failure rate was somewhat higher than with fibular grafts, with eventual union in 67% (38% requiring secondary bone grafting). Most authors are dissatisfied with the skin flap, noting a frequent tendency for necrosis of a part or all of the soft tissue. Suggested solutions include use of an osteomuscular flap without skin, with inclusion of the internal oblique muscle, or improving skin perfusion with a double pedicle of both the DCIA and SCIA. Revision of the anastomosis is more commonly required than for fibular grafts.

#### Fibular and Iliac Crest Grafts in the Upper Extremity

A review of fibula and iliac crest grafts for large upper extremity defects in 21 cases included graft placement in the humerus in 11 patients, radius in 5 patients, ulna in 3 patients, and clavicle in 2 patients. Overall, primary bone union occurred in 15 patients (71.4%), and eventual union was achieved in 17 patients (81%). The most favorable results were achieved in cases of forearm bone reconstruction (100% union). Clavicle nonunions and shoulder fusions with vascularized bone carry a worse prognosis. Failure to correct deformed clavicular nonunions may result in brachial plexus compression, as well as shoulder girdle weakness and pain. The use of the medial femoral condylar corticoperiosteal graft, combined with conventional graft and internal fixation, provides superior results for difficult clavicle nonunions (see Fig. 46.11D, later). Results for shoulder fusion can be improved with improved stability using allograft proximal humerus/fibula composite grafts with rigid internal fixation (Fig. 46.8), although satisfactory results with the fibula alone have also been reported. Segmental loss of the humerus requires careful technique and planning to avoid prolonged healing and stress fracture. Use of a bridging plate and/or frozen allograft structural segment in addition to the fibula is to be recommended (Fig. 46.9). In the forearm, radius reconstruction for sarcoma or aggressive benign tumors has provided acceptable healing and function. The average time to union for all locations is approximately 6 months, without significant difference based on anatomic location.

#### Results by Diagnosis

The results of reconstruction of large defects with vascularized bone grafting differ significantly by diagnosis. A history of infection had the least favorable results overall. In a large series of 160 vascularized grafts, defects with infection had a primary union rate of 49% and an overall union rate of 77%. Reconstructions when no infection was present had a primary union rate of 69%, with 84% ultimate success after a secondary graft.

**Grafting for defects caused by tumor excision.** Vascularized grafts are useful in limb salvage surgery of aggressive upper extremity tumors, including benign lesions such as giant cell tumor and aneurysmal bone cyst as well as primary bone malignant tumors (Fig. 46.10). Many patients will receive preoperative and postoperative chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, with their associated morbidities. Postoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy increases the incidence of stress fracture and delays healing in experimental models, although most clinical cases eventually heal, often without surgery. Han reported successful union in 81% of 69 tumor patients in 1992, and even more favorable results are seen in more recent studies.

There is also a proven role for fibular grafts in correcting nonunion at the host- allograft junction following tumor reconstruction with conventional frozen allograft diaphyseal segments. A review of 33 patients treated with fibular onlay graft salvage of allograft reconstruction failures resulted in successful salvage in 23, with 7 failures resulting in 5 amputations. Pathologic fracture due to tumor invasion is also effectively treated by fibular flaps.

Radiation necrosis of bone is a not- infrequent complication of radiotherapy for soft tissue and bone malignant tumors. This is a progressive fibrotic problem, which requires efforts to improve local blood flow. Fractures occur years later (mean, 111 months in one study).

Spanning the area of necrosis and fracture with vascularized onlay bone grafts provided healing in 16 of 18 cases in this series.

**Grafting in cases of infection.** The results are less satisfactory for patients who had had the reconstruction for bone loss due to osteomyelitis. Experience with infected pseudarthrosis with segmental osseous defect, treated by debridement and microvascular bone transfer, has demonstrated an increased potential for a number of complications. These include recurrent infection, occasionally leading to amputation, and nonunion. It is critical that vascularized bone transfer be delayed until sepsis is inactive. Criteria used for determining inactive sepsis include absence of sinus tracts, negative bacterial cultures, negative C- reactive protein levels, and a sedimentation rate of less than 15 mm/hour, maintained for at least 1 month following the last clinical evidence of infection. Recurrent infection was seen after vascularized bone grafting in 16% of osteomyelitis patients in the large Mayo Clinic series.

In the forearm, infected nonunions may not respond to conventional treatment. Dell reported success with the use of vascularized fibular grafts and conversion to a one- bone forearm after failure of as many as six procedures. The fibula was fixed to the ulna proximally and the radius distally with internal fixation, and this was supplemented with an external fixator in three of the four patients. All wounds healed and all bone junctures healed, with one requiring supplemental cancellous grafting.

**Grafting following trauma.** The prognosis is better for traumatic nonunion without infection. Union rates are typically greater than 90% when patients requiring supplemental grafting are included.Although some have reported poorer union rates for trauma than for tumor indications, trauma cases had the highest union rate overall in Han’s series (primary union, 76%; overall, 92%). Residual limb shortening and other problems are common, occurring more frequently in patients with scarred and relatively avascular soft tissues surrounding the long bone defects.

**Grafting for congenital pseudarthrosis of the forearm.** The use of vascularized grafts for congenital tibial pseudarthrosis has been shown to be demonstrably superior to conventional methods, including bone graft, electrical stimulation, corticoperiosteal graft, or osteotomy of the involved bone. Vascularized grafts have been used successfully in pseudarthrosis of the forearm as well. In the forearm, creation of a one- bone forearm may be reasonable, especially if conventional bone grafting has failed. Wide resection of associated neurofibromatosis is required. With this initial resection and a vascularized graft, good results may be anticipated.

**Grafting for epiphyseal transfer and growth.** The superiority of vascularized growth plate transfer over nonvascularized transfer for physeal replacement has been well documented in experimental models.Clinically, nearly normal longitudinal growth following digit replantation or toe transfer in children has been well documented. The application of epiphyseal transfers to problems such as radial club hand and physeal arrest of long bones is currently showing substantial promise. Long- term longitudinal growth has been seen in 12 children with proximal humerus or distal radius epiphyseal reconstruction using the fibula, averaging more than 1 cm/year.

## VASCULARIZED GRAFTS: SMALL BONE AND PERIOSTEAL FLAPS

### Indications for Grafting in the Carpus

#### Grafting for Fracture of the Scaphoid

The scaphoid is the most frequently fractured carpal bone. With prompt appropriate diagnosis and treatment, over 90% will unite.

Unfortunately, failure to diagnose acute injury or to surgically treat displaced scaphoid fractures increases the risk of nonunion substantially. The development of scaphoid foreshortening and associated carpal instability with elapsed time contributes to the risk of nonunion. In addition, the fracture may disrupt the proximal pole blood supply, particularly in proximal third fractures, resulting in avascular necrosis of the proximal fragment.

Humpback deformity alone does not currently appear to require vascularized grafting. Instead, reconstruction of scaphoid dimension with a conventional graft and a scaphoid screw provides reliable healing.

Many scaphoid fractures that fail to heal with inlay or wedge conventional grafts have impaired vascularity as a contributing factor, as demonstrated by sclerosis of the proximal pole on x- ray and MRI studies and absent or spotty areas of punctate bleeding from exposed bone at the time of surgery. The risk is further increased in smokers. The use of conventional grafts, such as the Matti- Russe volar inlay graft, may be contraindicated in the presence of an avascular fragment.

Recently the need for vascularized grafts has been questioned in scaphoid nonunion. Part of the authors’ case relates to the difficulty in making a diagnosis of avascularity. While quantitative measurement of bone blood flow is possible experimentally using microsphere entrapment or indicator dilution methods, no measure of ischemia in clinical use has a proven correlation with bone blood flow. MRI imaging of T1 and T2 signals does not correlate with blood flow. Histology is subject to sampling error, and density changes on radiographs are suggestive at best. Punctate bleeding from bone surfaces, observed with a deflated tourniquet, remains the best clinical assessment of blood flow.

Clinical outcomes of scaphoid treatment provide the best evidence for the value of vascularized grafts. They have long been proposed as appropriate methods to increase the rate and frequency of healing in fractures with a poor prognosis.

As early as 1965, Roy- Camille used the palmar tubercle of the scaphoid based on an abductor pollicis brevis muscle pedicle. More recently, palmar distal radius grafts, dorsal radius grafts, second metacarpal base grafts, free medial femoral condylar grafts,and free iliac grafts have demonstrated improved results compared with conventional grafts in difficult circumstances. These include displaced acute fractures with small proximal fragments or bone deficiency requiring grafting, failed conventional grafts, and nonunions associated with avascular necrosis. Alternatively, inlay or wedge bone grafting coupled with a small, vascularized graft or vascular bundle implantation may be considered. Vascularized grafts are also indicated in avascular necrosis of the entire scaphoid, prior to bone collapse and fragmentation.

#### Grafting for Kienböck Disease

Treatment of Kienböck disease remains controversial, with a large number of treatment options available. These include mechanical unloading and revascularization procedures. Examples of the former include joint leveling, angular radius osteotomy, capitate shortening, and intercarpal fusions. Arteriovenous pedicles and vascularized bone grafts are examples of the latter. In addition, salvage procedures for advanced changes with arthrosis may be necessary, including proximal row carpectomy or complete wrist arthrodesis.

Experimental studies have demonstrated the ability of vascularized bone grafts to revascularize ischemic bone. The main function of the procedure appears to be the provision of vessels into avascular bone. The use of vascularized bone to both revascularize necrotic lunate cancellous bone and, if necessary, restore its dimensions once collapse begins is an attractive alternative to joint leveling and other load- altering options.

### Medial Femoral Condyle Grafts

The MFC flap was described originally as a corticoperiosteal flap for local use. Its potential as a free corticoperiosteal flap was soon demonstrated and applied to upper limb defects and for wrist arthrodesis following infection or when soft tissue cover is needed using a chimeric ALT/MFC design. Periosteal flaps in general have been demonstrated experimentally to produce predictable new bone formation, and a variety of other donor sites have been identified, including the clavicle, fibula, ilium, humerus, and tibia. In the upper extremity, thin corticoperiosteal grafts and small periosteal bone grafts harvested from the supracondylar region of the femur have proven to be of great use, based on either the descending genicular or medial superior genicular vessels (Fig. 46.11A). This graft is elastic and readily conforms to small tubular bones. Originally described as a pedicle flap, it has been successfully used as a free tissue transfer for clavicular, humeral, and forearm applications, including pathologic fractures from radiation necrosis and other recalcitrant nonunions.

It is certain that the indications for this versatile flap will broaden in the near future.

As originally described, the vascular supply to the surface of the distal medial femoral metaphysis is provided by two vessels in most individuals. Since the anatomy of the region was originally described, further details have been added. The two arteries supplying the medial distal femur are the descending genicular vessel, present in 89% of 20 adult knee specimens in a recent study, and the superomedial genicular vessel, which is constantly present (see Fig. 46.11A). The descending genicular vessel arises from the superficial femoral artery and vein proximal to the adductor hiatus at a mean distance of 13.7 mm proximal to the joint line (see Fig. 46.11B). The artery has a mean diameter of 1.5 mm and is the dominant vessel in 89% of the specimens studied by Yamamoto and colleagues, and in 93% of 113 sequential MFC bone grafts. When absent or small in size, the medial superior genicular vessel is invariably of sufficient size and length to serve as the vascular supply for an MFC flap.

The descending genicular vessels separate into osteoarticular and saphenous branches at an average distance of 11 cm proximal to the femorotibial joint. The osteoarticular branch supplies several small branches to the posterior border of the vastus medialis, which lies immediately anterior to it in the distal thigh. It is joined by the medial superior genicular vessel just proximal to the posterior aspect of the MFC to supply the periosteum and bone of the medial distal femoral metaphysis. Together they form a nearly circular pattern of vessels lying on the bone just proximal to the knee joint capsule and medial collateral ligament origin. They form a nearly circular pattern on the surface of the medial femoral metaphysis. These osteoarticular vessels supplied an average of 30 perforators into the bone, with the largest number located in the distal posterior quadrant of the condylar surface (Fig. 46.12A). They penetrated the cancellous bone to an average depth of 13 mm, supporting the use of the MFC as an ideal periosteal and bone flap. The branching pattern of the conjoined medial and descending genicular vessels was further described by Hugon and associates. It separates into two main branches: an upper transverse branch and a central longitudinal artery. The former runs anteriorly to the patellar facet, and the latter toward the tibial joint line. The vessels are connected by an arcuate anastomotic vessel forming the previously described circular appearance easily seen on first inspection of the bone surface. The longitudinal vessel is used for harvest of vascularized bone, and the upper transverse vessel is required if an osteoarticular (medial femoral trochlear [MFT]) graft is to be used.

The safe dimensions of bone that may be harvested depend primarily on the location of the metaphyseal/diaphyseal junction. It is imperative that the bone graft not extend beyond the thin metaphyseal bone if weakening of the femur and potential disastrous femoral fracture is to be avoided. This complication is rare but has occurred when an overzealous graft is taken. This is due to the open- section effect that markedly weakens tubular diaphyseal bone when resected at this level. Thus even though some proximal perforators may be identified as much as 13 cm proximal to the joint line, no attempt should be made to harvest bone at this level. It is unlikely that grafts longer than 6 to 7 cm may be safely taken, based on the authors’ experience, but the length most importantly should be based on preoperative or fluoroscopic findings at surgery so as to not violate the robust proximal diaphyseal cortex. No complications were seen in 10 patients who had undergone MFC free flap procedures and were followed more than a year postoperatively with CT scans. The average bone harvested per procedure was 16.1 cm (range, 2 to 34 cm), representing an average condylar volume of 9% (range, 1% to 18%). The depth of the graft ranged from 4 to 6 mm in this study, and the longest graft harvested measured 7 cm in length. We have routinely used grafts as deep as 15 mm without problems when the other dimensions are similar or smaller. Because only minimal bone regeneration was seen in Rao’s study, one cannot expect the defect to fill in spontaneously. We routinely fill the MFC bone flap harvest site with bone substitute to facilitate remodeling for this reason.

Inclusion of a skin island is advocated by some for monitoring flap viability and can also be used for soft tissue cover if needed. Use of the saphenous branch will require a long proximal pedicle if it is to be included with the descending genicular vessel because it arises approximately 2 cm distal to the descending genicular artery origin.An additional distinct, reliable, further distal, descending genicular cutaneous branch can be found at condyle level, capable of supporting skin, as well. This vessel provides an average perfusion area of 70 cm centered over the medial knee. The saphenous artery branch was reported to support an average perfusion area of 361 cm along the medial aspect of the distal thigh and proximal leg. Perforators may be identified by direct visualization at the time of flap exposure.

Inclusion of patellar facet cartilage is desirable when reconstructing damaged or missing joint surfaces, most commonly in fragmented small proximal scaphoid pole fractures and the proximal (radial) articular surface of the lunate in Kienböck disease. The bone is harvested from the medial trochlea, ideally slightly distal to the most proximal cartilage border. This area has little or no patellar contact but serves as a pulley for the medial aspect of the quadriceps tendon during knee flexion. Harvest of bone no larger than 7 to 8 mm in lateral/medial width and 10 to 12 mm in proximal/distal length has been recommended, based on limited data.

#### Surgical Technique

**Flap exposure and pedicle dissection.** The patient is positioned supine on the operating table. A sandbag taped to the table distal to the foot allows the knee to rest in 90 degrees of flexion. The limb is elevated but not exsanguinated prior to tourniquet elevation to more easily visualize the periosteal vessels. Once the tourniquet is elevated, the surgical approach for any of the MFC flap variants (bone, corticoperiosteal, or osteochondral) is made along the posterior border of the vastus medialis muscle. The skin incision may be longitudinal for bone- only flaps, but it may best curve in a convex- posterior direction for osteochondral flap harvest or to more safely identify skin perforators if a buoy flap is to be included. This incision is thus directed from posterior/proximal at the Hunter canal toward the medial border of the patella and again posteriorly to the joint line. The proximal border of the skin incision will overlie the adductor magnus and sartorius in the distal medial thigh. By retracting the vastus medialis anteriorly and the sartorius muscle posteriorly, the descending genicular vessels and superomedial genicular vessels are visualized in the floor of the vastus compartment and behind the proximal medial condyle, respectively. They are generally closely applied to the vastus muscle to several muscular perforators that must be ligated and divided. As the vessels approach the femoral metaphysis, they follow its contour to lie more superficially, requiring attention of the surgeon in this area. At this level, they are joined by the medial superior genicular vessels, which follow the posterior surface of the femur toward their popliteal origin. They must be visualized and assessed for their diameter relative to the descending genicular pedicle. In 10% of cases, they will be larger and likely a superior pedicle. They are best found just anterior to the adductor longus tendon by incising the thin fascial layer at its anterior surface to expose the posterior surface of the distal femur. A distalfirst approach is often to be preferred, initially exposing the medial femoral metaphysis and the superficial and prominent periosteal vessels. This is easily done by following and mobilizing the posterior border of the quadriceps musculotendinous junction. Once the quadriceps tendon and distal vastus medialis muscle are mobilized and retracted anteriorly, the face of the MFC is immediately exposed and the periosteal vessels identified, forming a robust, arching arcade on the surface of the MFC (see Fig. 46.11A). A natural plane of dissection lies immediately superficial to the vessels, allowing their rapid more proximal exposure. As described previously, both the medial superior and descending genicular branches must be identified at the proximal condyle and a decision made regarding selection of the best vessels for microvascular anastomoses. Cutaneous branches are sought if a buoy flap is to be used, and muscular branches of the descending genicular ligated. It may be followed proximally to its superficial femoral origin some 13 cm above the joint line to maximize vessel diameter or to include a saphenous branch–based skin island. Shorter pedicles may be used with pedicle vessels of a smaller diameter. Similarly, the medial superior vessels may be followed toward the popliteal vessels with somewhat greater difficulty. Multiple bone and muscular branches will be encountered and require ligation. During this exposure process, the vessels are bathed with papaverine or other vasodilators. Care is taken to maintain the patient’s core temperature to help minimize intraoperative vasospasm.

**Medial femoral condyle corticoperiosteal flap harvest.** With the pedicles mobilized and the MFC exposed, a corticoperiosteal graft is outlined to include the large arcuate vessels that follow the margins of the condyle. Distally and posteriorly, care must be taken to avoid injury of the medial collateral ligament. The superior margin is the reflection of the knee joint capsule at the medial patellar facet. Proximally, the change in cortical thickness at the diaphyseal- metaphyseal margin limits elevation. The periosteum, together with fragments of the underlying outer cortex, is elevated with the accompanying vessels, with a dimension of generally no more than 5 by 7 cm (see Fig. 46.11C). This is done by incising the margins and using a small osteotome to alternately cut, then pry, the periosteum from the underlying femur. Note that one should include fragments of the underlying thin cortex. Once elevated, the corticoperiosteal graft should be pliable (see Fig. 46.11D), enabling it to be wrapped around the small- diameter diaphysis of the clavicle, radius, or ulna or to conform to the shape of a larger bone. Once elevated, the pedicle previously dissected as described above is ligated and divided.

At the recipient site, the corticoperiosteal flap may be wrapped around the nonunion site of the clavicle, radius, or ulna and secured with heavy nonabsorbable suture (see Fig. 46.11D). **Medial femoral condyle vascularized bone graft harvest.** Harvest of the MFC graft should be from the same side as the upper limb recipient, unless there is a compelling reason not to do so. This is potentially important because any donor site discomfort requiring support with a cane or crutch requires use of the opposite hand. Although significant temporary pain is uncommon, it is impossible to predict whether it will occur prior to surgery. With the pedicles mobilized and the MFC exposed, the bone graft site is determined by visual inspection of the condyle. For scaphoid nonunions with a humpback deformity, the graft dimensions are generally 10 to 12 mm in proximal and distal length, and 10 mm in anterior and posterior width and depth. As described previously, the optimal location for the graft is the distal posterior quadrant of the condyle (see Fig. 46.12A). The graft is outlined in this area centered over the longitudinal branch of the conjoined medial genicular vessels. It is necessary to free the longitudinal branch proximal to the flap from the underlying periosteum. We prefer to do this using a microbipolar cautery and a small scalpel blade, elevating the pedicle by dissection 1 to 2 mm to either side of the vessels and gently dissecting beneath them with the aid of a small- diameter vessel loop. Multiple small periosteal vessels can be seen and dealt with in this fashion with the microbipolar cautery at a distance far enough from the pedicle so as not to endanger it. Once the pedicle is entirely freed to the desired position of the bone graft, the graft is outlined to the desired depth with a microsagittal saw along all four borders. Next, a small wedge of bone is removed to one side of the flap (Fig. 46.13). This allows access to the deep surface of the graft with a small osteotome. It is used with a prying motion to complete the elevation of the graft from below. Attempts to free the graft in any other way risk fracture and separation of the cortex from the underlying cancellous bone. The excavated cavity may be filled with a bone substitute if desired prior to layered wound closure. Note that the graft must not extend into the thick cortex at the diaphyseal/metaphyseal junction. Such extension risks the possibility of a femoral fracture. Generally, the maximal length obtainable is no more than 6 to 7 cm. The graft is placed into the scaphoid as an interposition graft through a volar (Russe) incision in the floor of the flexor carpi radialis tendon sheath and is fixed with a cannulated scaphoid screw. Vascular repairs are end- to- side to the radial artery and end- to- end to a vena comitans (see Fig. 46.12B).

**Medial femoral trochlea osteochondral graft harvest.** As stated previously, the common genicular artery and venae comitantes divide into two major periosteal vascular branches at the level of the condylar flare. The transverse branch courses 90 degrees anteriorly from the main pedicle and its longitudinal branch continuation. The transverse branch is directed toward the medial trochlea. It is followed to the proximal knee capsule, which is opened to visualize the trochlea and clearly see the multiple nutrient branches supplying bone and cartilage in this region. Their position marks the target area for harvest. A margin of bone and periosteum bordering the articular cartilage that includes these nutrient branches must be included with the flap. Before the graft is elevated, however, the transverse branch vessels proximal to the graft are mobilized to the proximal condyle as described for MFC bone graft harvest, completing the pedicle exposure. The pedicle vessels are bathed in papaverine or another vasodilator thereafter. In elevating the graft, it is important to minimize its dimensions because of potential donor site morbidity. Some form of preoperative or intraoperative modeling is helpful in this regard. One method described by Bürger is the intraoperative molding of methyl methacrylate to the donor site defect. A maximal suggested size is 8 by 12 mm. Either small, sharp osteotomes or a microsagittal saw is used to elevate the bone.

#### Medial Femoral Condyle Graft Aftercare

All patients will experience discomfort at the donor site postoperatively. In the case of bone and corticoperiosteal flaps, the discomfort is caused primarily by motion of the soft tissues over the extraarticular area of the bone graft harvest. Weight bearing is not painful, but knee flexion and extension may be uncomfortable for up to 6 weeks. Patients begin immediate weight bearing without restrictions on the same day as surgery, although a knee immobilizer is provided for intermittent use at the patient’s discretion. The patient is instructed in knee flexion and extension exercises and advised to increase activity as discomfort permits, without specific restrictions.

#### Donor Site Complications

All clinical reports in the literature to date describe MFC donor site complications to be few and transient. All patients will have medial distal thigh discomfort as described previously. Suture intolerance, seroma, and numbness in the saphenous territory are occasionally seen and have been reported to be transient. The saphenous nerve is vulnerable to injury and must be protected.

A review of 75 grafts found that graft size was a factor in occurrence of these complications. While most averaged less than 2 cm in size, some harvested for other reconstructive needs exceeded 4 cm in length and had higher levels of postoperative complaints, as did smokers and diabetics.

No published reports of femoral fracture have been published, and CT analysis of donor site morphologic characteristics postoperatively has failed to demonstrate any bony concerns. Unpublished cases of femoral fracture have occurred, however, and remain a significant risk if graft harvest extends into the metaphyseal/diaphyseal junction. Safe dimensions of metaphyseal- only bone harvest are as yet not firmly established. Prudence is recommended. Rao found a graft volume of 16.1 cm (range, 2 to 34 cm), representing an average condylar volume of 9% (range, 1% to 18%), to be safe.

When harvested with cartilage (the MFT flap), symptoms are also transient, with a median duration of postoperative pain of 42 days and a return to a “normal- feeling” knee at 75 days, in a study of 45 patients.The knee and patellofemoral joints were stable in all individuals.

### Radius Pedicle Grafts

Pedicled bone grafts from both the palmar and dorsal radius are widely used for treatment of carpal avascular necrosis and nonunion. These grafts are relatively easily harvested through the same operative field used for carpal exposure, based on arteriovenous pedicles whose position relative to anatomic landmarks (extensor retinaculum and dorsal compartments dorsally, and pronator quadratus palmarly) is constant.

#### Dorsal Radius Grafts

The blood supply to the distal dorsal radius is robust and constant, provided by a series of longitudinal vessels originating from the radial artery or anterior interosseous vessels that demonstrate consistent spatial relationships to surrounding anatomic landmarks and distal anastomotic vascular connections. The anatomy of the extraosseous and interosseous vascular supply of the distal radius has been described in some detail, including the design of several reverse- flow pedicle grafts applicable to the carpus. Their location allows vascularized bone grafting for Kienböck disease or scaphoid nonunion, with minimal if any additional morbidity. Blood flow is provided by a reverse- flow mechanism in both the artery and the venae comitantes (analogous to the radial forearm flap), with demonstrable preservation of flow possible by inclusion of cutaneous islands, whether based on the radial artery or posterior division of the anterior interosseous artery.

Experimental studies in the canine distal radius have demonstrated that similar reverse- flow pedicle bone grafts have measurable flow immediately after elevation and marked hyperemia when reevaluated 2 weeks later.

### DORSAL RADIUS ANATOMY

Four extraosseous vessels contribute nutrient vessels to the distal radius and ulna. They demonstrate consistent spatial relationships to surrounding anatomic landmarks and supply bone with predictable groups of nutrient arteries (Fig. 46.14A). The anterior interosseous artery divides into anterior and posterior divisions proximal to the distal radioulnar joint. The posterior divisions, together with the radial artery, are the primary sources of orthograde blood flow to the dorsal distal radius. The vessels also supply nutrition to soft tissues, making possible fabrication of composite pedicle flaps that include the posterior interosseous nerve, skin, and muscle.

Fifth extensor compartment artery. The fifth ECA is generally the largest of all the dorsal vessels and is located in the radial floor of the fifth extensor compartment, or at times partially in the intercompartmental septum between the fourth and fifth compartments (see Fig. 46.14A). Its origin and distal anastomotic connections are the same as those of the fourth ECA. It generally overlies the distal radioulnar joint and most frequently has no nutrient branches to the radius. It is quite useful, however, as a large conduit of retrograde flow from the intercarpal arch. Ligation of the posterior division of the anterior interosseous artery proximally allows this blood flow to turn and flow in an antegrade direction through the fourth ECA, creating a vascularized bone graft with a long pedicle and a wide arc of coverage that can reach anywhere in the carpus.

**Specific dorsal radius pedicle grafts.** Although reverse- flow pedicle grafts may be taken based on any of the four dorsal vessels described above, the two grafts that are most practical in clinical application are the 1,2 ICSRA graft used for scaphoid nonunions and the 5 plus 4 ECA graft, which may be placed within a necrotic lunate in Kienböck disease.The 5 plus 4 ECA graft may reach anywhere in the carpus and has been used for a number of purposes, including avascular necrosis (AVN) of the capitate.

1,2 Intercompartmental supraretinacular artery graft. Grafts based on a retrograde 1,2 ICSRA pedicle were originally described by Zaidenberg et al. for scaphoid nonunion. Because the vessel lies superficial to the extensor retinaculum, it is easily visible after retraction of the skin and subcutaneous tissue. It is useful only for scaphoid nonunions because it cannot reach other carpal bones because of its limited arc of rotation.

Harvest technique. The extremity is elevated for exsanguination, and a tourniquet is inflated. Use of an Esmarch bandage before tourniquet elevation will make vessel visualization more difficult. The bone graft donor site and scaphoid are exposed through a gently curvilinear dorsoradial incision paralleling the course of the extensor pollicis longus tendon (Fig. 46.15A). Branches of the superficial radial nerve are identified and protected. Once the subcutaneous tissues are gently raised from the retinaculum, the 1,2 ICSRA and venae comitantes are visualized on the surface of the extensor retinaculum. They are dissected carefully toward their distal anastomosis, with the radial artery and venae comitantes in the floor of the anatomic snuffbox. A vessel loop is placed around the distally based pedicle to protect it during later dissection. Next, the first and second dorsal compartments are opened at the graft elevation site to create a cuff of retinaculum containing the vessels and their nutrient branches (see Fig. 46.15B). The center of the graft should be 1.5 cm proximal to the radiocarpal joint to include the nutrient vessels.

Scaphoid preparation. Before the graft is elevated, a transverse dorsoradial capsulotomy is made to expose the nonunion site (see Fig. 46.15C). In most cases, this will be a proximal pole fracture in which a dorsal inlay graft is most appropriate. If this is the case, a 2- to 3- mm bur or small, sharp osteotomes are used to prepare a slot to receive the bone graft, curetting any fibrous tissue from the nonunion site (see Fig. 46.15D). The slot will span the nonunion, unless the proximal pole fragment is very small, in which case the graft is shaped to lie within the concavity of the proximal pole fragment and slot into the distal fragment. Alternatively, a wedge graft may be used to expand the scaphoid length through the radial approach. In some cases, a limited radial styloidectomy may improve exposure, but it is not generally necessary. For either method, the size and orientation of the bone graft are carefully determined prior to graft elevation.

Placement of the dorsal inlay graft. Most of the cases in which vascularized grafts have been used have been proximal pole nonunions. In these cases, the use of a dorsal inlay graft is generally appropriate, allowing the graft to bridge the nonunion, facilitating stability and early union.

Preparation of the nonunion site should be performed following pedicle dissection but prior to graft elevation, as described previously. Careful inspection of the midcarpal and radiocarpal joint surfaces of the scaphoid is mandatory to prevent articular surface injury during creation of the trough. If the proximal pole fragment is very small, the bone graft is placed within it rather than fitted into a dorsal slot. The graft is removed from the radius, outlining the desired shape using small, sharp osteotomes and protecting the distally based pedicle. The proximal origin of the 1,2 ICSRA may be ligated or cauterized with a bipolar forceps. The graft is pried from the metaphyseal bone using an osteotome placed in the proximal cut at the desired depth (1 cm) (see Fig. 46.15E). Prior to placement of the graft, the scaphoid is stabilized with a cannulated scaphoid screw placed antegrade from the proximal pole. The graft is placed and carefully reshaped as needed so that it can be placed snugly in the slot with sufficient press- fit interference to maintain stability (see Fig. 46.15F).

Once the scaphoid is prepared, a graft of appropriate dimension, including the vessels and cuff of retinaculum between the first and second compartments, is raised. The location of harvest is positioned to include the nutrient vessels, centered a mean 15 mm from the radiocarpal joint margin. The 1,2 ICSRA pedicle is ligated proximal to the graft. Next, the vessels are meticulously dissected subperiosteally distal to the graft to separate them from the radial styloid and joint capsule. To improve the arc of rotation, the pedicle may be dissected to its distal radial artery anastomosis. With the vessels mobilized, the graft is carefully separated from the radius with sharp osteotomes. The cancellous bone is fragile, requiring attention to detail to prevent its fracture as the graft is levered out from the radius. The graft is transposed beneath the radial wrist extensor tendons to reach the nonunion site, where it is gently impacted into position. When used as a dorsal inlay graft, the vessels and cortical bone are positioned on the dorsal surface of the scaphoid. For use as a wedge graft, the cortical bone is positioned palmarly.

Harvest technique for the 4 plus 5 extensor compartment artery graft. A fourth compartment artery graft based on retrograde flow from the intercarpal arch may also be useful in Kienböck disease. Its arc of rotation consistently reaches the lunate, and the nutrient arteries are numerous and large. The fourth ECA vessel is quite small distal to the radiocarpal joint in many patients. As a result, in most cases we now prefer to use the fifth ECA as the source of retrograde blood flow, with antegrade flow to the fourth compartment vessel nutrient branches via their common origin (Fig. 46.16). The large diameter of the fifth ECA provides optimal blood flow to the bone graft, and straightening of the “Y”- bifurcation creates a very long pedicle twice the length of each vessel. This allows it to reach anywhere in the carpus, while easily protecting it from injury during wrist capsulotomy and carpal bone exposure due to its ulnar location. In nearly every wrist studied by Sheetz and colleagues, the fifth ECA had no nutrient branches to bone.

Harvest technique for the 5 plus 4 extensor compartment artery graft. Bone graft harvest requires identification of the fifth ECA by opening the fifth dorsal compartment (see Fig. 46.16). A dorsal wrist incision is made to expose the extensor retinaculum. A single retinacular flap is created for wrist capsule exposure, radially based, with its initial opening directly overlying the fifth compartment (see Fig. 46.16A). The fifth ECA and accompanying veins are easily visualized on the radial aspect of the compartment with retraction of the extensor digiti minimi tendon, lying adjacent to the septum separating the fourth and fifth compartments and occasionally lying within the septum. Its distal anastomotic connections must be meticulously protected. The artery is traced proximally from the intercarpal arch to its origin from the anterior interosseous artery. During this mobilization, the oblique dorsal artery to the distal ulna and the communicating branch with the posterior interosseous artery must be ligated and divided. Proximal to the distal radioulnar joint, the origin of the fourth ECA should be identified for inclusion with the distally based fifth ECA pedicle (see Fig. 46.16B). At this point, the vascular pedicle overlying the joint capsule is protected with a vessel loop and an arthrotomy made to expose the carpal pathologic condition. Once the need for a graft is verified, a bone graft is elevated overlying the fourth ECA, including its nutrient artery branches, which enter the cortex a mean 11 mm proximal to the joint (see Fig. 46.16C). This creates a lengthy pedicle with retrograde flow from the fifth ECA, continuing in an orthograde fashion into the fourth ECA. The bone graft is then raised.

Graft insetting for the lunate. Once the graft is elevated and retracted distal to the lunate on its pedicle, a capsulotomy is performed to expose the joint using Berger’s dorsal ligament- sparing approach(see Fig. 46.16C). The lunate is inspected. If it is not fragmented, vascularized bone grafting is feasible. Necrotic bone is removed with the aid of direct inspection and an image intensifier, leaving a shell of intact cartilage and subchondral bone through a dorsal opening. This opening is placed to avoid injury to the dorsal scapholunate or lunotriquetral ligaments. The lunate may be expanded gently to normal dimensions if it has collapsed, using a small elevator (see Fig. 46.16D). The bone graft is placed into the lunate, orienting the pedicle vertically and the cortical surface on which it lies arranged to serve as a proximodistally oriented strut to maintain lunate height during revascularization (see Fig. 46.16E and F). No internal fixation is necessary, provided the lunate is not fractured and the graft is carefully shaped and impacted into the bone.

#### Palmar Radius Pedicle Grafts

### ANATOMY

Palmar radius grafts have been described based on a pronator quadratus pedicle (palmar metaphyseal arch) or palmar radiocarpal arch. We have found the pronator flap to have essentially no significant nutrient blood supply to the radius and cannot recommend this flap. The palmar radiocarpal arch, however, has anastomotic connections to the ulnar artery, radial artery, and anterior division of the anterior interosseous artery (see Fig. 46.14B).

**Harvest of palmar radius bone grafts to reach either the scaphoid or the lunate.** If inflow is based on the anterior interosseous artery, the graft must be harvested from the radialmost aspect of the palmar radius to provide an adequate pedicle to reach the lunate. When a radial- based pedicle supplied by the palmar radiocarpal branch of the radial artery is used, the bone is harvested from the ulnarmost aspect of the palmar distal radius for placement in the scaphoid.

Although the location of the graft places the palmar radiocarpal ligaments and articular surfaces at risk, there is a robust supply of nutrient vessels to the radius from the palmar radiocarpal arch.Graft placement also requires a palmar carpal exposure, of necessity through important radiocarpal ligaments. This limits exposure and may risk radiocarpal instability postoperatively. The pedicle is short, requiring careful dissection and accurate placement of the graft harvest if the carpal nonunion or AVN site is to be reached and reconstructed. Nevertheless, excellent clinical results have been reported with this flap.

**Technique.** An extensile exposure is recommended to the palmar distal radius in the interval between the radial artery and flexor carpi radialis. For scaphoid placement, the scaphoid is first exposed and prepared through a standard Russe approach. The graft is raised by incising the distal 1 cm of the pronator quadratus and elevating a 1 cm–wide cuff of tissue from the ulnar aspect of the radius, to include a bone graft directly adjacent to the sigmoid notch and lunate facet of the distal radius. At the ulnar border of the graft, contributions to the radiocarpal arch from the anterior interosseous and ulnar arteries are ligated and divided. The pedicle is progressively raised from the radius, and the graft rotated into the prepared scaphoid. For lunate placement, the graft is placed radially and distally, with the base of the pedicle at the ulnar border of the radius to provide inflow from the anterior interosseous and ulnar arteries.

**Results.** The proximity of the graft to articular surfaces and radiocarpal ligaments requires great care in graft elevation and harvest. Despite these difficulties, Mathoulin has reported excellent results for both scaphoid nonunion and Kienböck disease with palmar radius pedicle graft. He reported union in 96% of 112 scaphoid nonunion cases and healing in 17 of 22 patients with Kienböck disease. Another recent report found a healing rate of only 73%; only two of the cases were proximal pole fractures with AVN, and neither united. Complications were many, including two articular radius fractures occurring during graft harvest. Five patients had subsequent surgeries, including four proximal row carpectomies and one conventional bone graft.

## OTHER VASCULARIZED GRAFTS

### Rib

The rib, although used in early reports, has not been widely used for upper extremity reconstruction because of its membranous, weak structure, and curved shape. When based on its anterior internal mammary or supracostal arterial blood supply, only periosteal vessels are supplied. The posterior rib graft, which includes its nutrient artery, requires ligation of the dorsal branch of the posterior intercostal artery. Another alternative, however, is the use of an osteomuscular composite graft, including one or more lower slips of the serratus anterior, which provide a periosteal supply to the ribs. Provided nonadjacent ribs are selected, a double- rib graft may be used for long bone reconstruction. This prevents chest wall instability and allows stable reconstruction and soft tissue coverage in cases of segmental bone loss as an alternative to the fibula. Because the entire thoracodorsal system may be included, composite flaps including the latissimus dorsi, scapular, and parascapular skin are also possible on a single vascular pedicle (Fig. 46.17). It may also be used as a pedicle graft for clavicular reconstruction.

### Scapula

Scapular free flaps may be based on the lateral border (lateral scapular osteocutaneous flap) or on the medial ridge of the scapula. The lateral scapular flap is based on the circumflex scapular circulation and is usually taken as a composite flap. The dissection requires division of the teres major and minor, resulting in some shoulder impairment. The medial flap, based on the same pedicle, depends on the periosteal blood supply for bone nutrition. No donor site morbidity has been reported, although scapular winging from division of the serratus anterior is a likely potential problem. The utility of the flaps as composite flaps in the hand and forearm has been well demonstrated.

### Humerus

Portions of the humeral diaphysis can be taken at the lateral intermuscular septum as part of an osteocutaneous lateral arm flap. Periosteal flaps may also be raised from the humerus.

### Pisiform

The pisiform receives its blood supply from the ulnar artery via its deep volar and dorsal carpal branches. The dorsal carpal branch is the major vessel, with at least two descending branches entering the proximal tip of the bone. This allows elevation of a pedicled pisiform graft, which may be used to replace the lunate, or, when placed as a decorticated graft with the lunate, to revascularize it. A recent series of 21 Kienböck disease patients treated with lunate excision and pisiform replacement reported preservation of preoperative ranges of motion and high patient satisfaction with wrist function. Over the longer term, however, carpal collapse and resultant osteoarthritis were seen in 50% of the patients.

### Second Metacarpal

A small amount of bone may be raised as a vascularized graft from the first or second metacarpal, based on the first dorsal metacarpal artery. The second metacarpal may be harvested entirely for skeletal thumb reconstruction associated with destruction of the index finger, or partially as an elective procedure for carpal pathologic conditions. The arc of rotation allows transposition within the radial side of the hand for applications in the thumb and carpus. Dorsal proximal metaphyseal grafts have been used successfully for scaphoid nonunions and Kienböck disease.

### Manufactured Bone Grafts

Another future possibility is the creation of pedicled vascularized bone grafts in desired shapes. This has been demonstrated in the laboratory by implantation of vascular bundles into bone fragments or demineralized bone matrix. When arteriovenous pedicles are enclosed in titanium or hydroxyapatite or polyethylene chambers, or wrapped with vascularized periosteum in vivo, bone formation and remodeling occur in the shape of the chamber, along with microangiographic evidence of a sprouting vascular network that may occur only 4 weeks later. Similarly, Khouri has demonstrated the ability to transform muscle into bone of any desired shape using silicone rubber molds containing the recently purified osteoinductive factor osteogenin, when coated with demineralized bone matrix. These findings support the concept of creating a preformed vascularized bone graft to reconstruct segmental bone defects.Demonstration of clinical applicability remains to be performed.

### Allogeneic Bone Grafts

The ability to safely restore viability of cryopreserved structural allografts or maintain long- term viability of allogeneic transplanted bone would be an important advance in reconstructive surgery that might in the future obviate other means of long bone reconstruction. Expendable autogenous sources for large, vascularized bone segments are limited, primarily to the fibula and iliac crest. Both are generally poor structural replacements for missing metaphyseal or diaphyseal bone, particularly in the humerus, femur, and tibia, due to gross size mismatch. In these locations, load sharing by rigid internal fixation, external fixators, or allograft plus autograft composites, or a protracted period of no weight bearing and/or bracing, is necessary if the risk of stress fracture is to be minimized. An ideal replacement for such large- segment bone and joint defects would be a living bone and/or joint allogeneic transplant, or a “revitalized” cryopreserved allograft revascularized and repopulated with living bone. Not only would such allogeneic bone match the dimensions and structural property of the missing tissue, but it would also have the same enhanced healing and remodeling potential as a vascularized autogenous bone graft. Survival of musculoskeletal allotransplants at present requires long- term immunosuppression, with a significant risk of opportunistic infections, malignant disease, and organ toxicity that is difficult to justify in transplantations not critical to preserve life. It is not surprising, therefore, that living musculoskeletal allografts have been seldom used clinically and that late failure due to rejection or infection has been the rule.

Characterization of the immune response to vascularized bone allografts may subsequently allow more specific modification of the host response and/or graft tissue. The use of surgical angiogenesis to revitalize cryopreserved allograft bone or replace allogeneic circulation of living allotransplanted bone with recipient- derived autogenous vessels is an alternative to conventional immune modulation that may provide a new source for living bone in future clinical practice.

## RECIPIENT SITE

### Clavicle

Although most clavicular fracture nonunions are successfully treated with conventional grafting and plate fixation, at times, bone loss, infection, or irradiation- induced pathologic fracture makes union challenging. Partial excision of the clavicle, often advocated in this situation, may result in persisting pain, weakness, sensation of instability, and brachial plexopathy. Because of the thin, soft tissue envelope covering the clavicle, bulky bone and/or soft tissue reconstruction is preferentially avoided. Several methods of surgical reconstruction have been described. In cases involving failed prior plating or infection, conventional nonvascularized iliac tricortical bone grafting may not be successful, especially if a significant defect remains in the clavicle. Microvascular fibular transfer combined with autografting has been reported as an appropriate treatment option for difficult nonunions associated with previously irradiated long bones; a high failure rate for the clavicular site has been shown, however. Other biologic options include the vascularized transfer of a rib as a composite flap, including the serratus anterior and/or latissimus dorsi, or the use of free corticoperiosteal grafts to envelop and revitalize the nonunion site.

Because of their osteogenic capability, vascularized periosteal grafts have considerable potential for the reconstruction of bone defects. The transfer of free vascularized corticoperiosteal bone flaps seems to be ideally suited for radiation- induced fractures or chronic nonunions of the clavicle without substantial bone loss but with a poor chance of healing on their own. Successful use of periosteal flaps from the MFC has been reported, with much higher success rates than other methods. The flap is thin and can be shaped according to the reconstructive requirements without compromising its blood supply. It can be wrapped around the clavicle without visible bulk, thereby avoiding further stress on the previously irradiated overlying skin. Rapid subperiosteal new bone and improved local blood flow both serve to correct many of the changes attributed to radiation necrosis of bone. Although microvascular expertise is necessary, flap exposure and elevation are straightforward. The dual blood supply from the medial superior and descending genicular vessels enables identification of vessels of sufficient diameter and pedicle length for reliable microsurgical anastomoses. Improved healing over other methods has been demonstrated.

Vascular connections are most easily accomplished by direct endto- end anastomoses to the thoracoacromial trunk artery and cephalic vein. A corticoperiosteal flap raised from the MFC will envelop at least three quarters of the bone circumference and span the nonunion site, leaving sufficient room for plate- and- screw stabilization on the remaining exposed bone surface. Additional cancellous bone graft may be used beneath the periosteal flap.

### Shoulder and Humerus

Proximal humerus resections are reconstructive challenges, particularly when tumor resection results in loss of the rotator cuff and/or deltoid tissue. Preservation of glenohumeral joint function may be possible with fibular free flaps, including the femoral head with its suspension from the scapula and the reattachment of these muscles. A vascularized fibular flap including the fibular head and physis has the potential to provide longitudinal growth as well as partial joint function, an important consideration in children. Deficient soft tissue in adults favors a glenohumeral arthrodesis, providing the patient with very satisfactory upper limb function. Arthrodesis after segmental resection of the proximal humerus is best accomplished with an allograft/fibula construct, in the authors’ experience (see Fig. 46.8), placing the fibula obliquely from interior glenoid to humerus. Fixation with lag screws proximally and distally requires a shoulder spica cast for stabilization, which is unnecessary with plate- and- screw fixation.

### Forearm

Bone stabilization with rigid internal fixation is relatively easily obtained. In cases of massive bone loss of both forearm bones, creating a one- bone forearm may be the best solution, placing the fibula between the proximal ulna and distal radius. Alternatively, both bones may be reconstructed with a double- barrel graft or dual fibular grafts. Positioning of the fibula may be either orthograde or retrograde, depending on the optimal location for vessel approximation. Fixation with dual plating at either end is reasonable. Vascular anastomoses to radial or ulnar vessels are easily performed, usually as end- to- side arterial anastomoses. The venae comitantes or superficial (cephalic or basilic) veins are satisfactory for end- to- end anastomosis. A long- arm cast is maintained until radiographic signs of union occur.

Reconstruction of the radius alone, commonly for giant cell tumor, is also successfully accomplished with fibular grafts. If the distal radius must be resected, one has the alternative of performing a wrist arthrodesis or attempting to spare some carpal motion. In the latter case, in which the incorporation of the proximal fibular head and its articular cartilage is needed, use of the anterior tibial artery pedicle rather than a peroneal artery pedicle is required to maintain circulation to the head and neck of the fibula. The procedure is technically more difficult, and results in carpal subluxation and arthritic changes in a substantial number of cases (5 of 12 patients in a recent series).Alternatively, it is the authors’ preference to use a fibular diaphyseal segment (the peroneal pedicle). Either a complete wrist arthrodesis with a standard arthrodesis plate is performed or a procedure is done with fusion to the scaphoid and lunate only, permitting some midcarpal mobility. Most commonly in these cases, the ulna is intact, and a bridging plate is not necessary. Two shorter proximal and distal plates are used instead.

Recalcitrant forearm nonunions with minimal bone loss are successfully treated with corticoperiosteal flap envelopment, as described above for the clavicle.

### Wrist and Hand

Vascularized bone grafts have demonstrated their utility in the treatment of carpal pathologic conditions. These include difficult scaphoid nonunions of the proximal pole or associated with AVN and bone loss producing a humpback deformity. The grafts have also been effective in the treatment of Kienböck disease. Pedicled grafts from the adjacent dorsal or palmar radius, pisiform, ulna, or metacarpals have been described and used for this purpose. Pedicled grafts allow harvest of the vascularized bone graft and preparation of the recipient site through a single incision. Increasingly, free microvascular bone flaps, most commonly from the MFC, are demonstrating their usefulness, as well.Because the exposure must not damage the vascular pedicle or its anastomotic connections, pedicle preparation precedes preparation of the recipient site. Free bone flap vascular anastomoses may be made to the radial artery from end to side. Graft placement and stabilization are carefully planned in advance. Recent evidence suggests that local pedicle flaps are appropriate in scaphoid nonunion with or without AVN, so long as the scaphoid dimensions and carpal alignment remain normal. The importance of restoring scaphoid dimensions has long been recognized and requires continued reemphasis. A humpback deformity with carpal collapse requires a substantial interposition graft, typically measuring 1 cm in all dimensions. Although it may indeed be possible to use a larger distal radius graft to correct the deformity and resultant carpal instability, the authors favor a stronger, larger graft from the MFC. An injury with small proximal pole fragments, particularly when fragmented or shell- like, or an irreparable lunate articular cartilage injury has been treated with a salvage procedure. New developments using osteoarticular flaps from the distal femur appear promising. They enable partial replacement of the proximal aspect of either bone, including the articular cartilage and strong subchondral bone, with a radius of curvature closely matched to that of the normal contours.

#### Results

**Scaphoid.** Vascularized bone grafting has been applied to the scaphoid in an effort to improve the results of treatment of nonunion. Roy- Camille used the palmar tubercle of the scaphoid on an abductor pollicis brevis pedicle in 1965. Grafts from the palmar distal radius based on a pronator quadratus pedicle resulted in successful treatment of 13 nonunions in two series, and palmar grafts based on the palmar radiocarpal arch have been similarly effective in cases with AVN. Zaidenberg et al. reported successful results with a dorsal reverse- flow pedicle graft from the distal radius in 11 cases, using the vessel subsequently described as the 1,2 ICSRA. Our initial experience was similar, with successful healing in 15 patients with established scaphoid nonunions. The time to union averaged 11.1 weeks (range, 5.5 to 16 weeks), judged by bridging trabeculae on CT scans. The experience with vascularized bone grafting of the scaphoid has not been uniformly successful, however. Although many authors report rates of healing of over 90%, others have demonstrated healing rates of 27% to 80%. Variations in type and length of internal fixation, numbers of individuals with documented AVN, and other variables certainly may affect the results. A metaanalysis of scaphoid nonunions has demonstrated that vascularized bone grafts provide better union rates overall in scaphoids with AVN, and a trend to better results than conventional grafts in patients who have failed previous surgery.

A recent comparison of conventional iliac crest, vascularized radius pedicle and free medial femoral condylar vascularized grafts has been recently reported. The MFC group had the highest union rate and shortest time to union, most especially in those patients with findings of AVN, carpal collapse, and failed previous surgery.

Certainly the most common use of the MFC bone- only vascularized free flap has been scaphoid nonunion with humpback deformity and resulting nondissociative dorsal intercalated segment instability (DISI) deformity as well as diminished blood supply to the proximal scaphoid pole. The use of an interposed segment of bone reconstructs normal scaphoid dimensions and improves carpal alignment as a result, as well as improving local vascularity.

Pedicle bone grafts from the dorsal or palmar surface of the radius have been used as both inlay and interposition grafts. Inlay grafts are most useful when little loss of scaphoid length has occurred, with failure to correct humpback and DISI a cause for treatment failure. The potential to use dorsal radius 1,2 or 2.3 ICSRA pedicle grafts as interposition grafts remains an option not requiring microsurgical technique.We prefer the use of the more robust MFC bone flap, due to the need to interpose a graft averaging 10 mm to restore scaphoid stability. This increases scaphoid length by approximately 10%, with a small amount of “overstuffing” compared with the contralateral scaphoid.

A recent analysis of 32 patients who underwent MFC reconstruction for scaphoid nonunion with DISI and absent proximal pole punctate bleeding demonstrated successful healing in 30 of the fractures at a median time of 12 weeks.

Role of internal fixation.Supplemental internal fixation is necessary for scaphoid nonunions, even with the use of vascularized grafts. In most cases, a scaphoid screw may be safely placed without jeopardy to the pedicle. Kirschner wires are required at times when insufficient bone is available to allow screw purchase. A recent metaanalysis of scaphoid nonunion literature has demonstrated that Kirschner wire fixation results in higher rates of pseudarthrosis than does the use of screws. This has also been our experience. The authors’ preferred method (discussed previously) is placement of the screw in an antegrade direction prior to graft placement. A recent review of 50 such cases demonstrated the method to be inappropriate in cases where the proximal pole was fragmented.

1,2 Intercompartmental supraretinacular artery inlay graft. Chances of successful union using a 1,2 ICSRA pedicled graft placed as a dorsal inlay graft were only 50% when associated with carpal collapse and a humpback deformity combined with an avascular proximal pole. We now reserve the use of this graft for proximal pole nonunions without significant scaphoid deformity and shortening because the graft is generally too small and perhaps too weak to provide the large interposition wedge generally required to restore scaphoid length.

With this more limited set of indications, we continue to find the 1,2 ICSRA graft useful and appropriate.

Medial femoral condyle bone flap.Wedge grafts that enhance vascularity are important for successful healing when AVN is combined with humpback deformity and carpal collapse. These may be conventional iliac crest grafts combined with an arteriovenous pedicle or large vascularized grafts placed to lengthen the scaphoid (Fig. 46.18). Both methods result in reliable scaphoid healing, although the healing time for conventional wedge graft with avascular bundle implantation is twice as long. We now favor the use of large vascularized interposition grafts from the MFC when both humpback deformity and AVN are present. An additional indication for the use of the MFC flap is a foreshortened scaphoid with a failed prior bone grafting procedure.

In these instances, it may be preferable to consider the use of a free osteochondral graft to replace the proximal scaphoid, as recently described by Bürger and colleagues. #### Lunate Graft in Kienböck Disease

Vascularized grafts in general have demonstrated satisfactory clinical results in Kienböck disease, with excellent pain relief and improvement in range of motion and strength. Radiograph progression of lunate and carpal height collapse occurred in 0% to 15% of patients in three recent studies and radiographic or MRI evidence of revascularization may be minimal for an extended period of time.Vascularized grafts may be especially useful in cases of ulnar- positive or neutral Kienböck disease, when joint leveling is contraindicated to avoid ulnar impaction syndrome. The lunate may be expanded with the graft, resulting in improvement of the lunate index and carpal height ratio postoperatively in stage IIIA disease. Use of the graft is questionable with displaced coronal fractures of the lunate, due to the unreliable healing of such fractures; in cases with marked carpal collapse, which may progress radiographically with osteoarthritic changes or fragmentation of the lunate; and when the articular surfaces are damaged. Protection during early healing is necessary to prevent lunate collapse with revascularization.

Twenty- six Kienböck patients treated with a 5 plus 4 ECA dorsal radius pedicle graft were followed for a mean of 31 months in one study; grip strength improved (from 50% to 89% of the opposite side), as did motion. Fully 92% of patients reported improvement in pain, with 85% having satisfactory results overall. MRI data demonstrated some progressive signs of revascularization with time, but months to years were often required. Wrists with evidence of lunate revascularization generally fared better than those without MRI findings of improvement. Improved results were found in postoperative grip strength, pain relief, and function when a vascularized graft was combined with 4 months’ temporary SC pinning.

Advanced changes resulting in destruction of the articular surfaces of the lunate have generally been an indication for a salvage procedure. Recently the osteochondral, or MFT, flap has been used for replacement of the majority of the lunate, including the proximal articular surface. Generally, only the distal (capitate) surface of the lunate is preserved for function. All but 1 patient had had a good clinical result, and the radiographic changes demonstrated either no further collapse or improvement in lunate and carpal height over time (in 14 of 16 patients). Thus the use of small osteochondral grafts in the near future may be expected to become more generally used, with the potential to provide improved clinical outcomes for this challenging clinical problem.

## SUMMARY

Vascularized bone grafting is indicated in the upper extremity following segmental bone loss of greater than 6 cm as a result of trauma, infection, or tumor resection. Grafting is also indicated in smaller long bone defects in the presence of radionecrosis, failed prior graft, scarred beds, or the need for composite tissue reconstruction. Currently, restoration of growth by inclusion of the epiphysis is possible as well. Primary sources of vascularized bone for long bone reconstruction include the fibula and iliac crest. In cases with minimal bone loss but with recalcitrant nonunion due to scarring, prior infection, or irradiation- induced damage, free corticoperiosteal flaps that envelop the bone permit revascularization and healing.

In the wrist, vascularized grafts have proven value in scaphoid fracture and nonunion, as well as in carpal AVN (Preiser disease, Kienböck disease, capitate AVN). For carpal indications, the primary options currently considered are reverse- flow pedicle grafts from the dorsal distal radius and both bone- only and osteoarticular flaps from the medial or lateral femoral condyle. We now reserve 1,2 ICSRA pedicle grafts for scaphoid nonunions with minimal displacement and 5 plus 4 ECA grafts for patients with Kienböck disease without collapse or injury to the articular surfaces. MFC grafting is now the procedure of choice for scaphoid nonunions when the unfavorable combination of humpback deformity and proximal pole AVN is present, or when a prior operation has failed with bone loss and/or AVN. Osteochondral MFT flaps may prove to be a useful option to replace fragmented and very small proximal pole scaphoid nonunions and proximal lunate bone and cartilage in Kienböck cases, as an alternative to more common salvage procedures.
