## ANATOMY AND CLINICAL EXAMINATION

Skin is divided into the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous layers (Fig. 58.1). The epidermis possesses the following appendages:

![[Fig.58.1.png]]
> **Fig. 58.1** Layers of skin to include adnexal structures and origin of benign and malignant skin tumors.

1. Eccrine glands

2. Apocrine glands

3. Sebaceous glands

4. Hair follicles

5. Nails

Glands and hair follicles arise as down growths from the epidermis and project into the dermis. The two types of sweat glands are eccrine and apocrine. Eccrine glands exist all over the skin and secrete clear, watery fluid. They begin as a coil in the dermis and open onto the skin surface. Apocrine glands are large sweat glands that open into hair follicles, to which they are attached, and rarely directly to the skin surface. They are located in the axilla, areola, and perianal region, among others, and they do not develop fully until puberty. Secretion is evoked in response to stress, pain, and fright. Sebaceous glands are found all over the body, except palms and soles. They have no lumen and are multilobulated, appearing as a pouch hanging on the side of the hair follicle.

Dermis is divided into two parts: (1) papillary and (2) reticular. Papillary dermis rests against the epidermis, which sends papillary extensions down into it called rete ridges. These ridges resist shear disruption between the dermal and epidermal layers. The reticular dermis is deeper than the papillary dermis. It is composed of coarser bundles of connective tissue fibers and extends to the subcutaneous layer. The dermis contains:

1. Connective tissue fibers: collagen, elastin, and reticulin

2. Cellular elements: migratory cells (leukocytes, histiocytes) and fixed cells (fibroblasts, mast cells)

3. Blood vessels

4. Nerves and specialized nerve endings

5. Smooth muscle

6. Lymphatics

7. Ground substance

The epidermis is superficial to the basal lamina and is composed histologically of four cell layers (five layers in the palm) (Fig. 58.2):

![[Fig.58.2.png]]
> **Fig. 58.2** Cellular layers of the epidermis.

1. Basal layer (stratum basale) consists of a single layer of columnar cells that stain deeply basophilic. At haphazard intervals between these basal cells are melanocytes, which are clear cells with small nuclei. Melanin is produced through the conversion of tyrosin to dopa, facilitated by the enzyme tyrosinase. Melanocytes may multiply and form nests in the deep epidermis and even spread into the subjacent dermis, where they lose the ability to synthesize melanin but retain the melanin storing property and remain pigmented. These intradermal clusters are called nevus cells and give the appearance of nevi. Melanocytes are actually dendritic cells and connect to each other over distances. They are derived from neural crest. Merkel cells, like melanocytes, are nonkeratinocyte intraepidermal cells. They also occur in the basal cell layer and are associated with sensory axons for slow-adapting mechanoreceptors and pressure sensors. They are also found in hair follicles.

2. Malpighian (or prickle cell) layer (stratum spinosum) is made up of cells connected to each other by intracellular bridges and so artifactually (during tissue processing) give the spiny or prickle appearance of this layer.

3. Granular layer (stratum granulosum) is made of a few layers of diamond-shaped cells filled with basophilic granules (keratohyalin).

4. Horny layer (stratum corneum) has cells that have no nuclei and are continuously shed from the surface.

5. Clear layer (stratum lucidum), in the palm only, gives a translucent appearance to the skin. It is composed of up to five layers of “dead” keratinocytes, and this layer is situated between the stratum granulosum and the stratum corneum. Cells are filled with eleidin, an intermediate form of keratin.There is a normal progression of maturation from deep to superficial layers of the epidermis. In epidermal cancers, this natural progressive maturation is lost and becomes disorderly and ultimately anaplastic. Relative invasiveness of these cancers is described first by invasion deep to the basement membrane, and then depth of invasion, next into the papillary dermis and on into the deeper reticular dermis. Thus carcinoma in situ is a true cancer that is disorganized in the progressive epidermal cell maturation but nonetheless has not invaded through the basement membrane of the dermis. Originally, Clark levels were applied descriptively to the depth of melanoma invasion into the papillary dermis, reticular dermis, and on into subcutaneous sites. However, Breslow levels of actual millimeter thickness of invasion are more accurate prognostically. In the subungual regions, rete ridges and papillary dermis are less well defined, and there is also no subcutaneous fat, rendering the distance of tumor penetration between epidermis and periosteum to be very small. In this region, Clark and Breslow measurements cannot be used as an effective treatment guide.

Benign and malignant skin tumors may arise from epithelial elements (in which case malignancies are termed carcinoma) of the epidermis, as well as from the adnexae in the dermis (hair follicles and sweat glands) (see Fig. 58.1). Basal cell carcinoma is derived from the basal cell layer of the epidermis and squamous cell carcinomas from the more superficial keratinocytes.

![[Fig.58.1.png]]
> **Fig. 58.1** Layers of skin to include adnexal structures and origin of benign and malignant skin tumors.

Epithelial cysts within the dermis may result from implantation into the dermis (epidermal inclusion cyst) or from blocked gland ducts (pilosebaceous cysts) and from hair follicles (trichilemmal cysts). Connective tissue tumors (malignant forms are termed sarcomas) may arise from the fibrous tissue of the dermis, such as dermatofibromas and the rarer dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans. Nerve ending tumors and those relative to blood vessels also occur in the dermis, such as neurofibroma, glomus tumor, and Merkel cell tumor. Hemangiomas and capillary stains and vascular malformations are discussed elsewhere.

Having knowledge of skin anatomy and tumor location is helpful in arriving at a clinical diagnosis. Intraepidermal lesions may be plaquelike but are not elevated into papules or nodules (the hallmark of intradermal lesions). Junctional (between dermis and epidermis) lesions are also not elevated, as is the case with a junctional nevus, which is a flat, pigmented lesion. Dermatofibromas and the more vertically invasive nodular melanomas, in contrast, are elevated into a nodule. Melanomas are usually pigmented because their cell of origin is the melanocyte, which produces melanin. However, melanocytes do also pass melanin into adjacent basal and spongy cells and so both basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) and squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) may be pigmented.

Epidermal lesions often have an “irritated” surface, which may be ulcerative or thickened. Hyperkeratosis is a thickening of the stratum corneum, leading to an exuberant, flaking, and cracked appearance of the skin as in actinic keratosis and verrucae (or warts). In acanthosis, there is a thickening of the stratum spinosum. It gives the skin a leathery folded appearance, and if it is pigmented, it is called acanthosis nigricans. This may occur in simple obesity, but it may be a marker for internal malignancy. Hyperkeratosis may be so pronounced as to become heaped up and form a cutaneous horn. It is most commonly associated with actinic keratosis but may have an underlying SCC or keratoacanthoma. Similarly, greasy scaling lesions that have a “stuck on” appearance are typical of seborrheic keratosis.

In contradistinction, dermal lesions form a papule or nodule. A dermal inclusion cyst retains its attachment to the overlying epidermis (a punctum) and a dermatofibroma arises in the superficial dermal layers, and so the overlying epidermis is adherent to the deeper lesion. In fact, there may be overlying pressure necrosis from a dermatofibroma, leading to an almost pathognomonic feature of central umbilication. The nodule in dermatofibroma is hard, but a nodular nevus is soft and indents with the back end of a cotton-tipped applicator.

The lesions of the deeper dermis will not have an overlying epidermal adherence. Lesions that are deeply attached will be nonmobile. One should always assess clinically for deep fixation and lack of deep mobility of skin cancers since this implies a deeper invasion.

Regional clinical evaluation of lymph node drainage basins for malignant lesions is also important, and one must have an understanding of this anatomy. There are superficial and deep lymphatics. Superficial lymphatics from the ulnar side of the hand may drain first to the supratrochlear nodes just proximal to the medial epicondyle, but most pass directly up to the axilla. Superficial radial lymphatics ascend to the deltopectoral glands and then on to the subclavicular axillary nodes. Deep lymphatic vessels accompany the deep blood vessels (radial, ulnar, anterior and posterior interosseous), along which there may be lymph glands. Most pass up to the lateral axillary nodes. Thus it is necessary to examine the forearm and epitrochlear and supratrochlear and deltopectoral regions, as well as axilla (medial, lateral, and apex) and supraclavicular lymph node drainage basins. When planning a “prophylactic” lymph node biopsy in the absence of palpable glands, one cannot rely on a seemingly logical ascent of lymph drainage up the extremity to the axilla, but one must assess for the “sentinel” node that can be at any one of these potential sites.

## DIAGNOSTIC PROCEDURES AND NONSURGICAL TREATMENT

Most cutaneous lesions do not require special imaging, unless, for example, there is a large, deeply invasive skin cancer, in which case magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be helpful in planning resection.

For small lesions, one may proceed directly to excisional biopsy. However, many small lesions are noncancerous or only minimally invasive cancers, and so various diagnostic and/or treatment options are available. Once again, understanding the depth of cutaneous involvement is an important guide. Many epidermal lesions can be treated by shaving or curettage. Where indicated, these have an advantage over elliptical excision, since the deeper dermal layers are not violated and there is less likelihood of adverse scarring (perhaps not as critical in the hand as on the more cosmetically important areas such as the face). Appropriate nonsurgical treatments may be as follows:

1. Patient surveillance: Total lesion ablation may not be feasible. Periodic clinical examination and follow-up photographs may detect malignant transformation. This course may be chosen when total excision of a large congenital nevus is impractical, when a patient has multiple dysplastic nevi, or for diffuse syndromal conditions with a high risk of development of cutaneous malignancy (such as xeroderma pigmentosum and basal cell nevus syndrome).

2. Topical medications: Diffusely spread conditions with malignant potential may be treated by topically applied 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (Fig. 58.3). Bowen lesions and superficial BCCs or SCCs cannot be treated with 5-FU. Verrucae may be treated by topical application of podophyllin or keratolytic agents, such as salicylic acid. 5% imiquimod cream (Aldara, Graceway Pharmaceuticals, Bristol, TN) is approved for treatment of superficial BCCs, applied five to seven times per week for up to 12 weeks. Incomplete data are available for its use in Bowen disease, SCC, or lentigo maligna melanoma.

![[Fig.58.3.png]]
> **Fig. 58.3** Topical application of 5-FU may be used to treat a variety of superficial epidermal lesions.

3. Curettage: A 5-mm curette is most commonly used, and curettage is performed after adequate local anesthesia. It yields material for histologic examination, but it does not allow assessment of tumor margins. It permits safe removal of superficial lesions with minimal scarring and is amenable for actinic and seborrheic keratoses and verruca vulgaris. It is an excellent treatment for subungual verrucae, leaving almost no residual nail deformity. Remove the nail plate, and one can identify a deep curettage cleavage plain using loupe magnification (Fig. 58.4).

![[Fig.58.4.png]]
> **Fig. 58.4** A, Periungual verruca vulgaris on two fingers. B, The lesions have been curettaged after nail plate removal. Reepitheliazation and normal nail growth will follow since the full-thickness epidermis has not been breached.

4. Cryosurgery: Intense cold is applied and all cells within the freezing wave are killed. Liquid nitrogen can remove warts, superficial keratoses, and actinic lentigines. It must be used with caution where there are adjacent peripheral nerves, such as on the volar finger and olecranon, as permanent freeze damage to the nerve may occur. Also, destruction to critical paronychial and nail bed structures can occur at the fingertip. Esthetic results are otherwise usually excellent, but cryotherapy must be used with caution in dark-skinned patients as hypopigmentation may result. It causes little pain and rarely requires anesthesia. A cotton-tipped applicator is used, with cotton teased out and tapered. As liquid nitrogen runs down the applicator, skin at the applicator tip turns white, and the ice front spreads outward (Fig. 58.5). Application ceases when visible freezing extends just beyond the lesion.

![[Fig.58.5.png]]
> **Fig. 58.5** Diagrammatic representation of cryotherapy with cottontipped applicator dipped in liquid nitrogen.

5. Electrosurgery: Electrical energy is converted to heat by tissue resistance. An office hyfrecator is a good electrosurgical instrument and may be used to destroy seborrheic keratoses, skin tags, lentigines, and spider nevi.

6. Electrodesiccation and curettage: A curette is used to scrape the lesion down to dermis. Scraping is then paused to use a hyfrecator. This causes electrocoagulation (electrodesiccation) of the raw surgical surface. This process is usually repeated three times or until the surgeon is comfortable with the margins. The technique is dependent on clinical experience, but it has the advantage of giving good cosmetic results. Additionally, recurrence is rapidly recognized as it is not obscured by wound closure or flap, and it is a straightforward office procedure that can be used for multiple superficial lesions that would otherwise be difficult or tedious to treat with multiple excisions. It is useful for seborrheic keratoses and viral warts but has also been used for multiple superficial BCCs, keratoacanthoma, and Bowen disease. For larger lesions, sometimes scarring can be obvious.

Before ablative surgery, a histologic diagnosis should always be obtained. The biopsy process may actually be the curative treatment for smaller lesions.

1. Shave biopsy: This may totally excise the lesion and be curative or may serve as a diagnostic biopsy. Lesion elevation by the mechanical effects of wheal of local anesthetic, and pinching the lesion between thumb and index finger above the plain of the surrounding tissue, enables a full-thickness shave biopsy down to dermis. This can be done with a scalpel blade or a razor blade (Fig. 58.6). Shave biopsies are not suitable for neoplasms that clinically appear to infiltrate dermis and generally not the pigmented lesions with suspicion for melanoma.

![[Fig.58.6.png]]
> **Fig. 58.6** A and B, Diagrammatic illustration of shave biopsy. The lesion is plumped up first by local anesthetic infiltration and then by pinching the skin between index and thumb. Tangential excision of the lesion is thus facilitated. C and D, Clinical demonstration of shave biopsy.

2. Punch biopsy: This may be incisional or excisional (if the lesion is small enough for the punch to fit around). Variable size cutting punches are available from 1.5 mm to 6 mm—4 mm is most frequently used. Local anesthetic is first injected. When the circular punch incision reaches subcutaneous fat, the punch is removed. The small cylinder of tissue is then lifted and its base transected at the level of the subcutaneous tissue with a small iris scissors (Fig. 58.7). Sutures are generally not used. Sampling error is minimized by careful choice of the site to be biopsied.

![[Fig.58.7.png]]
> **Fig. 58.7** A, Different size punches (3 mm, 4 mm, 5 mm). B, Illustration shows how the cutting punch creates a cylinder of tissue that can then be lifted up and cut loose. C and D, The core of tissue can be lifted up by fine forceps or by piercing it with the tip of a hypodermic needle.

3. Elliptical incisional/excisional biopsy: If the lesion is too large to make excisional biopsy impractical, incisional biopsy with a margin of adjacent normal skin is performed. Provided that subsequent excision is performed in a timely manner for histologically proven malignant conditions, prior incisional biopsy does not alter the natural history of melanoma and other cutaneous malignancies. Site incisional biopsies longitudinally so that the resulting scar (which may need to be excised later) does not compromise subsequent excision of a potential malignancy. Elliptical excision is usually designed in the line of least skin tension to facilitate linear closure. If the line of least skin tension is difficult to assess, simply excise around the lesion perimeter with appropriate margin and the wound will gape into an ellipse, indicating skin tension, and then simply excise the “dog ears” (Fig. 58.8).

![[Fig.58.8.png]]
> **Fig. 58.8** Method of “dog ear” excision following elliptical excision of a lesion.

4. Subungual lesions and longitudinal melanonychia: Remove the nail plate and perform either incisional or excisional longitudinal removal depending on the ability to finely repair the nail bed in a tension-free manner after lateral undermining.

## SURGICAL LESION EXCISION

These excisions range from simple elliptical excision and tumor closure, through excision with frozen section assessment of tumor margins, to Mohs micrographic surgery. If the lesion is small, and there is abundant surrounding tissue laxity, then excision and linear closure may be permissible. If permanent sections reveal persistent tumor, then reexcision of the scar is a simple matter. However, if one is planning a complex wound closure by tissue rearrangement or flap coverage, or if the lesion has complex invasion or is difficult to evaluate clinically by inspection and palpation (such as recurrence or certain infiltrative lesions), then intraoperative frozen section analysis of tumor margins is wise or recourse to Mohs micrographic surgery.

When performing a simple tumor excision under local anesthesia, first determine the tumor extent by loupe magnification inspection and by palpation, and mark this tumor margin out, together with the anticipated tumor margin of excision. Do this before local anesthetic infiltration as the infiltration distorts the tissues (Fig. 58.9).

![[Fig.58.9.png]]
> **Fig. 58.9** A, Margin around a pigmented lentigo maligna lesion is marked out prior to infiltration of local anesthetic. B, Resulting soft tissue wound. C, If clear clinical margins are assured, a flap reconstruction may possibly be performed, showing the outline of a Limberg transposition flap. D to H, However, always select the simplest and most effective method of wound closure. Wound edge undermining reveals that the wound can be closed directly. Subcuticular wound closure is performed after deep dermal closure, dog ear excision and the wound is supported by adhesive strips. Final outcome is shown.

Mohs micrographic surgery is generally reserved for certain widespread lesions with margins that are difficult to interpret clinically and where optimum conservation of uninvolved tissue is necessary, such as with diffuse Bowen lesions (Fig. 58.10). In contrast to the conventional bread-loafed sectioning of the excised specimen, Mohs surgery involves tangential layered excisions and creating maps of residual tumor for further excision, until the whole lesion is excised. It is applicable to recurrent carcinomas, high-risk squamous carcinomas, tumors with ill-defined clinical margins (such as dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans), and areas where maximal conservation of tissues is required to preserve function or cosmesis.

## EPIDERMAL LESIONS

*Verruca vulgaris* (wart) is caused by human papilloma virus (HPV) and is transmissible by direct contact or autoinoculation. Histologically, one sees hyperkeratosis (thickening of the stratum corneum), acanthosis (thickening of the epidermis), and parakeratosis. The characteristic lesion is a well-demarcated, raised, and a rough cauliflower-like lesion. The less frequent plane wart (verruca plana) is flat. Verrucae are common on the hand and fingers and may occur in periungual and subungual locations. They may be isolated lesions or in groups that combine to form plaques.

Most warts spontaneously regress in 2 years. However, in immunocompromised patients they may reach especially large sizes and may take longer to resolve. A variety of treatment options are available, all of which result in about 70% to 85% cure rate. Most commonly the wart is first pared down and then one may apply a topical keratolytic agent such as salicylic acid solution in collodium, the most commonly used topical agent. Treatment with an emery board has been recommended and various other topical applications (which may need to be repeated) have been used—imiquimod, 5-fluorouracil, and cryotherapy. Silver duct tape (not clear tape) has an unknown mechanism of action. The duct tape is applied for 7 days, and the lesion is then debrided with a pumice stone. Treatments are repeated.

Our preferred treatment is to shave the wart down to the level of the surrounding epidermis. A discrete edge is then seen with magnification loupes and the entire wart is sharply curetted from the surrounding normal epidermis. Since this is an entirely epidermal lesion and the basal epidermal layer is not involved by this technique, reepithelialization occurs rapidly. This technique is an excellent way of treating periungual and subungual warts, resulting in low risk of residual nail or perionychial deformity (Table 58.1).

*Seborrheic keratosis* is more common in middle-aged and elderly patients. Lesions are more common centrally and thus are more frequently seen on the upper arm and chest than on the hands. The depth of pigmentation is related to the degree of sun exposure. Occasionally, they mimic melanoma and require diagnostic biopsy. They are benign flat lesions that take on a characteristic greasy scale and appear to have been “stuck” on the skin surface (Fig. 58.11). Because they are entirely epidermal lesions, treatment takes advantage of their superficiality and includes curettage, shave biopsy, or application of liquid nitrogen. Since these treatments do not invade the dermis, there is minimal potential scarring.

![[Fig.58.11.png]]
> **Fig. 58.11** Characteristic appearance of seborrheic keratoses on dorsum of the hand.

*Eccrine poroma* is a sweat gland lesion. Because it arises from the eccrine duct within the epidermis and not in the deeper dermal region, it is truly an epidermal lesion. It presents as a moist and red papule with a surrounding collarette and may occasionally be mistaken for a pyogenic granuloma. They are said to be more common on the soles and palms. They may be totally epidermal or may straddle both dermis and epidermis. Malignant change is unusual. Treatment is surgical excision. Multiple palm and sole lesions may occur in the uncommon genetic syndrome of hidrotic ectodermal dysplasia.

## PIGMENTED EPIDERMAL LESIONS

*Lentigines* are benign pigmented macules with increased melanocytic activity. Unlike freckles, they do not fade in the absence of sun exposure. A simple lentigo (lentigo simplex) appears during childhood, is usually less than 5 mm in diameter and may occur on both sunexposed and non–sun-exposed sites. Solar lentigo (also called senile lentigo) is common on the sun-exposed forearm and dorsum of the hand. There is associated solar damage in the dermis, as indicated by histologic solar elastosis. Their incidence increases with age. They may resemble melanoma, and diagnostic biopsy may be required. Otherwise, specific treatment is unnecessary, though laser therapy may be helpful if the patient is bothered by cosmesis.

*Melanocytic nevus* is a benign proliferation of melanocytes. Nevi, or simple moles, are the most common tumors in humans. Few are present at birth, and they begin soon after birth, reaching their greatest incidence in young adults.

Junctional nevi have nevus cells confined to the dermoepidermal junction, and intradermal nevi have these nests in the dermis alone. Most nevi in younger patients are junctional and are flat. In adults, most are intradermal and are elevated. Compound nevi have nevus cells at both locations. It is the junctional component of both junctional and compound nevi that may more rarely transform into malignant melanoma. Most melanomas do not arise out of preexisting nevi. Unlike melanocytes, in the basal layer of the epidermis, nevus cells do not have dendritic processes (except in a blue nevus).

Melanocytic nevi may be either acquired or congenital. The latter are classified according to size (small, medium, or giant), and the former are classified according to pathologic findings (common mole, Spitz, blue, and atypical/dysplastic).

Most *common moles* (nevi) do not require any specific treatment unless malignant transformation is suggested by enlargement, darkening pigmentation, or ulceration—“ABCD”: asymmetry, border irregularity, color variation, diameter greater than 6 mm. Palmar nevi were thought to be prone to malignant transformation, but this view has changed. Palmar nevi occur in 13% of white people and 29% of black people, making routine excision of nevi in these locations both impractical and unnecessary.

*Giant hairy congenital nevi* occur most often on the face and bathing trunk area, but they may occur on the upper extremity (Fig. 58.12). It is unclear what the precise risk is for malignancy developing in giant congenital nevi. A systematic review suggests a range of 0% to 9%. The highest risk of malignant transformation with these lesions is actually in childhood and prepuberty, unlike the general incidence of melanoma, which is after puberty. Smaller congenital nevi are generally excised. However, excision of larger nevi may be less practical and may require use of skin expansion, flaps, or skin grafting to close the large wounds after excision. Where excision is not feasible, periodic cancer surveillance and biopsy of suspicious areas must be done.

With *spindle* (or *Spitz* ) *nevus,* which has also been called benign juvenile melanoma, the incidence of malignant transformation is no greater than with other compound nevi. The color ranges from pink to purplish-red, and the minority are darkly pigmented. Treatment is total excision.

*Blue nevus* contains dermal melanocytes that produce melanin, and unlike the intradermal nevus, these melanocytes also have dendritic processes. Dermal melanin scatters shorter wavelengths of light, giving a blue color. It is more commonly found on the dorsum of the hand as a solitary lesion and arises in adolescence or early adulthood. Malignant degeneration is unusual.

*Dysplastic nevus syndrome* occurs in both sporadic and familial forms (Fig. 58.13). There are multiple atypical nevi varying from 10 to over 100, occurring mostly on the upper trunk. Lesions are often greater than 1 cm in diameter, with irregular and variegated color. Microscopic appearance is characteristic with large, atypical melanocytes that are spindle or epithelioid in type. The papillary dermis shows fibroplasia. It is impractical to excise all the lesions. Excision of a representative lesion confirms pathologic diagnosis. These patients must be carefully observed, and suspicious lesions excised.

![[Fig.58.13.png]]
> **Fig. 58.13** A and B, Child with multiple dysplastic nevi on trunk and upper and lower extremities, including plantar and palmar surfaces (acral nevi).

*Acral nevi* (nevi occurring on the thickened palmar skin) can have histologic findings that may mimic dysplastic nevi.

*Longitudinal melanonychia* refers to the dark streaking of the nail caused by melanin deposition in the nail plate. Acral nevi that involve the nail matrix can present in this way. In dark-skinned patients, longitudinal melanonychia is common and is simply caused by increased melanin production by normal melanocytes. A longitudinal fullthickness nail bed biopsy is required for a suspicious lesion after removal of the nail plate and with repair of the hyponychial nail fold and nail bed. Suspicious new lesions for potential melanoma include those in older patients (50 to 70 years), a longitudinal band over 3 mm in width, change in lesion color and size, involvement of a single digit, nail dystrophy, and extension to the periungual skin (Hutchinson sign) (Fig. 58.14).

## DERMAL LESIONS

These are often elevated as macules or papules and nodules.

*Dermatofibroma* (histiocytoma) is a fibrous tumor that involves the dermis. The tumors most frequently occur on the extremities. They are usually solitary, slow-growing, and firm, attached to overlying skin but freely mobile in relation to deeper subcutaneous tissues. They may have a depressed central “umbilication.” Occasionally, they may be dark brown and be clinically confused with malignant melanomas. When indicated, simple excision can be done (Fig. 58.15).

![[Fig.58.15.png]]
> **Fig. 58.15** A, Dark-colored dermatofibroma showing central “umbilication.” B, Finger dermatofibroma.

*Digital fibrokeratoma* (acral fibrokeratoma) usually occurs on the acral surfaces of the hands. They are characteristically a horn-like projection with a raised collar of skin at the base. The overlying epidermis is hyperkeratotic, and the dermis demonstrates increased fibrosis. They may mimic fibromas, eccrine poromas, and pyogenic granuloma. They are treated by excision, though recurrence may occur.

*Epidermal inclusion cysts* arise from occluded pilosebaceous follicles, with some arising from traumatic displacement of epidermal cells into the dermis (Fig. 58.16). The skin is elevated over the underlying lesion, and adherent to it. Central keratin-filled punctum may be seen. They are asymptomatic but may become secondarily infected. The preferred treatment is excision to include the entire epidermal lining.

![[Fig.58.16.png]]
> **Fig. 58.16** A, Patient who had prior fingertip traumatic amputation now presents with an apical finger mass. B, Radiograph shows pressure indentation of the distal phalanx. C, Fingertip inclusion cyst is excised. D and E, Palmar finger inclusion cyst.

*Pilomatrixoma* (or calcifying epithelioma of Malherbe) occurs as a solitary tumor in the upper extremities of children and young adults (Fig. 58.17A and B). It is usually a nondescript small dermal nodule (0.5 cm diameter), and foci of calcification are seen histologically. Treatment consists of surgical excision.

*Neurofibroma* may occur as an isolated dermal lesion or as part of a more diffuse neurofibromatosis (see Fig. 58.17C to E).

*Infantile digital fibromatosis* occurs at the tips of the fingers (and not the thumb). These may be single or multiple as “kissing” lesions on adjacent digits (Fig. 58.18). They may cause functional deformity. Histologically, there are characteristic eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies. Wide excision is required to minimize recurrence. The risk of recurrence is high.

*Milia* are rare on the hand, but they can appear on the dorsum of the hand. They form when keratin becomes trapped under the skin creating tiny cysts. Treatment can involve topical medications such as tretinoin to encourage cell turnover. More aggressive treatments can include lancing the lesions, cryotherapy, chemical peels, or even laser ablation. Lancing involves piercing the individual milium with a hypodermic needle.

## NONHEMANGIOMATOUS VASCULAR LESIONS

*Telangiectasias* are fine linear vessels on the skin surface. They are common and may be normal and occur especially in the elderly and in sun-damaged skin. Their significance may lie in their association with other diseases, such as collagen vascular disorders.

*Pyogenic granuloma* is a proliferation of capillaries arising at the site of prior trauma (which often is relatively trivial). An associated infection may occur, but the condition is not an infectious process. Pyogenic granulomas have a friable surface and bleed easily (Fig. 58.19). They occur most frequently on distal extremities, probably because of minor trauma at these sites. Spontaneous resolution is unusual.

![[Fig.58.19.png]]
> **Fig. 58.19** A and B, Pyogenic granulomas are common on the fingers. They have friable surfaces that bleed on contact. C and D, Silver nitrate sticks may be used to treat smaller lesions.

They may be excised; smaller lesions may be destroyed with electrosurgery, laser, or silver nitrate cautery. Incompletely removed lesions may reoccur.

## PREMALIGNANT LESIONS, LESIONS ASSOCIATED WITH SKIN CANCER, AND LESIONS OF INDETERMINATE BEHAVIOR

*Cutaneous horns* arise from underlying epidermal lesions, most commonly actinic keratoses (Fig. 58.20). They are not premalignant lesions, but as many as 10% have underlying SCCs, and extremely rarely a BCC. Numerous other lesions may give rise to cutaneous horn outgrowth, such as keratoacanthoma, sebaceous adenoma, seborrheic keratosis, and verruca. Because of the possibilities of malignancy, biopsy is required together with excision of a 1-to 2-mm margin of normal skin. Subsequent treatment then depends on the underlying lesion.

![[Fig.58.20.png]]
> **Fig. 58.20** A and B, Examples of cutaneous horns on the hand and finger.

*Porokeratosis* is characterized by a surrounding sharply raised keratotic and horny rim. Histologically, there is a rim of parakeratosis. They often have autosomal dominant inheritance. About 13% transform into SCC. There are several subtypes, including a Mibelli type with raised plaques (Fig. 58.21), disseminated superficial actinic (DSAP), linear, and punctate—all of which may occur on the hand and upper extremity. Because of their association with premalignancy, treatment modalities include cryosurgery, topical 5-fluorouracil, or imiquimod.

![[Fig.58.21.png]]
> **Fig. 58.21** Extremity porokeratosis of Mibelli.

*Keratoacanthoma* is a benign, self-limited epithelial tumor, usually occurring on sun-exposed hair-bearing skin. It closely resembles SCC, both clinically and histologically. A firm, erythematous papule appears initially and rapidly enlarges up to 2 cm or more in diameter over several weeks. The characteristic central crater of the lesion is filled with a keratinous plug (Fig. 58.22). Large lesions, even though indurated, are often freely moveable over the underlying structures. Common for these lesions, a regressive phase then begins, the central plug is expelled, and the lesion heals spontaneously with scar. It is believed to arise from proliferation of pilar epithelium. The evolution and regression of the lesions are felt to be related to the hair cycle. Treatment is generally complete excision, especially when the lesion is first recognized and small. Some prefer to call this lesion SCC keratoacanthoma type, and thus for this reason as well, surgical excision is the preferred treatment, though metastases are rare.

![[Fig.58.22.png]]
> **Fig. 58.22** A and B, Keratoacanthoma at dorsal base of thumb. It shows the characteristic central keratinous plug.

Keratoacanthomas may have an aggressive course with metastasis and cannot be distinguished from SCC under such circumstances. The cause, however, for confusion may be an initial mistake in diagnosis and the histopathologic interpretation of keratoacanthoma may be problematic.

*Distal digital keratoacanthoma* is a rare, destructive form of keratoacanthoma of the distal subungual tissue. As such, it does not occur in hair-bearing skin. Like a true keratoacanthoma, it does exhibit very rapid growth. It causes nail bed distortion and may even result in bone destruction. Spontaneous regression has been reported, but it is probably a low-grade carcinoma.

If one cannot conclusively rule out SCC, distal digital amputation is warranted.

*Actinic keratosis* is the most frequently occurring premalignant cutaneous condition and is also called solar or senile keratosis. Lesions are rough and scaly with an erythematous border (Fig. 58.23). Histology shows hyperkeratosis and areas of parakeratosis. The granular layer of the epidermis is usually absent. There is an atypical cellular pattern.

![[Fig.58.23.png]]
> **Fig. 58.23** Scaly lesion of actinic keratosis.

Basement membrane is thickened but intact. They occur in genetically susceptible people (frequently older males of Celtic heritage) and on sun-exposed sites such as hands and forearms. They must be differentiated from chronic eczema, psoriasis, and Bowen disease. Estimates suggest that invasive SCC develops in fewer than 10% of patients with actinic keratosis.

For individual lesions, treatment may involve cryotherapy and electrodesiccation with curettage. In situations of severe sun damage and multiple areas of actinic keratoses, topical treatments spread over a wider area are more suitable. These might include 5-fluorouracil, dermabrasion, trichloroacetic acid chemical peel, and photodynamic therapies. Lesions resistant to treatment with 5-fluorouracil may contain foci of invasive SCC.

*Arsenical keratosis* occurs secondary to exposure to inorganic arsenic compounds through either ingestion or contact in the agricultural industry. It usually manifests on the arms and soles and is rarely seen in the United States.

*Bowen disease* is an intraepidermal SCC (or carcinoma in situ) that may become invasive after many years in fewer than 10%. SCC arising from Bowen disease is more aggressive than carcinoma arising from actinic keratosis, and metastases develop in up to one-third. Chronic sun exposure is etiologically related.

Lesions are irregular plaques with thickened and hyperkeratotic surfaces (Fig. 58.24). They are usually erythematous. They must be differentiated from psoriasis, eczema, verruca, chronic fungal infections (especially when involving the perionychium), actinic keratosis, and superficial BCC. Surgical excision with a margin of normal tissue is the most effective treatment (Fig. 58.25). Mohs micrographic surgical techniques may be helpful because the clinical boundary of the lesion may be difficult to define. Because of deep follicular involvement, curettage and topical applications such as 5-fluorouracil may be less effective. Distal digital lesions (Fig. 58.26) with significant nail distortion may best be treated by fingertip amputations. Large surface-area wounds remaining after lesion excision may best be treated by skin grafting.

## SYNDROMAL CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH SKIN CANCERS

*Basal cell nevus syndrome* is also called Gorlin syndrome (Fig. 58.27). Typical nevi are reddish-brown, papular, and may number many thousands, appearing after puberty. Lesions may become invasive BCC. Patients also have characteristic facies, palmar and plantar pits, brachymetacarpia, and occasional neurologic abnormalities—mental impairment and medulloblastoma.

![[Fig.58.27.png]]
> **Fig. 58.27** A and B, Characteristic facial appearance of patient with Gorlin syndrome.

*Xeroderma pigmentosum* is a genodermatosis that is an autosomal recessive defect in which the DNA repair mechanism for ultraviolet (UV) light damage is impaired. Skin and eyes are intolerant to UV light. With sunlight exposure, an exaggerated sunburn reaction occurs. The skin thickens and there is subcutaneous atrophy. The prognosis is dismal. Sun-protective clothing is required, as well as avoidance of UV exposure. Patients develop multiple malignant epithelial tumors and melanomas during the first decade of life (Fig. 58.28).

![[Fig.58.28.png]]
> **Fig. 58.28** A and B, Patient with xeroderma pigmentosum has developed melanoma.

Other unusual syndromes include *Bazex syndrome,* which is inherited as an X-linked dominant trait and results in multiple BCCs, hypotrichosis, and hypohidrosis. *Haber’s syndrome* is a familial variant of Bowen lesions (generally in the covered areas of the body).

## BASAL CELL CARCINOMA

Although BCC is the most commonly occurring skin cancer, it is relatively rare on some anatomic sites, such as the hand. SCC is much more common than BCC at this site. BCC arises without a precursor lesion. They are locally aggressive and recurrent. Metastasis is rare. BCCs arise from the basal layer of the epidermis and from pilosebaceous adnexae. The characteristic cell has a large basophilic nucleus with minimal cytoplasm. Tumor cells at the periphery of any nest of cells are oriented with long axes perpendicular to the nest, giving a “picket fence” appearance. In the so-called morpheaform cancer, there is significant dermal collagen that compresses tumor cells into cords.

Differential diagnosis includes factitious ulceration, eczema, actinic keratosis, psoriasis, and fungal infection. Nodular pigmented BCC may mimic melanoma in appearance but still behaves clinically as a well-circumscribed nodular BCC.

BCCs are distinguished primarily between circumscribed and diffuse lesions. Nodular and circumscribed lesions generally are less virulent, and Jacobs et al. were the first to differentiate tumor type and tumor behavior (Fig. 58.29). Within the two groups of circumscribed and diffuse, BCC is in turn classified according to degree of differentiation and depth of invasion (clinically noted with fixation to deeper structures).

![[Fig.58.29.png]]

Nodular and ulcerative are the most common types (Box 58.1). Of the diffuse types, superficial spreading may characteristically occur on the trunk and upper extremities (Fig. 58.30). Overall, the superficial spreading type is the next most common form of BCC.

*Solid (nodular) BCC* is the most common form. It may ulcerate, and this is called nodular ulcerative. It is dome-shaped with well-defined borders, is often a pink-red papule, and has a “pearly” appearance with telangiectasias. Although nodular BCCs may be histologically poorly differentiated, their growth pattern is circumscribed.

*Adenoid BCC* has strands of tumor islands that resemble tubular structures surrounded by myxoid stroma. *Cystic BCC* has cystic spaces in the tumor island secondary to degeneration of tumor cells. When “adenoid” areas are also present, the lesion is called adenoid cystic. These large lacunae give a distinct macroscopic appearance. Cysts rupture fluid to the lesion surface (see Fig. 58.29).

![[Fig.58.29.png]]
> **Fig. 58.29** Clinical different BCC subtypes: A, pigmented; B, superficial ulcerative; C, adenoid cystic; D, superficial spreading; E, sclerosing (morpheaform).

Diffuse BCCs have a higher recurrence rate than circumscribed lesions because they extend insidiously in the dermis beyond the clinically visible or palpable border. *Superficial BCC* has been called multicentric BCC because discrete foci seem to be present. However, three-dimensional histologic reconstruction reveals that the apparent multiple foci are actually connected. They are not easily treated and have a higher recurrence rate because of their extension beyond clinically apparent borders. They are scaly, crusted, erythematous, and flat with the skin surface. They may be ringed by a raised “pearly” border that slowly expands. Confusion may arise with eczema, actinic keratosis, psoriasis, and fungal infection.

*Morpheaform (sclerosing) BCC* is quite rare on the hand and more common on the face. It is a firm plaque surrounded by “scar” tissue and lateral spread has been reported as much as 7 mm beyond the clinically apparent tumor border. Dense, fibrous stroma precludes treatment by curettage.

Curettage and electrodessication is the most common method of treating BCC. This technique is reserved for primary lesions less than 1 cm in diameter. Hand extremity lesions are amenable to this form of treatment. Well-defined lesions may also be treated by cryosurgery.Potential risk is neuropathy due to uncontrolled depth of freeze, and this form of treatment should be avoided on digits and around the ulnar nerve at the elbow.

Although cure rate for curettage and electrodesiccation and for cryosurgery for small lesions has been reported as high as 97%, an inherent disadvantage is lack of microscopic verification of complete tumor removal. Surgical excision offers greater than 90% cure rate (Fig. 58.31). Loupe magnification allows more accurate visual assessment of tumor borders. Mark out the tumor borders before local anesthetic injection to avoid tumor distortion. A 2-mm surgical margin may be sufficient for less than 1-cm diameter nodular lesions. However, for lesions up to 2 cm, a 3 to 5 mm margin is more appropriate and extending deeply into subcutaneous fat. Lesions greater than 2 cm and those with aggressive growth patterns, such as morpheaform and superficial “multicentric” BCC, may require resection margins of 10 mm (where practically possible) but reserved for larger and infiltrative lesions. Frozen section of tumor margins is expensive and not recommended for every suspected BCC. If microscopic margins are found to be involved on permanent pathologic assessment, reexcision will not affect the likelihood of cure. However, for the higher risk lesions already discussed, frozen sections of margins are recommended and even a potential recourse to Mohs micrographic surgery. If flap reconstruction is planned, always ensure margins are uninvolved by tumor through either frozen section or awaiting the results of permanent section, meanwhile maintaining a moist dressing (Fig. 58.32). Therapeutic topical 5-fluorouracil or imiquimod does not offer acceptable cure rate for treatment of BCC.

## SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA

SCC does have premalignant precursors, such as actinic keratosis, as well as the in situ variant (Bowen disease). SCCs may also arise de novo. SCCs are more aggressive than BCCs and metastasize more frequently. Fair skin, previous history of sunburns, blue eyes, and blond or red hair increase risk of SCC. Additionally, immunocompromised patients, especially those with renal and heart transplants, are 65% more likely to develop SCC. These patients may also have multiple and more aggressively behaving SCC (Fig. 58.33).

![[Fig.58.33.png]]
> **Fig. 58.33** A and B, Renal transplant patient has multiple upper extremity SCCs.

Unlike BCC, where tumor type is the principal prognosticator in treatment, the following are more important factors influencing prognosis of SCC.

*Broader grades of differentiation* are graded 1 through 4 based on the ratio of differentiated to undifferentiated cells. Grades 3 and 4 (50% and 75% of undifferentiated cells, respectively) are twice as likely to recur and three times more likely to metastasize than grades 1 and 2.

*Location:* For example, paronychial SCC has a high incidence of local recurrence but a low metastatic rate.

*Tumor size:* T indicates lesion size less than 2 cm, T is 2 to 4 cm, and T is greater than 4 cm. SCCs of size greater than 2 cm are twice as likely to recur and three times more likely to metastasize than tumors less than 2 cm.

*Tumor depth:* Some feel this to be the most important factor determining whether SCC will metastasize. SCCs more than 4 mm deep or extending into the subcutaneous tissue (Clark level 5) are most likely to recur and metastasize.

*Perineural invasion* is a poor prognostic sign for both recurrence and metastasis, and it indicates need for postexcision radiation therapy. There is 47% recurrence, 35% metastasis to regional nodes, and 15% distant metastasis. Perineural invasion is often asymptomatic but may be associated with pain.

Thus high-risk lesions are those that show rapid growth of greater than 2 cm, have depth of invasion on biopsy of 2 mm or more or at least Clark level 4, have poor histologic differentiation, and show perineural invasion. Low-risk SCC can be treated by cryotherapy, electrodesiccation and curettage, photodynamic therapy, or excision. The margin of excision for low-risk lesions is 4 mm, and the margin of excision for high-risk SCC is at least 6 mm and down into subcutaneous fat or even to fascia (Fig. 58.34). Cure rates for surgery are about 92%. Mohs micrographic surgery may be indicated for the more high-risk lesions, especially for recurrent tumors, perineural invasion, poorly differentiated and deeply penetrating tumors, and areas where tissue conservation is desired, such as on fingers.

> **Fig. 58.34** A, Digital SCC is deeply adherent and measured. B, Excised lesion orientated in situ and marking sutures placed. C, Excised specimen showing marking orientating sutures. D, Resulting wound with exposed digital nerve.

Resulting excisional wounds may be able to be closed directly, but larger wounds can be covered with a skin graft. Local rotation flaps may be used, but before realigning tissues one must be certain of free margins of resection, either by frozen section or by awaiting permanent section results (Fig. 58.35). Amputation may be required to control aggressive tumors (Fig. 58.36). Once again, periungual invasive SCC lesions may be confused with fungal infection, eczema, or Bowen disease (Fig. 58.37).

It is estimated that 2% to 5% of SCCs metastasize to axillary nodes. Elective node dissection or sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) has not been shown to be of advantage in treating SCC unless nodes are actually clinically enlarged because of the relatively low incidence of lymph node metastasis. SCCs are slower to invade deeper tissue than are cutaneous malignant melanomas. Superficial dermis and epidermis are devoid of lymphatic drainage, making SCC less likely to spread via lymphatics. SLNB is under investigation for high-risk SCC. If there are axillary node metastases, 5-year survival drops to 40%.

## MELANOMA

There are several malignant melanoma morphologic subtypes. For cutaneous lesions thought to be melanoma, one may use ABCDE acronym as a diagnostic guide: asymmetric, border irregularity, color variation, diameter >6 mm, evolving or changing (Fig. 58.38). However, nodular melanomas grow vertically and may be <6 mm and melanomas may be amelanotic.

![[Fig.58.38.png]]
> **Fig. 58.38** Superficial spreading melanoma showing irregular borders and variable pigmentation.

These are subdivided into those with more rapid radial growth (lentigo maligna, superficial spreading, acral lentiginous) and those with relative absence of radial growth with prominent vertical growth (nodular melanoma) (Fig. 58.39). Vertical growth determines tumor depth and ultimate prognosis. Clark levels of invasion recognized the prognostic importance of vertical melanoma growth but was descriptive with regards to invasion into the papillary dermis, reticular dermis, and finally into the subcutaneous fat. We now use Breslow thickness of millimeter depth of invasion, which is measured from the epidermal basement membrane to the deepest melanoma tumor cells. Depth of invasion is difficult to accurately quantitate for subungual melanoma because the epidermal nail bed is compressed tightly against the dorsal periosteum of the distal phalanx.

![[Fig.58.39.png]]
> **Fig. 58.39** Different melanoma subtypes: A, superficial spreading; B, lentigo maligna; C, nodular.

Morphologic subtypes of melanoma include the following: *Superficial spreading melanoma* makes up 70% of melanomas. Of all melanomas, it is the most likely to arise within or contiguous to a melanocytic nevus. There is a long, horizontal growth phase (6 months to 6 years) before vertical growth occurs, and thus these lesions have a better prognosis. Metastatic spread correlates with vertical invasion and rarely occurs during the early radial growth phase.

*Lentigo maligna (solar) melanoma* accounts for 4% to 10% of all melanomas. It is associated with long-term sun exposure in elderly patients. Thus it occurs in the same areas affected by actinic keratoses, occurring especially on the extensor aspect of the forearm. In situ melanoma is called lentigo maligna (sometimes called Hutchinson freckle). It is a flat macule that has a horizontal growth phase that lasts 1 to 2 decades. Once a vertical growth phase occurs and the dermis is invaded (often apparent clinically by papules in the lesion), the invasive melanoma is then called lentigo maligna melanoma.

Histologic determination of margins may be difficult and often it is advisable to excise the lesion with at least 5-mm margins and await permanent histologic section confirmation before definitively closing the wound with a skin graft or flap.

*Acral lentiginous melanoma* makes up 2% to 8% of cutaneous malignant melanomas and occurs on the palmar surface and nail apparatus, and it is not associated with sun exposure. It is the most common type of melanoma in people in whom melanoma is generally uncommon (African Americans, Asians, Hispanics). It has a long horizontal growth phase before vertical growth occurs. Diagnosis is often delayed because these lesions occur in areas not frequently examined clinically.

*Subungual melanoma* most commonly affects the thumb in the upper extremity. It may be mistaken for a subungual hematoma. Formal biopsy is required for a pigmented nail lesion that has persisted for over 2 months.

*Nodular melanoma* accounts for 2% to 20% of melanomas. It is frequently thick at the time of diagnosis and thus has an increased metastatic potential. It generally arises de novo.

*Amelanotic melanoma* lesions may be gray or pink and devoid of dark pigmentation. They are a diagnostic challenge and can be mistaken for intradermal nevus or nodular BCC.

*Desmoplastic melanoma* may occasionally affect the hands. Histologically, it is similar to lentigo malignant melanoma but has a fibroblastic reaction in the dermis and neural invasion. Mean thickness at the time of initial clinical detection is 4.3 mm, but survival is more favorable for this tumor type than for other melanomas of equivalent thickness (5-year survival of 63% versus 48%).

### Prognosis and Staging

Independent prognostic indicators for patient survival with melanoma are Breslow thickness, macroscopic or microscopic ulceration, and lymph node involvement (determined by SLNB). Morphologic type, corrected for thickness, does not influence survival. Potential prognostic correlations exist for gender, mitotic rate, and truncal versus limb location (with the latter being more favorable). The most recent literature emphasizes the importance of ulceration as a negative prognostic marker.

Staging is determined by tumor size, regional lymph node metastases by presence of micro-or macrometastases, number of nodes involved, and three subgroups of distant metastases: skin and soft tissue (best prognosis), lung, and other visceral metastases (worst prognosis) (Table 58.2). Elevated lactate dehydrogenase in any of the first metastatic subgroups upstages to the last subgroup.

#### Distant Metastatic Melanoma

Laboratory investigations are unnecessary for early cutaneous melanoma (less than 1 mm thickness) in the absence of signs and symptoms to suggest metastatic disease. For larger melanomas, there are no uniform recommended initial staging evaluations, but these may include chest radiograph, blood count, liver function tests, and computed tomography scans. Positron emission tomography (PET) and computed tomography help identify developing metastases and facilitate decisions for surgical resection. Patients with melanoma may possibly be best evaluated in a multispecialty melanoma clinic.

### Local Surgical Excisional Treatment

Breslow depth classification assists in determining tumor excision margins. In the 1980s, safety margins of 5 cm independent of tumor thickness were standard. Recommended excisional margins, based on Breslow tumor thickness, have continued to shrink and now are the following:

In situ: 0.5 to 1 cm (5-year survival 99%)1.0 mm: 1 cm (5-year survival 99%)1.0 to 2.0 mm: 1 to 2 cm (5-year survival 93% to 96%)>2.0 to 4.0 mm: 2 cm (5-year survival 86% to 94%)>4.0 mm: 2 cm (5-year survival 82% to 90%)

These margins apply to the peripheral margin only since it is impossible to obtain equivalent deep margins on the hand. For in situ lesions, one can excise through subcutaneous fat, but for all other melanomas, extend the depth of excision to paratenon on the dorsum of the hand and to fascia on the forearm. For deeper histologic tumor extension, more radical excision is required on the deep surface.

Subungual melanoma of in situ type may be treated by surgical excision of the entire nail bed and paronychial region and covered with a full-thickness skin graft directly on periosteum. Invasive subungual melanoma is treated by amputation proximal to the next joint. Thus, if the distal phalanx is involved, then amputate proximal to the interphalangeal (IP) joint of the thumb or the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint of a finger. If tumor extends to the middle phalanx, then amputate proximal to the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint. For functional reasons, a ray amputation may be most appropriate.

### Evaluation of Lymphatic Basins

Lymph node dissection is performed if there are palpable nodes that are histologically proven to be involved with metastatic melanoma. This will probably not affect a cure, but it minimizes the morbidity of progressive malignancy in the axilla with tumor ulceration, pain from nerve plexus invasion, and lymphedema (Fig. 58.40).

## LESS COMMON MALIGNANT TUMORS

*Merkel cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma* : Merkel cells are in the basal layer of the epidermis and are associated with slow-adapting pressure-sensitive sentry nerve endings. Merkel cell carcinoma is a rapidly growing, painless, and nontender intracutaneous nodule that may grow up to 5 cm in diameter. The prognosis is dismal for these highly aggressive tumors—even those identified early with node-negative, localized skin disease have only a 54% to 63% 5-year survival. Patients with node-positive disease have a 5-year survival of 39%, while those with distant metastases fare even worse at 18%. Once a positive diagnosis has been made via biopsy, treatment involves wide local excision with 1 to 2 cm margins surgically and SLNB to appropriately stage the patient. Unlike other tumors, almost all patients with Merkel cell carcinoma will benefit from SLNB as node micrometastases are found in as many as 30% of patients at initial presentation. More aggressive disease may require amputation in addition to regional lymphadenectomy. Postoperative radiotherapy is common and offered as part of a standard treatment algorithm. Chemotherapy is less effective, but newer immunotherapies in clinical trials may be promising. Local recurrence, distant metastases, and lymph node metastases are common (Fig. 58.42).

*Lymphangiosarcoma (Stewart-Treves syndrome)* is a rare tumor that occurs in the upper extremity of patients with chronic lymphedema for over 10 years, following mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection and irradiation. Initially, the tumor may not be obvious clinically and may appear simply as a bruise or as multiple purple skin papules. The tumor then develops rapidly. Following biopsy confirmation, life-saving amputation is the preferred treatment in conjunction with adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy; there is no reported difference in survival between the two adjuvant therapies.Wide local excision alone—even with 3 cm margins—is known to be inferior to early amputation in the treatment of this highly aggressive tumor with a poor prognosis.

*Sweat gland tumors:* Even so-called benign sweat gland tumors have a tendency to local recurrence, and benign tumors may transform into malignancy over time. Treatment of these benign tumors must include a margin of surrounding normal tissue. Examples are eccrine poroma, chondroid syringoma, and eccrine spiradenoma. These tumors occur most commonly on the palm since this is where there is the highest concentration of sweat glands.

Malignant varieties of these sweat gland tumors occur. These malignant tumors are rare, so there are no clear guidelines on treatment. They develop painful ulceration. They are locally invasive and spread to lymph nodes and distant metastatic sites. Wide local excision is necessary as well as SLNB, with some recommending prophylactic regional lymph node dissection.

*Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV):* HIV patients have a higher risk of skin cancer, especially with known viral etiology such as Kaposi sarcoma (Fig. 58.43). There is also a higher incidence of nonmelanoma skin cancers with a twofold higher risk of BCC in HIV-infected Caucasian males, and a 2.6-fold higher risk of SCC in HIV-infected individuals irrespective of gender. HIV virus levels and antiretroviral therapy are not related to this higher risk. Melanoma may also occur at higher rates among HIV patients.

*Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP)* : This is a locally invasive fibrosarcoma of the dermis (Fig. 58.44). Clinically, DFSP presents as a violaceous, brown or skin-toned plaque that progresses to raised nodules with a protuberant appearance over time. It has poorly defined clinical margins with finger-like appendages, which create a challenge in achieving negative margins after resection. Given that the 5-year survival of patients with clinically negative resective margins is 99%, achieving this endpoint is paramount.Treatment is wide local excision with 2 to 3 cm margins and then appropriate wound closure, most often with a skin graft because the widely resected tumor often precludes direct wound closure and local flaps may be ill-advised unless one is certain of clear clinical margins. Mohs micrographic surgery has more recently been proposed as an effective excisional approach given the difficulty of capturing all of the finger-like tumor appendages in the resection. There is no consensus on exact surgical margins or use of Mohs surgery. Radiotherapy is often employed as adjuvant therapy following positive resection margins as well as for neoadjuvant therapy in recurrent or otherwise inoperable tumors. Immunotherapies such as imatinib are now U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- approved for treatment of metastatic or unresectable disease.

*Metastatic tumors:* Tumors rarely metastasize from other sites to the soft tissues or bone of the fingers or hand; however, always biopsy suspicious lesions (Fig. 58.45).
