The permanent sequelae of peripheral nerve injury can range from complete recovery to profound and debilitating functional loss. Regardless, many injury patterns are associated with a protracted convalescence, and both acute and chronic neuropathic pain can be more problematic than any associated sensory or motor loss. For injuries in- continuity, spontaneous recovery is certainly possible, though for more severe injury patterns, surgical intervention offers the only hope for functional return.

While general treatment principles for nerve injuries are widely accepted, many interventions depend on a great deal of experiencebased judgment. The specific technical details of how to best satisfy these principles are still the subject of rigorous debate. Reporting bias may also help explain the inconsistencies and disparities that appear in the clinical outcomes literature. Factors including time from injury, genetic or systemic patient differences, and wide zones of injury remain factors that are largely out of the surgeon’s control. Nerve surgery can be alternatingly one of the most rewarding and most frustrating procedures, and treating physicians must be adept at patient education, surgical techniques, decision making, emotional counseling, and chronic pain control. Despite a growing appreciation of the complex interplay between cells, growth factors, guidance cues, and genetics, most major recent advances have come in the form of shifting treatment strategies such as an increased emphasis on the use of nerve transfers.

## EPIDEMIOLOGY

Peripheral nerve injuries in general are quite common and are associated with 1% to 3% of all traumas. Severity and the need for intervention is often associated with the mechanism of injury. Although there are exceptions, penetrating and lacerating injuries that immediately preceded a neurologic deficit can be assumed to have resulted in nerve transection, while nerve deficits from crush injuries, fractures, and dislocations are more likely to be associated with nerve injuries in- continuity. Many of these can be treated expectantly and only a smaller number eventually require surgery. Regardless, when considering the consequences of undertreatment, population- based “rates of injury” and the percentage chance of “needing surgery” may be useful for patient counseling but in truth offer limited guidance, as assessment and treatment plans must be individualized to each patient.

### ANATOMY

The peripheral nervous system is made up of neurons, supportive glial cells (Schwann cells), connective tissue, and end targets including muscle and sensory organelles. Motor neuron cell bodies reside in the anterior horn of the spinal cord (and are referred to as anterior horn cells), and sensory neuron cell bodies are found in dorsal root ganglia, which run parallel but outside the spinal cord. Cellular projections, known as axons, emanate from both types of cell bodies and extend into the periphery to connect with corresponding end targets—sensory organelles or motor endplates. Though pain is experienced in the cut finger after a paring knife accident severs a digital nerve, the cell body that has suffered the injury actually is located approximately 36 inches proximally along the spinal column. The neuron produces cellular proteins, neurotransmitters, and growth factors carried via molecular fast and slow transport to the axonal endbulbs.

The nerve trunk consists of axonal extensions packaged in layers of specialized connective tissue: the endoneurium encases axons on the most microscopic level, the perineurium envelopes axons grouped together into macroscopically distinct fascicles, the inner epineurium cushions and protects the fascicles, and the outer epineurium encompasses and forms the surface of the entire nerve (Fig. 30.1). The inner epineurial padding varies with in situ mechanical compressive and tensile stresses (e.g., so it is increased at joints), and thickenings within the inner epineurium organize and group certain fascicles together. The tough perineurium provides tensile strength to the nerve and is an extension of the blood- brain barrier from the central nervous system.The increased pressure within the central nervous system is maintained within this layer and is the reason why cut fascicles bulge from a proximal nerve stump. Multiple redundant arcades of blood vessels run along the surface and within these different structural layers to meet the high metabolic demands of peripheral nervous tissue. Though historically the intraneural plexus was felt to be poorly organized and random, more recent work has suggested a more organized and predictable pattern.

As the quintessential support unit, the pluripotent Schwann cell has roles in peripheral nervous system development, nerve regeneration, neuron maintenance, and signal conduction. Multiple small nonmyelinated nerve fibers—involved in pain, the autonomic system, nonsomatic sensory and motor fibers—are supported by shared Schwann cells. Myelinated axons, such as large motor and sensory fibers, however, are completely entubulated by columns of individual Schwann cells. The Schwann cells wrap around the axon forming tightly linked segments of a lipid- rich insulating sheath. The microscopic gaps between these segments, nodes of Ranvier, are not insulated, and this combination of alternating insulated and noninsulated areas are essential for normal signal transmission (Fig. 30.2). Macrophages, mast cells, and fibroblasts are other prominent cells within the nerve.

Motor axons entering the muscle belly form focal connections at specialized synapses referred to as motor endplates. These endplates consist of neurotransmitter- rich terminal boutons on the axonal side, a microscopic intervening cleft, and a neurotransmitter receptor–rich segment of muscle lamella. Terminal Schwann cells, in yet another physiologic role, isolate and protect the motor endplates. A single motor axon has multiple branches and will innervate multiple muscle fibers (though each motor fiber is only innervated by one axon), and this population of motor fibers is referred to as a motor unit. In normal anatomic scenarios, the muscle fibers within the same motor unit are dispersed and intermixed with muscle fibers innervated by other axons.

The median, ulnar, and radial nerves represent the major peripheral nerves of the upper extremity distal to the brachial plexus. The medial brachial and medial antebrachial sensory nerves branch off the medial cord of the brachial plexus and should be familiar to the peripheral nerve surgeon since they offer potential graft material, can form painful neuromas from iatrogenic or traumatic injury, and need to be differentiated from other vital structures when exploring the medial brachium. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is a continuation of the musculocutaneous nerve that emerges from between the biceps brachii and brachialis muscle bellies to innervate the skin on the lateral aspect of the forearm.

## PHYSIOLOGY

Nerves are remarkably specialized cells uniquely designed to carry and transmit signals. Sodium- potassium intramembranous exchange pumps, as well as precise membrane permeabilities, create an ion concentration differential between the inside and immediate microenvironment outside the axon membranes (axolemma). Since ions are charged, this results in an electrical gradient between −60 and −70 mV, known as the resting potential. When a neuron is stimulated, ion gates within the proximal axonal membrane open, allowing a rapid influx of positively charged ions, which depolarize the membrane creating an electrical current. This current, known as the graded potential (since it degrades with time and distance), spreads almost instantaneously to neighboring intramembranous voltage- gated ion channels. As a specific voltage threshold is reached, these gates burst open, allowing the influx of sodium ions, which depolarize this segment of membrane wall. The generated current (known as an action potential) spreads to the next resting segment and, in this manner, the signal becomes self- propagating. The sodium influx is replaced by a potassium outflux that not only quickly repolarizes the membrane but also overshoots to temporarily hyperpolarize the membrane. The momentary hyperpolarization quickly stabilizes back to the resting potential, at which point it is capable of generating the next action potential when restimulated.

Though the generation of an action potential takes mere milliseconds, this is still relatively slow compared with a passive (or graded) current transmission. Analogous to a copper wire, passive current conduction is further enhanced with increasing diameter of the conductor (the axon) and its insulation (myelin sheath). The essential speed and efficiency of passive impulse transmission is combined with the self- propagation quality of action potentials to maximize both transmission speed and distance. Action potentials are only generated at the uninsulated nodes of Ranvier between the myelin segments. The combination of lightning- quick (passive) impulse conduction under the myelinated segments with the inception of fresh action potentials at the nodes is referred to as saltatory conduction (based on the imagery of a signal “jumping” between the nodes of Ranvier) (see Fig. 30.2).

![[Fig.30.2.png]]
> **Fig. 30.2** The combination of myelin segments (only passive conduction) and nodes of Ranvier (selfpropagating action potential formation) is referred to as “saltatory” conduction and allows very rapid transmission of nerve signals over long distances. (Redrawn from Fletcher A. Action potential: generation and propagation. Anaesth Intensive Care Med. 2019;29[4]:243–247.)

When the impulse reaches the synaptic bulb at the end of a motor axon, the depolarization of the terminal membrane triggers a calcium influx releasing acetylcholine- filled vesicles into the synaptic cleft of the neuromuscular junction. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft to activate nicotinic receptor- linked ion channels embedded in the sarcolemma (muscle fiber membrane). Analogous to the physiology within the axolemma, once a voltage threshold is reached, voltagegated sodium potassium channels open, allowing an exuberant influx of sodium and depolarizing the muscle fiber membrane. The underlying voltage- sensitive sarcoplasmic reticulum release calcium ions, which influence the actin- myosin relationship, allowing these proteins to shift and slide past each other. The cumulation of millions of microscopic protein reactions results in the macroscopic muscle contraction, allowing functional skeletal movement. Contraction intensity is proportional to the number of stimulated motor axons, and the force generated is proportional to the cross- sectional area and volume of the muscle.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Like any biologic tissue, structural disruption and cellular response is related to the mechanism of injury and the intensity of the inflicted trauma. Peripheral nerves can be cut, pulled, or crushed, resulting in a spectrum of injury patterns ranging from focal ischemia to partial or complete rupture, as characterized by the descriptive Seddon and more detailed Sunderland classification schemes introduced 7 decades ago (Fig. 30.3). Conceptualizing nerve injuries based on a predictable and progressive sequence of physiologic and microstructural disruption is useful in predicting healing responses and appreciating diagnostic and surgical hierarchies. Mackinnon modified the classification by adding a sixth category to reflect the common incidence of combined injury patterns within the same nerve trunk.

![[Fig.30.3.png]]
> **Fig. 30.3** The Sunderland classification describes progressively more severe nerve injuries based on the sequential disruption of connective tissue layers. Third-, fourth-, and fifth-degree injuries generally require surgical reconstruction. (Redrawn and modified from Steed MB. Peripheral nerve response to injury. Atlas Oral Maxillofac Surg Clin North Am. 2011;19[1]:1–13.)

- Sunderland I (Seddon neuropraxia): The least severe injury clas

sification implies a temporary conduction block caused by blunt trauma, stretch injury, compression, or vascular impediment. Interstitial edema, contusing, or disruption of neural metabolism can interfere with the membrane’s ability to maintain the physiologic resting potential so that, at least focally, an action potential cannot be generated. Most people have experienced a mild form of ischemic neuropraxia when sitting with their legs crossed. A more severe or prolonged form of neuropraxia involves focal degradation or loss of myelin. Depending on the cause and severity, neuropraxic dysfunction can last anywhere from a few minutes to days, weeks, or months. However, even in the most severe neuropraxic injuries, normal physiology is restored within 3 or 4 months and the prognosis for clinical recovery is excellent.

- Sunderland II (Seddon axonotmesis): Associated with more severe

trauma, the Sunderland II injury pattern involves disruption of the axon but preservation of its surrounding endoneurium. When divided, essential metabolites within the axoplasm cannot be transported within or across the zone of injury and the entire distal segment of the transected axon undergoes the well- characterized process of Wallerian degeneration, along with the axon immediately proximal to the injury (at least to the first node of Ranvier). An influx of extracellular calcium triggers a cascade of events including microtubule depolymerization, cytoskeleton breakdown, and axolemma disintegration resulting in cellular fragmentation and leaving disorganized cellular debris in place of the axon. As the neuron shifts into a “damage control” mode, the cell body response is marked by marginalization of its nucleus and dissolution of the Nissl bodies, known as chromatolysis. Despite the upregulation of neuroprotectants such as heat- shock protein- 27 (HSP27), up to 20% to 50% of sensory neurons do not survive. Schwann cells undergo a metamorphosis from a supportive cell to a reparative phenotypeand release cytokines and neurotrophic factors to attract circulating macrophages, which enter the intraneural environment via nearby capillaries. Both the Schwann cells and macrophages participate in phagocytosis of the cellular debris, leaving endoneurial tracts cleared to accept regenerating axons. The macrophages may remain within the nerve tissue, migrate to the lymphatic system, or undergo apoptosis.

- Sunderland III (Seddon axonotmesis): This represents a partial

internal rupture of the nerve trunk in which the degree of trauma is sufficient to disrupt the endoneurial connective tissue layer surrounding the ruptured axon. With this, the protective axonal pathways are lost, and spontaneous regenerative potential is severely limited. Additionally, the increased intraneural bleeding and inflammatory response associated with this more significant injury pattern introduces fibroblast- induced scarring that further diminishes axonal elongation.

- Sunderland IV (Seddon axonotmesis): This is a complete intraneu

ral rupture. With the loss of the longitudinally robust perineurial layer, the outer epineurium is the only connective tissue maintaining nerve trunk continuity. The resultant extreme internal derangement and fibrosis impedes all spontaneous regenerative efforts.

- Sunderland V (Seddon neurotmesis): All connective tissue layers

are involved, and the nerve has completely ruptured. Spontaneous regenerative potential to the motor and sensory targets is nil, and without surgical intervention, there is little hope of functional recovery.

- Sunderland/Mackinnon VI: Most likely representing a large pro

portion of nerve injuries, this category represents mixed injuries in which, for example, some endoneurial tubes are maintained while others are completely ruptured, creating the clinically difficult scenario of partial but often functionally unsatisfactory axon regeneration.

### Nerve Regeneration

The Schwann cell’s essential role in orchestrating nerve regeneration by both stimulating and guiding regenerating axons cannot be overemphasized. Following Wallerian degeneration, the Schwann cells undergo yet another phenotype metamorphosis and, in a new role, align in columns (known as bands of Büngner) within the now unfettered endoneurial space. The Schwann cells create channels of basal lamina, which are anchored via fibronectin connections to the underlying connective tissue. Rich with glycoproteins such as laminin, these tracts will direct and guide subsequent elongating axons. Schwann cell–derived neurotrophic factors additionally provide an important stimulant to drive axonal regeneration as well.

Triggered by the loss of end target–mediated trophic feedback as well as a complex concoction of cytokines and neurotrophic factors released by local Schwann cells and macrophages, tubulin, actin, and lipid production are all upregulated within the cell bodies and transported to the axon stumps to support regeneration. Growth cones form quickly after the injury and generate small finger- like filopodia to explore the local microenvironment and seek potential pathways for elongation. Ultimately each axon will send out multiple branches in a phenomenon known as sprouting, and unimpeded axons provided appropriate pathways will regenerate at the frustratingly slow rate of 1 to 3 mm (average 1 mm) per day. In Sunderland II injuries, this unobstructed pathway is maintained, and regeneration is straightforward and predictable (Fig. 30.4).

With greater degrees of intraneural derangement, fibroblastinduced scarring, or surgical scarring, the regenerative process becomes both more complex and less predictable. Though the propensity for axons to grow toward nerve tissue (versus nonnerve tissue) was established several decades ago, more precise physiologic guidance toward appropriate type- specific end targets (i.e., motor axons growing toward motor endplates and sensory axons growing toward sensory organelles) is less well understood and less potent. The leading theory is that the axons display “preferential motor reinnervation” in which the motor axons tend to selectively enter motor endoneurial tubes, while the sensory axonal elongation progresses more randomly to enter any available tracts. Perhaps related to differences in guidance cues or Schwann cell phenotypes, the axonal guiding mechanism is far from perfect and many axons elongate toward the wrong targets. With successful reinnervation, the misguided axons prune back in what can only be considered a remarkably inefficient regenerative process.

Once the axons reach target muscles, the axons still need to grow into the muscle, reestablish contact with motor endplates, and undergo a maturation process. Additionally, expected axon regeneration rates are not precise, are inversely affected by age, and differ based on the level of injury (more proximal repairs regenerate faster). Following reinnervation, terminal axonal sprouting provides axonal branch innervation to many additional neighboring muscle fibers. Motor units are typically larger (up to five times larger according to small animal data) and more compact and delineated than preinjury innervation patterns (in which motor units overlap and muscle fibers of that motor unit are more dispersed). This phenomenon can be appreciated on electrodiagnostic studies by the presence of wide, large electromyography (EMG) signals known as polyphasic potentials. Anticipated timing of clinical evidence of muscle recovery should be considered a range rather than a precise timepoint.

Several factors can compromise axonal regeneration with definite clinical implications. Axonal elongation only occurs if axons are able to span the zone of injury, which means that endoneurial tubes need to be intact (Sunderland II) or in very close proximity. Growth cone generated proteases and plasminogen activators can penetrate through some tissue barriers, though extensive intraneural fibrosis as well as persistent internal derangement obstructs and prevents axonal regeneration so that with extensive or complete disruption, elongating axons become trapped in scar tissue. The ensuing bulbous mass of undirected, swirling axons is known clinically as a neuroma (either “end bulb” or in- continuity). This is not to be confused with the bulbous mass that forms at the proximal end of an unrepaired distal nerve stump (Fig. 30.5). This “glioma” represents a Schwann cell and fibroblast- induced response but does not contain axons. With nerve repair, the scarred, deranged, or neuromatous tissue is resected and “close endoneurial tube proximity” is obtained surgically. Intuitively, poorly aligned macro nerve elements (i.e., fascicles) must mean that the micro elements are poorly aligned as well. Regardless of how well the ends are aligned, nerve coaptation must be maintained without excessive tension. Tension at the repair site has been shown to be detrimental to axonal regeneration, intraneural vascularity, and Schwann cell activity. Following repair, nerve connective tissue healing is relatively quick and 50% to 65% of native nerve tensile strength is returned within a few weeks (based on small animal data).

The biologic consequences of a chronic nerve injury are seen in the cell body, the proximal nerve stump, the distal nerve stump, and the target organs (especially muscle). Fu and Gordon have demonstrated that with prolonged axotomy, axonal regeneration is compromised. More specifically, in a rodent model, repairs delayed greater than 6 months resulted in a 65% degradation in axonal regeneration. The relative contribution of the distal nerve stump and the chronically denervated muscle to overall recovery is not clear. The distal nerve stump suffers a time- dependent loss of neurotrophic potential related to a combination of loss of guidance cues, Schwann cell senescence, and endoneurial tube degeneration. With senescence, the Schwann cells irreversibly downgrade into a dormant inactive state, the bands of Büngner disappear, and the cells no longer support axonal regeneration. With very proximal injuries, the distal nerve tissue may suffer these negative changes despite prompt repair simply due to the slow axonal regenerative rate.

Muscle, deprived of its neurotrophic feedback, undergoes predictable and well- described changes involving the endplates, muscle fibers, and terminal axonal pathways. The endplates rapidly degenerate and the entire muscle fiber surface temporarily forms acetylcholine receptor clusters, manifested by increased insertional activity and spontaneous fibrillations on EMG. The muscle fibers become catabolic with a general degradation of contractile proteins, loss of nuclei, and profound loss of mass and muscle fiber size (cross- sectional area)—up to 50% within 2 to 3 months. The normally self- replicating population of reparative satellite cells residing within the muscle basement membranes can preserve muscle fiber viability for many months, though this pool is eventually exhausted. With end- stage denervation atrophy, occurring at an average of 18 months in humans, the scar tissue walls off the potential endplates, the acetylcholine receptor clusters degrade, and the muscle becomes permanently electrically and functionally silent. Eventually, the muscle tissue is replaced by fibrotic adipose tissue.

Other factors affecting functional nerve regeneration and recovery include age, systemic disease processes, and other toxin exposures.Advancing age is one of the most consistently potent negative influencers of neural regenerative potential. Pediatric patients not only benefit from innate superior neural regeneration and shorter regeneration lengths (shorter arms mean shorter nerves), but also enhanced central nervous system plasticity allows rapid remodeling and adoption of even aberrant reinnervation patterns. Older patients, by contrast, have a distinctly diminished regenerative potential and even with physiologic reinnervation are unable to adapt to the new innervation patterns or regain strength in reinnervated muscles. Systemic neuropathy related to diseases such as diabetes, or exposure to neurotoxins in certain chemotherapies, alcohol, or tobacco, compromise normal nerve physiology and purport a poor prognosis for functional recovery.

The mechanism of injury can have direct and indirect repercussions on nerve regeneration and clinical outcome. Tearing, avulsing, and crushing traumas create large zones of injury, which may induce greater intraneural and perineural hemorrhage and scarring—both of which can impede neural regeneration (Fig. 30.6). Injuries resulting in segmental nerve loss may require grafting procedures, and, even though the intraneural plexus characterized by fascicular interconnections is not as complex as once theorized, the larger the intervening gap, the more difficult to reestablish functional axonal pathways. Finally, collateral damage to neighboring soft tissues may compromise nerve perfusion (or the diffusion necessary to support autografts) as well as ultimate functional recovery. The lack of a stable skeletal platform, contractile muscle tissue, or a pliable mobile soft tissue envelope have obvious functional implications.

## ASSESSMENT AND TIMING

As denoted by the detrimental consequences of prolonged axotomy and denervation, nerve exploration, repair, or reconstruction should be performed as soon as practical. While some scenarios offer clear indications for urgent surgery, the timing of intervention is often more complex, and observation in many circumstances is appropriate. Initial assessment of a peripheral nerve injury should include anatomic localization, extent of injury, expected natural history, and prognosis with or without surgical intervention. These goals may require serial examinations, though prolonged observation may diminish the likelihood of a favorable outcome if spontaneous recovery does not occur.

### Timing of Nerve Repair/Exploration

- Immediate: Isolated nerve injuries are not considered emergent,

but when associated with vascular injury or limb or life- threatening injuries, they can be treated concurrently.

- Urgent: Any acute, sharp laceration with anatomically correlated

neurologic deficits should be explored within a few days of injury. While the biologic advantages of early nerve repair as previously elucidated include improved axonal regeneration and decreased atrophy, practical benefits include decreased retraction, avoidance of fibrosis (limiting nerve elasticity and stretch tolerance),and avoidance of the formation of neuromas and gliomas (requiring additional nerve resection).

- Short delay: Nerve ruptures should be identified early and may

require a short period of equilibration (approximately 3 weeks) to allow demarcation of the zone of injury.

- From 3 to 6 months: Closed nerve injuries in continuity may be

neuropraxic (Sunderland I) or axonotmetic (Sunderland II) and, as such, have an excellent prognosis for spontaneous recovery. Alternatively, they may be associated with greater degrees of internal rupture (Sunderland III and IV), which are unlikely to recover without surgical intervention. The 3- to 6- month waiting period will allow neuropraxic injuries and some axonotmetic injuries to declare. Serial electrodiagnostic examinations will demonstrate those nerves capable of regenerating. After 3 to 6 months, intraoperative nerve assessment will be necessary to identify the remaining axonotmetic injuries that are regenerating despite the lack of clinical or electrodiagnostic evidence. Greater delays may compromise final recovery.

- From 6 to 12 months: Most explorations during this time period

are related to delays in diagnosis and referral or are related to nonclinical factors (socioeconomic or lack of access to quality medical care). Repairs and reconstructions are still possible during this time period, though results may be compromised, especially in older individuals or very proximal injuries.

- From 12 to 18 months: Though there are reports of successful nerve

transfers during this time period (especially for sensory nerves), most nerve surgery at this point is primarily focused on pain relief. Alternative strategies such as tendon transfer or free functioning muscle transfer should be considered.

- Greater than 18 months: Muscle denervated past this point has a

statistically low chance of regaining function. Alternative strategies for reconstruction should be considered. Most nerve surgeries after 18 months are for pain management.

### Assessment of Nerve Injuries

Though seemingly self- evident that acute trauma patients would report sensory and motor disturbances, this is not always the case. Any open wound, penetrating wound, or even closed injury that could cause nerve damage should be treated with a high index of suspicion. Nerves running in close proximity to disrupted blood vessels should be presumed injured until proven otherwise. Interestingly, “numbness” or “I can’t feel anything” are actually very uncommon presenting complaints. More commonly, patients with significant nerve damage initially describe tingling, burning, electric shocks, or the sensation of the limb being “asleep.” Patients who present later often emphasize the associated neuropathic pain as well as functional and more complete sensory deficits. Broad and exaggerated complaints (“I can’t feel anything” or “my arm doesn’t work”) can be focused down to specific deficits during the physical exam. Associated bone, joint, and vascular collateral damage must be recognized and taken into account when trying to accurately delineate the extent of peripheral nerve injury. Finally, traumatic loss of nerve function occurs at the time of nerve injury and alternative explanations such as malingering should be considered for the unusual case of gradual or delayed development of (especially) subjective neuromuscular complaints.

In acute injuries, the exam should focus on identifying open wounds and vascular injury. With blunt or high- energy trauma, early radiographic imaging of the injured spine, chest, or extremity can help direct a more detailed physical exam and minimize unnecessary discomfort to the patient. A well- documented and detailed examination including strength grading and objective sensory testing will be essential (especially when clinically apparent improvement does not match the patient’s memory). Strength testing of all muscle groups of the affected extremity should be meticulously documented utilizing the (1974) modified British Medical Research Council (MRC) grading scale (Table 30.1) recognizing that other injuries, pain, and anxiety may all confound this initial examination. Initial sensory testing must consist of more than the question, “Can you feel this?” Patients can experience proprioceptive feedback from neighboring intact sensory territories. “Does this feel normal?” or “Does this feel the same as the [contralateral extremity]?” or “Does this feel tingling or like it’s asleep?” are all better questions for identifying a nerve injury. Remaining suspicions can be most easily confirmed or alleviated by assessing sharp- dull discrimination using either a paper clip, broken wooden cotton swab stick, or tongue depressor. Follow- up serial examinations of strength and sensation can confirm or refute initial impressions and reveal nerve recovery. Other helpful clinical findings include the observation of atrophy to confirm muscle denervation, and, while not always present, some patients develop dry skin in the distribution of skin denervation. Due to the loss of sympathetic innervation, denervated skin does not sweat or produce oils like normal skin, and the dry skin pattern can be eerily geometric with sharply defined dermatomal borders. Finally, the electric shock elicited with tapping along the anatomic course of a nerve (Tinel sign) signifies regenerating sensory axons. For maximal utility, the test should always begin at the tip of the affected extremity and progress proximally to the repair site. With axonal regeneration (either from spontaneous regeneration or following a nerve repair), this percussion sign should progress distally from the zone of injury at approximately 1 inch per month. Failure to progress may indicate either failed axon regeneration or the formation of a neuroma. Though described as an accurate and prognostic sign for reinnervation, I have not found it as reliable. Many patients maintain Tinel sensitivity at the repair site even with successful regeneration, some patients seem to have percussion signs diffusely, and some patients (perhaps due to neuropathic painmodulating drugs) never seem to exhibit one.

Though some institutions use imaging modalities such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to study axonal regeneration, most medical centers have not translated this technology to clinical practice.However, high- resolution ultrasound (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can often demonstrate complete nerve rupture or fascicular disruption from traction or projectile injury, and they should be considered in high- energy closed trauma. US has the advantage of being able to scan over long distances, as well as attain an immediate comparative examination of the opposite limb, while MRI may have increased spatial resolution. In some multilevel injuries, nerve injury localization can be challenging. However, the author has seen a neuroma- in- continuity misinterpreted as nerve swelling associated with entrapment neuropathy; body habitus, nerve depth, nearby implants, and the radiologist’s skill or experience can all affect the reliability of these modalities. In many situations, neural imaging cannot differentiate nerve injuries with the potential for spontaneous regeneration versus those requiring repair.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and EMG are the most commonly utilized diagnostic modalities but also suffer from technical and practical limitations. A common error is to prematurely obtain these studies before the completion of Wallerian degeneration (7 to 10 days) and the establishment of the characteristic EMG profile of denervated muscle (around 2 to 3 weeks). After this time frame, NCS can differentiate conduction blocks (neuropraxia) from axonotmetic injuries. While both will fail to conduct across the injury site, a neuropraxia will maintain a recordable signal when stimulation and recordings are both distal to the zone of injury, while axonotmesis will not conduct secondary to Wallerian degeneration of disrupted axons. Axonotmetic injuries with the potential for recovery can also not be differentiated from more severe injuries, until subsequent examinations demonstrate nascent recovery potentials.

By combining diagnostic modalities, pure neuropraxic and gross neurotmetic injuries can be identified. However, this still leaves a subset of incompletely characterized nerve injuries whose treatment depends on whether or not axons can regenerate across the zone of injury. Serial exams provide the opportunity for viable axonotmetic axons to regenerate and allows previously undetected neuropraxic injuries to remyelinate. Not uncommonly, follow up examinations will reveal function not previously apparent. These situations likely represent a mixed injury pattern with a small population of neuropraxic axons or axons below the threshold of electrodiagnostic detection (also known as sampling error). Terminal collateral sprouting may expand the motor unit size and generate detectable muscle contraction and/or recordable EMG signals. These encouraging findings indicate the strong potential for further recovery.

However, excessive delay clearly worsens the prognosis if nerve reconstruction is ultimately deemed necessary. The principle of achieving reinnervation as rapidly as possible is balanced against the unnecessary excision of and reconstruction of regenerating axons by waiting the minimal amount of time necessary to make this decision. Within 3 to 6 months, neuropraxic axons will have recovered and early axonal regeneration should be detectable.

Ultimately, intraoperative nerve assessment can incorporate direct nerve- to- nerve conduction testing. Regenerating axons should have regenerated 1 inch per month of delay so that a 3- to 6- month observation period would allow 3 to 6 inches of regeneration—an adequate length to enable the placement of stimulation and recording electrodes for intraoperative nerve conduction testing. Though sound in principle, enthusiasm for this strategy has been tempered by inconsistent results and technician- dependent reliability. While the author utilizes this strategy, visualization and palpation of the damaged nerve often provide equivalent or superior information, and based on similar observation, other authors have advocated for even earlier exploration (in lieu of intraoperative NCS).

## TREATMENT OPTIONS

### Observation

For many nerve injuries, expectant observation is the definitive treatment. Reassurance, pain management, and therapy to maintain joint flexibility, however, must be individualized to each patient’s needs. The economic and emotional impact of major peripheral nerve injuries should not be underestimated; evaluation by a social worker or psychologist can be instrumental in uncovering and managing the ripple effects of an incapacitating nerve injury on occupation, family life, independence, and self- esteem.

Pain management can often be one of the most challenging aspects and should focus on nerve- modulating drugs. Practically, these fall into two categories: antiseizure and antidepressants. Amitriptyline (a tricyclic antidepressant) prescribed at a subclinical dose for depression can improve neuropathic pain, with the beneficial side effect of improving sleep when taken at night. Sleep deprivation is common with neuropathic pain and may exacerbate diurnal pain and deplete effective coping skills. Unfortunately, its sedating effects often spill over to daytime activities to an extent that many patients find unacceptable. Gabapentin (an antiseizure medication) and pregabapentin (marketed for neuropathic pain) are effective, but they also have sedating, disorienting, and vertiginous side effects that seem to lessen with prolonged use but which many patients cannot tolerate.Finally, as a second- line medication to modulate neuropathic pain in recalcitrant cases, duloxetine (trade name Cymbalta, a serotoninnorepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) can be useful, but its side effects include dry mouth, fatigue, loss of appetite, nausea, and constipation. Narcotics have profound addictive qualities and tend to lose efficacy very quickly. Tramadol, while still having the potential for abuse, in the author’s experience, tends to be slightly more effective (for nerve pain) and can be sparingly incorporated into the overall pain management strategy. Nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen can provide additional pain relief but are associated with cardiovascular, hepatic, and gastrointestinal risks, especially in patients with comorbidities. Though many other medications have been utilized for neuropathic pain, the author typically refers his patients for a pain management consultation when first- and second- line pain- modulating drugs are not successful or tolerated.

Therapy generally consists of a combination of supervised exercises and an intensive home program to maintain joint mobility during recovery. Compression stockings and other therapist- applied modalities can be helpful with swelling, stiffness, pain, identification and management of trophic signs, and development of compensatory and coping mechanisms. Transcutaneous electrical stimulation (TENS) units seem to only provide modest benefit but have little downside other than expense and may be worth trialing. Transcutaneous galvanic muscle stimulation has been used successfully in animal models to delay atrophy, though it is often not well tolerated and lacks convincing data of clinical efficacy.

### Neurolysis

Surgical intervention may be indicated when physical or electrodiagnostic evidence of nerve regeneration has not occurred by 3 to 6 months. The zone of injury is explored, and the damaged nerve is exposed for visual, tactile, and potentially intraoperative nerve conduction testing (Fig. 30.7). Obvious full- thickness fibrosis, a nonconducting neuroma- in- continuity, or nerve rupture will need resection and reconstruction. Soft, well- vascularized and pliable nerve tissue with easily identifiable fascicles will typically recover. Distal muscle contraction with a low amperage (0.05 to 2.0 milliamps [mA]) proximal nerve stimulation (utilizing a commercially available nerve stimulator) can be left alone with the expectation of eventual recovery. For many nerve injuries, regenerative potential cannot be confidently determined by surgical observation. More aggressive external and even internal neurolysis, typically performed under operating scope magnification, may remove scar tissue to allow the identification of any intact and viable fascicles. As described above, electrodiagnostic testing along the nerve trunk or specific isolated fascicles may be helpful. Demonstration of a compound action potential conducting across the zone of injury indicates ongoing successful axonal elongation and a favorable prognosis for continued recovery. Occasionally, a benign appearance will conflict with a negative electrodiagnostic assessment at surgery. In injuries less than 6 months old, the option of further observation with potential future reexploration may be considered.

Although clinical recovery following exploratory surgery has been attributed to the therapeutic benefit of neurolysis, this conclusion lacks scientific support. Though there may be cases in which axonal regeneration is inhibited by perineurial scarring, this is very hard to demonstrate, and the procedure should be conceptualized as a strategy to assess the nerve’s potential for continued recovery before the treatment window of opportunity is lost. Therapeutic neurolysis for posttraumatic neuropathy (in the absence of demonstrable entrapment or traction neuritis) is unpredictable and controversial.

### Nerve Repair

There are three primary principles to nerve repair: adequate debridement of damaged nerve, precise end- to- end alignment, and atraumatic coaptation.

- *Debridement:* Depending on the mechanism of injury, the zone

of damaged nerve may extend well beyond the point of rupture. Repairing damaged nerve to damaged nerve will not juxtapose healthy endoneurial tubes that are capable of supporting axonal regeneration, and these repairs will be doomed to failure. A variety of techniques and tools have been described to facilitate controlled, sharp slices through the damaged nerve tissue to minimize crushing while efficiently progressing toward healthy nerve tissue. Commercially available nerve- cutting guide forceps with a straight razor, a scalpel applied to a tongue depressor, “supersharp” carbon scissors, and bipolar electrocautery all have their advocates, and none have been found to be superior. Resection should proceed until a clear fascicular pattern (with fascicular pouting from the proximal side) and healthy bleeding are apparent (Fig. 30.8). Real- time, intraoperative histologic verification of at least 75% viable axons has shown promise in one study, though further validation of this strategy will most likely need to precede widespread adoption.

- *Alignment:* Though most reports on alignment focus on proper

orientation to optimize successful motor and sensory reinnervation, two cadaveric studies demonstrated that end- to- end fascicular alignment may not be achieved in up to 40% of repairs (Fig. 30.9). Surface markings (such as blood vessels) and fascicular patterns can facilitate proper orientation, though optimal end- toend fascicular approximation is probably most dependent on the surgeon’s technical skill and experience.

![[Fig.30.9.png]]
> **Fig. 30.9** Cadaver nerve repair demonstrating poor alignment. Arrows pointing to fascicles sticking out through coaptation.

- *Coaptation:* A variety of techniques have been described to accu

rately repair nerves. Microsutures are the gold standard and can be placed through the outer epineurium (epineurial repair), the internal epineurium (grouped fascicular repair), and, rarely, the perineurium (fascicular repair) in an attempt to maximize axonal alignment. The increased manipulation and suture material used to gain alignment must be balanced against the risk of iatrogenic intraneural damage and foreign body burden. In most situations, simple epineurial sutures, supplemented by a nerve wrap or fibrin glue, are adequate. For very distal nerves with well- known motor/sensory topography such as the median and ulnar nerves at the wrist, a grouped fascicular repair may be more efficacious. Suture caliber has been studied primarily as a determinant of resistance to tension. Based on rodent model data, one or two 8- 0 or 9- 0 suture (depending on the size of the nerve) should be able to maintain nerve stump approximation. Well-done suture neurorrhaphy is technically demanding, time consuming, and resource heavy.

#### Technique

Once the nerve ends have been debrided, microsutures should be placed under optical magnification. Though several reports indicate that nerve repairs can be adequately performed under loupe magnification, Bernstein et al. demonstrated in a cadaver study that the technical quality of nerve repairs was better when performed using the operating microscope. The smallest caliber and the fewest number of sutures necessary to achieve stable coaptation should be used. Care should be taken to place these sutures as atraumatically as possible by avoiding squeezing, crushing, or unnecessarily manipulating the fascicles. Microforceps can stabilize the nerve tissue by grasping the outer epineurium. Fascicular alignment in an epineurial repair is achieved by matching the fascicular and perineurial vascular patterns, precise needle penetration depth and position, and avoidance of overtightening so that the fascicles are barely touching (Fig. 30.10). If using double- throw surgeon knots, care must be taken to avoid overtightening. While tension may be relieved for convenience during the first few sutures by extreme joint positioning, one should be certain that nerve approximation is maintained with one or two sutures in neutral posture. Nonphysiologic tension across a neurorrhaphy diminishes local vascular flow and risks disruption during the postoperative period.

> **Fig. 30.10** Distal ulnar nerve with fascicular groups isolated and separately repaired.

For larger diameter nerves, a grouped fascicular repair can be performed by placing a few deep sutures through the internal epineurium or superficially through the perineurium of a few fascicles to optimize the internal fascicular alignment (Fig. 30.11). Alternatively, the inner epineurium can be stripped away to allow direct manipulation and individual coaptation of each fascicle. Though improved alignment is possible with this technique, if alignment is off by only a few degrees, every axon will be “misdirected,” and even if perfect, the increased intraneural trauma and scarring may negate any benefits. Fascicular repairs are much more technically challenging and time consuming, and they have not been shown to improve clinical outcomes.

#### Primary Nerve Repair Outcomes

Primary repair of digital or small sensory nerves in adults generally results in good but not perfect recovery in 50% to 70% of repairs.One large study of 93 digital nerve repairs with 3½-year follow reported an average static 2PD of 10.6 mm (compared with 4 to 6 mm as normal), restoration of sharp- dull and temperature discrimination in 66 and 83% of the repairs (respectively), and only a 2% incidence of painful neuroma formation.

Major peripheral nerve primary repairs do not fare as well as smaller diameter nerves but still have a better prognosis than grafted repairs. A thorough analysis of the clinical literature is difficult due to inconsistencies in outcomes measures, stratification of injury mechanisms, and elapsed time since repair. Primary repairs are often combined with the outcomes of grafted repairs, and variables such as patient age, duration from injury to repair, and collateral damage all further confound interpretation. Sixteen of 18 primary midhumeral radial nerve repairs achieved M4 or 5 wrist and digit extension. Emamhadi et al. reported 90% good neurologic recovery in 93 cases of direct ulnar nerve repair, but they did not subclassify these results based on the level of injury, and their outcome scale deemphasized intrinsic muscle recovery.Post et al. conversely reported only modest intrinsic recovery and no good sensory recovery out of their 15 patients who underwent primary high ulnar nerve repairs. In one study of combined median and ulnar nerve repairs, functional motor recovery was noted in 23 of 26 (with tactile sensation in 12 median and 7 ulnar nerves, and protective sensation in an additional 11 ulnar and 7 median nerve repairs). In a second study focused on median and ulnar nerve repairs, 51 of 65 primarily repaired nerves achieved good or excellent recovery.

#### Augmentation of Nerve Repair

**Fibrin glue.** Fibrin glue is a coaptation aid to augment or, in some cases, replace microsutures. Though autologous fibrin glue can be prepared, this is time consuming, requires specialized equipment, and demonstrates inferior biomechanical holding properties compared with commercially available alternatives. The commercially available fibrin glues are provided as separate solutions of fibrinogen and thrombin that when mixed form a gel- like “white” clot, which lacks the red blood cells found in physiologic clot.

A cadaveric biomechanical study demonstrated that fibrin glue as an augment to sutures resisted gapping but did not add to the ultimate tensile strength of the repair. Early dehiscence is highlighted in several rodent studies, though if the repair holds for 1 to 2 weeks, the risk of rupture lessens and equivalent axonal regeneration can be expected.Contrary to popular belief, fibrin glue inadvertently interposed between the nerve stumps will not block axonal regeneration. However, maintaining orientation and alignment during fibrin glue application can be challenging when used without one or two aligning sutures. Limited clinical evidence suggests at least equivalent efficacy to suture repairs.

Technique. Nerve stumps can be manually approximated or preliminarily secured with microsutures. A flexible radiopaque backing can be placed behind the nerve to catch and shape the fibrin glue mixture slowly dripped around the coaptation. The backing is wrapped around the congealing fibrin glue mixture (like a taco) to shape the clot into an adhesive cylinder. This is maintained for 3 minutes to allow initial set-up, the Esmarch removed, and any excess fibrin glue excised (Fig. 30.12).

![[Fig.30.12.png]]
> **Fig. 30.12** Fibrin glue application to a nerve repair. A, A radiopaque rubber backing is placed behind the coaptation and the fibrin glue dripped on and around the coaptation. Dripping slowly will minimize the “run off” and facilitate the accumulation of the mixture around the repair. B, The rubber backing is wrapped around the congealing fibrin glue like a taco and held in place for 3 minutes. C, The coaptation is now reinforced with the firm gel. Excess material can be excised.

**Nerve connectors.** Short conduits or “nerve cuff” wraps applied around the approximated nerve ends is an alternative coaptation strategy that may offer additional biologic and practical benefits. In a sequestered protected microenvironment of a conduit, a rich milieu of neurotrophic and growth factors accumulates, and scar tissue is inhibited from invading into and around the coaptation.Whether the same applies to an externally applied cuff that is slid over a nerve repair cannot be stated with certainty. A review of several clinical studies suggested less pain at the nerve repair site when wrapped with vein or commercially available conduits or scar barriers. Mechanical neuropathic pain at the coaptation is theorized to be related to escaping axons, and alleviation of this discomfort suggests a potential for mitigation of this effect.

For less-experienced surgeons, a conduit- assisted repair technique may offer advantages of fascicular orientation, as demonstrated in a recent cadaver study compared with conduits or sutures alone. Neither this (nor the theoretical biologic advantages outlined above) have translated to substantial improvement in clinical outcomes. Potential benefits of a nerve cuff must be weighed against its increased cost and the potential for iatrogenic compression at the repair site associated with postoperative swelling.

Technique: nerve cuff. Nerve cuff assisted repair requires a short conduit or tube of slightly greater diameter than the prepared nerve stumps. The tube is slid several centimeters proximal to one of the prepared nerve ends prior to suturing. A few microsutures are placed in the epineurium to obtain alignment and loose coaptation of the nerve ends. The conduit is then slid over the repair and either left alone, sutured in place (microsutures passed through the connector edge and the epineurium), or secured using fibrin glue. If sutures are placed, these may “unload” tension at the repair site, which is theoretically helpful, though care must be taken to not overapproximate these repairs so that the fascicles overlap. If the connector is too big, microclips or microsutures can be used to take up the slack and ensure that the connector is splinting and directing the nerve ends toward each other. However, the fit should not be tight or even snug (since nerve ends do swell after repair). Alternatively, the connector can be slit and wrapped around the loose suture repair. Either additional sutures or fibrin glue can maintain the wrap in position (Fig. 30.13).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: NERVE REPAIR

Lacerations and sharp puncture wounds are explored acutely to increase the likelihood of primary repair. A commercially available nerve cutting system coupled with a razor blade produces a clean, fresh transection (see Fig. 30.8A). Fresh injuries are often maintained in at least a grossly oriented position, and this can be fine- tuned based on fascicular patterns and surface markers such as blood vessels. The nerves are mobilized by proximal and distal dissection to facilitate direct approximation. If nerve loss is minimal and retraction appears related to soft tissue swelling and innate nerve elasticity, mild to moderate joint positioning is acceptable. Illustrative of this principle, knee flexion can overcome sciatic nerve gaps of up to 8 cm to allow direct repair, followed by immobilization in the flexed position. The functional recovery obtained after delayed and gradual extension is markedly better than obtainable with autogenous nerve graft. Bleeding from both the external and internal epineurial vascular systems can impair visualization. Epinephrine- soaked pledgets can be applied to the cut nerve surface, or microtip bipolar electrocautery can be used on the lowest applicable setting and with copious irrigation to carefully cauterize. The ulnar and median nerves in the forearm have particularly consistent topography and the motor and sensory components for both nerves are readily defined. By repairing groups of fascicles, alignment is maximized while minimizing suture burden. Sutures are primarily placed through the epineurium; though, if necessary to obtain alignment, a superficial pass through the perineurium is acceptable. Larger nerves should maintain approximation with one or two 8- 0 nylon sutures, while smaller sensory or digital nerves are generally repaired with 9- 0 or 10- 0 nylon sutures. Additional sutures are added to improve fascicular alignment and stabilize the coaptation. A spanning 6- 0 monofilament (placed as a horizontal mattress suture through the epineurium) can “protect” the repair when any incorporated joint positioning is gradually relieved in the postoperative period. The approximated ends should be barely touching. The tendency to overtighten the repair must be avoided as this leads to overlapping fascicles or fascicles protruding from the perimeter of the repair. If this occurs, the offending fascicles can be trimmed back. At the surgeon’s discretion, hollow conduits can be slit and placed around the coaptation to splint, stabilize, and augment the repair. Commercially available fibrin glue is dripped over the connector to secure this in place and further stabilize the repair. The neighboring joints are positioned to alleviate tension at the repair site and a protective splint applied for 3 weeks before gradually reintroducing motion (Fig. 30.14).

### Managing Nerve Defects

With enough delay, even cleanly cut nerves retract and develop end bulb neuromas and gliomas that require surgical excision and prevent a tension- free repair. However, nerve ruptures resulting from traction injuries, segmental nerve loss, and neuromas in- continuity invariably result in nerve defects that require alternate reconstructive strategies. For modest defects, nerve mobilization can allow reapproximation with acceptable tension, and 4 to 6 cm can be gained by dissecting the proximal and distal median nerve stumps in the forearm. Anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve for injuries at the elbow can gain a few centimeters, though this does not add much length to a forearm laceration. Persistent defects must be addressed by either bridging the gap or, when applicable, using nerve transfers.

#### Conduits

Conduits are small tubes capable of supporting regeneration over short distances. A fibrin clot or scaffold forms from exudate from the cut nerve stumps to support Schwann cell migration and subsequent axonal elongation across the gap. Commercially available conduits (in the United States) are made of either polycaprolactone, polyglycolic acid polymer, or processed collagen (bovine), which share the common characteristics: semipermeable to allow the diffusion of nutrients to support the fibrin bridge formation, semirigid to prevent collapse, and absorbable. Early clinical work in conduits used silicone tubes, which did seem to enhance nerve regeneration but caused nerve irritation or compression and required a second surgery for removal in some patients. These second- look surgeries validated the formation of nerve tissue across the defects, but absorbable conduits are more practical. Of the commercially available (in the United States) absorbable options, rodent data in 10 mm nerve gaps demonstrated a slight superiority of polycaprolactone. These tubes are quite rigid, have suboptimal handling characteristics, and may be problematic around joints. Autologous veins can also be used instead of the commercial products. Theoretical concerns regarding vein collapse have not been validated but either fresh or fresh- frozen muscle graft can be inserted into the lumen to add both structure integrity and linear columns of laminin (found in the basement membranes of muscle fibers). These columns of laminin may provide a scaffold and some guidance cues to support and guide regenerating axons. Collapse and early reabsorption have been described in polyglycolic acid- based conduits. While undersized conduits would be expected to inhibit axon regeneration, oversized conduits do not create an optimal microenvironment and adversely impact functional recovery.

**Outcomes.** Conduits offer convenience, avoid the donor morbidity of nerve grafts, and are technically easier than formal neurorrhaphy. The fibrin clot bridge essential for axon regeneration, however, becomes unstable within longer or larger diameter conduits. Therefore, the conduit critical length may be related to the diameter of the nerve being fixed. Lundborg et al. and Boeckstyns et al. reported consistently successful median and ulnar nerve repairs utilizing conduits for 5- to 6- mm gaps. Dienstknecht et al. reported six out of nine conduit median nerve repairs achieved M4 strength, and seven patients regained 10 mm or less 2PD. Others have not been as successful. Moore et al. reported four of four failed conduit repairs across 2- to 3- cm gaps in a variety of major peripheral nerves.

For smaller-diameter nerves, the clinical literature is more consistent and still more favorable for shorter gaps. Though considered pivotal in the widespread acceptance of nerve conduits, the Weber et al. study, which concluded that conduits were superior to autograft for defects of up to 3 cm, actually had no 30- mm gaps and averaged only 7 mm. Consistently good results have been reported in gaps less than 2 cm. Acceptable results were reported in 40% to 60% of conduit repairs across 12 mm average gap lengths. Lohmeyer et al. in two separate studies reported improved outcomes in gaps less than 12 mm and no recovery in gaps greater than 15 mm. Extrusions, especially though tenuous soft tissue envelopes in areas of repetitive motion (such as digital injuries), are a complication unique to commercially available conduits as well.

**Technique.** After nerve debridement and mobilization of the two ends, the gap between the nerve ends is measured. Though some surgeons recommend doing this with the involved joint (i.e., proximal interphalangeal joint) in maximum extension, this tends to result in the conduit being excessively long with a tendency for kinking of the conduit, or more commonly the nerve just distal or proximal to the conduit, with flexion. The nerve has some natural tension that should be taken into account when sizing. Extra conduit can always be trimmed away during implantation. Approximating the length should also take into account that the nerve stumps will need to be pulled into the nerve tube. The recommended amount is approximately equal to the diameter of the nerve being repaired on each end. Conduit diameter (commonly available in a variety of dimensions up to 10 mm diameter) is chosen by measuring the diameter of the nerve stump, and sizing guides are available. The prepared nerve should slide easily into the conduit with minimal manipulation; gross oversizing must be avoided. Small mismatches in nerve size can be accommodated. The conduit is soaked in normal saline for several minutes prior to implantation to soften the material and facilitate microneedle penetration. The suture (typically an 8- 0 or 9- 0) needs to be passed from outside- in though the conduit wall, at a distance approximately half the diameter of the affected nerve. The microneedle is then passed transversely though the outer epineurium (taking care to not overpenetrate the nerve tissue), again, at a distance from the nerve end corresponding to half the diameter of the nerve. The final pass is from inside- out within the conduit lumen, at the same level of the first pass, to create a horizontal mattress stitch. A second suture can be placed using the same technique, 180 degrees from the first (at the surgeon’s discretion); this is technically easier if done before the nerve is advanced into the lumen. As tension is applied to the suture(s), an assistant stabilizes the conduit while the nerve stump is guided into the lumen. Once seated, the suture(s) can be tied over the outer conduit surface. Mild traction is applied to the conduit and the remaining stump to estimate the appropriate final length and trim the conduit as needed. The same suture steps are repeated for the opposing nerve stump. The lumen is irrigated with saline (or heparinized saline) several times during the conduit application process to prevent blood clot formation and allow the conduit to support the formation of a fibrin exudate scaffold (Fig. 30.15).

#### Acellular Nerve Allograft

Acellular nerve allograft (ANA), in contrast to conduits, retains the same three- dimensional internal scaffolding as native nerve tissue. Detergent tissue processing renders the allograft grossly immunotolerant, primarily by removing all cells and cellular debris. Laminin staining in small animal models demonstrates the preservation of an albeit fragmented basal lamina layer, which may provide some guidance cues. Following implantation, Schwann cell migration into the graft to populate the allograft scaffold is an essential step to facilitate axonal elongation. Rodent data did not demonstrate statistical differences in muscle weights and distal nerve histomorphology 3 months following nerve reconstruction with 10- mm and 14- mm autograft (or isograft) compared with equivalent lengths of ANA. Muscle force recovery in the 10- mm groups was not statistically different for autograft and ANA, and post hoc analysis of the 14- mm grafts confirmed similar midgraft axon density. At 4 months, however, muscle force recovery was statistically superior in the 1- cm autograft repair group compared with the 1 cm ANA repair group. With a 28- mm graft length, isograft supported superior axonal regeneration as demonstrated by higher distal axon counts and increased muscle weights.

In a separate study of ANA and conduits, muscle force recovery was statistically inferior across a 1- cm gap when repaired with a collagen conduit at both 3- and 4- month testing points, and axon density was statistically lower at the middle of the conduit compared with isograft and ANA at both 14- and 28- mm lengths. Electrical stimulation failed to elicit any detectable muscle contraction after rodent nerve repair across a 28- mm conduit even after 22 weeks.

The only commercially available processed ANA in the United States is available in diameters 1 to 2 mm, 2 to 3 mm, 3 to 4 mm, and 4 to 5 mm and in lengths ranging from 1.5 cm to 7 cm. Diameter is categorized by ranges since the biologic nature precludes precise size matching, and available sizing limits clinical application to corresponding nerve defects. After detergent processing, the grafts are flash frozen and maintained at subfreezing temperatures until time of implantation. Though smaller diameter grafts can be cable grafted in an analogous fashion to autograft (see below), the larger diameter grafts can be used instead to appropriately match recipient nerves, or for larger nerves, to reduce the number of required cables. Based on subanalysis of an industry- sponsored outcomes database, the 4- to 5-mm diameter allografts demonstrate similar motor and sensory recovery compared with small- diameter ANA. In general, the ANA (especially the larger diameters) is slightly firmer than autograft and, in the author’s opinion, is technically easier to implant.

Commercial ANA is treated with an additional enzymatic process to remove chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans. These proteoglycans bind to laminin and inhibit axon regeneration. Treated ANA supported axon regeneration over a length twice that of untreated ANA in a rodent model. While this additional processing step may increase its effective length, ANA still suffers from critical length limitations. In one rodent study, axons regenerated across a 20- mm allograft but were unable to consistently regenerate across 40- or 60- mm lengths.Importantly, the axons regenerated 20 mm into the 40- mm grafts but only about 5 mm into the 60- mm grafts indicating the physiologic obstacle is more complex than a simple axon growth limitation. The ubiquitous Schwann cell has been implicated, and while these cells appear capable of populating even long ANA, in the stressful intraneural microenvironment, these cells enter a senescent state and no longer support and guide axon regeneration.

**Outcomes.** How this rodent data translates to human ANA is not clear (6 cm is longer than the rodent hindlimb), but there is strong clinical data suggesting that ANA can support axon regeneration across small- diameter sensory nerve defects of at least 3 cm, while advocates claim efficacy for larger-diameter nerves and for defects up to 7 cm. Means et al. reported eight out of eight digital nerves repaired with ANA across a mean gap of 12 mm recovered S3+ (MRC) or better with at least 6 months’ follow- up. Others have reported similar positive outcomes in small- diameter nerve ANA reconstruction up to 3 cm, with an average static 2PD of 5.5 mm and moving 2PD of 4.4 mm in eight digital nerve and two dorsal sensory nerve repairs and an average static 2PD of 7.1 mm and moving 2PD of 5.4 mm in 18 digital nerve repairs. Though from the oral maxillofacial literature, 5 out of 7 7-cm ANA reconstructions of the lingual and inferior alveolar nerves resulted in return of normal sensation. Unfortunately, the majority of the published clinical reports are based on data collected as part of an industry- sponsored multicenter observational registry study (“RANGER”). Participating centers report retrospectively obtained outcomes data to a centralized industry- maintained database. Multiple, often overlapping, publications have emanated from this RANGER database. The most recent study included 634 sensory, mixed, and motor nerve repairs with gaps up to 70 mm. Overall, 82% of repairs achieved “meaningful” recovery, which was defined as sensory recovery of greater than or equal to S3 and motor recovery of greater than or equal to M3. Though gaps less than 15 mm did better than gaps of 50 to 70 mm, the larger gaps were associated with more complex injuries and still achieved a 69% rate of recovery. Lenkenby et al. recently reported a large series that was independent of the RANGER database and involved 171 ANA repairs in 129 patients. One hundred and thirty- five repairs were done within 6 weeks of the injury, while 10 were performed between 6 weeks and 6 months and 26 at greater than 6 months of injury. Overall, S3 recovery was achieved in 77% of the repairs and M3 in only 36%. Longer lengths (greater than 30 mm), thicker diameter (greater than 3 mm), nerve type (sensory did better), and anatomic location (lower limb nerve repairs did worse) were all statistically significant confounding factors. A prospective randomized study comparing ANA with the gold standard autograft will be necessary before definitive conclusions can be made regarding the application of ANA for motor nerves and for longer or more complex nerve repairs.

**Technique.** Length estimate should take into account the in situ tissue elasticity as was described for conduit use. For a situation with several nerve gaps, choosing a longer graft and then cutting it into several smaller lengths may be more cost effective. Accurately matching the in situ nerve stumps and the ANA, in the author’s opinion, can be challenging, especially because the labeled diameter range on the ANA package is not very precise. The larger-diameter ANAs are usually derived from polyfascicular donor nerves, and one or two fascicles can be peeled away (like string cheese) if the chosen ANA is too large. The ANA is thawed in room temperature normal saline for several minutes and the ANA can be kept in this saline bath for up to 12 hours according to the manufacturer. The ANA is implanted using sutures, nerve connectors, or fibrin glue as described above for primary coaptations. For connector- assisted repairs, however, even small gaps should be avoided. ANA does not produce the fibrin exudate necessary to generate a fibrin scaffold, and in an animal model, even the few millimeters inconsequential for connector-assisted primary nerve coaptations did not support axonal regeneration when applied to ANA. If the reconstructed nerve is larger than the 4- to 5-mm diameter ANA (or if this size is not available), then smaller- diameter ANAs can be combined as “cable” grafts, as described in the autograft section (see page 1167) (Fig. 30.16). Nearby joints should be ranged to identify and address gapping, kinking, or tension at the coaptation sites. Excessive tension in any nerve repair, autograft, or allograft is unacceptable, is likely to fail, and must be revised, regardless of additional cost or operative time.

#### Autograft

Autograft, as the tissue that most closely mimics that being replaced, remains the gold standard for bridging nerve defects and has the theoretically optimal internal three- dimensional scaffold, guidance cues, and neurotrophic environment. Viable and active Schwann cells are highly preserved, and repopulation of the graft is not necessary. Harvested autograft undergoes the same Wallerian degeneration process and cellular upregulation as seen in the denervated distal nerve.

Any expendable nerve can theoretically be used as donor autograft. Though some physiologic differences between motor and sensory regeneration would suggest that motor donor nerves would be more effective at supporting motor axon regeneration, the data does not demonstrate a meaningful difference. This is fortunate since expendable motor nerves are rare. Since nerve autograft is living tissue (unlike conduits and ANA), cell metabolism must be supported by providing nutrients and oxygen and removing metabolic waste. Small- diameter nerve graft viability is maintained by tissue diffusion (assuming a well- vascularized tissue bed) until revascularization. This process occurs relatively quickly via both inosculation and, if the tissue bed is permissive, neovascularization. Large- diameter “trunk” autografts are beyond the limits of tissue diffusion (around 2 mm) and are not used because of the risk of internal necrosis. The precise cutoff for acceptable nerve graft diameter has not been well defined, but most surgeons will not use a donor nerve greater than 3 mm in diameter. Additionally, since graft behaves in a similar fashion to the distal nerve, chronically denervated autograft loses neurotrophic potential, so using a “downstream” branch (i.e., the ipsilateral sensory branch of the radial nerve for a delayed reconstruction of a proximal radial nerve injury) to repair the proximal trunk may be suboptimal.

Even with perfect tissue beds, grafts do not support axon elongation as effectively as native nerves. As graft lengths increase, clinical results have been shown to deteriorate. The intraneural fascicular plexus of the graft does not replicate the specific intraneural plexus of the missing nerve so that accurate restoration of fascicular orientation becomes more problematic with increasing gap size. Schwann cell senescence, one of the primary obstacles in ANA, has been demonstrated in long autografts in the rodent nerve repair model.

Donor nerve selection should be based on desired thickness and length, potential donor morbidity, and ease of harvest. Sural nerve is generally considered the “workhorse” graft option based primarily on the available length and minimal morbidity: 30 to 40 cm of 2 to 3 mm relatively uniform diameter graft per leg. Other conventional options include the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerves (3 mm diameter and 30 cm in length) and the superficial radial nerve (1 to 2 mm in diameter and 20 mm in length). Less conventional options include the saphenous and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves. The appeal of avoiding a second surgical site by harvesting ipsilateral upper extremity cutaneous nerves should be weighed against the further loss of sensation from an already compromised extremity. Donor site sensory loss associated with most nerve harvests, however, is generally well tolerated. Persistent neuroma pain has been reported in up to 25% of medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve harvests. Hallgren et al. investigated long- term follow- up post–sural nerve harvest: 19 out of 41 patients reported that the area of sensory loss diminished with time, few patients reported pain, less than a third reported cold sensitivity, and most patients did not feel that donor morbidity was a significant problem.

**Outcomes.** Patient age, time from injury, mechanism of injury, regeneration distance, surgeon experience, and length of gap are the most significant factors affecting clinical recovery. Intrinsic factors and systemic disease also can adversely impact outcomes, including cardiovascular health, diabetes, exposure to toxins (tobacco, alcohol, chemotherapeutics), and associated injuries (fractures, tendons, muscles, etc.). Certainly, even long autograft repairs of major peripheral nerves can do well—Wolfe et al. achieved 100% functional EMG confirmed deltoid recovery following axillary nerve grafts ranging from 11 to 15 cm in 10 patients, and Lee et al. achieved M4 to 5 recovery in five out of six radial nerve grafts ranging from 9 to 11 cm. However, the majority of the outcomes literature is much more sobering. Ruijs et al. published a large metaanalysis in 2005 (23 articles covering 623 median and ulnar nerve injuries) in which only 52% and 43% achieved M4 to 5 and S3+ to 4 recovery, respectively.More contemporary reports demonstrate good results in only 39%,33%, and 31% of median or ulnar nerve autograft repairs. Highenergy penetrating trauma (gunshot wounds and shrapnel) fare even worse and have been associated with clinical failure in 85% proximal, 70% midarm, and 50% distal ulnar nerve repairs; 90% proximal, 67% midarm, and 31% distal median nerve repairs; and 60% proximal radial nerve repairs.

Smaller- diameter digital nerves have a better prognosis with useful recovery (S3 to S4) following autograft reconstruction reported in at least 56% of digits in one study. S3+ to S4 sensation or static 2PD of 10 mm or less was restored in the majority of cases across four other series for defects up to 3 cm. For longer defects of 3 to 6 cm, these results degrade, and though only a limited number of cases have been reported, static 2PD >15 mm is not uncommon. Exceptionally, Nunley et al. reported on 2 patients with 2PD recovery of 8 and 12 mm, and 2 out of 6 patients from Tenny and Lewis’s series achieved normal sensation.

The anticipated autograft source will dictate positioning. If ipsilateral nerve grafting from the injured upper extremity is planned, a sterile tourniquet should be considered to allow proximal dissection. Depending on which nerve is being reconstructed, the “down” arm on a hand table will allow access to the median, ulnar, and distal radial nerves.

**Technique: sural nerve graft harvest** Sural nerve. The sural nerve runs with the tibial nerve between the two heads of the gastrocnemius muscles proximally and emerges from beneath the muscular fascia in the proximal leg. It gently courses laterally to run between the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon, and then breaks up into terminal branches (Fig. 30.17A). While “sloppy” lateral decubitus facilitates sural nerve harvest from the “up” leg, bilateral nerve harvest is impossible. If both sural nerves will definitely be needed, starting prone, harvesting bilateral sural nerves, and closing and dressing the wounds can all be accomplished relatively quickly. The autograft can be placed in a saline- soaked sponge and kept on ice during repositioning, injury exposure, and nerve defect preparation. If possible, the nerve grafts are isolated but left in situ until the recipient defect is prepared; at which point the ends are cut and the nerve grafts immediately transferred. If the anticipated quantity of nerve graft is not known but may require bilateral sural nerves, supine positioning is recommended. A 10 lb sandbag or 1 L saline bag can be taped “caddycorner” at each side of the foot of the operating gurney, such that each leg can be internally rotated and positioned with the hip and knee flexed to gain exposure to the posterior lateral distal leg. The dissection is generally straightforward and typically starts with a 6- to 8- cm incision directly in between the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon. The sural nerve can be identified on the deep aspect of the subcutaneous adipose tissue and must be isolated from the lesser saphenous vein, which is preserved. The nerve is mobilized, working from distal to proximal while extending the skin incision as needed (see Fig. 30.17B and C). Vessel loops can be placed around the nerve to facilitate handling and prevent injury. The final few centimeters of dissection in the genicular crease is done with the hip and knee extended by a resilient assistant. The graft is fully mobilized but not cut or removed from the leg until ready to transfer to the recipient site. The proximal end is marked, and the graft reversed in polarity before use, so that all the axonal channels will be continuous without exiting branches.

Alternatively, in many patients, the sural nerve has few branches and can almost be bluntly dissected out of the subcutaneous tissue. In these patients, a tendon stripper can be used to encircle the nerve distally and carefully pushed along the nerve proximally. This technique can only be used if the stripper passes smoothly without resistance (see Fig. 30.17D). If resistance is encountered, a small incision must be made to identify the end of the tendon stripper and release the offending nerve branch or fascial band. Often this can be accomplished with a few small incisions, while in other patients, the tendon stripper technique must be quickly abandoned in favor of an open incision. In the proximal calf, the proximal incision is made over the palpable stripper such that nerve identification and transection can be done safely (see Fig. 30.17E and F).

Medial antebrachial and medial brachial cutaneous nerves. If only a few centimeters of nerve graft are needed, the anterior division of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve can be readily identified approximately two finger breadths anterior and distal to the medial epicondyle. If longer lengths of graft are necessary, a midbrachial incision will allow identification of these sensory nerve branches running parallel and usually in intimate contact with the basilic vein (Fig. 30.18).

Superficial radial nerve. The superficial radial nerve branches from the radial nerve around the level of the elbow and runs underneath the brachioradialis before exiting the fascia between the brachioradialis and the extensor carpi radialis longus in the midforearm, 6 to 7 cm proximal to the wrist. The nerve breaks into its terminal branches that continue dorsoradially over the first dorsal compartment tendons in the subcutaneous layer toward the first web space. The nerve can be approached via a variety of incisions, though is typically most easily identified as it emerges from underneath the brachioradialis at approximately the junction of the mid- and distal thirds of the forearm and then followed proximally and distally until up to 20 cm can be harvested. Due to the theoretical propensity for symptomatic neuroma formation, the superficial radial nerve is not the first choice in graft options, and the proximal stump should be buried in a proximal muscle belly.

**Technique: autograft repair.** If the diameter of the nerve graft matches the size of the injured nerve, the ends of the graft can be directly coapted to the nerve stumps using sutures, connectors, or fibrin glue as previously described. If, as is often the case with a major peripheral nerve repair, the diameter of the injured nerve is greater than the nerve graft, strands of graft will need to be stacked on each other to create a “cable graft.” The number of anticipated cables must be taken into account when harvesting the graft, as well as a 10% to 20% surplus to account for tissue retraction and graft preparation. Three-, four-, or five-strand cable grafts are not uncommon depending on the level of injury for median, ulnar, and radial nerve repairs. The grafts are reversed in polarity, and each strand can be individually sutured into place with two sutures per cable, in a group fascicular pattern. The matching fascicles are identified in the distal stump, and the distal end of the graft sutured in place (Fig. 30.19). Care is taken to avoid tension but also avoiding excess length, which may kink. Though sound in principle, fascicular patterns can be hard to discern and may even differ between the two nerve stumps depending on the length of the gap and the complexity of the intraneural plexus. When all of the cables have been sewn in place, fibrin glue or entubulating connectors (or both) can be applied, depending on surgeon preference.

Alternatively, the size of the defect can be measured, and the appropriately sized cable graft assembled on the back table using the technique of Narakas. The full length of nerve graft can be divided into strands (typically 10% to 20% longer than the anticipated final graft length), reversed in polarity, and stacked on each other to create a bundle of nerve strands. The ends of the bundled nerve strands are placed onto a 2 × 3 cm malleable soft backing (as per nerve repair section, above). Fibrin glue is slowly dripped around the ends of the strands and the backing wrapped around both ends of the composition to shape and hold the fibrin glue until set (typically around 3 minutes) (Video 30.1). Vessel clips can be used to hold the backing together while the clot is setting. The backing is removed, excess fibrin glue trimmed away, and the ends of the cable graft trimmed cleanly using nerve holding forceps and a razor blade so that pouting fascicles are displayed. Care is taken not to immerse the entire cable in fibrin glue, so as to allow inosculation of nutrients from the surrounding tissue bed and prevent graft necrosis. The final construct is stable enough to be implanted en bloc and secured in place with a few epineural sutures, taking care to pass the needle though the fibrin glue to gain purchase on graft epineurium. The construct can be augmented with connectors or fibrin glue at the surgeon’s discretion (Fig. 30.20). Depending on the adequacy of the repair and the degree of residual tension, most repairs are protected for 3 weeks to allow initial connective tissue healing, though studies assessing digital nerve repairs in conjunction with flexor tendon repairs concluded that early range of motion was permissible.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: OVERCOMING A NERVE GAP

Unless a patient specifically refuses, ANA is routinely utilized for digital or sensory nerve repair gaps of 1.5 to 7 cm. For shorter gaps, conduits or ANA are used, and for longer gaps, autograft (usually the sural nerve) is used. With ANA and autograft, the decision to use a connector is based on the technical quality and stability of the neurorrhaphy as demonstrated through an arc of motion. If gapping or a tendency for the nerve ends to be malaligned is noted, a single nerve connector can be cut in half and split longitudinally to be wrapped around the coaptation. The wrap is secured in place using just one suture or fibrin glue to avoid unnecessary extra manipulation of the nerve. If multiple nerve repairs are being performed, fibrin glue is a more efficient strategy to stabilize the wrap (or repair).

For larger diameter nerves, conduits are only used to reinforce and stabilize the repair alignment, while both ANA and autograft are considered for gap lengths up to 7 cm. Patients are offered both, with a detailed explanation of the relative pros and cons of each choice including the relative superiority of autograft demonstrated in small animal studies and the current limitations of the clinical literature (lack of a prospective comparative study with autograft and minimal data on longer nerve gaps and mixed motor repairs). Some patients have a strong preference (typically related to fear of a second incision and donor morbidity versus concern regarding the cadaveric source of ANA). If the patient does not have a preference, the patient is consented for both and ANA is typically utilized for smaller (3 to 5 cm) defects based on increased surgical and technical efficiency. For nerve gaps longer than 5 to 7 cm, autograft is applied as a cable graft (lacking any suitable nerve transfer options). Cable grafts are preferentially assembled on the back table using fibrin glue and implanted using just enough sutures to stabilize the repair. As described for smallerdiameter nerve reconstruction, connectors are typically placed around the repair to help align fascicles, though alternatively, a connector can be slid onto one nerve stump, neurorrhaphy performed, and the connector slid over the coaptation to fine-tune and protect the alignment. Postoperative immobilization protects the reconstruction for around 3 weeks.

### Distal Nerve Transfers in the Upper Limb

Nerve transfers are, in many ways, analogous to tendon transfers. Both types of transfers involve sacrificing a nonessential donor function to regain a more important but currently absent function. Similar in concept to tendon transfers, the planned donor nerve is preferably synergistic and redundant with minimal or no morbidity associated with its harvest. A thorough inventory of motor and sensory deficits can be compared with the available donor nerves and muscles. Successful nerve transfers may be perceived as a more “natural” function compared with tendon transfers. While nerve transfers may require less postoperative immobilization, this must be balanced with the substantially quicker return of function via tendon transfer. A complete discussion of proximal nerve transfers for brachial plexus injuries is found in Chapter 34.

The concept of nerve transfers has been incorporated into the treatment of irreparable nerve injuries for several decades. What is new, however, is the increasing utilization of nerve transfers to restore forearm, wrist, and hand function (so- called distal nerve transfers) even when conventional nerve grafting or repair is still possible. This represents a focus toward functional rather than anatomic restoration. The rationale behind this paradigm shift seems attractive as one looks at the suboptimal results reported with conventional repair of major peripheral nerve injuries in the proximal arm. Provided the donor deficit is acceptable, nerve transfers can be performed closer to the end target, shortening the regeneration length and time. Grafts are typically avoided, so there is only one suture line. When a pure motor or sensory donor nerve can be matched with a similar recipient, end organ reinnervation is optimized. Additionally, nerve transfers can be performed outside the zone of injury, so as to avoid scarred tissue beds and challenging surgical dissections.

Lengthening the transfer with a nerve graft or using a compromised donor nerve will predictably diminish the result. As with any other surgical procedure, some transfers are better than others, and the decision to incorporate nerve transfers into the reconstructive algorithm must be weighed against the alternatives. Synergism becomes less of an issue when there are few other reconstructive options. For example, the supinator motor branch has been successfully transferred to the anterior interosseous nerve, the pronator teres motor nerve, and the posterior interosseus nerve. Depending on the situation, conventional nerve techniques or tendon transfers may still be the preferred approach, though a combination of different reconstructive strategies is often necessary to optimize the outcome.

#### Specific Distal Nerve Transfers

**Principles and perioperative considerations.** A benefit of nerve transfers, in contrast to tendon transfers, is that the native muscle is reinnervated, and theoretically, the native length- tension relationship can be reestablished. Further, joint stiffness does not preclude the nerve transfer, and the muscles and joints can be mobilized with therapy during the reinnervation period. The identification of functioning and expendable donor nerves that are close to the end target is the critical first step of distal nerve transfer, and confirmatory physical findings and electrodiagnostic studies are helpful to assess the health of the donor nerve. The donor muscle must have at least MRC grade 4 strength with electromyography demonstrating normal or only minimally reduced motor unit potentials. Additionally, the axon counts of the donor nerve must ideally approach that of the recipient, though transfers with axon count ratios as low as 1:3 have been successfully utilized.

During surgery, the anesthesiologist should avoid using long- acting depolarizing agents, which might interfere with intraoperative nerve stimulation. Tourniquets should be used for brief periods or not at all, as ischemia can also confound the intraoperative stimulation of the recipient and donor nerves.

The donor and recipient fascicles or nerve branches should be harvested in a way to eliminate tension on the repair as demonstrable through a full arc of motion. Nerve coaptations should be performed under the microscope and with 9- 0 or 10- 0 microsuture. The use of fibrin glue or coaptation devices are left to surgeon preference but can be particularly helpful when trying to transfer very small nerves. Release of distal potential compression points (such as the carpal tunnel or Guyon canal) may improve axon regeneration, though this has been difficult to validate.

Motor and sensory reeducation are instituted early after nerve transfers. For motor nerve transfers, cocontraction and strengthening of the donor muscles is beneficial for patterning behavior. Rehabilitative strategies include bilateral motion exercises using cocontraction of the donor muscle and the desired function, that is, wrist flexion and finger extension for a median nerve branch (flexor carpi radialis) to posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) transfer. Once palpable contractions are felt in the recipient muscle, therapy visits are increased, and exercises are focused on increasing strength and mobility.

#### Nerve Transfers to Restore Radial Nerve Function

High radial nerve injuries result in loss of wrist and finger extension, diminished grip strength, and compromised hand function. Tendon transfers are effective for restoring function for these injuries; however, nerve transfers have the potential to restore individual native muscle function, which includes independent finger extension. The obvious disadvantage of nerve transfers is the time required for nerve regeneration and muscle reinnervation. Evidence of reinnervation after radial nerve transfers is usually observed by 9 months, and continued motor reeducation and strengthening are required thereafter to maximize functional recovery.

**Median to radial nerve transfers.** Conceptualized in a way that capitalizes on tenodesis and synergism, the standard transfers to address functional loss in radial nerve palsy include coaptation of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) motor nerve to the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) motor nerve to restore wrist extension, and the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) motor branch to the PIN to restore finger and thumb extension. Sukegawa demonstrated in a cadaver model that these transfers have the necessary “swing distance” to easily reach the target nerve. Average axon counts for the FDS branch (885 axons) are similar to the average axon count for the ECRB (548 axons), though a large discrepancy exists between the FCR branch (1501 axons) and the PIN (5162 axons), suggesting that the observed clinical success may be related to collateral sprouting.

The decision to proceed with median to radial nerve transfers is based on frank discussions with the patient and must include consideration of time to recovery, occupation, access to hand therapy, and injury pattern. The median and ulnar nerves must be intact to prevent finger or wrist dysfunction.

Outcomes. Many have reported success with the median to radial nerve transfers; however, reports are limited to small patient series. In 2011 Ray and Mackinnon reported on the largest series of 19 patients. Though 9 patients underwent simultaneous pronator teres (PT) to the side of ECRB tendon transfer, 18 recovered MRC grade 4 wrist extension strength. For finger and thumb extension, 12 recovered MRC grade 4 or better results, 2 had MRC grade 3 strength, and 5 had MRC grade 0 to 2 recovery. Ukrit et al. reported MRC grade 4 wrist extension strength after FDS to ECRB nerve transfer. Garcia- Lopez et al. transferred a branch of PT to extensor carpi radialis longus and FCR to PIN in 6 patients, and reported M3 or greater wrist extension, thumb extension, and finger extension at 20 months after surgery.Bertelli, in a clever modification, flipped the distal anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) (after the flexor pollicis longus [FPL] motor branch) proximally to reach the ECRB motor branch and combined this with a more conventional FCR motor branch to the PIN transfer. Out of 14 patients, all had functional wrist extension (13 with MRC, 4 and 1 with MRC 3) and 12 had functional finger extension (with 2 failures). Though every patient in a matched cohort of tendon transfer recipients achieved functional wrist and finger extension, average wrist extension was 35 degrees (versus 67 degrees in nerve transfer group) and finger extension frequently required a tenodesis effect of wrist flexion, which was not necessary in the successful nerve transfers.

Technique. A 15- cm curvilinear incision is made over the proximal radial forearm (Fig. 30.21A). The cephalic vein and, just medially, the accompanied lateral antebrachial cutaneous (LABC) nerve are identified and preserved. The muscle fascia is incised and the radial vessels and brachioradialis (BR) muscle are identified. The BR is elevated and the radial superficial sensory nerve (RSN) is identified. The superficial sensory nerve branch is followed proximally under the BR, and just dorsal/lateral to the RSN, branches to the ECRB are identified adjacent to the muscle belly. The ECRB usually has two branches, and the longer distal branch is encountered first. The dissection then continues proximally to identify the other branches of the radial nerve (the PIN and then supinator branches). The supinator branch often arises more posterior and lateral to the PIN (see Fig. 30.21B). During this dissection, “leash of Henry” vessels must be ligated and the tendon of the ECRB incised. A handheld nerve stimulator set at 2 mA is used to confirm complete denervation. This is an important last step before transecting these recipient nerves. Microdissection of the ECRB branches away from the parent nerve proximally will ensure as much length as possible to facilitate a tension- free coaptation.

#### Nerve Transfers to Restore Median Nerve Function

The median nerve supplies several critical hand functions including sensation to the radial three and a half digits, forearm pronation, thumb opposition, and wrist, finger, and thumb flexion. The ulnar nerve provides innervation to wrist and ring- little finger flexors; consequently, if the ulnar nerve is intact, these functions do not need to be addressed with high median nerve injuries. Middle finger flexion and pronation loss are more controversial. While pronation is a critical function for independent activities of daily living, including feeding and personal hygiene, Bertelli et al. found that 10 out of 11 patients with high median nerve deficits retained an average 52 degrees pronation with M4 strength. All 11 patients maintained M5 middle finger flexion strength though a full arc of motion and the majority maintained acceptable thenar control. Innervation overlap and intramuscular connections explains preservation of middle finger function. The BR, the ulnar FDP, and the extensor carpi ulnaris have all been identified to compensate for pronator denervation, though shoulder abduction and gravity most certainly have some role as well.

Thus, the most important motor transfer for high median nerve loss is to restore function of the AIN, as well as restoration of sensation. In the case of an isolated AIN palsy, the radial nerve branch to the ECRB offers a strong and synergistic transfer to the AIN. This transfer restores tip- to- tip pinch with very simple motor reeducation. Bertelli reported on four patients who received an ECRB to AIN nerve transfer. At 13 months, the patients achieved MRC grade 4 finger and thumb strength with full range of motion. Hsiao et al. reported MRC grade 4 FPL strength and grade 4 index finger FDP strength following supinator branch to AIN transfer in a single patient. Murphy et al. performed a combined transfer of the radial nerve branches to ERCB and the supinator branches to the AIN and demonstrated recovery of MRC grade 3 finger flexion after 1 year follow- up and improvement of pinch and grip strength at 4 years. Some return of AIN function was achieved by a transfer of the BR branch to the AIN in one patient. At 12 months postoperatively, the patient demonstrated MRC grade 3 FPL function and grade 2 index and middle finger FDP function. Any of these transfers can be combined with FDP side- to- side transfer to the ulnar innervated FDP tendons to improve flexion strength.

In the rare case when reconstruction of pronation is indicated, two isolated cases have been reported, using a transfer of an ECRB branch or flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) branch. In both cases, 4+/5 MRC grade strength was reported. These outcomes have not been replicated in other clinical series.

**Technique.** The approach to the radial to median nerve transfers is in the volar forearm through a 15-cm curvilinear incision. The exposure of the median and radial nerves is as described for the median to radial nerve transfers detailed above. Careful attention to adequate donor and recipient dissection is needed for a tension- free coaptation (Figs. 30.23 and 30.24).

#### Nerve Transfers to Restore Ulnar Nerve Function

Proximal ulnar nerve injuries devastate hand function, with loss of power grip, pinch strength, and hand dexterity. Although loss of FCU and FDP to the ring and small fingers occurs with ulnar nerve injury, their loss is either noncritical (FCR provides wrist flexion) or effectively treated with a tendon transfer (side- to- side tenodesis transfer of the ulnar FDP tendons to the median FDP). The intrinsic muscles, in contrast, are much harder to replace with tendon transfers, and their loss leaves a profound functional deficit—loss of digital dexterity, inability to grip with the ring and little fingers (due to pseudoclawing), inability to “cup” the palm, and loss of pinch strength. For these reasons, the distal AIN to motor branch of ulnar nerve transfer is a common and useful distal nerve transfer.

The AIN is harvested distal to the FPL motor branch and causes no donor morbidity. Loss of pronator quadratus function is negligible in patients with a functioning PT and tolerable even in those without (due to compensation via shoulder positioning). The transfer is performed 8 to 12 cm above the wrist crease in close proximity to the intrinsic muscle targets. However, expectations need to be tempered when performing this nerve transfer. It is important to understand that there is no real synergism between the AIN and the motor branch of the ulnar nerve, especially given the fact that the AIN innervates only a single muscle and is being transferred to a nerve that innervates nearly a dozen intrinsic muscles with differing functions. Also, reports differ in their estimates of AIN and ulnar nerve motor branch axon counts (the ratio ranges from 1:2 to 1:4.8), and the AIN is often smaller in diameter than the motor branch of the ulnar nerve, resulting in a technically challenging coaptation.

Regardless, the transfer has been reported to restore grip strength, lateral pinch, and intrinsic balance. Novak and Mackinnon reported 8 out of 8 patients (mean time from injury to surgery 3 months) with functional improvement. One patient required a subsequent tendon transfer, and no patients experienced donor morbidity from the AIN harvest. Battiston and Lanzetta reported 6 out of 7 patients (surgery performed by 6 months postinjury) recovering M4 or better intrinsic strength, and Sallam et al. reported 20 out of 24 patients (mean time from injury 8.6 months) regained functional (defined as M3 or better) intrinsic function (Fig. 30.25).

Though these branches are very small, Bertelli et al. reported no loss of thenar function and return of first dorsal interosseus and adductor pollicis muscle bulk and normal pinch strength (based on a modified pinch test).

**Technique: AIN to ulnar motor.** The AIN to ulnar motor nerve transfer is performed in the distal forearm. The hook of the hamate should be identified by deep palpation, and the incision begins just ulnar and distal to this landmark. The incision is carried proximally, angled across the wrist flexion crease, and extended to approximately the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the forearm (12 to 15 cm proximal to the wrist) (Fig. 30.26A). The ulnar nerve can be identified either in Guyon canal distally or proximally in the forearm where it runs between the FCU and the ulnar artery. Guyon canal is released and the motor branch of the ulnar nerve identified as it dives deep and around the hook of the hamate (see Fig. 30.26B). Typically, there are tight facial bands over the motor branch whose release not only improves access to the nerve but also relieves any potential compression points.

Proximal to this take- off point, the ulnar nerve consists of three fascicular groupings. The motor component is sandwiched between the dorsal sensory branch and the sensory component of the ulnar nerve. This anatomy is sufficiently consistent to reliably identify the motor branch; however, confirmation is relatively straightforward and can be accomplished by tracing out the motor branch in a “virtual dissection” after identification at the hamate hook. With a little practice, the motor component can be visually tracked from distal to proximal with minimal intraneural dissection. A vessel loop is placed around the isolated motor branch (see Fig. 30.26C).

The flexor tendons and median nerve are then retracted in a radial direction and the pronator quadratus is exposed. The distal AIN and accompanying anterior interosseus artery are identified as they enter the pronator quadratus (see Fig. 30.26D). The nerve and artery are carefully separated, and the nerve is mobilized proximally for several centimeters and then dissected into the muscle distally. The overlying muscle is incised or cauterized with a bipolar, and the AIN is exposed until it starts to arborize (usually about halfway through the pronator quadratus) (see Fig. 30.26E). The nerve is sharply incised at this level and transposed toward the ulnar nerve. The motor component of the ulnar nerve is then identified at the site at which the AIN meets the ulnar nerve without tension (see Fig. 30.26F). In high ulnar nerve palsies, a side- to- side flexor digitorum profundus transfer of the ring and little to the median- innervated FDP tendons is performed at this time.

The distal AIN is small and requires the use of the operative microscope. The multiple nerve branches from the pronator quadratus (PQ) muscle are tiny and can be bundled together with fibrin glue before coapting to the ulnar motor branch. The neurorrhaphy is typically performed with two or three 9- 0 or 10- 0 nylon stitches placed so that the ends are barely touching but well aligned. If used, a nerve cuff can then be slid over the nerve coaptation site and maintained in position with a small amount of fibrin glue.

Bertelli’s OPB to terminal ulnar nerve transfer can be performed though a Brunner incision between the carpal tunnel and thenar muscles. The median nerve is identified proximally and the thenar motor branch isolated. The OPB is identified deep to the abductor pollicis brevis muscle and dissected (under microscope visualization) into the muscle belly to maximize length. Ulnar to the index flexor tendons, the adductor pollicis brevis muscle is noted and the plane between the oblique and transverse head (identified by a white tendinous line) is deepened down to the index metacarpal. The terminal ulnar nerve branch is dissected proximally to maximize length and the transfer is completed using 10- 0 microsutures (and a small amount of fibrin glue).

**“Supercharge” reverse end- to- side nerve transfer.** In 2005 the reverse end- to- side nerve transfer was conceptualized by the author as a strategy for augmenting axonal regeneration in a damaged or compromised nerve. The classically described “end- to- side” nerve transfer involves coapting an injured or denervated distal nerve stump to an epineural defect in the side of an intact donor nerve, theoretically to allow for axonal sprouting into the injured nerve from the intact nerve. Though animal and human data is inconsistent and unreliable, a primary appeal behind the development of this transfer strategy was that the donor nerve function was not affected. In contrast, the “reverse end- to- side” nerve transfer reverses this relationship: The donor nerve is transected and its “active” axons are sutured into an epineural or perineural defect of an intact but injured recipient nerve (Fig. 30.27). The added axons “supercharge” the injured nerve without sacrificing regenerating in situ axons. The clinical applications include enhancing recovery in a partially injured nerve (including severe proximal entrapment neuropathy) or a repaired nerve with incomplete regeneration.

![[Fig.30.27.png]]
> **Fig. 30.27** Schematic of end-to-end nerve transfer and supercharge reverse end-to-side coaptations. (Redrawn from Isaacs JE, Cheatham S, Gagnon EB, Razavi A, McDowell CL. Reverse end-to-side neurotization in a regenerating nerve. J Reconstr Microsurg. 2008;24[7]:489–496.)

A series of animal experiments confirmed the physiologic potential of this strategy. The author demonstrated in a rodent model that donor axons would enter the side of a proximally transected recipient nerve and achieve distal muscle reinnervation. Follow-up small animal studies further established that a reverse end- to- side nerve repair could complement the regenerating axonal pool from a proximally repaired nerve and introduced the more commonly accepted descriptive term “supercharging” to describe this transfer. In attempts to replicate more clinically relevant scenarios, Farber et al. and Nadi et al. “supercharged” rodent nerves in conjunction with either an isograft reconstruction or a proximal crush- induced neuroma- in- continuity.Both groups noted supercharging enhanced distal axonal regeneration. However, while Farber et al. noted improved muscle recovery, Nadi et al. did not demonstrate functional benefit and noted that the donor axons seemed to dominate the regenerative pool, suggesting that the donor axons may have obstructed in situ axonal regeneration.

Translation to clinical application at this point has focused on the AIN nerve transfer to motor component of the ulnar nerve in cases of severe cubital tunnel syndrome or in conjunction with more proximal ulnar nerve repair. Davidge et al. recently reported on 55 patients treated with an AIN supercharge nerve transfer for a variety of diagnoses. Particularly in patients with injuries- in- continuity (severe cubital tunnel syndrome), significant improvement in first dorsal interosseous strength, key pinch, grip strength, and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire scores were reported. The authors acknowledged that it was difficult to determine the degree of recovery attributable to the supercharge transfer, and the functional improvement reported could be attributed to the nerve decompression, regeneration of the injured axons, the supercharge nerve transfer, or a combination. NCS that demonstrated larger amplitude response in the intrinsic muscles with median nerve stimulation versus proximal ulnar nerve stimulation and clinical recovery that was temporally compatible with donor axon regeneration were encouraging but not definitive.In contrast, Baltzer et al. were unable to demonstrate benefit associated with supercharging coincident with cubital tunnel release. However, both they and Koriem et al. reported improved intrinsic recovery with supercharging plus proximal ulnar nerve repair compared with proximal ulnar nerve repair alone in 6 out of 7 and 10 out of 11 patients, respectively.

Claims that supercharging can “babysit” and protect distal muscle from denervation atrophy while proximal axons regenerate are, at this time, not supported by either bench or clinical data. Individual muscle fibers cannot be dually innervated, and early reinnervation of a subset of muscle fibers within a denervated muscle belly will not preserve the uninnervated muscle fibers. Populations of degenerated muscle fibers coexisting with reinnervated muscle fibers will continue to develop irreversible denervation atrophy. Though “supercharging” axons may stimulate the neurotrophic environment in the distal nerve stump, the clinical benefit of this effect has not been demonstrated.

As evidenced by the popular acceptance of distal nerve transfers, the rapid reinnervation of target muscles with fresh motor axons appears to be an efficacious strategy for dealing with significant distal nerve injuries. Whether intrinsic muscle recovery is achieved by proximal repair plus supercharging or direct end- to- end distal nerve transfer has not been determined and may best be considered on a case- by- case assessment.

Technique. The approach and isolation of the motor component of the distal ulnar nerve and the AIN is the same as for the end- to- end transfer described above; however, in contrast to the end- to- end nerve transfer, the ulnar motor branch is not transected. Once the AIN has been mobilized and transected at the level of arborization, the nerve is transposed toward the ulnar nerve. The Reverse End to Side (RETS) transfer will be performed more proximally than the end- to- end transfer (since the motor branch cannot be transposed toward the AIN) (Fig. 30.28). An epineurial window in the motor branch of the ulnar nerve will expose two to three fascicles. Though the perineurium is carefully opened (as evidenced by bulging through the perineurial windows), the AIN is sutured to the epineurium with typically three or four 10- 0 nylon sutures under microscope magnification. Fibrin glue may be utilized to augment the repair at the surgeon’s discretion. Particularly when supercharging is being performed with an acute proximal ulnar nerve repair, the ulnar aspect of the FDP has not atrophied and the muscle bulk may potentially disrupt the nerve transfer (upon removal of the surgical retractor or with passive wrist and finger motion).

**Sensory nerve transfers.** Although motor recovery is more tangible and easier to objectively measure, the importance of sensory recovery, especially in critical zones such as the borders of the first web space, cannot be overstated. Severe sensory deficits lead to further loss of dexterity, less incorporation of that extremity into activities of daily living, and, in the most severe cases, fingertip ulceration and tissue loss. Additionally, evidence suggests that restoration of even rudimentary sensation can blunt neuropathic pain.

Unlike motor recovery in which nerve regeneration is rigidly time sensitive, the recovery window of sensation is more flexible. Like motor transfers, a less critical sensory nerve is sacrificed for a more critical sensory function. However, unlike motor transfers, sensory nerve transfers are not intuitively adapted and there are no “synergistic” sensory transfers. At least initially, sensation is often “felt” in the distribution of the donor nerve even with stimulus to the distribution of the recipient nerve. This may be less pronounced when using donor nerves in line with the recipient nerve’s distribution.

Though often well tolerated, sensory transfers have donor deficits, which can lead to dysesthesias and neuropathic pain. Therefore, the indications for distal sensory nerve transfer are narrower than that of motor nerves, and sensory restoration is often sought via more conventional tactics. A common nerve reconstruction strategy might consist of proximal sensory nerve repair at the injury site and distal motor nerve transfer closer to the end target. Sensory recovery may be delayed but will not require the same level of cortical adaption associated with sensory nerve transfer. Sensory nerve transfers are still useful when the proximal stump of the injured nerve is missing or inaccessible.

Sensory reeducation and postoperative hand therapy are important for adapting and maximizing function after sensory nerve transfers. Sensory transfers benefit from sensory retraining and time. Rehabilitative efforts can be increased following the first signs of sensory recovery and generally consist of applying repetitive stimuli with visual confirmation and reinforcement.

Ozkan et al. offers the largest recent outcomes data on 25 distal digital nerve transfers (from noncritical sensory digital nerves and branches of superficial radial nerve). 2PD was achieved in 15 out of 25 hands and 90% of patients reported subjective improvement. Bertelli and Ghizoni reported return of protective sensation (or better) to the fingertips of eight patients after transfer of distal branches of the radial sensory nerve to the ulnar digital nerve of the thumb and radial digital nerve of the index. A few cases have been described in which some sensation has been restored in the median nerve distribution by transferring the superficial branch of the radial nerve to the median nerve at the level of the wrist, but insufficient data exists to support these transfers clinically.

**Digital nerve transfer.** Sensation of the first web space and the ulnar border of the small finger and hand are considered critical sensation areas. The first web space innervation can be achieved by transfer of the proper digital nerves from the fourth web space (the radial side of the little finger and the ulnar side of the ring finger) to the radial digital nerve of the index and the ulnar digital nerve of the thumb, respectively. The procedure is technically straightforward, and while not intended to restore sensation throughout the anesthetic area of the hand, adequately addresses the critical area of the first web space with minimal donor site morbidity. The primary disadvantage is incomplete cortical reorientation of localization. Patients report either dual sensation or persistent sensation in the donor territory with stimulus to the thumb or index.

Technique. Under tourniquet control, an incision is made over the ulnar aspect of the carpal tunnel and extended as two Bruner incisions: one distally up the fourth web space and one radially toward the base of the thumb. After dissection through skin and palmar fascia, the transverse carpal ligament is divided to expose the median nerve and its branches. The septum between the carpal tunnel and Guyon canal is released to expose the ulnar neurovascular bundle. The ulnar nerve is followed distally and the sensory branches to the ulnar side of the little finger and the common digital nerve to the fourth web space are isolated (Fig. 30.29A). The common digital to the fourth web space is traced distally to the level of the bifurcation, the two proper digital nerves are divided as distally as possible, and the nerve is mobilized for transposition across the palm. In the carpal tunnel, the proper digital nerves to the ulnar side of the thumb and the radial side of the index are identified. They may need to be followed distally for a few centimeters to ensure correct identification. The nerves are transected proximally at the recipient and distally at the donor to allow for a tension- free coaptation (see Fig. 30.29B).

With careful dissection in the dorsal aspect of the incisions, the “donor” dorsal sensory branches of the radial nerve to the index finger and thumb are identified. **End- to- side nerve transfers.** An end- to- side nerve transfer involves suturing a distal denervated nerve stump into an epineurial (or perineurial) window of a donor nerve. The goal is for axons to emerge from the donor nerve, enter the denervated stump, and grow toward the target motor endplates or sensory receptors. Descriptions and clinical application of the end- to- side nerve transfer preceded the reverse (or supercharge) end- to- side transfer by several decades. The primary indications, including the lack of a suitable proximal stump or excessive regeneration length, are similar to those for a standard end- to- end nerve transfer. The rationale for end- to- side transfer is generally that no expendable donor nerve is available, or the loss of donor function is greater than the anticipated increased function to be gained by the transfer. A theoretical advantage of an end- to- side nerve transfer is that reinnervation can be restored without sacrificing donor nerve function.

Though an exciting concept, the animal and human literature on this transfer is inconsistent, making definitive conclusions difficult. The most supportive data for end- to- side nerve transfer comes from the small animal model. Nerve histology in this model has demonstrated regenerating axons in the distal stump, while backlabeling techniques have confirmed their donor nerve “origin.”The indication for end- to- side nerve transfers is narrow, and until more consistently supportive clinical studies are published, their use should be limited. The best clinical results have been demonstrated in digital nerves. Artiaco et al. documented 22 of 24 patients with S3+ or better sensation and a mean of 9.7 mm 2PD without a donor deficit.

Technique. The technical aspects of the end- to- side nerve transfer are straightforward. An epineurial window is made by creating a longitudinal incision through the epineurium just long enough to accommodate the cross- section of the nerve being transferred. The window is “teased” open and the perineurium opened while trying to avoid fascicular or axonal injury. Microsutures are placed through the backwall first followed by proximal and distal stitches. Finally, the anterior stitch is placed. Additional stitches are placed as needed or fibrin glue can be applied to support the repair.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: FOREARM NERVE INJURIES

Based on superior and more predictable clinical results, quicker reinnervation, and less stressful surgical dissections (especially with scarred and traumatized surgical beds), nerve transfers have taken a much more prominent position in the treatment algorithm even when conventional nerve repairs and reconstructions are still feasible. Like any surgical decision, the risks and benefits must be weighed relative to alternatives.

For high radial nerve injuries, especially with a large or poorly defined zone of injury, both nerve and tendon transfers offer more predictable and appealing treatment options (than conventional grafting). In comparing the two, the potential for superior outcomes (more independent function, more natural and fluid motion, and less reliance on tenodesis effect) of nerve transfers must be weighed against the fact that some nerve transfer patients will not regain any function at all. Unlike failed radial nerve repair, tendon transfer options following failed nerve transfer are limited—the procedure denervates potential donor muscles. This must be recognized as a high- risk, high- reward surgical strategy. Certainly, some patients will choose this route, but all patients must be accurately and fairly appraised of their options.

Motor nerve transfers for isolated high median nerve are lower risk but also lower reward. Loss of PT innervation does not appear to be a major functional deficit and compensatory mechanisms include the BR, the ECU, and the shoulder. Loss of thumb and finger flexion, which can be restored by nerve transfer to the AIN, can also be effectively corrected utilizing side- to- side FDP tendon transfers and using BR to FPL tendon transfer. Thumb opposition can be predictably reproduced using a variety of tendon transfers. In the author’s experience, however, sensory recovery with large gaps or challenging median nerve reconstructions is best achieved with nerve transfer with a preference to the superficial radial nerve to border digital nerves of the first web space due to technical straightforwardness and “in line” sensory overlap.

The most impactful distal nerve transfer, in the author’s practice, is the AIN to motor branch of ulnar nerve, which has reasonable odds of functional recovery if done acutely following a high ulnar nerve injury. Pinch strength, however, is often a persistent disability, and while Bertelli’s opponens pollicis to adductor pollicis transfer is only recently described, the early results and concept are compelling. The more difficult decision, reverse end- to- side “supercharging” versus classic AIN end- to- end nerve transfer, should be based on an understanding of the pros and cons of each strategy. Intuitively, more AIN axons will be directed toward the intrinsic muscles with end- to- end transfer than with supercharging. The “single function” axons destined for the PQ are being asked to control multiple intricate muscles directing complex hand motions, and there may not be enough axons to reinnervate especially the muscles on the radial aspect of the hand. However, the literature documents consistently positive results, and the author’s experience confirms the potential for excellent functional outcomes with end- to- end transfer.

For supercharging to offer a prognostic advantage, the net number of axons (i.e., supercharging plus in situ regenerating ulnar nerve axons) reinnervating (not just reaching) the intrinsic muscles must be greater. There may be an inherent disadvantage to dual sourced axons, and the axons (specifically the ulnar nerve derived axons) must reach the muscle fibers before irreversible denervation atrophy precludes functional recovery. The concept of supercharged “babysitting” to delay muscle atrophy and prolong reinnervation times, while attractive, cannot be clinically or scientifically endorsed at this time.

A pragmatic approach to ulnar nerve injuries should entail an operative assessment of the potential for ulnar nerve axons, end- to- end AIN axons, and supercharging axons to achieve reinnervation. Two recent cases illustrate this concept. A young male with a sharp laceration to the ulnar nerve above the elbow was posted for proximal ulnar nerve grafting (for sensory recovery, FCU and FDP reinnervation, and neuroma control) and end- to- end AIN to ulnar motor transfer for intrinsic reinnervation. At surgery, with nerve mobilization, a direct ulnar nerve repair was achieved, improving the prognosis of ulnar nerve axon regeneration. Distally, the AIN was atypically small, which predictably decreases the axon count from that source and creates an unexpected technical challenge. The operative plan was changed, and a supercharging nerve transfer was performed instead of an end- to- end AIN transfer. A second patient in a slightly older age group presented with an abrasion/laceration to his posterior medial elbow and was posted for nerve mobilization and repair, with supercharging transfer at the wrist. At surgery, the zone of injury was much greater than anticipated and even with mobilization required a 7-cm cable graft. The AIN was large, and with the degraded prognosis for in situ ulnar nerve regeneration, the decision was made to perform an end- to- end transfer to the ulnar motor nerve in the forearm.

## NEUROMAS

All unrepaired proximal nerve stumps and most nerve repairs develop some degree of neuroma formation due to misdirected axons entrapped within connective tissue. However, not all neuromas are symptomatic, and for those that are, the intensity of neuropathic pain does not always corelate with either the importance or the appearance of the involved nerve. Neuroma pain can be grossly divided into either mechanical neuropathic pain or deafferentation pain. The latter is a form of central nervous system imprinting (like phantom pain) and is more difficult to treat. Focal pain to palpation at the site of nerve injury, “electric shocks” with tapping (Tinel sign), and temporary relief with local anesthetic injection all support the diagnosis of neuroma. Constant burning pain and severe hypersensitivity with allodynia in the distribution of the involved nerve are more compatible with deafferentation pain.

Conservative treatment of neuroma pain should consist of medical management, including pain- modulating medications, behavior modification, therapy, and modalities (including protective garments, desensitization, massage, nerve gliding, and TENS). Local corticosteroid injections have been described, though atrophy of local adipose tissue can exacerbate neuroma pain.

The best treatment for a symptomatic neuroma is prevention. To this end, even nonessential sensory nerves (i.e., medial antebrachial cutaneous, the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve, and the superficial radial nerve) are repaired whenever possible. For established neuromas there are multiple surgical treatment strategies depending on the severity and character of symptoms, anatomic location, and chronicity. The best strategy is to excise the neuroma and give the axons an innervation target. This helps to avoid neuroma regrowth, and also restores some degree of central nervous system connectivity. For a small- diameter neuroma- in- continuity, the neuroma may be excised and the nerve ends mobilized and primarily repaired. For small gaps, the freshened proximal stump can be repaired via graft, conduit, or ANA to the distal nerve stump. If the distal nerve is unavailable, the freshened nerve end can be sewn in a reverse end- to- side (supercharging) fashion to a nearby nerve trunk. Guse and Moran in a 25year retrospective review of the Mayo Clinic experience, reported that nerve reconstruction was the most consistently effective treatment for neuroma pain. Partial nerve injuries can also form partial neuromas in which part of the nerve is functional and another part either nonfunctional or painful. Though technically challenging, under magnification, the functioning fascicles can be separated and preserved, while the injured fascicles are resected and reconstructed.

When not repairable and not associated with neuropathic pain, simple surgical excision and proximal transposition to a less vulnerable location are often effective. By mobilizing and applying traction on the nerve stalk, resecting the neuroma, and letting the nerve stump retract into deeper tissue, the regenerating neuroma will be better cushioned and protected. More intensive surgical methods include open exposure and formal repositioning of the nerve stump into a deeper anatomic location such as within a muscle belly or within a medullary canal.

Another strategy is to modify the microenvironment around the nerve stump to inhibit or limit neuroma formation. Mackinnon et al. demonstrated in a primate model that a nerve stump placed within an innervated muscle belly formed a smaller and less-dense neuroma.Splitting the nerve and suturing the split ends to each other, suturing two cut proximal nerve stumps (i.e., digital nerves) to each other, or repairing the cut nerve end to a conduit, vein, or long ANA to create neurotrophic deficient local environments have all been described.Though the historical use of silicone “nerve caps” to block axonal outgrowth never gained popularity, contemporary designs utilizing absorbable materials with “dead end” pouches or split have been touted as more effective in inhibiting neuroma formation (Fig. 30.31).

Recently, the concept of targeted muscle reinnervation has rapidly gained popularity due to its simplicity and success. In contrast to burying the nerve end in muscle, targeted muscle reinnervation involves identifying and dividing a motor branch of a neighboring functional muscle and performing a nerve transfer of the prepared nerve stump to the motor branch near its entry point into the muscle. The axons from the nerve stump grow into and reinnervate the muscle. First described as a surgical strategy to provide independent motor signals in proximal muscles to control sophisticated multichannel myoelectric prostheses in amputees, Dumanian and others quickly recognized a profound reduction in neuropathic pain in this patient population. The results are better if performed as part of the initial amputation procedure or soon after neuropathic pain begins, highlighting the treatment challenges if central nervous system imprinting has already occurred.

In a rabbit model, resection of preformed median, radial, and ulnar nerve neuromas with subsequent transfer to segmental motor branches of a pedicled rectus abdominus flap in a rabbit model demonstrated efficacy in preventing new neuroma formation. The substantial size mismatch that may be encountered clinically when transferring a large mixed motor nerve to a much smaller motor nerve branch can be addressed by suturing a rim of muscle around the coaptation so that axons not directed into the recipient nerve stump will instead directly neurotize the freshly denervated muscle. Extrapolating from this concept, slips of vascularized (denervated) muscle or small, nonvascularized free muscle grafts can also be wrapped around the nerve stump after neuroma excision. The denervated piece of muscle, which survives by diffusion until neovascularization, offers a similar target for axonal ingrowth.

### Technique: Target Muscle Reinnervation

The first step is to identify an expendable and functioning or recently denervated muscle in the proximity of the neuroma. Use of a portable nerve stimulator is ideal for identifying nearby motor branches and motor points as candidates for transfer. In the distal forearm, the PQ or FDS are two examples of expendable muscles; the FDS has multiple motor branches. In the arm, one of the heads of the biceps, one branch of the brachialis, or one head of the triceps can be denervated with minimal morbidity. When performed in amputees (or at time of amputation), nonfunctioning (but innervated) muscles can be utilized. The neuroma is resected, and the proximal nerve stump mobilized for transfer. A motor branch of the selected muscle is identified with the nerve stimulator, mobilized, and divided within a few millimeters from its entry into muscle. Sufficient length of the recipient nerve is preserved to allow a tension- free repair to the nerve stump. The repair can be done even with substantial size mismatch and generally does not require the operative microscope. 6- 0 or 7- 0 sutures complete the transfer, and fibrin glue, a nerve cuff, or a cuff of muscle can be used to augment the repair (Fig. 30.32).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THE PAINFUL NEUROMA

Many neuromas can be managed nonoperatively. Patients are counseled that the damaged nerves continue to carry electric signals and that minor trauma or irritation of to the damaged nerve sends signals that the brain interprets as “painful.” Like learning a new language, the brain can interpret signals in different ways, and desensitization therapy is a way to “teach” the brain to interpret these signals as “not painful.” Surgical treatment is reserved for the recalcitrant mechanical neuroma or persistent neuropathic pain. Patients with focal neuroma pain (especially with mechanical stimulation) have the best prognosis with surgical intervention, and several of the multiple surgical strategies described will be successful. Simpler techniques tend to be more efficient and less morbid; typically, the author either buries the resected nerve end in a deep muscle belly or, when not anatomically feasible, applies a commercially available nerve cap.

Patients with chronic pain and severe allodynia unresponsive to medication have a worse prognosis and require a multifaceted approach, often in conjunction with a pain management specialist. Surgical strategies, such as nerve reconstruction (if a distal target is available), reverse end- to- side nerve transfer, or targeted muscle reinnervation, focus on providing a target for the symptomatic axons. The patients are counseled that once the axons have regenerated, these more recognizable nerve signals will be easier for the brain to interpret as not painful. Intensive pain control is necessary with these surgeries, and long- acting regional anesthesia with sympathetic blockade, indwelling catheters, and portable reservoirs is utilized as needed.

## REVISION NERVE SURGERY

Clinical outcomes of nerve repair are not always satisfactory, and definitive recommendations on how to deal with this difficult scenario are lacking. Surgical options can include revision nerve surgery, nerve transfers, tendons transfers, and innervated local or free tissue transfer; though, depending on the functional deficit, strategies to compensate and effectively manage the deficit can be sufficient. Lack of recovery in itself is not an indication for another surgery. If further surgical intervention is considered, the emotional and economic impact of this decision needs to be carefully weighed against the potential benefits. The patient’s goals and expectations must be well aligned with a realistic prognosis.

Many factors have been linked to clinical outcome and include patient age, mechanism of injury, size of nerve gap, proximodistal level of injury, and the chronicity of the injury. Before recommending revision nerve repair, a correctable problem should be identified. Inadequate resection of the zone of injury, poor fascicular alignment, and secondary scar tissue entrapment are all correctable issues, while advanced muscle atrophy and nerve denervation are not. This can be challenging since few surgeons admit technical concerns or record them in the operative note. “Red flags” that the initial repair quality may have been suboptimal could include repair by a nonnerve (or hand) surgeon, unorthodox technique (large caliber suture), or stretched indications (large defect bridged with a conduit); though most often, a definitive problem cannot be ascertained.

Though the appeal of correcting any previous technical error is obvious, there are many obstacles to success. Revision nerve repair risks sacrificing any regenerating axons that had or still might reach the end target. There is clinical data suggestive that recovery may continue well beyond the conventional expectant time frames. Almost all patients in one follow- up study had gained substantial strength and function in key muscle groups between years 2 and 11 post–brachial plexus repair. Additionally, redo repairs might not do as well as primary attempts.

Less robust and organized axonal regeneration compromised recovery in secondary nerve repairs in two rodent studies. Finally, even if denervation atrophy did not contribute to the initial failure, delays in initial treatment, expectant observation since treatment, and the anticipated length of time before reinnervation (if nerve reconstruction were technically successful) must all be considered. These elements may be less of a factor in sensory nerves. Yuksel et al. reported improved sensation but not motor function after revising 28 wrist- level nerve repairs.

Unfortunately, by the time clinical failure is recognized, the window of opportunity may have passed. NCS and EMG can detect signs of reinnervation (or lack of reinnervation) earlier than clinical exam, and to this end, their incorporation into routine post–nerve repair follow- up can be justified. A progressing Tinel sign is reassuring but not definitive evidence of axonal regeneration, and a strong, persistent Tinel at the site of the nerve repair is more concerning. Detailed sensory testing (sensory mapping by a therapist) and sharp/dull discrimination may be able to differentiate subjective dissatisfaction from true persistent anesthesia. Advanced imaging can be helpful if nerve repair rupture is suspected, though the significance of enlargement at the repair site is hard to interpret.

The goals of treatment should be well defined. Persistent motor loss may be better addressed with tendon transfers, loss of sensation with nerve or innervated soft tissue transfers, and patient dissatisfaction (despite objective findings of nerve regeneration) with reassurance and empathy. Neurolysis should be preserved for situations in which scar tissue is likely causing the problem. A degradation of previous recovered function or pain with passive stretch (indicative of traction neuritis) are two examples.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: REVISION SURGERY

While the goal of every primary nerve repair is to optimize the chance of recovery, in hindsight, some unexpected failures in the author’s practice can be likely attributed to technical or judgment errors. The selfprotective impulse of denial must be suppressed, and a logical analysis of the potential failure and options pursued. Typically, revision nerve reconstruction is only considered if a “red flag” has been identified, or if definite clinical failure has been confirmed (via EMG and sensory mapping) before the development of irreversible denervation atrophy. Usually this involves a repair close to the end targets (such as a median or ulnar nerve repair at the wrist). Rarely is a reexploration performed before 3 to 4 months post-repair. If axons are regenerating across the repair, this delay will allow enough axonal growth to be detectable using operative nerve- to- nerve conduction studies. While nerve transfers have become the author’s preferred reconstructive strategy in many primary situations, the inevitable time delay associated with revision nerve surgery makes this option (if available) even more appealing. Likewise, this may be the perfect application of a supercharging nerve transfer if successful axonal regeneration is suspected or still anticipated. Neurolysis and wrapping of the repair can be helpful when functional recovery is hindered by neuropathic pain, though this strategy is unpredictable at best, and the potential for iatrogenic deterioration must be considered. Prolonged expectant observation and medical management is preferred in most situations. Finally, unrealistic expectations and secondary gain are practically predictive of failure. While the author has many patients who have clearly benefited from revision, some situations cannot be helped by further surgery.
