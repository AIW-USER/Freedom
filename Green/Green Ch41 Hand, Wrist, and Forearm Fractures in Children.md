## HAND FRACTURES

Children are not small adults. Although many similar treatment principles and techniques are utilized, a number of special considerations are required in the treatment of hand fractures in skeletally immature patients. Examination of the injured hand can be challenging, particularly in an anxious or nonverbal child. Accurate diagnosis is dependent on clinical history, careful observation, and skilled physical examination. These challenges are compounded by the smaller size of the affected musculoskeletal structures and the abundant surrounding subcutaneous fat and soft tissue. In addition, the smaller size of the underlying skeletal structures necessitates the use of smaller implants placed with precision during surgical procedures. Although the robust healing potential of skeletal injuries in children reduces the risk of delayed union and nonunion compared with their adult counterparts, it also narrows the window of opportunity to correct deformities using closed means. Much of this robust healing potential arises from the thick and biologically active periosteum, which contributes to appositional bone growth, promotes remodeling, and provides a smooth gliding surface for overlying tendons.

The presence of the physis leads to a unique set of treatment considerations. In many situations, an initial bony deformity will remodel with continued skeletal growth. This remodeling potential is dependent on a number of factors, including the magnitude and pattern of bony deformity, proximity of the injury to the adjacent physis, and amount of remaining skeletal growth. In general, the greatest remodeling potential is seen with displacement in the plane of adjacent joint motion, in close proximity to the physis, and in young children with many years of remaining growth. Rotational deformity has little remodeling potential and will persist if not addressed acutely. Similarly, remodeling of radioulnar (coronal) angulation is limited and is dependent on the amount of adjacent radioulnar joint motion. Efforts to preserve physeal growth may also influence the choice of surgical techniques for operative injuries.

Because children demonstrate varying degrees of compliance following injury or postoperative care, more restrictive immobilization is required to maximize long- term outcomes. Fortunately, the use of longer and more restrictive immobilization following hand fractures in children is not associated with the stiffness seen in adults. Indeed, children are often their own best therapists and quickly resolve issues of stiffness and strength through daily activities of play and sports.

In addition to these considerations, a number of hand injuries are characteristically found in children. Awareness of these injuries and their treatment options is essential for the practicing hand surgeon.

The purpose of this section is to review the epidemiology, pertinent anatomy, and treatment principles for fractures of the child’s hand, with particular emphasis on pediatric injuries requiring surgical care.

### Epidemiology

Hand fractures are common in children and adolescents. In their analysis of the 1998 National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NHAMCS), Chung and Spilson reported on the epidemiology of hand and forearm fractures in the United States. Based on their four- stage probability model and subsequent extrapolation of the NHAMCS data, an estimated 1,465,874 hand or forearm fractures present to U.S. emergency departments annually. Phalangeal and metacarpal fractures represented 23% and 18% of these cases, respectively. Interestingly, 15% of all metacarpal fractures and 33% of all phalangeal fractures occurred in patients between 0 and 14 years of age. Furthermore, the highest rate of multiple hand fractures was seen in patients between 0 and 4 years of age (29.8%). The calculated incidence of phalangeal fractures in patients 0 to 4 years old was 207.2 per 100,000. The incidence of metacarpal and phalangeal fractures in patients 5 to 14 years old was 100.0 and 184.9 per 100,000, respectively. Although this analysis was limited by the study design and the nature of the NHAMCS database, the results clearly highlight the frequency with which hand fractures occur in the pediatric population.

Feehan and Sheps performed a population- based study evaluating the epidemiology of hand fractures in British Columbia, Canada, from 1996 to 2001. During the 5- year study period, 72,481 fractures were identified, for an overall annual incidence of 3.63 per 1000 across all age groups. The peak incidence for hand fractures was between 10 and 14 years of age (8.69 per 1000). When divided according to gender, the highest incidence was seen in males between 15 and 19 years of age (12.3 per 1000) and females between 10 and 14 years of age (5.3 per 1000). The authors attributed this peak incidence during adolescence to a combination of accelerated skeletal growth and participation in higher- risk sporting and recreational activities.

Over a 6- month period in 2000 at the Pulvertaft Hand Center in Derby, England, 360 children younger than 16 years were seen for hand injuries. Skeletal injuries accounted for 66% of cases, for an annual incidence of 418 per 100,000 children. The peak incidence was in patients between 10 and 16 years of age. Although crushing injuries were the most common mechanism overall, sports- related fractures occurred most commonly in patients older than 10 years. The small finger was most commonly fractured (52%), followed by the thumb (23%). The proximal phalanx was the most frequently injured phalanx (67%). Metacarpal fractures were most commonly diaphyseal, whereas phalangeal fractures typically involved the proximal metaphysis or physis.

Shah et al. more recently reported their retrospective analysis of the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System of patients less than 18 years of age treated at U.S. emergency departments from 1990 to 2009.They estimated an incidence of hand injuries at 11.6 per 1000. Approximately one- quarter of all hand injuries consisted of fractures, most commonly affecting patients between 10 and 14 years of age from recreational and sporting activities. In 2019 Lee and colleagues reported 11.7 million emergency department encounters from 2008 to 2012 for pediatric hand and upper extremity injuries using the National Emergency Department Sample. During these 4 years, there were $21.2 billion of charges related to these injuries.

In addition to the wealth of information on the frequency of hand fractures in children and adolescents, a number of published reports have provided insights into the mechanisms and patterns of injury. Combining these reports and previous epidemiologic studies, there is a biphasic distribution of hand injuries, with peak incidences in young toddlers and older adolescents. Bhende and coworkers analyzed 464 children with 477 hand injuries presenting to a level I pediatric emergency department during a 6- month period in 1989. Fractures accounted for 19.3% of all hand injuries, and a bimodal distribution of injuries was seen. Toddlers and younger children presented with lacerations, burns, and fingertip injuries—such as distal phalangeal fractures—as they explored the world around them. Conversely, adolescents tended to present with hand fractures sustained during sporting activities, fights, and self- inflicted trauma. Liu et al. and Young et al. support these findings in their separate analyses of children presenting to plastic surgery emergency clinics. There also appears to be a seasonality to the incidence of these injuries, with peaks in the spring and fall and lower volumes during the winter.

Rajesh and associates similarly attempted to characterize the pattern and nature of pediatric hand fractures in their radiographic review of 280 children treated at the Leicester Royal Infirmary in the United Kingdom. Isolated distal phalangeal tuft fractures were most common in the 0- to 4- year age group. Distal phalangeal tuft and shaft fractures were most common in 5- to 8- year- old children. Small finger proximal phalangeal fractures were most common in patients 9 to 12 years of age, and small finger metacarpal neck fractures were seen most often in 13- to 16- year- old adolescents (Fig. 41.1). Other published reports have also documented a higher frequency of injury to the border digits, especially the small finger.

Salter- Harris type II fracture patterns predominate, most commonly of the proximal phalanx. Despite the relative frequency of physeal fractures, posttraumatic physeal arrest is an uncommon sequela in isolated closed injuries.

### Preoperative Evaluation

Physical examination of a child’s injured hand can be challenging, particularly in an anxious, noncompliant, or nonverbal patient. Observation and gentle maneuvers are mandatory to obtain the information needed for an accurate and timely diagnosis. The presence of swelling, ecchymosis, guarded active motion, or soft tissue injury can guide the examiner to the region of pathology.

The tenodesis effect or gentle pressure applied to the more proximal muscle bellies of the forearm (away from the site of injury) aids in the assessment of both tendon integrity and angular or rotational deformity. Assessment of rotational deformity is critical because rotational remodeling is limited and may result in compromised hand function due to digital overlap or underlap. Malrotation can also be assessed by careful examination of the nail beds of adjacent digits in extension; rotation of greater than 10 degrees out of the plane of adjacent nail plates should alert the examiner to rotational malalignment, although these differences can be subtle and difficult to quantify. Rotational deformity can be better assessed by evaluating digital alignment with attempted finger flexion (Fig. 41.2). In general, the index through small fingers point to the scaphoid tubercle in flexion, roughly in parallel alignment. Often, patients normally exhibit some degree of digital underlap, such as the small beneath the ring finger. Comparison to the uninjured contralateral hand is valuable in these situations to determine baseline alignment. In young, anxious, or nonverbal patients who are unable to actively flex their digits during physical examination, the tenodesis effect can be used. Gentle passive extension of the wrist yields obligate digital flexion to assess for rotational alignment. Only after all pertinent information has been derived from observation and indirect examination techniques should direct palpation or stress testing be performed.

Sensibility is difficult to elicit in young patients, and threshold testing or two- point discrimination is generally unreliable until children are older than 8 years. In rare situations where there is concern about an associated digital nerve injury, warm water immersion can be used. The presence of skin wrinkling or pruning after several minutes of water immersion implies innervation and intact digital nerves (Fig. 41.3).

![[Fig.41.3.png]]
> **Fig. 41.3** Clinical photograph demonstrating a digital nerve injury to the index finger. Note the wrinkling of the long, ring, and small fingers, but no wrinkling of the index finger after submersion in water for several minutes.

Thorough and directed radiographic evaluation is imperative in the evaluation of a child’s injured hand. In general, dedicated anteroposterior (AP), lateral, and oblique radiographs of the injured digit are preferred over hand radiographs, which project artifacts (e.g., shadowing or distortion) and degraded image quality that can lead to errors in diagnosis. As part of the systematic evaluation of hand radiographs, the phalangeal line test has been proposed as a means of identifying joint malalignment or bony angulation in younger children whose skeletal structures are not yet ossified (Fig. 41.4). When expedited or comprehensive radiographic assessment is needed, fluoroscopy or mini- imaging units may assist in obtaining appropriate views of the injured digit, comparison views of the contralateral hand, or dynamic assessment of bony and articular stability. As ultrasonography becomes increasingly ubiquitous, ultrasound imaging may similarly be used to detect subtle or radiographically occult fractures. The normal sequence of skeletal ossification, as well as common normal radiographic variants (discussed later), must be recognized to avoid mistaking these for traumatic injuries. Indeed, Chew and Chong previously reported on 204 children referred to a tertiary care center for hand fractures; injuries were misdiagnosed in 16 cases (8%) with mistaking the epiphyses for fractures the leading cause.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

In the skeletally immature hand, the physes of the phalanges lie perpendicular to the long axis of the bones and are located proximal. The metacarpal physes are distal, with the exception of the thumb metacarpal, which is proximal.

The secondary centers of ossification in the epiphyses appear in a timely and orderly fashion. Although there is variation among individuals, the proximal phalangeal epiphyses become radiographically apparent between 10 and 24 months of age, in females before males.The epiphyses of the middle and distal phalanges ossify approximately 6 to 8 months later. Conversely, physeal closure proceeds in a distal to proximal direction, typically between the ages of 14 and 16 years. The index through small metacarpal epiphyses ossify at 12 to 27 months of age and fuse between 14 and 16 years of age. The thumb metacarpal epiphysis, however, does not typically ossify until 24 to 36 months of age.

Common normal radiographic variants include the appearance of a pseudoepiphysis of the distal thumb metacarpal and the proximal index metacarpal. The pseudoepiphyses become radiographically apparent at an early age, do not contribute to the longitudinal growth of the metacarpal, and typically fuse to the metacarpal diaphysis by the seventh year of life. Other anatomic variants include the double epiphysis, most commonly appearing in the index and thumb metacarpals. These physeal variants can be misinterpreted as fractures.

As in other long tubular bones that develop via enchondral ossification, the physis of the phalanges and metacarpals is divided into a number of histologic zones. Proceeding from the epiphysis to the metaphysis, the physis contains a resting zone, proliferative zone, zone of hypertrophy, and zone of provisional calcification. Because most physeal fractures propagate through the zone of hypertrophy without disrupting the resting or proliferative zone, posttraumatic growth disturbance is an uncommon sequela of pediatric hand fractures. In the less common circumstance in which the plane of cleavage traverses the zone of proliferation or the resting zone (e.g., Salter- Harris type III, IV, and V fractures), physeal arrest may occur. As the physis becomes more irregular and calcified as patients approach skeletal maturity, Salter- Harris type III and IV injuries are more likely to occur, mimicking more adult fracture patterns (e.g., Bennett fracture at the base of the thumb metacarpal).

Knowledge of the soft tissue anatomy is critical in understanding common fracture patterns and guiding nonoperative as well as surgical treatment. In the interphalangeal (IP) joints, the collateral ligaments arise from the collateral recesses of the phalangeal neck proximally and insert to the epiphysis and metaphysis of the adjacent phalangeal base distally. Conversely, at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, the radial collateral ligament arises from the metacarpal epiphysis proximally and inserts on the proximal phalangeal epiphysis distally; the ulnar collateral ligament originates from both the metacarpal epiphysis and metaphysis and inserts on the proximal phalangeal epiphysis. For these reasons, Salter- Harris III fractures are common about the MCP joint but rarely involve IP joints because the epiphysis is “protected” by the collateral ligaments. Conversely, diaphyseal or Salter- Harris II fractures of the distal phalanx or fractures of the proximal phalangeal head or neck more commonly arise from laterally directed forces on the IP joints.

Understanding of the tendinous insertions in the hand also helps elucidate fracture patterns and guide treatment. The terminal tendon of the long digital extensor inserts onto the dorsal phalangeal epiphysis. The flexor digitorum profundus insertion to the index through small fingers spans both the volar epiphysis and metaphysis. This anatomic relationship is particularly relevant in the treatment of distal phalangeal physeal fractures, as described later. In the thumb, the extensor pollicis longus inserts onto the dorsal epiphysis of the distal phalanx, the extensor pollicis brevis inserts onto the epiphysis of the proximal phalanx, and the abductor pollicis longus inserts broadly on both the epiphysis and metaphysis of the thumb metacarpal. The flexor pollicis longus inserts on the metadiaphysis of the distal phalanx.

### Historical Review

Most hand fractures in children can be managed nonoperatively on an outpatient basis. Only 10% to 20% of pediatric hand fractures require surgical care.

Hastings and Simmons published a retrospective analysis of 354 hand fractures in skeletally immature patients. Border digits were most commonly fractured, and the metacarpal and proximal phalanx were most commonly affected. There were only two cases of growth disturbance following severe crush injuries or infection. Overall results were excellent in most cases, but a number of risk factors were associated with fracture malunion and poor results, including failure to obtain adequate radiographs at the time of injury, false assumptions regarding the remodeling potential of phalangeal fractures, and failure to evaluate clinical deformity (i.e., malrotation). Displaced articular fractures, physeal fractures of the distal phalanx, phalangeal neck fractures, and open fractures were identified as high- risk fractures for suboptimal outcomes (Box 41.1). Given this information, identification and treatment of these fractures are emphasized in the following sections.

### Distal Phalangeal Fractures

Most distal phalangeal fractures are successfully treated without surgery. Identifying open fractures and subsequent operative treatment uniformly leads to a satisfactory outcome.

#### Shaft and Tuft Fractures

Fractures of the distal phalanx typically result from crush injuries in younger patients. In general, these fractures are extraphyseal injuries with varying fracture patterns, including transverse, longitudinal split, and comminuted configurations. These bony injuries are often accompanied by soft tissue avulsions and/or nail bed lacerations. Treatment is predicated on stabilization of the skeletal injury and appropriate soft tissue injury care, including wound irrigation and debridement, nail plate removal, and nail bed repair as indicated. Although the majority of distal phalangeal fractures can be treated effectively with splint or cast immobilization, surgical stabilization is occasionally needed for widely displaced injuries. In these situations, a single Kirschner wire placed through the digital tip and across the fracture site will suffice. Some authors have proposed using a hypodermic needle placed under local anesthesia for fracture stabilization.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: FRACTURES OF THE DISTAL PHALANX

The majority of extraphyseal distal phalangeal fractures are treated with simple splint or cast immobilization for 4 weeks, with excellent healing and outcomes. Rarely, if complete displacement or translation occurs in transverse fracture patterns, Kirschner wire fixation is used to maintain reduction. A single Kirschner wire is passed in a retrograde fashion through the fingertip, across the fracture, and across the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint, engaging the head of the middle phalanx. The wire is bent and cut outside the skin. The wire is removed in the office after 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises and return to activities as symptoms abate.

#### Physeal Fractures

Salter- Harris I fractures result in the so- called pseudomallet fracture as the distal diaphyseal fragment is displaced in a volar direction, with apex dorsal angulation, by the pull of the flexor digitorum profundus.Treatment requires extension splinting of the distal phalanx and DIP joint for 4 to 6 weeks.

Seymour fractures are characteristic pediatric injuries of the distal phalangeal physis that merit special attention. The injury mechanism is typically a crush injury to the distal phalanx and DIP joint.

These fractures are displaced physeal fractures of the distal phalanx accompanied by an overlying nail bed laceration (Fig. 41.6). Often, there is interposed soft tissue (i.e., germinal matrix) within the displaced physeal fracture. Technically, a Seymour fracture is an open physeal fracture. Therefore treatment requires nail plate removal, irrigation and debridement, extrication of any interposed soft tissue, open reduction of the physeal fracture, and meticulous nail bed repair (see Fig. 41.6). Dorsal skin incisions extended in a proximal direction from both corners of the dorsal nail fold toward the DIP joint extension crease elevate the dorsal skin and provide exposure of the germinal matrix and nail bed laceration. The terminal tendon attachment to the dorsal epiphysis must be preserved. Replacement of the trephinated nail plate beneath the dorsal nail fold after nail bed repair often results in sufficient bony stabilization. These procedures can be performed by a qualified provider in the emergency department. In cases of excessive instability, transarticular longitudinal pinning of the distal phalanx and DIP joint is performed. KruscheMandl et al. reported results of 24 children treated for Seymour fractures with mean 10- year follow- up. With adherence to the above stated treatment principles, 23 of 24 patients regained full motion and there were no infections. Although appropriate and timely treatment results in excellent functional outcomes, failure to recognize and treat accordingly may lead to osteomyelitis, physeal arrest, and nail plate deformity (Fig. 41.7).

Fracture displacement is caused by the proximal pull of the terminal tendon on the epiphyseal fracture fragment. Although the vast majority of these injuries can be treated successfully with extension splinting for 4 to 6 weeks, markedly displaced injuries accompanied by volar subluxation of the distal phalanx or articular incongruity unamenable to closed reduction merit surgical consideration. Treatment options include formal open reduction and internal fixation via a dorsal approach (with care to preserve terminal tendon attachments to the epiphyseal fragment), or closed reduction followed by transarticular or extension block pinning.

Rarely, injury to the distal phalangeal physis may result in extrusion of the epiphysis, the so- called epiphyseal dislocation (Fig. 41.9).When the epiphysis is not ossified or incompletely ossified, radiographic findings may be very subtle. For this reason, careful correlation between radiographs and clinical examination is necessary. A heightened suspicion for underlying epiphyseal injury is necessary when there is a “flake,” “crescent,” or poorly defined opacity and an irregularity or incongruity of the DIP joint. Because the epiphysis typically displaces in a dorsal direction, a true lateral radiograph of the digit and DIP joint is necessary for diagnosis.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PHYSEAL FRACTURES OF THE DISTAL PHALANX

Physeal fractures resulting in pseudomallet deformity are treated with closed reduction and immobilization for 4 to 6 weeks. Variations of mallet splinting are viable options in older patients; however, cast immobilization is often needed in the young child to ensure compliance and protection of the injured digit. A Seymour fracture is suspected when there is an apex dorsal physeal fracture and an associated subungual hematoma. If there is any question, the nail plate should be removed, and appropriate soft tissue repair and bony stabilization performed, as described earlier. Widening of the dorsal phalangeal physis following closed reduction suggests soft tissue interposition and merits surgical intervention. Surgical treatment for dorsal Salter- Harris III fractures of the distal phalanx is reserved for patients with greater than 40% articular surface involvement and volar subluxation of the DIP joint, similar to adult bony mallet injuries. Percutaneous pinning techniques are preferred when adequate closed reduction can be achieved. Otherwise, open reduction is necessary to achieve fracture reduction.

### Middle and Proximal Phalangeal Fractures

Fractures of the middle and proximal phalanx are categorized according to their location, pattern, and displacement. Fractures tend to occur at the distal articular surface, diaphysis, physis, or epiphysis. Each fracture has specific anatomic and treatment considerations and is discussed separately.

Prompt recognition and surgery for displaced phalangeal neck and intraarticular fractures reliably result in union and restoration of motion. A delayed presentation complicates surgical management and diminishes outcome.

#### Articular Fractures of the Phalangeal Head

Articular fractures of the head of the proximal and middle phalanges include unicondylar, bicondylar, and comminuted fracture patterns.Similar to adult injuries, treatment is predicated on restoration of articular congruity and correction of angular and rotational deformity. Nondisplaced injuries can be treated with cast immobilization. In these situations, however, weekly radiographic examinations should be performed to confirm maintenance of reduction and articular congruity. In fractures with greater than 5 to 10 degrees of angulation or 1 to 2 mm of articular incongruity, surgical intervention is recommended. Closed or open reduction with smooth Kirschner wire or interfragmentary screw fixation is recommended (Video 41.1). Although closed reduction is desirable, an anatomic reduction is imperative to restore articular congruity. If closed reduction is not obtainable, open reduction is performed via a dorsal approach with visualization of the articular surfaces. The collateral ligaments and other soft tissue attachments to the condylar fracture fragments must be preserved to maintain fracture fragment vascularity. With careful surgical technique, restoration of an articular congruity, preservation of joint stability, and return of motion can be expected. Patients and families should be counseled, however, regarding the potential for long- term stiffness and arthrosis, particularly with severely displaced injuries requiring open treatment.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PHALANGEAL HEAD FRACTURES

For displaced fractures of the phalangeal head, open reduction and internal fixation using smooth pins (or, less commonly, screws) is performed (Fig. 41.10). A dorsal incision is centered over the IP joint, elevating soft tissue flaps while preserving the dorsal veins and subcutaneous nerves. The approach is between the extensor tendon and lateral bands mobilizing the extensor apparatus. The joint capsule is incised to visualize the fracture line(s) and articular surface. Soft tissue attachments to the displaced fracture fragments are preserved to maintain vascularity and ligamentous stability. Under direct visualization, the fracture is reduced and articular congruity is restored. Fixation is performed with smooth Kirschner wires and/or interfragmentary screws. Two points of fixation are necessary to provide rotational stability and stable fixation. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirms reduction, and the pins are retained in a percutaneous position to facilitate removal. The skin is closed, and the patient is placed in a splint or short- arm cast. Pins are removed after 3 to 4 weeks and range- of- motion exercises are initiated.

#### Phalangeal Neck Fractures

Phalangeal neck fractures are distinctive injuries of childhood. They are usually the result of “doorjamb” injuries where the digit is crushed or trapped in a door and forcefully withdrawn, imparting both extension and rotational forces. Border digits are most commonly affected, and the middle phalanx is injured more frequently than the proximal phalanx. The fracture occurs at the level of the phalangeal neck, and typically the distal articular segment is displaced into extension, with varying degrees of rotation. Patients present with swelling, tenderness, ecchymosis, and limited interphalangeal joint motion, although limited motion is often difficult to elicit in an acutely injured child. A slight angular deformity may be seen.

Careful radiographic evaluation is critical for a timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Because the distal fragment typically displaces dorsally, with apex volar angulation, standard AP views of the affected digit often appear benign. A true lateral radiograph of the injured digit is necessary. In addition, in very young patients the phalangeal head remains predominantly cartilaginous, so subtle or seemingly innocuous “bony flecks” should raise the suspicion of a displaced cartilaginous cap or osteochondral fracture. Although the radiographs may appear innocuous, displaced phalangeal neck fractures result in both abnormal digital alignment in the sagittal plane and disruption of the normally concave subcondylar fossa. Obliteration of the subcondylar fossa results in a bony block to IP joint flexion, as the adjacent phalangeal base abuts the bony prominence at the distal end of the proximal fracture fragment.

Al- Qattan’s classification of extraarticular phalangeal neck fractures is clinically useful because it infers prognosis and guides treatment. Type I fractures are nondisplaced; type II fractures are partially displaced, with some bony or cortical contact; and type III fractures are completely displaced without bony apposition. Cast immobilization is sufficient in cases of nondisplaced injuries, although serial radiographs are required to confirm maintenance of reduction. Closed reduction and splinting or cast immobilization are often insufficient for type II and III fractures. In the vast majority of cases, surgical treatment with closed reduction and percutaneous pin fixation is recommended (Fig. 41.11). In Al- Qattan’s series of 66 patients with 67 phalangeal neck fractures, 13 fractures were nondisplaced and treated with nonoperative care with good to excellent results. Type II fractures accounted for the majority of injuries, and results were highly dependent on treatment. Patients treated with closed or open reduction and Kirschner wire fixation subsequently healed with good to excellent results. Type II injuries treated with closed reduction without surgical fixation achieved fair to poor results in 66% of cases. Among the seven type III injuries, failure to perform surgical reduction and Kirschner wire fixation uniformly led to fracture nonunion and poor results. Matzon and Cornwall have proposed a treatment algorithm for the care of acute type II phalangeal neck fractures, beginning with minimally invasive interventions and advancing to more advanced techniques as needed. In their series of 61 consecutive children, 49 patients were successfully treated with closed manipulation with percutaneous pin fixation and 12 underwent percutaneous pin- assisted reduction. No child with acute injuries required open reduction. Overall, the vast majority of patients achieved good to excellent results with low complication rates. In a more recent study, Boyer and colleagues found as high as a 4.8% complication rate, with short- term stiffness being the highest complication. However, patients reportedly had full range of motion 1 year following injury.

Since these injuries occur in the phalangeal neck and the physis is located at the proximal phalangeal metaphysis, remodeling potential is limited. There have been a handful of reported cases of complete skeletal remodeling of phalangeal neck malunions. Puckett et al. have previously published a retrospective case series of eight children with displaced phalangeal neck fracture malunions followed for a mean of 5 years after injury. In this small series of select, nonconsecutive patients, sagittal plane angulation improved from 31 to 0 degrees, whereas coronal plane alignment changed from 11 to 4 degrees. No patients reported functionally limiting loss of motion; however, two complained of persistent deformity in the coronal plane. The authors still recommended surgical reduction and stabilization for acute displaced phalangeal neck fractures; however, their results indicate these injuries have some remodeling capability.

Review of these case reports leads to a number of observations. First, phalangeal neck remodeling may be expected only in very young patients with considerable remaining growth. Second, sagittal plane deformity has the greatest remodeling potential, and coronal plane angulation or bony malrotation will not improve to the same extent.

Third, while radiographic remodeling may be evident and IP joint motion improved, some cases achieve IP joint flexion only to 80 or 90 degrees. Finally, many reported cases involved the middle phalanx where the deformity is better tolerated due to the relatively less amount of DIP joint flexion needed for composite digital flexion and fist formation. Based on these published reports, observation of displaced phalangeal neck fractures should be considered only in the following circumstances: (1) if there is no rotational or coronal deformity, (2) the adjacent IP joint is intact, (3) substantial skeletal growth remains, (4) the resultant loss of IP joint flexion would be tolerable without undue functional compromise, and (5) the patient or family is willing to wait months to years for gradual remodeling.

In cases of late presenting, displaced phalangeal neck fractures with incipient malunion, percutaneous pin osteoclasis has been advocated as a way to restore bony alignment, improve IP joint J flexion potential, and minimize the risk of osteonecrosis. The most appropriate patients for percutaneous pin osteoclasis are those with persistent tenderness at the fracture site, a fracture line that is still radiographically apparent, and less than 90 degrees of corresponding IP joint flexion (Video 41.2).

In cases of phalangeal neck malunions, there is a temptation to perform a corrective osteotomy to restore bony alignment and IP joint motion. Formal open corrective osteotomy carries the substantial risk of osteonecrosis of the phalangeal head and is not recommended. In cases of long- standing phalangeal neck fracture malunions, subcondylar fossa reconstruction may be considered. The concave fossa is recreated by removal of the healed fracture line and bone that has obliterated the fossa. Indications include persistent bony malalignment associated with restricted IP joint motion and functional compromise.

Phalangeal neck fracture nonunions are rare. Patients will present with complaints of instability, deformity, or stiffness. In the absence of osteonecrosis of the small articular fragment, bone grafting and longitudinal Kirschner wire fixation can result in healing, though often some residual stiffness remains.

In type II or III injuries, closed reduction and percutaneous pinning via cross- pins are performed. Another potential technique is using a single intramedullary Kirschner wire for fixation. Patients are casted for 3 to 4 weeks, after which pins are removed and motion is initiated. #### Phalangeal Shaft Fractures

Diaphyseal fractures of the phalanges represent approximately twothirds of phalangeal injuries. The most common mechanism is combined bending and torsional forces sustained during falls or sporting activities. Proximal phalangeal shaft fractures usually exhibit apex volar angulation owing to the combined forces of the extensor mechanism on the distal fragment and intrinsic musculature on the proximal fragment. Conversely, fractures of the middle phalanx exhibit either apex palmar or dorsal angulation, depending on the location of the fracture in relationship to the flexor digitorum superficialis insertion. Fractures distal to the flexor digitorum superficialis insertion typically angulate apex volar, with the extensor mechanism pulling the distal fragment dorsally. Although up to 10 degrees of angulation is acceptable, closed reduction is recommended for greater amounts of angulation or any malrotation resulting in digital overlap or underlap. Al- Qattan et al. previously reported on 92 children studied prospectively with proximal phalangeal base fractures. The authors noted excellent outcomes in patients treated with closed reduction with or without percutaneous pinning for cases with greater than 10 degrees angulation, whereas remodeling was seen in patients with less than 10 degrees of radiographic angulation.

Following closed reduction and cast immobilization, serial clinical and radiographic evaluations are necessary to ensure maintenance of adequate alignment. In patients with irreducible or unstable fractures, surgical treatment via closed or open reduction and smooth pin fixation are recommended. In cases of long oblique fractures, two or more smooth Kirschner wires placed orthogonal to the fracture plane provide both axial and rotational stability (Fig. 41.13). In transverse fractures, oblique cross- pinning may be used, although care should be taken to avoid crossing pins at the fracture site as this results in suboptimal fixation. Pins are left percutaneous and removed after 4 weeks, followed by motion exercises. Boyer et al. previously reported a series of 105 patients treated with percutaneous pinning for displaced proximal phalanx fractures. In this retrospective cohort, 36 patients developed postoperative stiffness with 31 requiring therapy to restore motion. Despite this high proportion of initial postoperative stiffness, patients with greater than 12 months follow- up regained full motion and excellent function.

#### Physeal Fractures of the Proximal Phalanges

Physeal fractures of the phalanges are common injuries and occur secondary to combined bending and rotational moments sustained during falls and sporting activities. Patients and families may express anxiety upon learning of the occurrence of a growth plate fracture; however, multiple studies have demonstrated that one- third of all bony injuries of the growing hand involve the physis, and physeal arrest is uncommon. The proximal phalanx of the small finger is most commonly affected, resulting in the so- called extraoctave fracture (Fig. 41.14). The thumb is injured in roughly one- fifth of cases. Physeal fractures of the middle phalanx are considerably less common than those involving the proximal phalanx. Salter- Harris type II patterns predominate owing to the anatomic features of the collateral ligaments described previously.

Closed reduction is recommended for fractures with greater than 10 degrees of angulation and any malrotation, owing to the limited remodeling potential of radioulnar (coronal plane) or rotational deformity. During closed reduction maneuvers, flexion of the MCP joint assists in stabilizing the proximal fracture fragment by tensioning of the collateral ligaments. A freer elevator or writing utensil can be placed in the adjacent web space at the apex of the deformity to serve as a fulcrum during angular correction. Following closed reduction, assessment of rotational alignment and radiographic confirmation of correction are essential for a successful outcome. Cast immobilization in the intrinsic- plus position is required for 3 to 4 weeks, followed by a gradual return to activity. Although splint immobilization and earlier motion have been advocated by some authors, the risks of noncompliance with a removable splint and the potential for loss of fracture reduction generally outweigh the benefits of early motion, particularly in young patients that do not develop posttraumatic stiffness.

In rare cases, these fractures may be unstable. Such fractures tend to be high- energy injuries that disrupt the stabilizing soft tissue envelope. In these cases, closed reduction followed by pin fixation is performed. The pins are left percutaneous to facilitate removal after 3 to 4 weeks. Smaller wires (ranging from 0.028 to 0.035 inch) and fewer wires may be utilized in young children compared with adults.

Stable fracture reduction is achieved in the majority of cases. Cast immobilization is discontinued after 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises. Percutaneous pinning is reserved for unstable or irreducible injuries with malrotation or greater than 10 to 15 degrees of angulation. Cast immobilization is discontinued and pins are removed at 3 to 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises.

#### Articular Fractures of the Phalangeal Base and Epiphyseal Fractures

Fractures of the phalangeal base with intraarticular extension represent Salter- Harris type III and IV injuries. In cases of articular incongruity, joint instability, or greater than 1 to 2 mm of displacement, closed or open reduction and smooth pin fixation are recommended to restore articular congruity, physeal integrity, and bony alignment. These injuries should be distinguished from volar plate avulsion fractures of the epiphysis. Similar to adults, small volar plate avulsions can be safely and effectively treated with simple Coban wrapping and “buddy taping” with early motion to avoid stiffness, provided the IP joint is stable and congruently reduced. In a prior study of 121 proximal IP joint hyperextension injuries randomized to either buddy taping or aluminum splinting, buddy taping was equally as effective as splinting. In general, children had better outcomes than adults.

Comminuted, unstable proximal interphalangeal fracturedislocations are rare in children. Treatment principles are similar to those in adults. On occasion, closed techniques can be used (Fig. 41.15). However, open reduction and internal fixation may be necessary to restore joint stability and reconstitute the articular surface. The volar base of the middle phalanx is approached via a volar zigzag incision. The A3–C1 pulleys are elevated in either a radial or ulnar direction, and flexor tendons are retracted to allow visualization of the joint surface and displaced fracture fragments. Anatomic reduction and restoration of joint congruity are performed, followed by stabilization with sizeappropriate smooth pins or screws. Bone grafting may be required to fill any impacted metaphyseal defect. Although early motion is desired, 3 weeks of cast or splint immobilization are typically necessary owing to the tenuous fixation achieved and compliance issues in young patients.

### Metacarpal Fractures

Treatment principles for metacarpal fractures in children are similar to those in adults. Metacarpal fractures arise from bending forces or axial loads sustained during falls, sporting injuries, or direct blows with a clenched fist. These fractures result in apex dorsal angulation secondary to the intrinsic musculature. Mild to moderate apex dorsal angulation results in little to no compromise of hand function. In contrast, excessive angulation results in both esthetic consequences, difficulties with digital extension (i.e., pseudo–claw deformity), and/or discomfort with forceful grasp. Closed reduction and pin fixation or open reduction for multiple metacarpal fractures results in union and return of function. Open reduction and internal fixation of intraarticular metacarpal head fracture can achieve articular restoration, but stiffness is common.

There is substantial variation in what is deemed “acceptable” angulation in metacarpal neck and shaft fractures. Each patient merits individualized decision making. Most authorities agree that 30 degrees or less of sagittal plane deformity is acceptable in physeal fractures, particularly in young patients with growth remaining and remodeling potential. Twenty to 45 degrees of apex dorsal angulation is acceptable in metacarpal neck fractures, with greater deformity allowable in the more ulnar digits. Shaft fractures tolerate less angular deformity, 10 to 15 degrees of angulation is acceptable in the index and long fingers and 30 to 40 degrees in the ring and small fingers. Even mild rotational deformity (>10 degrees) results in digital overlap with functional problems and should be avoided. Malrotation is often undetectable on radiographic evaluation and the importance of a thorough physical examination cannot be overstated.

In general, metacarpal fractures can be considered according to their anatomic location: epiphyseal, diaphyseal, neck, physeal, and articular fractures.

#### Epiphyseal Fractures

Fractures of the metacarpal epiphysis are rare and are usually SalterHarris III or IV fractures. These injuries result from axial loads during falls or closed fist injuries. Although nondisplaced injuries may heal satisfactorily with cast immobilization, open reduction and internal fixation are recommended for displaced fractures with articular incongruity. Internal fixation using countersunk interfragmentary screws can be used in older adolescents; however, smooth Kirschner wire fixation is preferred in younger children. Kirschner wire fixation provides adequate fracture stability and minimizes the risk of iatrogenic physeal injury. Rarely, there is extensive comminution of primarily cartilaginous fragments, and bioabsorbable pin or suture fixation is used. Osteonecrosis has been reported following these severe injuries, and preservation of any soft tissue attachments to the displaced fracture fragments is recommended. Torre has proposed that increased intracapsular pressure secondary to fracture hematoma may play a causative role, but there is little evidence to support this assertion.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: METACARPAL EPIPHYSEAL FRACTURES

Open reduction is performed via a curvilinear dorsal incision. The interval between the extensor tendon and ulnar sagittal band is developed, leaving a cuff of tissue for later sagittal band repair. After dorsal capsulotomy, the fracture hematoma is evacuated, and reduction is performed. Any soft tissue attachments are preserved to maintain vascularity to the displaced fracture fragments. Internal fixation is performed with smooth pins, screws, or sutures depending on the size and integrity of the articular fragments. After fracture fixation, the dorsal capsule, sagittal band, and skin are closed. Cast immobilization is instituted for 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises.

#### Metacarpal Neck Fractures

Metacarpal neck fractures account for 56% to 70% of all metacarpal fractures in the pediatric and adolescent population. These fractures often result from closed fist injuries but may also occur after falls and sporting injuries. The small finger is most commonly affected, and the characteristic deformity is apex dorsal angulation with varying degrees of rotation. These fractures usually occur through the distal metaphysis, although physeal involvement is fairly common.

Relatively large degrees of apex dorsal angulation are acceptable in ring and small metacarpal neck fractures, resulting in minimal impairment owing to the compensatory MCP and carpometacarpal (CMC) joint motion. In young children, because the metacarpal physis is distal, remodeling of angular deformity is expected with growth. In general, 10 to 30 degrees greater angulation than the corresponding range of CMC motion is well tolerated in skeletally immature patients.

Nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures are effectively treated with cast immobilization for 3 to 4 weeks. Displaced injuries with malrotation or excessive angulation are treated with closed reduction and cast immobilization. Reduction maneuvers are performed under digital or wrist block using local anesthesia or conscious sedation in young patients. Reduction consists of longitudinal traction and correction of the apex dorsal angulation using threepoint bending. The Jahss maneuver—flexing the MCP to 90 degrees and applying a dorsally directed force on the metacarpal head through the flexed digit—is commonly used. The Jahss maneuver also facilitates the correction of rotational malalignment because the MCP collateral ligaments are taut in flexion, and torque applied to the flexed digit is imparted to the malrotated distal fracture fragment. Adequate rotational correction is confirmed by restoration of the normal digital cascade.

Traditionally, a cast was used to immobilize the hand in the intrinsic- plus position to avoid extension contractures of the MCP joint. Extension of the MCP joint often results in loss of reduction, particularly in more displaced injuries. Studies have established, however, that cast immobilization for 4 weeks with the MCP joints in extension (the so- called pancake cast) is safe and is not associated with longterm stiffness or MCP extension contractures in young adults or children. Undeniably, cast application with a three- point bending mold to maintain desired fracture alignment is easier with the MCP joints in extension.

In displaced fractures that cannot be maintained with closed reduction and cast immobilization, percutaneous pin fixation via either cross- pinning or transmetacarpal pinning techniques is performed. Intramedullary fixation, or “bouquet pinning,” has been successful in adults, but these techniques are not commonly used in children owing to the small size of the metacarpal intramedullary canals and the presence of the intervening physis. The Jahss maneuver is particularly helpful for closed reduction and pinning because it allows correction of both the apex dorsal angulation and the rotational alignment. Pin placement can be performed within the collateral recesses without violating the collateral ligament. Closed reduction and pin fixation should minimize iatrogenic trauma to the metacarpal physis.

Late presenting metacarpal neck fractures are common in the pediatric population. Intervention is usually not required as the large amount of MCP and CMC motion can compensate for considerable angulation. Excessive angulation or malrotation—particularly in the index and long finger rays—requires percutaneous pin osteoclasis or formal open reduction and internal fixation. In subacute situations (i.e., <4 weeks from injury, fracture line still visible on radiographs, tenderness still elicited at the fracture site), percutaneous pin osteoclasis can liberate the displaced fracture fragment and allow closed reduction and pin fixation. When percutaneous pin osteoclasis is not possible, open reduction or late corrective osteotomy is considered. As with other late presenting displaced physeal fractures with remodeling potential, repeated forceful manipulations are avoided to prevent iatrogenic physeal arrest.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: METACARPAL NECK FRACTURES

Closed reduction and cast immobilization are used for displaced injuries with excessive angulation or malrotation. In general, 10, 20, 30, and 40 degrees of angulation are acceptable in index, long, ring, and small finger metacarpals, respectively. Casting with the MCP joints extended for 3 to 4 weeks is well tolerated and can be used without reservation. MCP extension facilitates the ability to maintain reduction and does not lead to long- standing stiffness in children. In unstable fractures that fail reduction and casting, closed reduction and percutaneous cross- pinning are used. Pins are removed in 3 to 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises.

#### Metacarpal Shaft Fractures

Metacarpal shaft fractures usually result from torsional or bending mechanisms, yielding spiral or transverse fracture patterns. As in adults, isolated diaphyseal fractures, particularly of the central rays, do not displace considerably owing to the stabilizing forces of the intermetacarpal ligaments and central CMC articulations. Conversely, fractures of the border rays or multiple metacarpal fractures are inherently more unstable and display greater displacement and angulation. Nondisplaced fractures are treated with 3 to 4 weeks of cast immobilization, followed by range- of- motion exercises. Displaced injuries are treated with closed reduction and well- molded cast immobilization in the majority of cases. Serial radiographs during cast immobilization are required to ensure maintenance of reduction as the soft tissue swelling subsides. Four weeks of cast immobilization are usually sufficient, followed by range- of- motion exercises.

Surgical indications in children are similar to those in adults and include open fractures, multiple fractures, irreducible fractures, and unstable fractures. Additional indications include fractures with extensive soft tissue injury or neurovascular injury that precludes cast immobilization. Numerous fixation options are available for metacarpal shaft fractures, including percutaneous pinning, transmetacarpal pinning, interfragmentary screw fixation, plate and screw fixation, intramedullary fixation, and external fixation. Choice of the fixation technique depends on the fracture pattern and associated soft tissue injury, as well as surgeon preference. In general, the least invasive technique that achieves the desired goals is the best choice. Surgical techniques are similar to those in adults (see Chapter 18), although the presence of the metacarpal physis must be considered during the decision- making process.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: METACARPAL SHAFT FRACTURES

Isolated metacarpal shaft fractures are usually stable and are treated with cast immobilization. In cases of unstable fractures or persistent malrotation, closed reduction and percutaneous pin fixation via transmetacarpal or cross- pinning techniques are performed (Figs. 41.16 and 41.17). Pins are removed at 4 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises. In cases of multiple metacarpal fractures, open reduction and internal fixation with interfragmentary compression screws (long oblique fractures) or size- appropriate compression plates (transverse, short oblique, comminuted fractures) are performed via a dorsal approach.

#### Metacarpal Base Fractures

Metacarpal base fractures account for approximately 13% to 20% of metacarpal fractures in children. The vast majority involve the small finger ray. Rarely, intraarticular extension is possible, with subsequent CMC subluxation or dislocation of the metacarpal shaft (the reverse Bennett equivalent). Clinical evaluation includes a careful assessment of digital rotation, flexion, and extension, as well as the associated soft tissue and neurovascular status. In crush injuries or high- energy trauma, compartment syndrome can occur, and emergent decompression is required. Radiographic evaluation includes AP and lateral radiographs of the affected hand, as well as oblique views to better visualize the border metacarpals.

Nondisplaced or minimally displaced injuries are treated with 3 to 4 weeks of cast immobilization. In displaced injuries, closed reduction is performed under local anesthesia or conscious sedation. Longitudinal traction and direct pressure in a volar direction over the typically dorsal apex of the deformity is the reduction maneuver. Malrotation can be corrected simultaneously by flexing the MCP joint of the affected ray, rotating the digit, and restoring rotational alignment. After cast immobilization, radiographs are necessary to confirm reduction and treatment can be continued as described earlier. In unstable reductions or CMC fracture- dislocations, surgical stabilization is required. If reduction is achieved with closed manipulation, percutaneous pin fixation using transmetacarpal pinning techniques or oblique pins into the proximal fracture fragment or carpus usually suffices. When the fracture is irreducible, formal open reduction is performed via a longitudinal incision, followed by pin fixation. Pins are removed at 4 weeks, and range- of- motion exercises are initiated.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: METACARPAL BASE FRACTURES

Closed manipulation and casting are mainstays in the treatment of most metacarpal base fractures. Surgical stabilization via percutaneous pinning is used for malrotated, widely displaced, or multiple adjacent metacarpal base fractures. Transmetacarpal pinning or transarticular pinning into the adjacent carpus can be used with minimal morbidity or concern for long- term arthrosis.

### Thumb Fractures

#### Phalangeal Fractures

Salter- Harris type III fractures of the thumb proximal phalanx are common injuries in children and represent the pediatric equivalent of “gamekeeper’s thumb” or “skier’s thumb” in adults. The anatomy of the thumb MCP collateral ligament complex explains the pattern of injury encountered. The collateral ligaments insert almost exclusively onto the proximal phalanx epiphysis and a valgus force results in an avulsion fracture of the ulnar epiphysis rather than a pure ligamentous disruption. In cases of displaced Salter- Harris III fractures of the proximal phalanx of the thumb, surgical treatment is recommended to restore articular congruity and ligamentous MCP joint stability.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THUMB PHALANGEAL FRACTURES

Surgery is performed under general anesthesia and tourniquet control (Fig. 41.18). A dorsal curvilinear incision is made over the thumb MCP joint. Subcutaneous dissection is performed in line with the skin incision, identifying and protecting terminal branches of the radial sensory nerve. The adductor aponeurosis is incised along its insertion on the extensor tendon. Because the ulnar collateral ligament is usually intact, the MCP joint is exposed through the fracture site and the collateral ligament is protected. If better visualization of the articular surface is necessary, the dorsal capsule is incised. The fracture is reduced under direct visualization, restoring congruity of the articular surface. Two parallel Kirschner wires are placed into the reduced epiphyseal fragment, across the fracture site, and through the opposite radial cortex. In very young children or in cases of small, predominantly cartilaginous fracture fragments, fixation with only one wire may be all that is obtainable. Intraoperative fluoroscopy is then used to confirm anatomic reduction and appropriate placement of the wires. Additional sutures can be placed between the ulnar collateral ligament and the underlying periosteum. Wires are bent and cut outside the skin. Closure is performed in layers, carefully reapproximating the adductor aponeurosis. A thumb spica cast is applied for 4 to 6 weeks, followed by range- of- motion exercises. Formal physical and occupational therapy is usually not required.

#### Metacarpal Diaphyseal Fractures

Metacarpal diaphyseal fractures of the thumb are treated according to principles similar to those used for fractures of the index through small finger rays.

#### Base of Thumb Metacarpal Fractures

Base of thumb metacarpal fractures are quite common, typically resulting from falls or sporting injuries. The fracture may be metaphyseal, physeal, epiphyseal, or intraarticular. Recent investigations suggest that physeal fractures are most common. Owing to the proximity to the proximal thumb metacarpal physis, these injuries demonstrate tremendous remodeling potential in young children. Impairment of hand function following displaced base of thumb metacarpal injuries is further mitigated by the near universal motion of the CMC joint. As a result, as much as 30 degrees of angular deformity is well tolerated, with minimal esthetic or functional compromise (Fig. 41.19). In these situations, thumb spica cast immobilization for 3 to 4 weeks may suffice.

Kozin and Waters have classified base of thumb metacarpal fractures into four types. Type A fractures are those fractures that propagate between the physis and junction of the proximal and middle diaphysis. These fractures are transverse or oblique and often amenable to closed reduction and cast immobilization with excellent functional outcomes.

Type B fractures are Salter- Harris II fractures with an ulnar Thurston- Holland metaphyseal fragment and apex radial angulation. The metacarpal diaphysis is adducted by the pull of the adductor pollicis and displaced proximally by the abductor pollicis longus, thereby effectively narrowing the first web space. Excessive displacement or angulation requires closed reduction and cast immobilization to restore bony and physeal alignment. If the reduction is unstable, percutaneous pin fixation into the intact epiphysis or adjacent trapezium is performed. Rarely, open reduction is required when severe soft tissue injury and swelling preclude the ability to manipulate and maintain reduction of the fracture fragments.

Type C fractures are Salter- Harris II injuries with a radial ThurstonHolland fragment and apex ulnar angulation. Severe displacement or angulation requires closed reduction and pinning; however, comminution or soft tissue interposition may prevent bony realignment, necessitating open reduction and pin fixation.

Type D fractures are Salter- Harris III or IV fractures with intraarticular extension. This fracture pattern is the pediatric equivalent of the adult Bennett fracture, and similar treatment principles apply. Closed or open reduction with pin fixation is recommended to restore articular congruity and maintain bony reduction. As in adults, the reduction maneuver consists of longitudinal traction, pronation, and direct adduction pressure applied to the thumb metacarpal base to reduce the thumb ray.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: BASE OF THUMB METACARPAL FRACTURES

Base of thumb metacarpal fractures are usually treated with closed reduction and short- arm thumb spica casting. Excellent functional outcomes can be anticipated with up to 30 degrees of angulation in skeletally immature patients. In instances of fracture instability or excessive displacement not amenable to closed manipulation, closed or open reduction followed by percutaneous pin fixation is recommended (Fig. 41.20). When needed, open reduction is performed via a radial skin incision at the junction of the glabrous and nonglabrous skin, centered at the thumb CMC joint. Subperiosteal dissection allows retraction of the thenar muscles in a volar direction, facilitating fracture exposure. Subsequently, fracture reduction is achieved, and percutaneous pin fixation is performed. An oblique crossed- pin configuration is typically used. Pinning the thumb ray to the index metacarpal across the first web space is generally avoided unless necessary to achieve adequate stability.

## WRIST FRACTURES

### Epidemiology

Although relatively infrequent, scaphoid fractures are the most common carpal bone fractures in children as well as adults, with a peak incidence in the preadolescent and adolescent age group. The lower incidence of scaphoid fractures in children may be due to the relatively thick cartilaginous cover of the developing ossification center, which protects the scaphoid. Furthermore, anatomy and ossification pattern affect which regions of the scaphoid are susceptible to injury. The scaphoid ossifies from distal to proximal, explaining the greater frequency of distal pole fractures in young children. Previous studies demonstrated that distal pole scaphoid fractures are most common in children, followed by distal third fractures and waist fractures.The patterns of scaphoid fractures are changing, however, owing to the greater athletic and functional demands of children, resulting in an increasing frequency of scaphoid waist fractures. In their retrospective analysis of 351 scaphoid fractures in children and adolescents treated from 1995 to 2010, Gholson et al. noted that 71% of fractures occurred at the scaphoid waist. Distal and proximal pole fractures accounted for 23% and 6%, respectively, of that cohort. The authors concluded that with changes in activity patterns and patient characteristics, pediatric scaphoid injury patterns are currently more similar to adults.

Unlike carpal injuries, distal radial fractures are among the most common fractures in children and adolescents, constituting approximately 20% to 35% of pediatric fractures. Similarly, distal radial physeal fractures account for approximately one- third of all physeal fractures, second in frequency only to physeal fractures of the phalanges. These fractures occur most often in patients between the ages of 10 and 14 years, in part due to the increased porosity of the distal radial metaphysis during accelerated skeletal growth. In addition, recent evidence suggests that the incidence of distal radial fractures in pediatric patients may be increasing due to greater sports participation and higher rates of childhood obesity. The vast majority of distal radial fractures occur from a fall onto an outstretched upper limb. The wrist position (extension versus flexion) and amount of force imparted influence the ultimate fracture pattern and displacement.

### Preoperative Evaluation

As in adults, fractures of the scaphoid may be subtle in clinical presentation. There may be minimal deformity or swelling, but the presence of focal, reproducible tenderness at the anatomic snuffbox or scaphoid tubercle should alert the examiner to a possible carpal fracture. Additional clinical signs of a scaphoid fracture include pain on axial loading of the thumb metacarpal, pain during forceful pinch, and a positive scaphoid compression test.

A suspected scaphoid fracture requires standard AP and lateral wrist radiographs and an additional image dedicated to the scaphoid. An AP radiograph of the wrist with ulnar deviation and oblique views of the wrist allow better visualization of the scaphoid (scaphoid view). A frequently encountered clinical scenario is anatomic snuffbox tenderness and negative plain radiographs, raising the question of an occult scaphoid injury. In these situations, brief immobilization in a thumb spica cast or splint is recommended, followed by repeated clinical and radiographic evaluation in 2 to 3 weeks. If persistent tenderness is noted and the plain films are negative, advanced imaging with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is recommended to confirm or exclude a scaphoid fracture (Fig. 41.21).

Similarly, patients with other fractures of the carpus may present with subtle complaints after relatively innocuous traumatic injuries. Careful observation and systematic clinical examination—with specific palpation of each bony structure of the carpus and wrist—should guide the subsequent diagnostic workup and treatment.

Unlike fractures of the carpus, the diagnosis of distal radial fractures is not subtle. Patients present with pain, swelling, and deformity of the affected wrist following trauma. Clinical examination of the skin is performed to assess for open injuries. A neurovascular examination is performed to assess for injury or impending compartment syndrome. Compartment syndrome is particularly prevalent in children with both a forearm and elbow fracture (“floating elbow” injuries) (Fig. 41.23). Plain wrist radiographs usually confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment. A thorough radiographic evaluation should be performed, x- raying the joint above and below the injury. Relatively innocuous findings, such as ulnar styloid fractures or plastic deformation, can affect the treatment necessary. For example, the classic Galeazzi fracture- dislocations are uncommon in children. The equivalent pediatric pattern is distal radial fractures accompanied by distal ulnar physeal or styloid fractures. Identification of associated injuries guides treatment decisions regarding both nonoperative and operative strategies. In general, imaging of the entire forearm, including the elbow, is performed to evaluate for concomitant ipsilateral upper extremity fractures, which may occur in up to 13% of cases.

Distal radial fractures are generally characterized according to anatomic location (metaphysis, physis, epiphysis, intraarticular) and amount of displacement, angulation, and rotation. In general, it is important to remember that the metaphyseal Thurston- Holland fragment in type II fractures lies opposite the disruption in the periosteum. This anatomic point assists fracture reduction maneuvers and guides the surgical approach when open operative treatment is necessary.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

#### Ossification Centers

The radiographic appearance of the carpal centers of ossification occurs in a predictable, ordered sequence. The capitate and hamate typically appear at 6 to 8 months of age. The triquetrum ossifies at 2 to 3 years of age, followed by the lunate at 4 years. The scaphoid becomes radiographically apparent at 4 to 5 years of age, followed by the trapezium and trapezoid at 5 years. The pisiform is the last carpal bone to ossify, typically at 6 to 8 years of age.

The scaphoid ossific nucleus appears between age 4 and 5 years.The bony scaphoid forms via enchondral ossification from distal to proximal until the bone is fully ossified, typically by age 14 to 16 years. Multiple ossification centers may appear, and bipartite scaphoids have been described. As discussed earlier, the gradual distal to proximal ossification process may explain the predisposition for distal pole fractures in young children and more adult patterns of scaphoid fractures (waist and proximal pole fractures) in older children and adolescents.

The secondary center of ossification of the distal radial epiphysis becomes radiographically apparent by the first year of life, whereas the distal ulnar epiphysis appears at approximately 6 years of age. The distal ulnar epiphysis often arises from two distinct centers of ossification, giving rise to a bipartite appearance that may be confused with an ulnar styloid fracture. In general, the distal ulnar physis closes at 16 years in females and 17 years in males, with the distal radial physis closing, on average, 6 months later. Knowledge of this timing and the variable patterns of ossification assist in distinguishing acute skeletal injuries from developmental norms. Although the radiographic appearance of the distal radius and ulna changes with development, similar parameters delineate “normal alignment” in both children and adults. Evaluation of radial inclination, radial length, ulnar variance, and volar tilt is appropriate in all but the youngest patients.

#### Growth

Because the distal physis contributes approximately 70% to 80% of the longitudinal growth of the radius and 40% of the upper extremity, distal radial fractures have tremendous inherent remodeling potential.Prior investigations confirmed that up to 10 degrees of dorsal volar angulation per year may remodel with continued skeletal growth.As with other fractures of the developing skeleton, remodeling potential is dependent on the amount of skeletal growth remaining, distance from the bony injury to the adjacent physis, magnitude of the deformity, and plane of the deformity. Fractures close to the distal radial physis with mild to moderate angulation in the sagittal plane in younger patients have the greatest remodeling potential. Therefore in patients younger than 10 to 12 years, 20 to 30 degrees of angulation in the sagittal plane and 50% to 100% translation of the distal radial fracture fragment may remodel with continued skeletal growth. Radioulnar angulation in the coronal plane is less well tolerated and has less remodeling capacity. Therefore greater than 10 degrees of angulation in the AP plane may not remodel with continued skeletal growth.

#### Soft Tissue Structures

A number of soft tissue structures impart stability to the wrist and must be considered in the treatment of skeletal injuries. Carpal stability is imparted by both extrinsic and intrinsic ligaments, with similar anatomic and pathologic considerations as in adults. A discussion of carpal instability due to ligamentous injury is beyond the scope of this chapter (see Chapter 15 for further information). Owing to the relative mechanical strength of these ligaments in relationship to the adjacent bony structures, intrinsic carpal ligamentous injuries are far less common in children than in adults. Furthermore, due to the gradual ossification process of the carpus in skeletally immature patients, the “normal” radiographic parameters used to identify intrinsic ligament disruptions (e.g., scapholunate ligament tears) may not be applicable in younger children.

The triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) refers to a confluence of structures on the ulnar side of the wrist, including the triangular fibrocartilage, dorsal and volar radioulnar ligaments, meniscal homologue, ulnolunate and ulnotriquetral ligaments, and subsheath of the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon. Functionally, the TFCC provides a smooth articular surface in support of the ulnar carpus and stabilizes the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ). In ulnar- neutral wrists, approximately 20% of the axial load across the wrist is borne by the ulnar carpus through the TFCC. Small changes in ulnar variance—as might be seen with traumatic or posttraumatic deformity—may result in considerable alterations in loads transmitted across the TFCC and ulnar wrist.

### Scaphoid Fractures

As in adults, treatment of scaphoid fractures in skeletally immature patients is dependent on the fracture location, fracture pattern, and degree of displacement. Distal pole fractures have excellent healing potential due to the vascularity of the distal pole of the scaphoid. Thumb spica cast immobilization for 4 to 6 weeks is adequate, particularly in cases of incomplete or avulsion injuries (Fig. 41.24).

Nondisplaced waist fractures can be treated with cast immobilization, with union rates greater than 90% (see Fig. 41.21). However, cast immobilization for 2 to 3 months may be required for healing secondary to the limited retrograde vascular supply to the scaphoid body.

The optimal type of cast immobilization continues to be debated.Incorporation of the thumb into a spica cast has been advocated by some to eliminate forces across the scaphoid, which may inhibit healing or cause displacement. In addition, immobilization of the elbow eliminates forearm rotation, which has been demonstrated to induce scaphoid fracture motion. Gellman and associates performed a prospective randomized study comparing long- versus short- arm thumb spica casting for the treatment of acute nondisplaced scaphoid waist fractures. Patients treated with long- arm casts for the first 6 weeks had a faster time to healing (9.5 versus 12.7 weeks) and lower nonunion and delayed union rates compared with those patients treated with short arm casts alone. Other recent investigations have demonstrated no significant differences in times to union between short- and longarm casts. Several biomechanical and clinical studies evaluated the effect of wrist position on scaphoid healing during casting. There is no consensus regarding the correct position of extension/flexion or radial/ulnar deviation.

As in adults, there is growing enthusiasm for percutaneous screw fixation for nondisplaced injuries allowing a faster return to sports and activities. However, adult principles do not necessarily apply to the pediatric patient, and there is some evidence that percutaneous screw fixation of nondisplaced injuries in children and adolescents does not decrease time to union. Discussion of percutaneous screw fixation is beyond the scope of this chapter; however, considerations and surgical techniques are similar to those in adults (see Chapter 18).

In cases of displaced waist fractures or fractures of the proximal pole, surgical treatment with open reduction and internal stabilization is recommended. Because these injuries tend to occur in older children and adolescents, the treatment principles and surgical techniques are similar to those in adults. Internal stabilization with smooth Kirschner wires, headed compression screws, or variable- pitch headless compression screws have all been used safely and effectively in children.

Scaphoid fracture nonunion does occur in skeletally immature patients, due to either late presentation or inadequate immobilization of acute injuries (Fig. 41.25). Gholson et al. performed a retrospective analysis of 351 scaphoid fractures in children and adolescents and found that 29% of patients presented greater than 6 weeks from injury. Typically, these are older adolescent patients with waist or proximal pole fractures. Treatment options for pediatric scaphoid nonunions range from nonoperative to operative strategies. Historically, cast immobilization has been advocated in late presenting injuries. Bone grafting via the Matti- Russe technique, Kirschner wire fixation, and compression screw fixation have also been reported, with successful outcomes in the majority of cases, consistent with the published adult literature (Case Study 41.1). Autologous bone grafting is common to facilitate healing and/or provide structural support.

Reconstructive procedures using pedicled vascularized bone grafts have been recommended in these rare but difficult situations. There is little information concerning the natural history of scaphoid fracture malunions (e.g., “humpback” deformity) in skeletally immature patients. There have been reports of spontaneous correction of secondary carpal instability and remodeling of scaphoid malunions; however, there is insufficient information to make a definitive statement regarding treatment.

### Capitate Fractures

Capitate fractures are rare and typically result from high- energy mechanisms of injury, such as motor vehicle collisions or falls from great heights. Occasionally, capitate fractures occur in association with a greater arc perilunate fracture- dislocation injury. Thus the x- ray should be inspected for concomitant lesser or greater arc injuries. Radiographs usually confirm the diagnosis, but advanced imaging studies (e.g., CT, MRI) are helpful to identify fracture displacement and concomitant injuries (Fig. 41.26).

Treatment is predicated on fracture pattern and displacement. Nondisplaced capitate fractures are effectively managed with cast immobilization. Displaced injuries require open reduction and internal fixation using smooth pins or compression screws. Associated pathology (e.g., lesser or greater arc perilunate fracture- dislocations) should be addressed simultaneously.

### Triquetrum Fractures

Triquetral body fractures are rare and occur in the setting of greater arc perilunate fracture- dislocations. Isolated dorsal avulsion fractures do occur, particularly in older adolescents (Fig. 41.27). The greater arc injuries require open reduction and internal fixation, but the isolated fractures can be treated with a splint or cast immobilization for 3 to 4 weeks.

### Hamate Fractures

Hamate fractures are relatively uncommon in the pediatric population. Fractures of the hamate hook predominate, secondary to falls. Patients present with point tenderness along the palm and hook of the hamate, along with mild swelling or localized ecchymosis. Patients with chronic hamate hook fractures may complain of paresthesias or exhibit weakness in an ulnar nerve distribution secondary to the proximity of the ulnar nerve. In addition, they may complain of pain during small and ring finger flexion secondary to irritation of the adjacent flexor digitorum profundus tendons.

Standard radiographs may not delineate the fracture, and the carpal tunnel view may be helpful in suspected cases. CT is the standard of care for the diagnosis (Fig. 41.28). Although open reduction and internal screw fixation have been advocated by some authors, shortarm cast immobilization for 4 to 6 weeks often alleviates the symptoms and allows a full return to activities, despite persistence of a radiographic fibrous nonunion. In persistent cases of symptomatic nonunion, fracture fragment excision is performed for relief of symptoms, although athletes should be advised of the possibility of scar sensitivity during grasping their bat, racket, or club. The surgical approach for either fixation or excision is a standard zigzag approach to the Guyon canal or through a carpal tunnel release incision. The hamate hook is a deep structure, and ulnar neurovascular structures must be protected. Hence, the incision is always longer than expected.

### Distal Radial Fractures

Distal radial fractures are characterized by their anatomic location, fracture pattern, and magnitude of displacement, angulation, and rotation. Fractures may involve the distal radial metaphysis or physis. Metaphyseal injuries can be further subdivided into torus or “buckle” fractures and bicortical fractures. Each fracture pattern has its own set of treatment principles and considerations.

#### Torus Fractures

In torus fractures of the distal radial metaphysis, cortical failure occurs in compression (Fig. 41.30). As a result, these injuries present with minimal deformity and are inherently stable. Brief immobilization with a short- arm cast or a wrist splint for 3 weeks provides adequate symptomatic relief. Multiple randomized prospective trials have demonstrated that true torus fractures can be treated safely and effectively with splint immobilization removed by the parents after 3 weeks, without the need for additional clinical or radiographic follow- up.

> **Fig. 41.30** AP (A) and lateral (B) radiographs of a distal radius buckle or torus fracture. There must be two points of inflection, no cortical breech, and the fracture must not lie within 1 cm of the growth plate in order to truly be considered a buckle fracture.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: TORUS FRACTURES

Torus fractures are treated with short- arm splint immobilization for 3 weeks, followed by resumption of activities. Radiographs are unnecessary at the time of cast removal because these fractures are inherently stabile. Likewise, no follow- up clinical evaluation is necessary after cast immobilization is discontinued.

#### Bicortical Metaphyseal Fractures

Bicortical fractures of the distal radial metaphysis occur secondary to bending, rotational, or shear forces imparted on the wrist. Fractures may be transverse, spiral, or oblique and may present with considerable displacement (Fig. 41.31). While bayonet apposition will remodel reliably in young children, fractures with unacceptable angulation or translation are typically treated with closed reduction and cast immobilization. Depending on the patient and care setting, this may involve hematoma block, conscious sedation, or even general anesthesia. Because of the thick periosteum in younger patients, reduction maneuvers consist of exaggeration of the deformity, followed by longitudinal traction and reversal of the mechanism of injury (Fig. 41.32). The thick, intact periosteum acts as a tension band in facilitating reduction and conferring stability. However, exaggerating the deformity slackens the periosteum and allows restoration of length and alignment. Straight traction alone will not suffice. Application of a well- molded cast with three- point bending or an appropriate interosseous mold is critical in maintaining reduction and minimizing the risk of later displacement. Excessive wrist flexion should be avoided.

The optimal position of forearm rotation in a long- arm cast remains controversial. Some advocate pronation, given that pronation may better correct rotational malalignment and maintain fracture reduction in the more common apex volar injury. Others advocate supination to allow improved fracture molding and relaxation of the brachioradialis. Still others advocate immobilization in neutral, citing an improved ability to apply an interosseous mold and theoretically minimize loss of motion at the extremes of forearm rotation.

Two prospective, randomized clinical trials compared the efficacy of short- versus long- arm casts in maintaining reduction following closed fracture manipulation. Both investigations concluded that short- arm casts are as efficacious in maintaining fracture alignment as long- arm casts; however, careful analysis of the data reveals a number of important points. First, more important than the length of cast is the quality of fracture reduction and cast molding. Indeed, in one study of 103 patients, risk for loss of fracture reduction was higher in patients with a cast index greater than 0.7. In addition, fractures that were incompletely reduced (i.e., had residual displacement or angulation following manipulation) were at higher risk for subsequent loss of reduction, emphasizing the importance of the reduction maneuver over the type of immobilization. Patients with concomitant fractures of the distal ulna were at higher risk for loss of reduction, regardless of casting technique. Finally, the effect of long- arm casting on the ability to perform activities of daily living is not inconsequential. Short- arm casts were associated with fewer days of missed school and less assistance required during daily activities. Subsequent studies and metaanalyses have confirmed these findings.

Despite successful initial closed reduction and cast immobilization, late displacement of fractures is common. Prior published series reported loss of reduction rates between 10% and 91%. In general, approximately one- third of all distal radial fractures requiring reduction will demonstrate late displacement. Factors implicated in the loss of reduction include initial displacement (bayonet apposition, >50% translation, >30 degrees of angulation), isolated distal radial fracture, associated same- level ulnar fracture, inadequate initial closed reduction, poor casting technique, muscle atrophy, and resolution of initial soft tissue swelling while in the cast. Hence, serial radiographic evaluations must be performed, initially on a weekly basis, to assess bony alignment and rule out loss of reduction.

Efforts have been made to characterize the quality of fracture reduction and casting techniques to predict which injuries might be more prone to later displacement (Fig. 41.33). The cast index refers to the ratio between the diameters of the cast at the level of the fracture, as measured on lateral and AP projections. Used as a measure of the quality of the cast mold, cast indices greater than 0.7 have been associated with an increased risk of loss of fracture reduction. A gap index has also been proposed to assess the quality of cast molding, as well as excessive cast padding and resolution of soft tissue swelling.The gap index is calculated by dividing the aggregate distance between the skin and cast material by the overall diameter of the cast. If the sum of the gap indices as measured on both AP and lateral radiographs is greater than 0.15, there is a higher risk of loss of fracture reduction with a sensitivity of 88%. Three- point index has recently been proposed as a more sensitive and specific radiographic measure of cast mold and predictor of subsequent displacement. Axis deviation has also been proposed to assist in risk stratification and is discussed in the following section on forearm diaphyseal fractures.

The high rate of loss of fracture reduction following closed fracture manipulation and cast immobilization has led some authors to question whether all fractures deemed at risk for late instability should be treated with surgical stabilization. McLauchlan and colleagues performed a prospective, randomized clinical trial of 68 children treated with closed reduction and cast immobilization versus immediate percutaneous pinning. Although loss of reduction occurred in 21% of the casting cohort and in 0% of pinning patients, equivalent clinical outcomes were reported. Miller and associates similarly performed a prospective, randomized trial of closed reduction and cast immobilization versus pin fixation in 34 patients older than 10 years with severely displaced distal radial fractures. Thirty- nine percent of casted patients had loss of reduction requiring remanipulation and 38% of the pinning cohort had minor pin- related complications, including superficial pin tract infections and superficial radial sensory nerve irritation. Clinical outcomes were equivalent, however, and there was no significant difference in overall cost analysis. At this time, broad treatment recommendations cannot be made based on the limited evidence available. Certainly, when performed with meticulous technique, pinning of severely displaced distal radial fractures is safe and efficacious. However, owing to the frequency of these injuries, the remodeling potential of the distal radius, and equally efficacious results with proper cast immobilization, routine pinning of all displaced pediatric distal radial fractures is not currently recommended.

Indications for surgical treatment of pediatric distal radial fractures include open fractures, unstable or irreducible fractures, floating elbow injuries, and fractures with soft tissue or neurovascular compromise precluding cast immobilization (Fig. 41.34). Open reduction and surgical stabilization are also indicated in the rare, displaced Salter- Harris type III, IV, or triplane fractures to reconstitute the articular surface and restore the integrity of the physis. Despite the growing enthusiasm and experience with open reduction and volar locking plate fixation for displaced extraarticular and intraarticular fractures in adults, the majority of pediatric distal radial fractures can be managed successfully using percutaneously placed pins. Smooth pin fixation is typically performed with an oblique pin directed distal to proximal and radial to ulnar, entering the radial metaphysis proximal to the physis, avoiding iatrogenic injury to the distal radial physis. A second oblique pin or dorsoulnar pin entering between the fourth and fifth extensor compartments can be added to provide additional fracture stability, but this is not always necessary. If adequate fixation cannot be achieved with physis- sparing pinning techniques, transphyseal pinning or intramedullary fixation across the physis into the epiphysis have been reported, with successful outcomes and a low rate of complications. In addition, intrafocal pinning similar to Kapandi’s technique can be performed with success. External fixation via wrist spanning constructs is an option for cases of extensive soft tissue injury (e.g., crush injuries with soft tissue loss) or to supplement comminuted fractures with inadequate internal fixation.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: BICORTICAL METAPHYSEAL FRACTURES OF THE DISTAL RADIUS

In displaced bicortical metaphyseal fractures of the distal radius, closed reduction and cast immobilization are advocated while the patient is under conscious sedation. Our preference is to immobilize with long- arm casts to mitigate the motion imparted by forearm rotation. Long- arm casts are applied well- molded (cast index <0.7, gap index <0.15). Although the position of forearm rotation remains controversial, we prefer a long- arm cast with the forearm in neutral rotation.

Serial radiographs in the cast are obtained at 1 week, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks following fracture manipulation to assess the fracture reduction.

If adequate alignment is maintained and fracture callus is apparent, patients are transitioned to a short- arm cast at 4 weeks. Following 2 weeks of short- arm casting, final radiographs are obtained and cast immobilization is discontinued.

If fracture reduction is lost, we prefer to proceed with remanipulation and percutaneous pin fixation. Prior studies supported that transphyseal pinning can be performed without a high risk of growth arrest. However, we still attempt to avoid the physis by placing the pin proximal to the growth plate in an oblique direction (see Fig. 41.34). With the patient under general anesthesia, the fracture is manipulated, and bony alignment is restored. A small 1- cm incision is made along the radial border of the distal radius, roughly at the level of the distal radial physis. Careful blunt dissection in the subcutaneous tissues is performed and the superficial radial sensory nerve is retracted and protected. Smooth Kirschner wires are then passed from the distal fracture fragment just proximal to the physis, traversing the fracture and engaging the proximal ulnar cortex. A single pin is often sufficient; however, additional pins can be placed for greater stability, avoiding crossing the pins at the fracture site. The pins are bent and cut outside the skin followed by long- arm cast application. The pins are removed in the office at 4 weeks after radiographic confirmation of fracture callus formation. The patient is transitioned to a short- arm cast for an additional 2 weeks.

#### Physeal Fractures of the Distal Radius

Distal radial physeal fractures represent approximately 20% of all growth plate fractures in children, second in frequency only to those of the phalanges. Remodeling potential, treatment principles, and surgical indications are similar to those for distal radial metaphyseal fractures with several additional considerations. Specifically, reduction maneuvers should be performed gently, with adequate analgesia or sedation, to avoid iatrogenic physeal injury and growth disturbance of the distal radial physis. Similarly, multiple reduction attempts or repeated late reduction attempts are discouraged to minimize the risk of physeal arrest. If fracture reduction is lost late during the period of cast immobilization, the deformity is often observed for bony remodeling with skeletal growth. If adequate alignment is not achieved, late corrective osteotomies are considered to correct the deformity and avoid secondary carpal instability patterns. Prior studies suggested that fractures with less than 15 degrees of angulation and up to 1 cm of shortening will completely remodel without functional impairment in skeletally immature patients.

The overall risk of physeal arrest after distal radial physeal fracture, even following the foregoing principles, is approximately 4%.In displaced distal ulnar physeal fractures, the risk of posttraumatic growth disturbance is higher, approaching 50%. Patients and families should be counseled about this risk, and follow- up radiographs are mandatory to assess for physeal arrest.

Distal radial physeal arrest may lead to ulnar overgrowth, abnormal wrist mechanics, ulnocarpal impaction, TFCC tears, and DRUJ instability. In symptomatic patients, surgical treatment options include physeal bar resection, ulnar epiphysiodesis, ulnar shortening osteotomy, corrective radial osteotomy, or combinations thereof, depending on the pattern of arrest, severity of deformity, and amount of remaining skeletal growth (Box 41.2).

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PHYSEAL FRACTURES OF THE DISTAL RADIUS

Displaced fractures of the distal radial physis are reduced under conscious sedation and immobilized in a long- arm cast with appropriate three- point molding. Fracture reduction is usually stable and maintained in a cast. Excessive wrist flexion in the cast is avoided to minimize the risk of neurovascular compromise due to swelling. If late loss of reduction is noted on serial radiographs, the deformity is accepted and families are counseled regarding remodeling potential. The possibility of corrective osteotomy for persistent malalignment is addressed closer to skeletal maturity. Repeated forceful manipulations are avoided. Owing to the risk of physeal arrest, patients with distal radial physeal fracture are reexamined 6 months after injury with repeat radiographs.

Surgical indications for distal radial physeal fractures include irreducible fractures, fractures associated with soft tissue compromise, and fractures with neurovascular injury precluding cast immobilization. Closed reduction and pinning techniques are similar to those described for metaphyseal injuries (Video 41.4).

Distal ulnar physeal fractures are often seen in association with distal radial fractures and possess a high risk for subsequent growth arrest. Although appropriate reduction of the radial fracture usually reduces the ulnar fracture, intervention is recommended for persistent translation greater than 50% or angulation greater than 20 degrees. Failure to obtain a closed reduction often indicates soft tissue interposition at the fracture site. After extrication of the soft tissue, fixation is accomplished with smooth pin fixation for 4 weeks.

The principles for treating distal ulnar styloid fractures are similar to those applicable to adults. Fractures through the base are associated with injury to the TFCC and DRUJ instability. Unstable injuries are best treated with open reduction and internal fixation to avoid late DRUJ instability. These injuries occur in older adolescents, and smooth pining with tension band fixation is recommended (Fig. 41.35).

### Postoperative Expectations and Management

Torus fractures reliably heal without functional deficits. Displaced distal radial fractures require reduction and cast immobilization for realignment. Subsequent healing is generally uneventful. Open reduction for irreducible distal radial fractures results in union and an acceptable outcome.

Excellent functional outcome is expected with adherence to the foregoing principles and surgical technique. Complications of distal radial and ulnar fractures include fracture malunion, nonunion, synostosis, physeal arrest, and neurovascular compromise.

The relatively high rate of late fracture displacement following initial closed reduction results in distal radial malunions. The tremendous remodeling potential of the distal radial physis encourages observation, especially in the young child. In cases of persistent deformity of the distal radius or ulna, corrective osteotomy is indicated.

Fracture nonunions are rare in children. Nonunion of the distal radius or ulna is due to disruption of the biologic healing potential, which may be seen following open fractures or in children with intrinsic bone pathology (e.g., neurofibromatosis, congenital pseudarthrosis). In these situations, open reduction and internal fixation with autogenous corticocancellous bone graft or vascularized bone grafts are needed for healing.

Posttraumatic radioulnar synostosis is uncommon following distal radial and ulnar fractures in children. Cross- union occasionally develops after fractures with excessive soft tissue or periosteal stripping of same- level fractures. Established synostosis with functionally limiting loss of forearm rotation may be amenable to resection with or without soft tissue.

Physeal arrest occurs in 4% of distal radial physeal fractures and 50% of distal ulnar physeal fractures. Furthermore, there have been case reports of growth arrest after metaphyseal fractures. Monitoring and treatment of posttraumatic physeal arrest should follow the principles and techniques previously described. In cases of established distal radial growth arrest and ulnar overgrowth, corrective osteotomies with or without associated soft tissue procedures can alleviate symptoms due to ulnocarpal impaction, TFCC tears, or DRUJ instability (Case Study 41.2). Distraction osteogenesis and physeal bar resection have also been advocated in selected patients. Waters and coworkers published their results in 30 adolescents with posttraumatic distal radial growth arrest. Combinations of ulnar shortening osteotomies, ulnar epiphysiodeses, and radial corrective osteotomies were performed to restore normal bony alignment and wrist mechanics. Considerable improvements in wrist motion, pain, and function were noted.

## FOREARM DIAPHYSEAL FRACTURES

Forearm fractures are common injuries and the most common childhood skeletal injury requiring surgery. Despite their frequency and the plethora of information available regarding their management, the treatment of forearm fractures remains challenging. There is a wide range of fracture patterns, a diversity of nonoperative and surgical techniques, varying degrees of skeletal growth and bony remodeling, and ever- changing patient and family expectations. Furthermore, interpreting the published information regarding the treatment and outcomes of forearm diaphyseal fractures is difficult to decipher. Historically, almost all pediatric forearm fractures were treated nonoperatively. Recent information regarding functional outcome, however, has challenged the traditional tenets of forearm fracture care. The desire

for an expedient return to full activity and along with normal motion and strength has added to the treatment challenge. Furthermore, the advent of new instrumentation and techniques have expanded surgical treatment options.

### Epidemiology

Forearm fractures are common, occurring in approximately 1 of 100 children each year. When fractures of the distal radius and ulna are considered as a distinct injury, diaphyseal forearm fractures represent the third most common long bone fracture in children, following distal radial and supracondylar humeral fractures. Peak age of incidence is 12 to 14 years. Diaphyseal forearm fractures are the most common open fractures of the upper extremity and represent the most common location of refracture in the pediatric population.

The mechanism of injury is usually a fall onto an outstretched upper limb, imparting both axial loads and rotation or bending forces to the forearm. The position of the limb at the time of injury and the forces applied dictate the pattern of diaphyseal fracture. Radial and ulnar fractures at the same level result from predominantly bending forces, whereas fractures at different levels occur due to rotational forces. The radius fracture is typically proximal to the ulna fracture as the load is transmitted via the interosseous membrane. High- energy torsional mechanisms can cause both skeletal injuries and soft tissue disruption of the ligamentous complexes stabilizing the proximal and DRUJs (e.g., Monteggia and Galeazzi fracture- dislocations).

### Preoperative Evaluation

Patients present with pain, deformity, swelling, and limited forearm motion after a fall onto an outstretched upper limb. Inspection of the overlying skin and soft tissue is mandatory to assess for an open fracture. Furthermore, a thorough neurovascular examination is performed to rule out associated nerve palsies, vascular insufficiency, or impending compartment syndrome. The wrist and elbow are also carefully assessed for a possible ligamentous injury (e.g., Monteggia fracture- dislocation) or concomitant ipsilateral upper extremity fracture (e.g., “floating elbow”).

Standard AP and lateral radiographs of the forearm—including visualization of the wrist and elbow—are performed in all cases of suspected forearm fracture. The x- rays are evaluated to ensure that there is no disruption of the DRUJ or radiocapitellar (proximal radioulnar joint) articulation. A congruent relationship should exist between the distal radius and ulna on both AP and lateral projections, and the longitudinal axis of the radius should bisect the capitellar ossification center on all views.

Diaphyseal fractures of the forearm are classified according to anatomic location (proximal, middle, or distal thirds), fracture displacement and angulation, and fracture pattern. In general, three patterns of diaphyseal fractures occur: plastic deformation, greenstick or incomplete fractures, and complete fractures. Each fracture pattern has its own set of treatment considerations.

Plastic deformation, or traumatic bowing, macroscopically presents as bones that are “bent but not broken.” Typically, this type of injury occurs during the first decade of life, and there is often little gross deformity, swelling, or ecchymosis. There are varying degrees of pain and tenderness. Radiographic findings may be subtle, characterized by abnormal curvature of the radial or ulnar shafts in association with narrowing of the interosseous space (Fig. 41.38A). Understanding of the normal radiographic appearance of the radius and ulna, particularly the normal radial bow, is imperative to identify plastic deformation. Histologically, plastic deformation injuries are characterized by slip lines, parallel zones of disruption between bent or wrinkled fiber bundles, and disruption of canaliculi. Biomechanically, plastic deformation injuries occur as the load applied to the bone exceeds the bone’s elastic limits but does not exceed its ultimate strength. As a result, the material properties of the bone enter the plastic region of the stressstrain relationship. As the load is withdrawn, plastic deformation remains along with deformity (see Fig. 41.38B).

Greenstick fractures represent the intermediate stage of bony failure between plastic deformation and complete fractures (Fig. 41.39). Radiographically, one to three cortices may be disrupted on orthogonal views with the remaining cortices intact or bowed. These injuries result from torsional mechanisms, and the apex of the bony angulation provides insight into the rotational deformity. In general, apex dorsal fractures are caused by hyperpronation and therefore are reduced with forearm supination. Conversely, apex volar fractures are due to hypersupination and are best reduced with pronation. The “rule of thumbs”—in which the thumb is rotated toward the apex of the deformity—has been proposed to guide care providers in choosing the appropriate reduction maneuver. For example, in the common apex volar angulated fracture, rotating the thumb imparts pronation of the distal fracture fragment, thus counteracting the hypersupination injury. In situations when the radius and ulna are fractured at the same level, pure bending is more likely to be the mechanism of injury, and reduction can be achieved with simple three- point molding. Complete fractures involve complete cortical disruption and are akin to their adult counterparts in terms of mechanism of injury, clinical presentation, and radiographic appearance.

Although a number of classification systems exist to describe pediatric forearm fractures, few are practical for guiding treatment or providing prognostic information. For this reason, anatomic descriptions of the fracture location, fracture pattern, degree of angulation, and displacement along with any associated soft tissue injuries are employed. In general, there are two bones (radius, ulna), three levels (proximal, middle, distal), and four fracture patterns (plastic deformation, greenstick, complete, comminuted). Although rotational alignment is important to consider and may affect the ultimate outcome in terms of forearm rotation, malrotation is difficult to quantitate using radiographic indices and may be incorrect by as much as 45 degrees.

### PERTINENT ANATOMY

The radius is a complex, S- shaped bone with approximately 15 degrees of apex ulnar angulation in the region of the radial neck and a gentle bow of 10 degrees of apex radial angulation in the middiaphyseal region. The bicipital tuberosity and radial styloid lie approximately 180 degrees from each other, an important relationship that aids in the assessment of rotational malalignment. The ulna is triangular in cross- section and predominantly straight in its longitudinal alignment, with the exception of a mild apex radial bow in the proximal olecranon region. The ulnar styloid and coronoid process lie approximately 180 degrees from each other, likewise aiding in the estimation of rotational deformity. Normal forearm rotation averages 150 to 180 degrees and occurs around an axis between the radial head and distal ulna.

The nutrient artery of the radius enters in the volar proximal half of the radius and travels in a distal ulnar direction. The nutrient artery of the ulna enters in the volar proximal half and courses distal and radial. Awareness of the location of these nutrient vessels avoids misdiagnosing these radiolucencies as fractures.

Soft tissue connections between the radius and ulna impart stability and are implicated in limiting forearm rotation in patients with stiffness despite near- normal radiographic alignment. These connections constitute the ligamentous stabilizers of the distal and proximal radioulnar joints as well as the interosseous ligament (see Chapter 16 for further discussion of these soft tissue restraints and their disorders).

### Historical Review

The majority of the available literature guiding diaphyseal fracture treatment and anticipated outcomes is based on the desire to achieve bony healing and avoid loss of forearm motion or esthetically displeasing results.

Esthetic problems following diaphyseal malunion are caused by deformity of the ulna, owing to its subcutaneous location and straight longitudinal shape. Conversely, loss of forearm rotation is often a result of a radial malunion, with or without concomitant ulnar deformity. Although loss of pronation is typically compensated for by shoulder abduction, forearm supination is poorly accommodated owing to the thorax, blocking attempted shoulder adduction. Multiple published series have demonstrated that diaphyseal fracture malunions contribute to loss of forearm rotation and functional impairment.Cadaveric studies have demonstrated that whereas 10 degrees of angulation results in minimal loss of forearm rotation, greater than 10 degrees of angulation truly impedes forearm rotation. Proximal forearm deformity imparts greater restrictions in forearm rotation, and less angular or rotational deformity is tolerated in more proximal fractures compared with more distal fractures.

Rotational deformities, though difficult to quantify by x- ray, cause concomitant loss of forearm rotation. For every degree of rotational deformity, 1 to 2 degrees of forearm rotation is lost. In general, ulnar malrotation does not alter the overall arc of forearm rotation but does change the “set point” of forearm motion. Conversely, radial malrotation affects the overall range of forearm supination- pronation. Clinical outcomes regarding forearm rotation do not always directly correlated to radiographic alignment.

Bony remodeling with continued skeletal growth adds additional complexity to treating pediatric diaphyseal fractures of the radius and ulna. Remodeling is a complicated process with multiple elements including reorientation of the adjacent physis with continued physeal growth, bony lengthening that reduces the apparent angulation between the proximal and distal diaphyseal segments, and appositional periosteal bone formation at the concavity of the deformity. As with bony remodeling of other long bones, remodeling is greatest in younger patients, with fractures close to the adjacent physis, and with deformity in the plane of adjacent joint motion. In general, up to 20 degrees of diaphyseal angulation can remodel in children younger than 8 years. Beyond the age of 10 years, more than 10 degrees of angulation is unlikely to remodel.

Review of the literature provides guidelines as to how much radiographic deformity will remodel with growth. Up to 1 cm of shortening may be accepted with little impact on forearm rotation, perhaps due to relaxation of the interosseous membrane. Complete or 100% translation reliably remodels in younger patients with fractures of the middle and distal thirds. Radioulnar angulation or translation is less well tolerated, particularly in older children with less remaining growth secondary to narrowing of the interosseous space and possible bony impingement resulting in loss of forearm rotation. Mehlman and Wall proposed the “20- 15- 10” rule for determining acceptable angulation in radial and ulnar fractures. Dividing the forearm into thirds, 20, 15, and 10 degrees of angulation may be accepted in the distal, middle, and proximal thirds of the forearm, respectively.

Other radiographic measurements have been proposed to predict the final position of diaphyseal forearm fractures following reduction and cast immobilization along with the ultimate outcome. Younger and associates proposed that axis deviation be used to predict outcomes and thus guide when closed reduction or surgical intervention is required (see Fig. 41.33). Axis deviation is defined as the distance between the fractured and anatomic axes of the bone measured at the fracture site, divided by the total length of the bone multiplied by 100. An axis deviation greater than 5 at the time of bony healing (i.e., cast removal) has been correlated with restricted forearm motion even after completion of skeletal growth and bony remodeling. The theoretical advantages of the axis deviation index are that the measurement eliminates the confounding variables of radiographic projection, magnification, and fracture location and does not rely strictly on radiographic angulation or translation to predict functional loss.

Because clinical motion does not directly correlate with radiographic alignment, a more compelling and relevant question is how much deformity is acceptable for a reasonable functional outcome. Historically, studies have demonstrated that 10 degrees of angulation, complete translation, and 30 to 45 degrees of malrotation may be well tolerated without functional limitations. These data require careful interpretation, however, as it is difficult to quantify “reasonable function” in the setting of objective losses in forearm rotation.

Treatment goals for pediatric radial and ulnar diaphyseal fractures are to achieve bony union while preserving forearm motion and minimizing complications. Although surgical techniques and implants have improved, the importance of skilled closed reduction and artful cast application cannot be overemphasized.

### Plastic Deformation

Some authors believe that closed reduction should be attempted for all plastic deformation injuries with greater than 20 degrees of angulation in patients older than 4 years. Plastic deformation has limited remodeling potential as the periosteum is not elevated or disrupted and the apex of fracture deformity are often a distance from the adjacent physes. Prior studies demonstrated that forces as high as 30 kg sustained over several minutes are required to correct traumatic bowing. As a result, controlled three- point bending under general anesthesia or well- administered conscious sedation over a fulcrum (e.g., sandbag or rolled towel) is recommended to achieve reduction.

Good casting technique is imperative for maintaining reduction and optimizing outcome. Casts should be applied with a straight ulnar border, interosseous mold, and three- point molding over the apex of the deformity. The resulting cast is often esthetically displeasing to the patient and family; however, “curved plaster is necessary in order to make a straight limb.” With appropriate reduction and casting, up to 85% correction of the deformity can be expected, with minimal long- term restrictions in forearm motion.

In rare situations, a large amount of plastic deformation is not correctable, despite vigorous closed reduction. In these situations, percutaneous pin or drill osteoclasis at the apex of the deformity is performed, followed by reduction and appropriate cast immobilization. This technique converts a plastic deformation injury into an incomplete or complete fracture to effectuate reduction.

### Greenstick Fractures

Greenstick fractures are effectively treated with closed reduction and well- molded cast immobilization in the majority of cases. Following an analysis of the radiographic findings and understanding of the causative mechanism of injury (e.g., pronation, supination, bending), closed reduction is performed by reversing the rotational deformity followed by correction of the angulation. An appropriately applied cast with appropriate interosseous and three- point molding is critical in maintaining fracture reduction (see Fig. 41.39). There is ongoing controversy regarding the need to complete the greenstick fracture by disrupting the intact cortex at the concavity of the deformity. Proponents of fracture completion cite the theoretical advantages of easier anatomic realignment, lower risk of loss of reduction, and lower refracture rate owing to the exuberant periosteal callus formation during the healing process. Opponents cite the greater fracture instability and difficulty maintaining reduction after fracture completion.

### Complete Fractures

In patients with complete fractures of the radius and ulna diaphyses and unacceptable deformity, closed reduction is initially recommended. With adequate analgesia and sedation, closed reduction is performed by first exaggerating the deformity to relax the intact periosteum, applying longitudinal traction, and correcting the rotational malalignment, then reversing the angular deformity. A long- arm cast with appropriate interosseous and three- point bending mold is applied.

The position of cast immobilization is debated. Although the majority of casts are applied with the elbow flexed 90 degrees, long- arm extension casts have been used with great success in proximal third fractures with apex posterior or apex ulnar angulation (Fig. 41.40).If extension casts are used, addition of a thumb spica component and placement of a D- ring with a neck strap help prevent distal migration of the cast during walking and upright activity.

The optimal position of forearm rotation is even more debatable, with little published information to provide guidance. Theoretically, proximal one- third fractures of the radius may be better immobilized in supination. The proximal fragment is typically supinated by the unopposed action of the biceps and supinator muscle. Hence, forearm supination realigns the distal fragment relative to the proximal fragment. Similarly, middiaphyseal fractures occurring proximal to the insertion of the pronator teres may be better immobilized with the distal segment supinated. Conversely, fractures distal to the pronator teres insertion may be best immobilized in neutral rotation or forearm pronation.

Serial radiographs are recommended weekly for the first 2 to 3 weeks to ensure maintenance of reduction. Loss of reduction may be seen in 5% to 25% of fractures and may occur up to 4 weeks after injury. Cast wedging may be considered in patients with loss of reduction attributed to fracture angulation. Provided adequate fracture reduction is maintained, patients can be safely transitioned to shortarm casts at 4 weeks. Short- arm casting is continued until 6 weeks after injury. Some recommend a brief period of splint immobilization after cast removal. The patients and families are counseled about refracture risk and judicious choice of activities.

Surgical indications for diaphyseal fractures of the forearm include open fractures, soft tissue swelling or neurovascular compromise that precludes circumferential cast immobilization, floating elbow injuries, and irreducible or unstable fractures that fail nonoperative treatment. In addition, refracture is considered a relative indication for surgical stabilization, given the difficulties in achieving and maintaining fracture reduction. Although there are numerous surgical options, percutaneous pinning techniques are generally insufficient and external fixation is poorly tolerated. As a result, most forearm diaphyseal fractures are currently treated with intramedullary fixation (Fig. 41.41) or internal fixation with plate and screw constructs (Fig. 41.42).

Intramedullary fixation is a well- accepted technique for forearm diaphyseal fracture stabilization in children and adolescents.Smooth stainless steel Kirschner wires, titanium elastic nails, Rush rods, and Steinmann pins have all been used with reported success, emphasizing the importance of surgical principles over implant choice. All radial implants can be prebent to facilitate restoration of the normal radial bow. Although length and angular correction is achieved by fracture reduction and intramedullary stabilization, rotational stability is typically conferred by the intact periosteum and surrounding soft tissues. Intramedullary devices are introduced into the canal via small incisions and passed across the fracture site after closed reduction or limited open reduction. This technique preserves the soft tissue envelope and avoids extensive periosteal stripping, thus preserving the robust biologic healing potential in children.

Radial intramedullary implants are typically introduced into the distal metaphysis either just proximal to the Lister tubercle or between the first and second extensor compartments. Ulnar intramedullary devices are introduced in the proximal ulnar metaphysis or through the olecranon apophysis. Maximal stability is achieved if the intramedullary nails are passed to within 1 cm of the far physis. Following intramedullary fixation, implants are cut beneath the skin, leaving protrusion from the bone enough to facilitate removal. Less commonly, implants may be bent and cut outside the skin to allow easy removal in the office setting. Early removal of implants outside the skin may obviate the need for a second anesthetic but poses a risk for infection and refracture if removed early. If implants are cut beneath the skin, elective removal is recommended 6 to 12 months after the index procedure.

In young patients, single bone fixation of both bone fractures has been proposed. Single bone fixation is indicated when after intramedullary nailing of either the ulna (more commonly) or the radius, the adjacent bone is aligned and stable during intraoperative fluoroscopic evaluation of elbow and forearm motion. If these criteria are met, single bone fixation supplemented by cast immobilization can be performed.

Formal open reduction and internal fixation using plate and screw constructs can also be performed, and although often reserved for older adolescents with less than 2 years of remaining skeletal growth (i.e., boys older than 14 and girls older than 12), some authors contend that the radial bow can be better recreated with open reduction and internal fixation. The surgical technique is similar to that in adults, with several modifications. First, adequate stability may be achieved with four cortices of fixation proximal and distal to the fracture site, particular if supplemented by postoperative cast immobilization. In addition, given the smaller size of the radius and ulna in children and adolescents, size- appropriate implants (2.4- or 2.7- mm compression plates, one- third tubular plates) should be used. Large implants should be avoided to lessen the stress risers at the ends of the plate. The theoretical advantages of plate and screw fixation include the ability to achieve anatomic reduction and maximize return of full forearm rotation, particularly in older adolescents with little skeletal remodeling potential. Furthermore, given the mechanical stability achieved, early motion can be initiated, perhaps mitigating the soft tissue contractures that may contribute to loss of motion. Disadvantages of plate fixation include the need for more extensive dissection, slower bony healing, and increased soft tissue scarring.

The need for implant removal after bony healing remains controversial. Some advocate the routine removal of plate and screw constructs after bony healing of forearm fractures, citing the risks of refracture, growth alteration, metallic allergies, and adverse effects on bone density due to mechanical stress shielding. Others cite the low frequency of symptomatic implants and complication rates of up to 40% as arguments against implant removal from the upper extremity in children.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: BOTH- BONE FOREARM FRACTURES

For children and younger adolescents, intramedullary fixation is performed for open fractures, unstable fractures, floating elbow injuries, and fractures in which circumferential casting is impossible or undesired (Fig. 41.43). General anesthesia and intravenous antibiotics are used. Attention is first turned to the ulna. Fluoroscopy is used to assess the ability to achieve a closed reduction, and the intramedullary canal size is evaluated to ensure use of the proper size Kirschner wire or titanium elastic nail. The narrowest canal diameter is best seen on the lateral projection of the radius and ulna. If titanium elastic nails are used, the surgeon should recognize that the tips of the nails are typically curved. This curve facilitates intramedullary manipulation and capture of the distal fracture fragment, but it also increases the effective working diameter of the nail.

AP (E) and lateral (F) radiographs at 2 months demonstrating good alignment and healing.

The ulna is treated first, in part because of the ease of intramedullary rod placement and because restoring ulnar length and alignment improves the alignment of the radius via soft tissue connections. A small stab incision is created over the tip of the olecranon process, just radial to the center to account for the apex radial bow of the proximal ulna or in the anconeus region. If using a metaphyseal starting site (see Fig. 41.41C), the anconeus muscle is gently spread until the starting point is determined. If using the olecranon starting site (see Fig. 41.43C), the triceps is incised in line with the surgical incision, and a drill or smooth wire is used to create a corticotomy for intramedullary rod placement. Care is taken to avoid penetrating the ulnohumeral joint, which typically requires drilling more dorsally than anticipated. The appropriately sized implant is then passed in an antegrade fashion down the canal of the ulna until it reaches the fracture site. The ulna is reduced, and the nail is passed down the canal of the distal fragment. The implant is passed down until the tip of the rod resides 5 to 10 mm from the distal ulnar physis.

Attention is then turned to the radius. We favor the radial entry site between the first and second extensor compartments. A small 2- cm incision is made over the “bare spot” of the dorsoradial metaphysis, just proximal to the distal radial physis. Subcutaneous dissection is performed to identify and retract the superficial sensory branch of the radial nerve. The retinaculum overlying the first dorsal compartment is identified and incised. A small periosteal window is created, and a drill is used to create a corticotomy for nail placement. Creation of an oblique path (distal radial to proximal ulnar) greatly facilitates nail placement. The appropriately sized nail is then precontoured to restore the radial bow and passed up the intramedullary canal in a retrograde manner to the level of the radial fracture. Closed reduction is performed, and the nail is passed into the canal of the proximal radial fracture fragment until the tip lies at the level of the bicipital tuberosity. Multiplanar fluoroscopic images are obtained to confirm the adequacy of reduction, stability, and implant placement.

Multiple forceful attempts at closed reduction and nail passage are discouraged, owing to the risk of increased soft tissue swelling and subsequent compartment syndrome. If closed reduction and intramedullary nail passage cannot be achieved after several attempts, an open reduction is performed through a small incision centered over the radial fracture site, using the brachioradialis–flexor carpi radialis or brachioradialis–pronator teres interval distally or proximally, respectively.

Although the majority of surgeons cut the nails under the skin until elective removal 3 to 6 months postoperatively, we prefer to bend them and leave the rods extending outside the skin. The theoretical risk of soft tissue irritation and infection has not been a considerable problem, given that a sterile bandage is applied and the limb is immobilized with a long- arm cast following surgery. Buried nails result in a small incidence of soft tissue irritation and tendon disruption, particularly if a dorsal entry site (extensor pollicis longus) is used for the radius. Furthermore, removal of buried rods may not be a trivial procedure.

The cast is bivalved to allow for postoperative swelling, and the intramedullary nails are removed at 4 to 6 weeks in the office or in the operating theater under sedation or brief general anesthesia, followed by an additional 2 weeks of cast immobilization. Early rod removal theoretically increases the risk of refracture. This problem has not been a common occurrence; however, families are counseled about refracture risk and appropriate activity modification.

In rare situations, particular in younger children, single bone fixation may suffice. If the radius assumes an acceptable position after ulnar rod placement, single bone fixation can be considered. Although alignment may be improved, it is imperative that the stability of the radius reduction be checked with elbow and forearm motion under fluoroscopic visualization. If the radius is unstable, nailing of both bones is performed.

For patients with less than 2 years of skeletal growth remaining, we favor open reduction and internal fixation using plate and screw constructs for middle and proximal third fractures with more than 15 and 10 degrees of angulation, respectively (see Fig. 41.42). In adolescent patients, 3.5- mm implants may be too wide or too large for the radius or ulna, so smaller implants (2.7- or 2.4- mm implants) are used. Two one- third tubular plates can also be stacked on each other to increase the bending rigidity. Four to six cortices of fixation are achieved with standard cortical screws; locking screws are rarely needed in this patient population for fractures of the radius and ulnar diaphysis. If adequate stability is achieved, patients are immobilized in a bivalved short arm cast or splint, and early supervised range of motion is initiated. We do not routinely remove plate and screw constructs after healing, as the morbidity and risk of plate removal in the upper extremity outweigh the benefits.

### Single Bone Fractures

True single bone fractures of the radius or ulna (i.e., without associated soft tissue disruption of the distal or proximal radioulnar joints) result from direct blows. Owing to the stabilizing effect of the adjacent intact bone, reduction maneuvers are difficult. With fracture displacement and angulation, particularly when the apex of deformity is directed toward the interosseous space, failure to correct the bony alignment may result in loss of forearm rotation due to bony abutment. Reduction maneuvers consisting of longitudinal traction, varus- valgus stress using the intact bone as an indirect means of controlling the distal fracture fragment, and subsequent direct pressure at the fracture site to correct translation have been proposed in these situations.

### Open Fractures

Open fractures merit special comment. Several issues distinguish the treatment of these injuries from their closed counterparts. First, open fractures result from higher- energy forces with greater periosteal and soft tissue disruption. This leads to greater instability and the need for surgical stabilization. Second, there is a higher incidence of associated neurologic injuries and compartment syndromes with open fractures. Surgical stabilization obviates the need for circumferential cast immobilization and allows care and monitoring of the soft tissues. In addition, when intramedullary fixation is performed, prolonged or repeated forceful attempts to achieve reduction and nail placement are discouraged to lessen the risk of compartment syndrome. Finally, open fractures carry the inherent risk of soft tissue contamination and subsequent infection. Although the overall rate of infectious complications is low, early and thorough irrigation and debridement are requisite components of open fracture care.

### Monteggia Fracture- Dislocations

Monteggia fracture- dislocations are fractures of the ulna associated with proximal radioulnar joint dissociation and radiocapitellar dislocation. These injuries constitute less than 1% of all pediatric forearm fractures, typically affecting patients between 4 and 10 years of age. The mechanism of injury is classically described as a fall onto an outstretched hand with forced rotation of the forearm.

Acutely, patients present with pain, swelling, deformity, and limitations in elbow motion. A neurovascular examination is critical as 10% to 20% patients have evidence of a neurologic deficit at presentation, usually involving the posterior interosseous nerve as the radial head commonly dislocates in an anterior direction. Accurate diagnosis and timely intervention allow reduction and soft tissue healing with excellent functional outcomes. Unfortunately, despite the increased awareness and understanding of Monteggia fracture- dislocations, the initial diagnosis is often missed. Late reconstruction is technically challenging and outcomes are often suboptimal. Therefore a high index of suspicion is paramount.

The radiocapitellar articulation must be assessed during the radiographic evaluation of any pediatric forearm fracture. Normally, the longitudinal axis of the radius should bisect the capitellar ossification center on all radiographic views. Careful assessment of the radiocapitellar joint is particularly critical during the initial treatment of “isolated” ulnar fractures. Similarly, in cases of presumed isolated radial head dislocations, abnormal ulnar bowing or plastic deformation must be sought. Although congenital radial head dislocations occasionally present as “acute” injuries, the presence of a hypoplastic capitellum and convex radial head usually confirms the congenital diagnosis.

Understanding of the pathoanatomy of Monteggia fracturedislocations is critical in providing appropriate treatment. In the majority of cases, the annular and quadrate ligaments are disrupted at the proximal radioulnar joint. In contrast, the majority of the interosseous membrane and the entirety of the TFCC remains intact. Anatomic reduction of the ulna often restores proximal radioulnar and radiocapitellar joint congruity. For this reason, treatment is predicated on the nature of the ulnar fracture. Historically, the Bado classification has been used to describe these injuries, based on the direction of the radial head dislocation and the apex of the associated ulnar fracture.This classification scheme may aid in both describing the pattern of injury and guiding care.

In acute lesions, the method of treatment is determined by the pattern of ulnar fracture (Table 41.1). In patients with plastic deformation, typically a greenstick ulna fracture, closed reduction under general anesthesia is recommended. In these situations, closed reduction of the ulna is typically performed with a combination of longitudinal traction, forearm rotation, and direct pressure applied to the apex of the fracture. The radiocapitellar joint usually reduces spontaneously as the ulna is straightened. The need to apply substantial force to the radial head should raise the suspicion of interposed soft tissue blocking radiocapitellar joint reduction. In the most common Bado type I lesions, for example, the radial head is anteriorly dislocated, and the ulna has apex volar angulation. Correction of the ulnar deformity followed by posteriorly directed pressure on the radial head, elbow flexion, and forearm supination is performed. Typically, the ulnar fracture and radiocapitellar joint are stable after reduction of plastic deformation or incomplete fractures. A well- molded above- elbow cast is then applied. Elbow x- rays are obtained weekly for the first 2 to 3 weeks to ensure maintenance of radiocapitellar joint reduction. The long- arm cast is removed between 4 and 6 weeks and range- of- motion exercises are initiated.

In patients with complete transverse or short oblique fractures of the ulna, closed reduction alone frequently results in loss of reduction while in the cast. Therefore, closed reduction and intramedullary fixation of the ulna are recommended to maintain ulnar alignment and radiocapitellar reduction (see Fig. 41.43). In these cases, an ageand size- appropriate stainless steel Steinmann pin, Kirschner wire, or flexible titanium nail can be utilized. Via a small longitudinal split in the triceps tendon overlying the olecranon process, this nail is inserted into the intramedullary canal of the ulna and advanced in an antegrade fashion to the fracture site. The ulnar fracture is reduced, and the nail is advanced into the intramedullary canal of the distal fracture fragment, allowing restoration of both ulnar length and alignment. During ulnar reduction and fixation, closed reduction of the radiocapitellar dislocation is also performed. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirms appropriate ulnar and radiocapitellar reduction. An above- elbow cast is then applied with the elbow in 90 degrees of flexion and the forearm in supination, which confers additional stability to the radiocapitellar articulation. Cast immobilization and intramedullary fixation are continued for 4 to 6 weeks to allow annular ligament and bony healing. After radiographic confirmation of bony healing, the cast is discontinued, the intramedullary nail is removed, and range- of- motion exercises are begun.

Long oblique or comminuted ulnar fractures cannot be adequately stabilized with intramedullary devices. In these cases, open reduction and internal fixation with plate and screw constructs are necessary, even in young patients with open physes. Through a longitudinal incision based on the subcutaneous border of the ulna and centered on the fracture site, reduction is performed, and a one- third tubular or small fragment plate is applied in the standard fashion. Four to six cortices of fixation proximal and distal to the fracture provide adequate stability in pediatric patients. Radiocapitellar joint congruity is achieved during reduction and fixation of the ulna fracture. If closed reduction of the radiocapitellar joint is unattainable, an open reduction may be facilitated by extending the ulnar incision in a proximal direction over the posterolateral aspect of the elbow. Cast immobilization for 4 to 6 weeks with the forearm in supination is required following surgery.

### Galeazzi Fractures

Galeazzi fractures are distal radius fractures associated with DRUJ disruption. Although such injuries are rare in children, Galeazzi equivalents may present with distal ulnar physeal fractures or ulnar styloid avulsions. These injuries result from falls onto an outstretched upper extremity with a combination of axial load and rotational moment. Patients present with pain and restrictions in forearm rotation. Radiographs reveal incongruity of the DRUJ, best seen on a true lateral view of the wrist. Descriptions of Galeazzi fractures are based on the direction of ulnar displacement. However, it is important to remember that the axis of forearm rotation lies from the radial head to the ulnar styloid, and in fact, it is the radius that displaces in relation to the ulna.

Unlike in adults, children with Galeazzi fracture- dislocations and their equivalents can be successfully treated nonoperatively. In general, restoration of anatomic radial alignment using the reduction maneuvers described earlier yields a congruent and stable DRUJ. Closed reduction maneuvers are predicated on the pattern of injury. In situations where the ulna is dorsally displaced in relation to the radius and there is apex dorsal angulation of the radial fracture, three- point bending combined with forearm supination is the reduction maneuver. Conversely, for apex volar radial fractures where the ulna is volar, pronation is applied.

If anatomic reduction is achieved, a long- arm cast is applied for 6 weeks, with weekly radiographic evaluation to confirm maintenance of reduction. In irreducible cases or in older adolescents with less than 2 years of growth remaining, formal open reduction and internal fixation of the radius with appropriate treatment.

### Postoperative Expectations and Management

The outcome following a forearm fracture is dependent on the injury and reduction, but adherence to the previously stated principles and techniques results in bony healing and restoration of motion in the majority of patients. Internal fixation of irreducible, open, or unstable forearm fractures reliably restores anatomy and motion. Monteggia fracture- dislocations require prompt recognition and appropriate treatment for an acceptable outcome, as chronic Monteggia fractures are much more difficult to manage (Figs. 41.44 to 41.46).

Failure to adhere to the previously stated principles often results in fracture malunion (see Fig. 41.39). Price and colleagues reported on a series of 39 children with malunions following closed treatment of displaced forearm fractures. Average patient age at the time of injury was 9.3 years (range, 1 to 16 years). Average follow- up was 5.7 years, with a minimum 2- year follow- up. Malunion was defined as radiographic angulation greater than 10 degrees, greater than 50% displacement, malrotation, or encroachment of the interosseous space. Clinical results were characterized as excellent (no limitations in activity, loss of forearm rotation <10 degrees), good (mild complaints with strenuous physical activity, loss of forearm rotation <30 degrees), fair (mild complaints with activities of daily living, 31- to 90- degree loss of forearm rotation), and poor. Despite malunions, 82% of patients had excellent results, 10% had good results, and 8% had fair results; there were no poor outcomes. All patients were reportedly “satisfied” with esthetics and function, regardless of age or radiographic findings, which did not correlate with final clinical motion. Although no correlation was seen between age or fracture displacement and outcome, the authors observed that angulation, shortening, narrowing of the interosseous space, and proximal fracture location were associated with worse functional results. Based on their observations, the authors concluded that up to 10 degrees of angulation, complete translation, complete loss of radial bow, and 45 degrees of malrotation may be well tolerated with minimal esthetic or functional consequences. Similar findings have been reported by others.

Closing wedge osteotomies are preferred because they are technically more reproducible than single- cut oblique osteotomies. Refracture of diaphyseal fractures of the forearm occurs in 4% to 8% of patients. Refracture typically occurs in males approximately 6 months after the index injury. Patients and families should be counseled about the refracture risk at the time of cast removal. In active males, a transitional period of splint immobilization should be considered to minimize refracture risk. In cases of refracture and unsuccessful in closed reduction, surgical treatment is recommended. If intramedullary fixation is to be performed, consider open reduction and drilling of the intramedullary canal to create a path for nail passage, given the prior fracture callus and interval healing. In these situations, internal fixation with plate and screw constructs may be preferable.

Compartment syndrome is a rare but potentially devastating complication of diaphyseal forearm fractures. Early recognition and prompt treatment with forearm fasciotomies are critical to avoid permanent sequelae. Diagnosis may be difficult, particularly in a young, agitated, or nonverbal child. Instead of the traditional signs of compartment syndrome seen in adults, a rising analgesic requirement, increasing agitation, and anxiousness (the 3 A’s) are indicative of an impending compartment syndrome.

## Case Studies

### CASE STUDY 41.1 Scaphoid Nonunion

A 10-year-old right-handed patient hurt his left wrist 6 months previously. He complains of persistent pain and decreased range of motion (eFig. 41.1).

![[eFig.41.1.png]]
> **eFig. 41.1** A and B, Radiographs.

The diagnosis was scaphoid nonunion. The treatment options included:
- Long-arm cast
- Electrical stimulation
- Open reduction and vascularized bone grafting
- Open reduction and nonvascularized bone grafting

Open reduction and nonvascularized bone grafting (eFig. 41.2) was performed.

![[eFig.41.2.png]]
> **eFig. 41.2** A, Fingertrap distraction is applied to the thumb to stabilize the wrist and facilitate exposure. B, Curettage fracture. C, Distal radius bone graft. D, Kirschner wire joystick and angiocatheter as drill guide. E, Kirschner wire across nonunion site. F, Screw insertion and bone graft.

Postoperative and 3-month follow-up x-rays are shown (eFigs. 41.3 and 41.4).

> **eFig. 41.3** A and B, Postoperative radiographs.

### CASE STUDY 41.2 Distal Radius Growth Arrest With Ulnar Positive Variance

An 11-year-old right-handed boy hurt his left wrist 2 years previously while catching a wet Nerf ball. He complains of left wrist prominence (eFigs 41.5 through eFig. 41.8).

![[eFig.41.5.png]]
> **eFig. 41.5** A and B, Range of motion.

![[eFig.41.6.png]]
> **eFig. 41.6** Dorsoulnar prominence.

![[eFig.41.7.png]]
> **eFig. 41.7** A and B, Preoperative range of motion.

![[eFig.41.8.png]]
> **eFig. 41.8** A and B, Preoperative radiographs.

The diagnosis was physeal bar radius with growth arrest, and ulnar positive variance. The treatment options were as follows:
- Observation
- Bar resection
- Radial lengthening
- Ulnar epiphysiodesis

Because of the patient's age and sick physis, the treatment selected was ulnar epiphysiodesis and radial lengthening (eFig. 41.9).

![[eFig.41.9.png]]
> **eFig. 41.9** Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging.

Postoperative and 3-month follow-up x-rays are shown (eFigs. 41.10 and 41.11). Clinical photos are shown in Fig. 41.35M and N, and eFig. 41.12.
