## SYNDACTYLY

### Epidemiology

Syndactyly, one of the most common congenital abnormalities of the hand, occurs in approximately 1 to 2000 live births. Syndactyly occurs bilaterally in 50% of cases, with a 2:1 ratio of males compared with females and 10 times more frequently in Caucasian compared with African American patients. In approximately 30% of cases, syndactyly is inheritable as an autosomal dominant trait with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

Syndactyly can occur in association with other upper extremity musculoskeletal anomalies including cleft hand, ulnar longitudinal deficiency, symbrachydactyly, and synpolydactyly or as part of a syndrome. Poland syndrome is the association of a hypoplastic hand with symbrachydactyly and hypoplasia of the pectoralis major muscle. Constriction ring sequence (CRS) may present with terminal syndactyly when the tips of the digits are joined by a soft tissue bridge producing a jumble of fingertips (acrosyndactyly) or there is fenestrated syndactyly with proximal webbing and apparent sinuses (pseudosyndactyly). Acrocephalosyndactyly disorders (e.g., Apert syndrome, Chotzen syndrome), acrocephalopolysyndactyly disorders (Noack syndrome, Carpenter syndrome), and orofaciodigital and oculodentodigital disorders often have a component of syndactyly (Fig. 36.1).

![[Fig.36.1.png]]
> **Fig. 36.1** Oculodentodigital dysplasia showing a typical face, carious teeth, and complex syndactyly.

In isolated syndactyly, the third web space is the most commonly affected (55%), followed by the ring- small finger web space (25%), index- long web space (15%), and thumb- index web space (5%). In syndromic syndactyly, the involvement of web spaces is more equally distributed.

### PATHOANATOMY

Syndactyly is a failure of apoptosis of the interdigital mesenchyme. The mesenchymal apoptosis typically proceeds from distal to proximal between gestational days 47 to 54. Skin and subcutaneous tissues are normal but the fascia bridging the interdigital space is thicker compared with normal Grayson and Cleland ligaments.

The amount of skin covering the conjoined digits is inadequate to circumferentially cover each digit individually. The circumference of two separated digits is between 20% and 30% greater than that of two connected digits. However, in clinical practice, the lack of skin is more variable and depends on the complexity of the syndactyly and the number of digits involved in the syndactyly. Pinching the interdigital space can provide a general feeling of the amount of coronal skin and the “tightness” of the syndactyly.

In simple forms of syndactyly, the interdigital bridge is composed solely of soft tissues. In complex forms, bone comprises part of the fusion mass, and most commonly the distal phalangeal tufts are connected (Fig. 36.2A and B). When skeletal structures are aberrantly oriented and present between adjacent fingers, the syndactyly is classified as complicated (see Fig. 36.2C).

![[Fig.36.2.png]]
> **Fig. 36.2** Complex syndactyly is associated with bone union, most commonly the third phalangeal tuft (A), although other parts of the phalanx can be involved (B). Complicated syndactyly exhibiting disorganized bone tissue connecting adjacent fingers (C).

The various forms of syndactyly are classified according to the extent of digital union (incomplete versus complete) and the tissues involved (simple versus complex versus complicated). In incomplete forms of syndactyly, the skin bridge does not extend the entire length of the interdigital commissure (Fig. 36.3). Conversely, complete syndactyly involves the entire web, sometimes associated with fusion of adjacent fingernails, which is called a synonychia (see Fig. 36.3).

### Management

#### Surgical Timing

Syndactyly that involves the first (thumb- index) or fourth (ring- small) commissures or between size- mismatched fingers should be separated early, when the child is 3 to 6 months old (Fig. 36.4). Early separation is recommended because the shorter digit (thumb or little finger) has a slower rate of growth and will tether the adjacent finger leading to flexion contracture and rotational deformities (see Fig. 36.4). Conversely, syndactyly that exists between digits with a similar longitudinal growth rate (e.g., second [index- long] and third [long- ring] commissure) can be separated later. We prefer to separate such digits when the child is between the ages of 18 and 24 months. By that point, the hand has doubled in size since birth. This facilitates surgery and produces more predictable and reproducible outcomes.

![[Fig.36.4.png]]
> **Fig. 36.4** Annular digital flexion and rotation deformity as a result of little finger tethering in the context of fourth commissure syndactyly. Web release is recommended early.

#### Surgical Technique

Successful surgical treatment requires understanding the principles applicable to syndactyly separation. In addition, there are varieties of techniques to address the numerous obstacles to optimize the outcome.

**Premises.** The main surgical principles that guide syndactyly surgery:
- Surgical separation of complex or complicated forms of syndactyly that places the digits at risk for joint instability and diminished function should be avoided (Fig. 36.5).
- Commissure reconstruction requires supple and vascularized skin that will not interfere with digital motion in either flexion- extension or adduction- abduction. Skin grafting within the commissure should be avoided and will result in a narrow web that is prone to scar contracture and web creep. If possible, avoid placing a skin graft at the base of the finger. Techniques that recruit local skin as flaps and do not use skin grafts can be employed (see section Graftless Techniques below), which may decrease the risk of web creep (Fig. 36.6).
- Longitudinal incisions along the digits must be avoided. Subsequent scar contracture will lead to digital flexion contractures. Thus zigzag incisions are preferred.
- Removal of digital fat, while preserving the dorsal venous system, can facilitate finger closure. However, overaggressive defatting should be avoided as the child inevitably loses fat as he or she ages, and removing too much fat can result in an excessively thin finger.
- Complete multiple digit syndactyly that involves two adjacent web spaces should be staged. Only one web space should be separated per surgery. This reduces the risk of digital flap necrosis and vascular complications.
- Preoperative torsional and coronal malalignment of a digit will persist in the long term unless addressed at the time of surgery (Figs. 36.6 and 36.7).

The initial incision is made on the dorsum of the digits. Identification of the volar vessels is also from the dorsal approach, which is facilitated by spreading the interdigital space with a curved hemostat (Fig. 36.8). This allows for safe vessel identification followed by digital defatting dorsal to the neurovascular bundle.

![[Fig.36.8.png]]
> **Fig. 36.8** Identification of digital vessels from the dorsal approach allows for a safe, easier, and faster defatting.

The level of division of the common digital vessels is identified. When a distal division occurs, one of the proper digital vessels can be ligated. Ligation is only done if the involved digit maintains perfusion after vessel clamping and tourniquet release. This situation is commonly encountered in the first web syndactyly where the radial proper digital artery of the index finger requires ligation to enable adequate widening of the first web space.

**Commissure reconstruction.** The *standard technique,* combining the use of skin flaps with full- thickness skin grafts, was first described by Cronin in 1943. Lennander pioneered the use of free skin grafts in syndactyly in 1890. Multiple variations have been described (Fig. 36.9). Full- thickness skin graft can be obtained from several locations (Fig. 36.10). Grafts obtained from the volar wrist provide an ideal color and texture match with finger skin (see Fig. 36.10A). A similar quality of skin can also be obtained from the medial arm, where a larger quantity of skin is available (see Fig. 36.10B). Not all donor skin is optimal. Full- thickness skin graft from the groin can result in hair growth and altered pigmentation in 70% of patients.

Using a hyaluronic acid scaffold for skin defects following syndactyly release has been reported recently, with excellent short- term results. Longer follow- up, however, is needed prior to uniform use to assess for delayed complications.

**Graftless techniques.** The rationale for graftless techniques lies in avoiding the complications arising from skin graft at the base of the fingers, which results in higher proportions of web creep.

Of the reported graftless techniques, all obtain extra skin from the dorsum of the hand (see Fig. 36.10C) or the dorsum of the digits (see Fig. 36.10D) or both. The “V- Y” dorsal metacarpal flap advances distal intermetacarpal skin to the web but may leave an unsightly longitudinal dorsal scar (Fig. 36.11). In the modified seagull flap technique, excess skin is obtained from the dorsum of the proximal phalanx and shifted to the base of the fingers. Closure leaves a transverse scar over the dorsum of the digits (see Fig. 36.6).

Comparative studies have confirmed that skin grafting procedures are associated with more complications (flap necrosis/graft failure, contracture, web creep, hypertrophic scarring) and a greater need for revision. However, graftless techniques may produce more unsightly dorsal scars leading to worse cosmetic results. In an attempt to overcome this disadvantage, we have described a vascularized volar intermetacarpal skin flap technique for coverage and widening of the commissure (Figs. 36.13 and 36.14). When performing this procedure, release of the cutaneous septa to the digital canal and release of the lumbrical canal is mandatory to obtain the appropriate flap mobility required for distal advancement. Our short- term preliminary results are promising, but long- term follow- up is necessary prior to uniformly recommending this technique.

**Thumb- index web space reconstruction.** Syndactyly involving the first web space requires early separation. Surgery is performed at approximately 6 months of age, both to avoid tethering deformities and to allow the development of thumb pinch. #### Complications

Acute intraoperative complications are uncommon. Vascular problems can occur because of too tight skin closure. Stitch release and supplemental skin grafting usually resolves this problem.

In contrast, long- term problems are common. Following classic grafting techniques, web creep rates have been reported as high as 8%. An additional 42% of patients experienced web thickening without creep (see Fig. 36.7B). Severe contractures can also occur (see Fig. 36.7C).

![[Fig.36.7.png]]
> **Fig. 36.7** Long-term complications following syndactyly release include web creeping (A), rotational deformity (B), and severe distal interphalangeal flexion contracture (C).

Residual deformities are especially common in complex syndactyly cases and are associated with poorer esthetic outcomes. Most separated digits in cases of complex syndactyly ultimately develop long- term lateral deviation or rotational deformities, as well as notable nail deformities (see Figs. 36.6F and 36.7).

Finally, keloid formation is common in separated digits that exhibit concomitant macrodactyly (Fig. 36.18). Surgeons need to be aware of this problem as keloid formation in this subset of patients can be prevented with methotrexate therapy.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: SYNDACTYLY

Second and third commissures are separated after the age of 18 months. First and fourth commissures are separated between 3 and 6 months of age. Our preferred skin grafting technique is the method described by Bauer (1956) and modified by Flatt. The commissure is reconstructed using a large trapezoidal dorsal random- pattern skin flap (see Fig. 36.9). Full- thickness skin graft is obtained from the volar wrist or from the medial arm if more extensive surface coverage is needed. A zigzag incision is preferred for digit separation with Buck- Gramcko coronal skin flaps for nail fold reconstruction.

Proper postoperative dressings are an essential part of syndactyly surgery. In young children, the hand dressing is reinforced by immobilization in an above- the- elbow plaster or soft fiberglass cast (Scotchcast, 3M, St. Paul, MN). The elbow is flexed to 100 degrees to prevent inadvertent removal. A supracondylar mold helps to keep the cast in place. The dressings are removed 3 weeks after surgery. The wounds are protected until they are dry and healed. Therapy is not usually required.

### Special Cases of Syndactyly

#### Digits in Constriction Ring Sequence

CRS is associated with syndactyly (acrosyndactyly and pseudosyndactyly), transverse defects (amputations), constriction rings, dorsal cutaneous nubbins, hypertrophic fibrous subcutaneous tissue, and first web contracture. Many of these features can be present in the same patient with CRS (Fig. 36.19).

![[Fig.36.19.png]]
> **Fig. 36.19** Acrosyndactyly in constriction ring sequence with a sinus tract or cleft from the dorsal to the palmar surface of the conjoined digits.

In unilateral presentations, amniotic band syndrome may be confused with symbrachydactyly. The absence of nail remnants in the foreshortened amputated fingers, the presence of constriction rings, and the symmetric size of the hand are hallmarks of a CRS.

Soft tissue connections in CRS are discontinuous, creating three distinct patterns:

1. Acrosyndactyly: The tips of the digits are conjoined distally, pro ducing a jumble of fingertips (Figs. 36.20 and 36.21).
2. Pseudosyndactyly or incomplete syndactyly: The syndactyly is interrupted or fenestrated by transfixing sinuses of varying size (see Figs. 36.20 and 36.21).
3. Nonadjacent finger syndactyly: distal syndactyly between two noncontiguous digits without syndactyly with the adjacent digit.

![[Fig.36.20.png]]
> **Fig. 36.20** Severe banding resulting in a conglomeration of digits with a vessel loop through a small sinus. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

![[Fig.36.21.png]]
> **Fig. 36.21** A to H, Constriction ring sequence combining pseudosyndactyly, amputations, constriction rings, dorsal cutaneous nubbins, and first web contracture. Index constriction was released with hemiresection and Z-plasty, syndactyly was released using a vascularized volar intermetacarpal flap, nubbins were resected, and, finally, first web was reconstructed using a Stiletto flap.

Syndactyly management (technique, timing) in CRS follows the previously discussed principles. Sinus tracts are excised or maintained as a part of the finger resurfacing depending on their size. Debulking, through excision of the hypertrophic subcutaneous tissue, is complex and often leads to “swollen,” unsightly digits (see Fig. 36.19). Excision of dorsal cutaneous nubbins is performed simultaneously with syndactyly release (see Fig. 36.21). Constrictions can also be treated at the same time. In these cases, a dorsal hemiband excision and Z- plasty is performed. A one- stage excision can be performed in instances where the band is less deep and the vascular structures are visualized and intact. In cases with severe distal edema, amputation may be necessary. Very distal constrictions, when associated with acrosyndactyly, may also be better treated with amputation. In some cases of acrosyndactyly with narrow soft tissue connections, separation can be performed very early and allowed to heal via secondary intention.

#### The Apert Hand

Apert syndrome combines a craniosynostosis and midfacial hypoplasia with complex syndactyly of the hands and feet. Apert syndrome is genetic and secondary to a mutation in the fibroblast growth factor receptor type 2 (FGFR2) gene located on chromosome 10q. Several other acrocephalosyndactyly syndromes have been identified; however, the hand deformities are less complex. Apert syndrome has other upper limb manifestations, including shoulder and elbow dysplasia with stiffness, which usually do not require surgical intervention.Prior to hand surgery, craniofacial anomalies should be addressed by neurosurgery and maxillofacial teams, usually around 3 months of age to improve anesthesia safety.

The hand deformity is classified according to the involvement of the first web space and the appearance of the central mass (Table 36.1). The Apert hand has several key problems that must be addressed including syndactyly (including the first web), synonychia, symphalangism and/or finger stiffness, thumb brachydactyly and clinodactyly, and ring- small intermetacarpal synostoses (type II to III) (Figs. 36.22 and 36.23).

There are many different treatment approaches to the Apert hand with variability between surgeons regarding timing and extent of management. The goal is to complete separation of the digits before 2 years of age to allow for unimpeded growth and functional hand development. Although one stage reconstruction has been described, most surgeons have adopted staged reconstructive strategies. The first stage is performed around the age of 9 months with subsequent surgeries occurring every 3 to 6 months.

Reconstruction of first web space is the first priority and is performed during the first stage. The goal is to position the thumb ray in 45 degrees of abduction. This requires release or lengthening of tight structures including the skin, fascia, intrinsic muscles, and carpometacarpal (CMC) joint (Fig. 36.24). Type I and II hands first web space skin deficits can be addressed with a four flap Z- plasty. Type III or II with severe first web space contractures require a dorsal advancement rotation flap. Authors have proposed preliminary tissue expansion on the dorsum of the hand. A pedicled or free flap is also an option to provide a wide first web reconstruction (Figs. 36.25, 36.26, and 36.27).

A distally curved central bone mass, if present, is also osteotomized into the phalangeal components during the first stage via a fish mouth incision. This converts the hand into the flat “spade” configuration (type I deformity) and allows for untethered growth of the central digits.

The order and number of finger syndactyly releases and the need for ray resection depend on the type of deformity and surgeon preference. In the type III Apert hand, a ray resection of the index, long, or ring ray are all possibilities. The advantage of resecting the index ray, especially if radially deviated, is increasing the width of the first web space. The advantage of resecting the ring ray and the intermetacarpal synostosis is increasing small finger motion. There are no comparative functional studies between four- and three- fingered Apert hands.

An option in the Apert hand is a dorsal commissure flap combined with longitudinal incisions. This technique separates the fingers and leaves a longitudinal scar. However, the fingers are uniformly stiff in extension at the interphalangeal (IP) joints and do not develop flexion contractures. The soft tissue envelope of the Apert hand is usually extremely tight and fails to stretch (may be related to the fibroblast growth factor mutation). Separation results in extensive skin defects requiring coverage. This large amount of skin graft can be obtained from the lower abdomen and/or the medial arm. Soft tissue expansion in the coronal plane with an external fixator (Cube fix) to avoid skin grafting has been reported with good results.

Most authors address thumb clinodactyly during the second stage. Thumb deformity is the result of delta- shaped and often fused phalanx and the abnormal insertion of the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) onto the distal phalanx causing metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint radial deviation. Reconstructive options include:
- A radial Z-plasty with opening wedge osteotomy and bone graft of the thumb phalanx. Bone graft sources include the iliac crest, any resected metacarpal, or bone from the resected intermetacarpal synostosis. Some authors perform this surgery after the age of 6 years to decrease recurrence rate.
- Release of the abnormal APB insertion with reinsertion in the proximal phalanx combined with realignment osteotomy.

The small finger ray is typically the least dysplastic and most mobile digit. However, intermetacarpal synostosis between the small and ring fingers lessens the mobility and prohibits the small finger from reaching the thumb (type II and III Apert hands). Synostosis resection and interposition improves small finger motion for opposition. The risk of synostosis recurrence is decreased when resection is performed after 5 years of age.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: THE APERT HAND

Hand surgery is not performed until cranial issues have been addressed for a safe anesthesia.

Type I and II Apert hands are less urgent compared with a type III hand. Surgery is staged with the first stage at 9 to 12 months (first and third commissures) and second stage 6 months later (second and fourth commissures), ring metacarpal ray resection if necessary (type III hand).

For the first web space, we prefer a four-flap Z- plasty for type I to II and Ghani flap for type II to III. For the second to fourth webs, we prefer straight incisions and dorsal rectangular flap. Skin graft is obtained from the medial arm.

Thumb clinodactyly correction and synostosis resection are addressed after the age of 5 years.

#### Poland Syndrome

Poland syndrome is a nonhereditary congenital difference. There is a male and right-sided predominance. Subclavian artery disruption in embryogenesis has been postulated as etiology. A similar cause has been implicated in Klippel- Feil syndrome, Möbius syndrome, and Sprengel deformity (16%). Dextrocardia is present in left- sided cases.

There is unilateral hypoplasia or absence of pectoralis musculature. The sternocostal head of pectoralis major muscle is absent in 30% of the cases, and the entire pectoralis major is absent in 70%. The chest wall deficit is associated with ipsilateral hand anomalies in 60% of the cases (Fig. 36.28).

![[Fig.36.28.png]]
> **Fig. 36.28** Poland syndrome with a complete absence of the pectoralis major muscle, hand size discrepancy, and a released symbrachydactyly.

There can be other concomitant deformities including unilateral nipple hypoplasia/agenesia (40%), breast hypoplasia/agenesia (20%), thoracic wall anomalies (30%), and periscapular muscles hypoplasia (latissimus dorsi, serratus anterior, infraspinatus, supraspinatus, deltoid muscle) (20%).

The most common hand deformity is short finger- type symbrachydactyly with mild thumb involvement including a first web contracture and a smaller hand (60%). Other more severe types of transverse deficiencies can also be seen. Forearm and arm size discrepancy is present in 40% and 10% of cases, respectively.

Syndactyly separation follows the aforementioned principles regarding timing and techniques. One stage reconstruction may be possible in short incomplete syndactyly using graftless techniques (Fig. 36.29).

![[Fig.36.29.png]]
> **Fig. 36.29** Poland syndrome with a complete absence of the pectoralis major muscle. A partial second web syndactyly was released using a graftless volar intermetacarpal flap technique.

### Expected Outcomes

Syndactyly release in children with simple syndactyly usually results is a natural- appearing web commissure with pliable skin, unrestricted function, and minimal web creep over time. However, the results may be complicated by contracture or scar hypertrophy, which downgrades the function and outcome. Web creep may require additional surgery to reposition the web. The results following syndactyly release for complex or complicated cases are less predictable. Underlying factors directly affect outcome including the extent and number of bony connections, the presence of a synonychia, concomitant symphalangism, and any associated anomalies. The surgical technique and the surgeon are also variables that directly affect outcome.

## ULNAR POLYDACTYLY

### Epidemiology

Ulnar or postaxial polydactyly prevalence and presentation varies between races. In African Americans, the prevalence is estimated to be 1 in 150 live births. In these patients, the ulnar polydactyly is frequently inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable penetrance, and only 1% are associated with syndromes. In Caucasian individuals, the incidence is 10 times less than in the African American population, and 10% have an underlying syndrome (e.g., chondroectodermal dysplasia or Ellis–van Creveld syndrome). Approximately 75% of cases are bilateral, and in two- thirds of cases, the feet are also involved. Arabic population prevalence and presentation are between Caucasian and African populations.

### PATHOANATOMY

The supernumerary digit is either well developed (type A, 25%) or rudimentary and pedunculated (type B, 75%) according to Temtamy and McKusick (Fig. 36.30). Several type A ulnar polydactyly subclassifications have been proposed as the presentation is variable (Fig. 36.31).

![[Fig.36.31.png]]

### Management

Caucasian individuals are referred to the geneticist for study and counseling. A small nubbin, considered a postaxial element type B, can be safely removed by tying the base with a suture or vessel clip in the nursery (Fig. 36.32). The digit will turn gangrenous and autoamputate. However, this method results in a residual bump in 40% of individuals, with an average diameter of 2 mm (range, 1 to 6) (see Fig. 36.32). A ligature results in torsion of these type B digits, resulting in painful edema or ischemia (Fig. 36.33). The type B can be safely removed in the office using local anesthesia and a bipolar electrocautery.

![[Fig.36.32.png]]

![[Fig.36.33.png]]

Type A forms, like a duplicated thumb, require operative digit ablation and small finger reconstruction, after the age of 18 months. After the extra digit is removed, any important functional parts (e.g., ulnar collateral ligament and abductor digiti quinti) are transferred to the adjacent finger (proximal phalanx base). An elliptic incision around the digit or a proximally based mirrored triangular flap is utilized and extended in a proximal direction to allow adequate exposure of the ligaments and muscles (Fig. 36.34). A long- arm cast for 4 weeks is required following ligamentous reconstruction. Some complex type A forms exhibit stiffness of both digits and may be better treated with ray resections instead of reconstruction.

Type B elements are ablated in the office with the use of a bipolar electrocautery. The stalk is cauterized after injecting the base with local anesthesia combined with epinephrine (Video 36.1). This technique avoids the residual nubbin and can safely be performed in infants.

Large type B or type A forms require digit ablation and small finger reconstruction after the age of 18 months. When two digits sit on a single metacarpal head, a ligamentous- periosteal flap should be raised from the distal metacarpal- phalanx of the extra digit, and any extra articular facet should be resected (see Fig. 36.34C to E). The ligamentous- periosteal flap and detached ADM tendon is reattached to the base of the fifth proximal phalanx of the preserved digit. A Kirschner wire is inserted into the MCP joint when needed (see Fig. 36.34F). Corrective osteotomy for angular deformity and/or rotational deformity of the small finger is performed if necessary. In young children, a long- arm cast is placed for 4 weeks and the Kirschner wire removed in the office.

## CENTRAL POLYDACTYLY

### Epidemiology

Central polydactyly consists of a duplication within the hand involving the rays of the index, long, or ring finger. The ring finger is most commonly involved, followed by the long finger and index finger (Fig. 36.36). The involved digit, as well as the adjacent digit, often demonstrate stiffness and abnormal deviation. Surgery attempts to improve function by addressing the stiffness and the deviation.

![[Fig.36.36.png]]
> **Fig. 36.36** Type IIA central polydactyly.

Prevalence and genetics vary according to geographic location. In the Western hemisphere, central polydactyly is 30% less frequent that ulnar polydactyly, and approximately 50% of patients demonstrate an autosomal dominant inheritance. In addition, 70% of the cases are bilateral.

Conversely, Japanese reports indicate that central polydactyly is 30% more frequent than ulnar polydactyly and occurs without a family history.

Ring finger synpolydactyly uniformly has a familial inheritance pattern that is linked to a gene mutation (HOXD13 gene) on chromosome. Between 70% and 90% of central polydactylies are associated with syndactyly and can also be associated with cleft hand (Figs. 36.37, 36.38, and 36.39). The pathoanatomy of central polydactyly, syndactyly, and cleft hand are similar with each involving some degree of insult to the central apical ectodermal ridge (AER). As we discover more about embryology and genetics, some authors have proposed classifying them together as “abnormal induction of finger rays.” In fact, bilateral cases can have all three deformities (central polydactyly, syndactyly, and cleft hand) in various combinations.

### Management

Type I and type III central polydactylies can be managed successfully with excision. For type III, ray amputation with reconstruction of web space and repair of transverse intermetacarpal ligament is performed. Concomitant corrective osteotomy may be required for persistent malalignment.

Type II polydactyly is difficult to treat. The affected and adjacent digits are often stiff at the MCP and IP joints. In addition, there can be a concealed angular or rotational deformity. Despite numerous wellintended surgeries, the ultimate results are poor with limited motion and persistent deviation. For complex type II central polydactyly, primary ray amputation may produce the best result. The surgeon may also consider nonoperative treatment as central polydactyly with complex connections may be more functional without surgery.

## MIRROR HAND

### Epidemiology

Mirror hand is a very rare congenital anomaly with less than 100 reported cases. Mirror hand is characterized by a duplication of the postaxial hand, forearm, and elbow with an absence of preaxial structures (thumb, radius, and capitellum).

### PATHOANATOMY

A symmetric duplication of the ulnar limb structures occurs in the midline. The typical mirror hand presents as a central digit (index) with three digits on each side that represent the middle, ring, and small digits in mirrored symmetry (Fig. 36.46). Ulnar carpal elements are also duplicated. Within the forearm, there are two ulnas, and the distal humeral epiphysis is also abnormal (Figs. 36.47 and 36.48). The duplicated ulna has led to the term “ulnar dimelia.”

![[Fig.36.46.png]]
> **Fig. 36.46** A and B, A 1-year-old girl with a left mirror hand and seven digits. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

![[Fig.36.47.png]]
> **Fig. 36.47** A 2-year-old girl with a left mirror hand. A, Hand x-ray showing seven rays and early ossification of the ulnar carpus. B, Forearm x-ray showing two ulnae and no true radius. (Courtesy of Shriners Hospital for Children, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.)

![[Fig.36.48.png]]
> **Fig. 36.48** Ulnar dimelia with two ulnar hands (A) that can mirror each other (B). MRI shows a double ulna without radius conforming an abnormal elbow (C).

Abnormal anatomy of the wrist, forearm, and elbow results in a wrist flexion contracture, limited forearm rotation, and elbow extension contracture. Wrist flexion contracture is due to a volar carpal subluxation combined with hypoplastic wrist extensor muscles.

The clinical presentation varies according to the variable dysfunction of the zone of polarizing activity (ZPA) that governs radioulnar differentiation (see Chapter 35, Embryology of the Upper Extremity).

### Management

Selective ablation of the supernumerary digits and thumb reconstruction are the mainstays of surgery. The procedure must reconstruct the first web space and augment the motor function of the preserved thumb by tendon transfers. The principles of pollicization with the use of “spare parts” are involved in reconstruction of the thumb (Fig. 36.49). Pollicization of the most mobile radial digit and removal of the stiffer radial digits is performed (Fig. 36.50). The skin from the ablated digits can be used to augment the first web space (Fig. 36.51).

### PATHOANATOMY

The pattern of involved bones and digits is highly consistent for each isolated or syndromic brachydactyly. The most commonly used classification of isolated brachydactyly is by Bell and elaborated by Temtamy and McKusick (see Table 36.2). 2. Grebe chondrodysplasia is a severe brachydactyly involving both hands and feet. All digits are affected, and there is absent MCP and metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joints (Fig. 36.56).
3. Rubinstein- Taybi syndrome typically has a thumb with features of clinodactyly and brachydactyly (Fig. 36.57).

### Management

The vast majority of patients have no functional problems, and digital lengthening is not indicated owing to the risk of stiffness and loss of function. Angular malalignment correction is indicated when crossing or scissoring is present. Closing wedge or opening wedge osteotomies are options.

Many patients, or families of patients with brachymetacarpy, request metacarpal lengthening of the shortened rays. We discourage lengthening as the risks outweigh the benefits. Most described lengthening techniques utilize a dorsal approach, which results in unsightly scar. A palmar approach for lengthening of short central metacarpals has also been described.

Most authors who are proponents of lengthening utilize lengthening distraction osteogenesis with an external fixator. Distraction osteogenesis allows for substantial lengthening and avoids bone grafting. However, risks of external fixation include pin infection and loosening. Distraction rate varies from 0.5 to 1 mm/day. Even in older patients, a distraction rate of 1 mm/day typically results in healing. The need for additional grafting after distraction is rare in patients younger than 18 years of age, even with lengthening of >30 mm.

Dhalla and colleagues described a technique applicable to small bones with only one pin on each side of the osteotomy, supplemented with an intramedullary Kirschner wire to maintain alignment.

Single- stage lengthening has also been described. Immediate lengthening of the metacarpal may require soft tissue reconstruction as the transverse metacarpal ligaments may need to be divided and the origins of the interosseous muscles may require advancement (Fig. 36.58). Intercalary bone graft is usually required. Fixation can be accomplished with external fixation, Kirschner wires, or internal fixation. Disadvantages of one stage technique included the limited lengthening capacity (10 to 15 mm) and the need for bone graft.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: BRACHYDACTYLY

Lengthening of shortened phalanxes is rarely indicated. Phalanx angular deformities are corrected with a closing wedge osteotomy stabilized with a Kirschner wire. The Kirschner wire is drilled retrograde from the fingertip crossing the IP joint and residing just proximal to the level of the proposed osteotomy. A midaxial approach is performed at the osteotomy site. The periosteum is elevated and a small rongeur used to remove a wedge of bone preserving the contralateral cortex. Next, the wedge is firmly closed by creating a greenstick deformity or cracking the intact cortex. The Kirschner wire is drilled across the osteotomy. The wound is closed, and the hand immobilized in a cast covering the Kirschner wire. The cast and Kirschner wire are removed at 4 weeks after surgery in the office.

Physicians considering lengthening a shortened metacarpal must have a thorough discussion regarding the risks and benefits of the procedure. In the rare case of metacarpal lengthening, we use a dorsal uniplanar distractor with two pins on each side of the osteotomy. All pins must be perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the bone. A subperiosteal osteotomy is performed between the pin carriages. A Kirschner wire is used to perforate the cortices and an osteotome used to complete osteotomy. Distraction begins 4 and 7 days after surgery at a rate of 1 mm/day. Our preference is 0.25 mm lengthening four times a day. The external fixator is not removed until consolidation of the metacarpal is evident on radiographs. Supervision by a hand therapist is required to prevent stiffness during the lengthening process. Regular radiographs are essential to assess the progress of the distraction. The rate of distraction can be adjusted to the amount of bone forming across the osteotomy site. Radiographs are also assessed for pin displacement, pin site lucency, and failure of the apparatus.

## CENTRAL HAND DEFICIENCY

Central deficiency of the hand represents a broad group of deformities primarily affecting the central rays of the hand. Historically, central deficiency was subdivided into typical and atypical types on the basis of clinical features, but the atypical cleft hand is now recognized as symbrachydactyly and considered a transverse rather than a longitudinal deficiency. This section will focus on central hand deficiency or the typical cleft hand. The hallmark of central hand deficiency is characterized by a “V”- shaped cleft in the center of the hand, which may or may not be associated with the absence of one or more digits (Fig. 36.59). Clinically there is a spectrum of soft tissue and skeletal deficiency that Maisels, building on the observations of Walker and Clodius, proposed progressed in severity from a central soft tissue defect to complete absence of all digits with a centripetal suppression of digits in a radial direction from the central cleft so that in the monodactylous form, the most ulnar finger is preserved. This orderly progression of the phenotype of this condition is complicated by the commonly associated findings of syndactyly, central polydactyly, and skeletal deformity with additional cross- bones between the residual digital rays (Fig. 36.60).

These clinical observations, in combination with the experimental observations of animal models of teratogenicity, led to the proposal that cleft hand is related to a failure of induction of the central digital rays, rather than being a longitudinal deficiency as it was originally classified by Swanson. In light of advances in limb bud development and the pathogenesis of central deficiency attributed to a defect in the central portion of the AER, central deficiency is classified as a malformation of the hand plate in the proximo- distal axis in the Oberg, Manske, and Tonkin (OMT) classification.

### Etiology

The etiology of the cleft hand anomaly has been deduced from animal models, particularly the *dactylaplasia* mouse that has specific *Dac* gene mutations in two loci on chromosome 19 and expresses a cleft phenotype in its heterozygote form. In this model, the central segment of the AER undergoes cell death in the early stages of limb bud development with consequent failure of central digit development. This theory of etiology has been corroborated by Ogino and colleagues, who have produced rats with cleft hand/foot deformity, central polydactyly, and/or osseous syndactyly with exposure to the teratogen busulfan. Fetal studies in this model have identified a combined ectodermal and mesodermal injury in the developing limb bud with disruption of normal FGF8 signaling in the AER and BMP- 4 signaling in the underlying mesoderm. It is proposed that this disruption leads to failure of normal digital ray induction.

A cleft hand can occur sporadically, presumably by de novo mutation or chromosome imbalance, or is inherited. Cleft hand is usually inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, though autosomal recessive and X- linked pedigrees have been recognized, and is associated with a number of syndromes (Box 36.1). The most common syndromes are split- hand/split- foot (SHSF) and ectrodactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, and cleft lip/palate (EEC) syndrome. SHSF syndrome is usually inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable penetrance. Seven subtypes with specific gene mutation loci have been identified on a range of chromosomes. Within each subtype, the phenotype can vary substantially between and within individuals, though some characteristics such as preaxial involvement appear to relate to specific mutations. The variable expressivity and reduced penetrance cause difficulty in predicting future phenotypes in an affected family.

### PATHOANATOMY

The condition may be unilateral or bilateral and may or may not involve the feet. The manifestations of cleft hand may vary through a spectrum from a very minor cutaneous cleft in the second web to absence of one or more of the central digits to the severe form with only the small finger remaining. Syndactyly between the digits bordering the cleft is common. Functionally, the failure of web space formation often affects the first web space. Duplications can also occur adjacent to the cleft. Within the cleft, metacarpals may or may not be present, and transverse tubular bones may be present that further widen the cleft as they grow. Phalangeal anomalies often coexist, including longitudinally bracketed epiphyses or double phalanges creating angular deformity within the remaining digits. The metacarpal anomalies are variable, including absence within the cleft, bifid metacarpals supporting one finger, and duplication. Neurovascular and musculotendinous anatomy is variable within the hand.

A variant form of central deficiency, the ulnar cleft hand, has been reported where the cleft is situated in the fourth web space and hypoplasia primarily affects the ulnar digits. Al- Qattan has proposed an alternate pathophysiological pathway as the cause of the ulnar cleft hand.

### Classification

Many classifications of cleft hand have been proposed. In our experience the most useful classification is that of Manske and Halikis, which focuses on the status of the first web space (Table 36.4). This classification acknowledges that more extensive clefts lead to the additional suppression of the index ray, and the cleft therefore merges with the first web space, producing a wide and competent web. More extensive cleft formation, however, can involve the thumb, which can be hypoplastic or even absent.

### Management

#### Indications for Surgery

Flatt memorably described the cleft hand as a “functional triumph, but a social disaster.” This statement emphasizes that many cleft hands, although stigmatizing the child, function well. A family with one or more generations of cleft hands may question the value of surgery for their children as their family members have functioned well. They express concerns about the possibility of lessening hand function and achieving marginal esthetic improvement. Such opinions require sensitive handling when discussing the surgical indications as the surgeon may be talking to a parent with a cleft hand that has never undergone surgical correction. Surgery should improve both appearance and function, but never downgrade either. Many authors have attested to the psychological consequences of cleft hand, and the moving account of Walker in Flatt’s text highlights the psychosocial complexities faced by families with this condition. No physician should add to the distress of these patients and their families by the outdated practice of using animalistic comparators such as “ *lobster claw* ” or “ *pincer.* ”

Several broad indications for surgery are recognized: (1) progressive deformity (caused by the restriction of a syndactyly or the growth of transverse bones); (2) the cleft deformity of the hand, including the deficient first web space; (3) the absent thumb; or (4) the feet. The cleft deformity comes as a shock in a family previously unaffected. Therefore proposing early surgery to parents still adjusting to these unexpected circumstances should be avoided. Most parents, however, will accept surgery to prevent worsening of the deformity. Indications for early surgery include the separation of syndactyly between digits of unequal length (especially the thumb and index) and the removal of transverse bones that result in widening of the cleft during growth. Most of the other surgical procedures are less urgent and should be reserved until the child is between 1 and 2 years of age.

#### Surgical Technique

**Management of the progressive deformity** Removal of transverse bones. Removal of the growing transverse bones from within the cleft can prevent progression of deformity and serves as a prerequisite for closure of the cleft. The anatomy of cross bones is varied. They are present in the setting of other skeletal deformities such as bifid metacarpals or synostoses and may not be the only cause of progression of deformity. The bone may articulate at the MCP joints, and removal requires preservation of soft tissue structures to allow for reconstruction of the collateral ligaments for joint stability. Articular surface sculpting of the remaining joint or proximal osteotomy may be required for axial alignment. There are circumstances where the cross bone or components of the osseous remnants may be retained to favor maintenance of the first web or stability of a digit.

Syndactyly release. Syndactyly may affect the border rays of the hand. Early release is indicated to prevent progressive deviation of the longer rays (Fig. 36.61). Release of the syndactyly is performed according to the principles detailed previously with specific attention to the digital artery anatomy, which may be abnormal particularly for digits adjacent to the cleft where the common digital artery is often deficient.

**Management of the cleft deformity** Release of the first web space. The classification proposed by Manske and Halikis has focused attention on the importance of the first web space morphology in dictating surgical treatment of the cleft hand (Fig. 36.62). In type IIA cases, the first web space is mildly narrowed, and a cleft is present between the index and the ring finger with complete absence of the central ray. Concomitant syndactyly of the ulnar digits is often present. In type IIB hands, the first web space is more severely constricted and the cleft element more variable. Type III cleft hands have a complete syndactyly between the thumb and index finger. Type IV cleft hands possess a thumb web space that has merged with the cleft. Type V cleft hands have suppression of the thumb elements without a thumb web space.

Release of a narrow first web space requires release of skin, fascia, and the muscles of the first web and importing skin to resurface the web. Ideally, this skin is imported from the excess skin in the central cleft. In some cases of complete first web syndactyly, the thumb may be rotated into the plane of the other digits, and a metacarpal rotation osteotomy is required to realign the thumb. Such an osteotomy is not simply a matter of transverse division of the tubular bone and rotation with fixation. Soft tissue structures including tendons, intrinsic muscles, and periosteum must be reoriented or the digit will progressively derotate, returning to the original orientation. Frequently a short disc of bone is removed during rotation osteotomy to lessen the tension along the soft tissues during rotation.

Closure of the cleft. There are three components to the procedure for closure of the cleft: creation of a commissure for the newly created web space, closure of the cleft and resurfacing the skin of the cleft, and skeletal realignment.

The skin flap for the commissure is designed as a distally based hexagonal flap with its base at the level of the new web position and inset to adjacent digit (Fig. 36.63).

In type I and IIA cleft hands, skin may not be required for the first web space. In more severely contracted web spaces, closure of the cleft can free skin that can be used for resurfacing the first web space. Snow and Littler described an elegant but complex technique that has been elaborated on by Flatt (Fig. 36.64; Video 36.2). The cleft skin is raised as a palmar- based flap and transposed into the first web space (Fig. 36.65). The viability of this palmar flap depends on preservation of the intrinsic blood supply, and dissection must maintain the subdermal plexus. If possible, a digital artery should be included with the flap to transform its blood supply from a random to axial pattern. The Snow- Littler procedure is far from a simple procedure: The planning and execution can be difficult, and the transposed flap may not inset into the correct position. Supplementary skin grafts may be necessary. In addition, the flap may undergo partial necrosis, since its length substantially exceeds its breadth. Rider and coworkers reported a low rate of flap necrosis, but one- third of patients required revision procedures, and supplementary skin grafting was required in tight first web spaces. A number of other techniques of cleft closure have been described. Ueba described a less complicated procedure using transverse flaps from border of the cleft and transposition of the index digit (Fig. 36.66). The flaps from either side of the cleft are used to resurface the palm, the dorsal aspect of the metacarpal head of the transposed digit, and the ulnar part of the first web space. This technique allows a simpler approach to cleft closure but has the esthetic disadvantage of transferring palmar skin to dorsal and dorsal skin to palmar. Miura and Komada described a technique of transposition of the index finger via a dorsal incision that rotated the skin of the cleft into the first web. Upton and later Oberlin and coworkers elaborated on this approach with techniques that mobilize the index finger on its vascular pedicle and transpose it to a central position. The cleft is incised from side to side across its leading edge. The index finger is raised on its neurovascular bundles and transposed to the third metacarpal or reoriented by angulation osteotomy. This allows the skin to be mobilized as broadly based palmar and dorsal flaps and redraped to form a widened first web (Fig. 36.67). The surgeon must be cognizant of aberrant arterial anatomy both on the cleft side of the index and within the first web. Adequate blood supply must be preserved to the thumb and index finger. Preoperative computerized tomographic angiography (CTA) may be of assistance in planning more complex cases, although we rarely use them.

Skeletal realignment is a critical component of the cleft closure. Alignment can be achieved by reconstruction of the transverse metacarpal ligament, osteotomy of the metacarpal or carpus, translocation of the metacarpal, or combination of these maneuvers. Malalignment of the metacarpals will lead to scissoring of the digits and compromise function. In the cases with a mild cleft, soft tissue reconstruction of the transverse metacarpal ligament alone may be sufficient. In contrast, wide clefts require osteotomy or translocation.

Reconstruction of the intermetacarpal ligament can be achieved by using the A1 pulleys from the digits (Fig. 36.68) unfolded toward each other and overlapped to form a stout transverse ligment. An alternate technique described by Ueba uses slips of tendon as a graft weaved between the common extensor tendons of the digits bordering the cleft to prevent digital separation during extension. In more severe clefts, relocation of the index finger to the base of the third metacarpal narrows the cleft and widens the first web space. The osteosynthesis at the base of the metacarpal is usually performed with a Kirschner wire with or without a cerclage wire, or plate fixation, depending on the age of the patient. In those cases, without a base of the third metacarpal to receive the index transposition, the cleft may be closed by wedge excision of the index metacarpal or within the carpus to widen the thumb- index web and realign the fingers. For most cases, the focus is on restoring the alignment of the radial digits to recreate a functional first web space. There are those cases where the ulnar digits diverge widely from the axis of the hand. Foucher and coauthors addressed this deformity with carpal osteotomies to translocate the ulnar digits radially while preserving their CMC motion to optimize grip function.

For example, when there is a small digital remnant within the cleft, the digit can be filleted and the skin transposed with a bilobed flap technique into the first web (Fig. 36.69). Rarely, when a tight or narrow first web space is associated with a small, narrow, or shallow cleft, a distant flap to import skin may be required.

An acceptable outcome requires adherence to the principles of adequate first web release: resurfacing of the first web with vascularized pliable tissue, transposing the most radial finger, structuring an intermetacarpal ligament, and recreating a commissure about the closed cleft.

In the type IV cleft hands, the first web space is merged with the cleft, and function does not require creation of a first web space. Surgical effort is usually directed toward the stability, mobility, and/or position of the thumb or ulnar digit to promote pinch and grasp.

In type V cleft hands, there is no cleft or web space, and the thumb is very deficient. Surgery for this group is considered in the next section.

**Management of the absent thumb deformity.** Creation of a radial digit is the priority in the management of the type V cleft hand. In some cleft hands, there may be a “radial” digit available even though it is aligned with the ulnar digits. Separation and rotation of this radial ray may yield a functional and useful hand but require distant flap coverage for the first web space. Options include free or pedicled flaps from the forearm and groin. An absent radial ray can only be replaced by free toe transfer. In contrast to microsurgical toe transfer in children for other indications, toe transfer for cleft hand is fraught with problems.There are multiple confounding factors including the hand anatomy that is often highly abnormal. There is absence of many of the proximal longitudinal structures that are necessary to drive a transferred toe, and the feet are often abnormal. In the cleft foot with a tibial and fibular ray, the fibular ray usually bears weight. Hence, the angulated and abnormal tibial ray may be amputated for shoe wear and is available for transfer. Experience with such free toe transfers is limited.

**Surgical management of the feet.** The foot deformity in cleft hand often parallels the hand deformity. The hand surgeon may or may not be involved in the feet, but his or her opinion is often sought at the time of consultation. Blauth and Borisch have classified the foot deformities that occur with cleft formation into six types. Types I and II have only minor abnormalities without absence of metatarsals, whereas the metatarsals are progressively absent in the remaining types. Type VI is the monodactylous cleft foot with only the fibula ray remaining.

Despite the quite bizarre appearance of the feet, function is remarkably good, and gait is rarely impaired. Cleft foot surgery is indicated for difficulty in footwear fitting secondary to duplication or deviation of the border digits. This may be corrected by either osteotomy or excision of trapezoidal phalanges. As in the hand, resection of a transverse bone leading to progressive widening may be necessary. Surgery may also be indicated for cosmesis with direct closure of the narrow cleft or even obturation of the cleft defect with an alloplastic prosthesis.

The status of the great toe ray or tibial ray presents a challenge in surgical decision making. Commonly, patients have great toe polydactyly with deviation, and a decision is necessary regarding preservation and correction of the tibial ray. In our experience, the fibular ray is always the more important, and the tibial ray may be present but does not participate in weight bearing. In those rare cases that are candidates for a microsurgical transfer from the feet, the feet are often severely affected with only two rays remaining. In these cases, there is reluctance to sacrifice the tibial ray. Nonetheless, this ray can be transferred to the hand, although waiting until the child is older and can undergo gait analysis is prudent.

In summary, surgical treatment for children with central deficiency is challenging but can provide good functional and esthetic results.In more severe forms, ingenuity of the surgeon is required. In these cases, the surgeon must discuss with parents that despite the adverse appearance, children will adapt and learn to use their hands in ingenious ways that were initially considered impossible. Surgery should improve appearance and function or both but should never downgrade either. Surgical correction should be planned according to a schedule that recognizes the urgency of some procedures and the discretionary of other treatments. The overriding determinant of hand function is the first web space, and this forms the basis of the most useful classification. Microvascular transfers from the foot can be useful in highly selected cases. If foot amputation is being contemplated, microvascular transfers of the part in the hand should be considered.

Complete syndactyly can be released, and the cleft closed in a single set ting. The skin from the cleft is rotated into the ﬁrst web space to create supple tissue.

## ABSENCE OF FINGERS

Digits that are partly or completely absent due to congenital difference can be classified as being absent in either a transverse or longitudinal direction. In a transverse absence, the digit(s) is absent, but the proximal supporting structures required to support digital function are present to a variable extent. Examples of transverse absence include congenital amputation due to CRS or the failure of digital development due to either symbrachydactyly or transverse deficiency (where no ectodermal remnants persist). In a longitudinal absence, the supporting structures are severely deficient or absent. Examples are thumb absence in radial deficiency, absence of ulnar digits in ulnar deficiency, or central absence(s) in central longitudinal deficiency.

Reconstruction of transverse absence may be achievable by replacing the absent distal part (e.g., by microvascular toe transfer), which is then enabled by the proximal neurovascular and musculotendinous structures. Reconstruction of longitudinal absence requires replacement of both the absent part and most of the enabling proximal structures, for example, pollicization of the index finger for thumb absence. The following section focuses on the transverse absences of the hand as the longitudinal absences are covered elsewhere.

### Constriction Ring Sequence

CRS is an intriguing congenital condition that has many labels, including constriction ring syndrome, constriction band syndrome, amniotic disruption sequence, amniotic band syndrome, and premature amnion disruption sequence. The authors have adopted the term codified in the International Federation of Societies for Surgery of the Hand (IFSSH) classification for consistency. It is characterized by the presence of partial or complete circumferential constrictions around limbs or digits or other body parts, and consequences include acrosyndactyly of digits, terminal absence or amputation, and localized swelling with digital edema distal to constrictions (Fig. 36.70).

![[Fig.36.70.png]]
> **Fig. 36.70** Manifestations of the amniotic disruption sequence. A, Typical ring constrictions of digits. B, Pallor of the finger distal to the ring constriction in a neonate. C, Acrosyndactyly with the digits fused at their distal parts and proximal cutaneous clefts. D, Amputation secondary to constriction rings.

#### Etiology

There are two theories as to etiology. The most current and prevalent theory is that the deformity is due to extrinsic deformation of the fetus with the formation of the constriction rings and due to amniotic disruption with release of amniotic bands that encircle and strangulate limbs or parts of limbs in utero. It is also postulated that the limb may become entrapped within the ruptures of the amniotic wall.The alternative theory is that the deformity is an intrinsic etiology popularized by Streeter in 1930, proposing that the rings formed due to focal failure of tissue formation or “germplasm.” Subsequently, Patterson in 1961 concluded the rings formed due to focal dysplasia of the subcutaneous tissue and in severe cases failure of development of blood vessels at the level of the ring with consequences for overlying skin and distal parts.

### PATHOANATOMY

The incidence of CRS in a population- based series was 1 in 16,000, remarkably similar to Patterson’s estimation of incidence from his personal series in 1961. The constriction rings may be completely or incompletely circumferential and may occur anywhere on the body, but most commonly seen on the limbs. A consequence of these constrictions may be either amputation or near amputation with distal lymphedema or, less commonly, compromised limb viability. The constriction may also produce fusion of the digits to adjacent or nonadjacent digits producing either a complicated syndactyly (see Fig. 36.70C) and/or acrosyndactyly with a distal fusion of the digits and a proximal sinus between them. From the hand surgeon’s perspective, the most important item about the transverse absence in CRS is that the proximal structures of the hand are relatively normal (Fig. 36.71). Amputations or absences are most common within the digits but may occur at any level of the limb. Lesser constrictions may be responsible for some rare cases of facial clefting. In the hand, the digital stumps may exhibit tapering skeletal elements with tight skin covering. In contrast, the digits in symbrachydactyly typically have preserved ectodermal elements. Where the distal limb is present, the skin distal to the constriction ring may be hard and swollen with an uncompressible edema. Nerve palsies have been associated with constriction ring sequelae that are present after birth (Fig. 36.72). Heterotopic reimplantation of amputated digits rarely occurs.

#### Management

**Treatment of the digit or limb threatened in utero or at birth.** The condition is increasingly diagnosed in utero, and the hand surgeon may be called upon to offer a clinical opinion. In utero release of limb constriction rings has been performed successfully with preservation of the limb and is worth considering in proximal severe constrictions identified on antenatal ultrasound. However, the risks to the mother and child must be considered. Currently, fetoscopic band release is limited to progressive edema and loss of circulation that likely will result in loss of limb. The place for fetoscopic surgery is still emerging, and the benefit of the procedure must be weighed against the risk of antenatal complications, including premature delivery and spontaneous abortion. For the limb that appears compromised at or soon after birth, segmental band release similar to an escharotomy has been described to decompress the constriction and restore circulation compromised by the lympho- venous tourniquet effect of the constriction.

**Nerve palsies.** A peripheral nerve palsy may occur underlying a constriction ring (see Fig. 36.72). The diagnosis is based on clinical assessment. While some improvement following decompression of the nerve compression has been described, the authors and others have noted that the results of decompression alone have been disappointing. A more aggressive approach of resection of the constricted nerve and reconstruction may be a better option.

![[Fig.36.72.png]]
> **Fig. 36.72** A, A constriction ring around the arm in a newborn child. B, Associated radial nerve palsy.

**Constriction ring.** Treatment is aimed at both functional and esthetic improvement. The mainstays are excision of the ring and scarred subcutaneous tissue combined with Z- plasty or W- plasty to elongate the scar and limit recurrence of the constriction. It is critical to correct the contour of the subcutaneous layer with interdigitation of fat flaps from the margin of the constriction. Circumferential surgery was previously not recommended in a single setting; however, complete circumferential excision may be performed safely with preservation of axial vessels.An exception to circumferential surgery is two rings that are closely adjacent. In this case releasing the rings one at a time is prudent.

**Acrosyndactyly.** Acrosyndactyly secondary to amniotic disruption sequence is addressed according to the principles of syndactyly reconstruction discussed earlier. Separation requires attention to the commissure, resurfacing of the digital surfaces, and reconstruction of the nail fold and pulp. The sinus is usually too distal to incorporate into the commissural skin flaps, and often it is excised. The ectodermal skin can be used as a full- thickness graft. Additional attention toward release of concomitant constriction rings and contour deformities of the distal digits via judicious debulking is warranted. The timing of separation is important, because connected but nonadjacent digits are often of unequal length, which creates angular deformities during growth. Release of the distal connection is often enough to resolve this tethering, allowing deferral of definitive separation and commissure reconstruction until the child is older.

**Skin protuberances.** Skin protuberances are often present in amniotic disruption sequence. These lumps are present on the dorsum of the fingers and appear firm and edematous. These protuberances may be treated via numerous techniques. Z- plasty is often unsatisfactory, and excision is preferred with resurfacing using full- thickness skin grafts (Fig. 36.73).

### Symbrachydactyly

Symbrachydactyly is the term used to describe a spectrum of congenital transverse limb deficiency deformity where the primary deficiency is of the mesodermal element of the distal limb but ectodermal elements, such as the nail complex, persist. The extent of the deficiency ranges from digital hypoplasia through digital aplasia to a transverse deficiency of the hand or forearm.

#### Classification

The condition has been difficult to define and position through the various iterations of the classification of congenital hand anomalies.The original classification of congenital limb malformations proposed by Swanson and coworkers proved difficult to apply to this group of patients. Depending on the morphology of the hand, failure of formation, failure of differentiation, or undergrowth categories were used to describe the current diagnosis of symbrachydactyly. Barsky used the term *atypical cleft hand* to distinguish hands with absence of the central digits with features of symbrachydactyly from true cleft hands. Barsky felt this diagnosis to be a separate condition from the true cleft hand, consistent with Lange’s description in the German literature in 1936. Flatt also used *atypical cleft hand* to describe hands with a U- shaped central absence but felt symbrachydactyly was a better descriptor for these hands. For a period of time, the terminology was interchangeable, but to clarify any confusion, the term *atypical cleft hand* was replaced in 1992 by symbrachydactyly. With progression in the understanding of limb bud development, a new classification for congenital upper limb differences was proposed and accepted by the IFSSH in 2014. This classification explicitly placed symbrachydactyly in the Malformation category as an abnormality of formation/differentiation in the proximodistal axis as part of either an entire limb anomaly or a hand plate only anomaly. Of note, symbrachydactyly is distinguished from transverse deficiency as a diagnosis in this classification. This distinction is further reinforced in the most recent update of this classification where the diagnosis of symbrachydactyly requires the presence of ectodermal elements to distinguish it from the transverse deficiency diagnosis.

In the classification of symbrachydactyly, Blauth and Gekeler classified symbrachydactyly into four clinical types in a progressive spectrum of deficient digits: short finger type, cleft hand type with the central digits absent, monodactylous type with preservation of the thumb, and peromelic type where all digits are absent. The most severe types, monodactylic and peromelic, are characterized by transverse absence of digits and the presence of rudimentary distal nubbins or stumps, which may include remnants of nail plate, bone, or cartilage (Fig. 36.74). Foucher has offered a classification that proposed options for treatment dependent on the digits present and their characteristics. The authors prefer the classification of Yamauchi and Tanabu, who described a radiologic classification that subdivides the affected hands based on the skeletal elements missing (Table 36.6).

#### Clinical Features

Symbrachydactyly occurs sporadically and is usually unilateral. There appears to be a spectrum of deficiency, beginning with short middle phalanges, extending to absence of the central rays, and culminating in absence of the entire hand. The defect is believed to be mesodermal, which explains the remaining distal finger ectodermal structures (pulp, nail fold, and nail). This results in the characteristic “nubbins” or “buds” that represent vestiges of digits and are the sine qua non of symbrachydactyly (see Fig. 36.74).

![[Fig.36.74.png]]
> **Fig. 36.74** Types of symbrachydactyly. A, Symbrachydactyly with a reasonable thumb but absent fingers represented by nubbins with nail remnants. B, Complete absence of the fingers that becomes more pronounced toward the ulnar side of the hand.

Symbrachydactyly may occur in isolation or as a part of Poland syndrome. Poland’s initial report described the combined anomaly of an absent sternal head of the pectoralis major muscle, absent pectoralis minor muscle and a hand typical of the short- fingered type of symbrachydactyly. Over time, however, the definition of Poland syndrome has broadened to encompass disturbances of pectoral muscle development and chest wall deformity with or without manifestations of hand malformation. This association forms part of the subclavian artery supply disruption sequence hypothesis of pathogenesis, which proposes that early embryonic interruption of the subclavian artery produces a predictable pattern of developmental defects, including isolated terminal transverse limb defects. This theory is applicable to Poland syndrome, Klippel- Feil syndrome, and Möbius syndrome.

### PATHOANATOMY

Each form of symbrachydactyly occupies a place on the continuum from the normal hand through the various degrees of deficiency to complete absence of the hand. The severity of deficiency correlates with the degree of aberrant anatomy (see Table 36.6). As stated previously, the presence of proximal enabling structures offers the possibility for reconstruction of functioning digits (Fig. 36.75).

![[Fig.36.75.png]]
> **Fig. 36.75** A, Oligodactylic form of symbrachydactyly. Exploration revealed the presence of extensor and flexor tendons and nerves providing adequate components to allow reconstruction with double second toe transfer. B, Good prehensile function achieved with free toe transfers contributing to stability and strength of pinch.

In all degrees of symbrachydactyly, the central rays are the most abnormal. In the mild forms (types 1, 2, and 3), the skeletal elements are the most affected. The middle phalanges are reduced in size and may be absent. Because mild symbrachydactyly rarely requires surgical exploration, little is known about the aberrant soft tissue anatomy apart from the incomplete simple syndactyly that is often present.

In more severe symbrachydactyly (type 4), the central rays virtually disappear and are replaced by finger nubbins with aponeurotic attachments to extrinsic tendons around the end of the hypoplastic metacarpals. The nubbins may contain small remnants of phalanges that articulate with the metacarpal. These nubbins have vestigial connections via the palmar aponeurotic and retinacular system to the extrinsic tendons. This connection is apparent by the ability of the nubbins to pucker into the palm during hand use. In the absence of phalangeal remnants, the metacarpals are usually small and may only be present at their bases. The fifth finger is least affected; however it typically lacks a middle phalanx but usually has a competent MCP joint and a variably stable distal joint, which is commonly radially deviated. This angulation is partly the result of the oblique pull by the extensor tendon, which bridges the U- shaped cleft. The thumb is also variable in form. The first web space may be narrowed, and the thumb may lack full circumduction at the CMC joint. The extrinsic flexor tendons often share a muscle belly and may be coalesced into a single tendon mass within the carpal canal. Lumbrical muscles are usually absent even when digits are present, and interosseous intrinsic muscles are either absent or highly abnormal. The extensor tendons along the dorsum of the hand appear quite normal, and often there are independent extensor indicis proprius or extensor digiti quinti tendons.

In the advanced monodactylic or single- digit form of symbrachydactyly (type 5), the anatomy is widely abnormal. Surgical attempts at reconstruction have provided considerable information about the aberrant anatomy. The thumb may or may not have a proximal phalanx. The extrinsic tendons end in a conjoined fashion at the level of the metacarpal end. The aberrant rays may lack even vestigial metacarpals, and the fifth ray is usually represented by a short metacarpal. Flexor tendons are conjoined and end at the tips of the skeletal rays. The flexor tendons attach to the extensors and to the aponeurotic system, which yields a variable amount of movement of the finger nubbins. The extensor tendons are disordered and may be conjoined, although the extensor indicis proprius or extensor digiti quinti are often still distinct. The wrist extensors appear normal. The excursion of the extensor tendons is variable. In general, a wrist and nubbins that are active and mobile correlate with tendons that have some useful excursion. The tendons may be conjoined and, depending on the excursion of the tendons, explored into the forearm to divide the donor tendon from any common muscle belly to prevent a quadriga effect.

In the more severe degrees of symbrachydactyly (types 6 and 7), there are no useful muscles to the fingers. Nerve abnormalities are common. In our experience, the radial nerve is almost always present, regardless of the severity. In contrast, the median and ulnar nerves are small or even absent. The presence of a thumb or fifth finger implies the presence of the corresponding median or ulnar nerve.

Rarely, complete absence of all major nerves in the hand may be found. The peromelic form of symbrachydactyly (type 6) has severe skeletal absence. There may or may not be a carpal row and particular attention should be paid to the status of the thumb ray CMC joint (see below). The wrist motors may be present. The flexor or extensor tendons of the fingers may be vestigial but have minimal excursion.

#### Management

**Treatment of short ﬁnger types (types 1, 2, and 3).** This group of symbrachydactyly has short fingers with small or absent middle phalanges. There may be an association with syndactyly, and the fifth finger often deviates in a radial direction. Function is usually excellent (Video 36.3). Release of the incomplete syndactyly of the webs may produce an apparent lengthening of the digits and improved span of the hand. Where the skeletal elements of the digits are hypoplastic, and the soft tissues and skin of the digits are relatively preserved, the digit can be stabilized and augmented with a free phalangeal bone graft, although expectations for useful growth in such grafts should be muted as discussed below.

**Treatment of the oligodactylic (cleft hand) type (type 4).** This type of symbrachydactyly is characterized by some degree of absence of the central three rays (Fig. 36.76). In the more severe forms of this type, the central digits are represented by nubbins only. The nubbins usually have little function, and their nail remnants can cause local irritation and infection due to the paronychial deformity. Nail remnants and/or nubbins can be removed by simple excision. Prehension can usually be achieved with a pincer grasp between the thumb and fifth finger. Depending on the stability and orientation of these digits, the capacity for prehension can be improved with osteotomy of the metacarpals. The potential for tendon transfers to augment the power of pinch is limited because of the paucity of adequate long tendons in the limb. The first web can be narrow, and the span of grasp can be improved by soft tissue release and lengthening of web with local flap reconstruction.

![[Fig.36.76.png]]
> **Fig. 36.76** Oligodactylic symbrachydactyly with suppression of the central rays. There is associated syndactyly of the web spaces limiting the span of the hand and independent function of the small finger.

The addition of digits to the deficient central hand with vascularized toe transfers is possible in these hands as skeletal remnants and extrinsic tendons are usually present. The rationale for digital construction is to improve the power of pinch with a greater opposable pulp area to improve pinch. The carrying function of the hand can also be improved by increasing the surface area of the hand as a whole, which helps to stabilize objects positioned on the palm and additional digits. While the transferred digits retain the appearance of the toe, they are less discordant than may be expected, and the appearance of the oligodactylic hand is improved. There is controversy as to whether this surgery is justified in children who are likely to adapt well and develop reasonable prehensile function with the thumb and ulnar elements present. In the authors’ experience, digital reconstruction in these hands is justifiable. If a fifth ray and thumb are already present, these digits will be used in preference for fine manipulations, but the addition of two central rays by transfer of second toes from each foot offers further options for large or heavy object grasp with subsequent benefits in function and patient- reported benefits in the appearance and completeness of the hand.

**Treatment of monodactylic (single- ﬁnger) type (type 5).** The hand with this type of symbrachydactyly lacks all fingers and has only a thumb, which is of varying size and quality. A markedly deficient thumb may be present with a very short metacarpal and underdeveloped CMC articulation. The digits are represented by metacarpal bases and finger nubbins with nail remnants. These nubbins have vestigial connections via the palmar aponeurotic and retinacular system to the extrinsic tendons. This connection is apparent by the ability of the nubbins to pucker into the palm during hand use. The wrist is usually sufficient for function. Free vascularized toe transfer is a surgical option to improve function as provision of opposable digits improves hand function. The authors’ preference is to use two second toes transferred to the central or ulnar metacarpals to increase the surface area for opposition pinch and grasp in a similar manner to the above, pending available proximal enabling structures.

**Treatment of peromelic (short- limb) types (types 6 and 7).** The hand with this type of symbrachydactyly has only nubbins with nail remnants. There is a transverse absence at or about the metacarpal row, although more proximal absences may be seen. Finger nubbins may even be noted on a midforearm stump. Reconstruction of prehensile function is difficult as there are limited proximal structures. Vilkki has used a free vascularized toe transfer to the radial side of the distal forearm to achieve lateral pinch, although this should be performed either distal to the radial epiphysis or after growth has ceased.Otherwise, the toe will be left behind in the midforearm as the radius grows in length (Fig. 36.77).

### Transverse Deficiency

Transverse deficiency is a congenital malformation in the proximodistal axis of the limb leading to truncation of the limb most commonly at the forearm level, but it can be seen at any level from phalangeal (aphalangia) to the entire limb (amelia). In the current classification, it is distinguished from symbrachydactyly by the absence of ectodermal elements. Ectodermal elements include the nail plates associated with either the protruding nubbins, skin dimples, or skin invaginations tethered to the underlying hypoplastic tendons, which represent vestigial digits on the stumps of deficient hand or limb. In the past, these have been described as a feature of transverse deficiency or evidence that transverse deficiency and symbrachydactyly occupy a continuum. In contrast, Knight and coworkers reported on a group without ectodermal elements with truncated digits, giving them the label of *hypodactyly* to distinguish them from the symbrachydactyly group and proposed a distinct but as yet unknown pathogenesis.

For the purposes of this chapter, we have applied the current classification nomenclature and have distinguished transverse deficiency from symbrachydactyly, although the assessment and management are similar.

For transverse deficiency of the digits, assessment is based on the length, mobility, stability, and the redundancy of the soft tissue envelope of the truncated digit(s). The aim is to provide adequate digits to push, carry, point, grasp larger objects, and pinch small objects in increasing order of sophistication of function and digital requirements. For many, the digits will be adequate and no treatment required, particularly where the thumb is competent. For digits that have an intact and stable MCP joint(s), release of any syndactyly is indicated to increase the span of the hand, capacity for independence (particularly the index or middle finger that can be used to operate a tablet or keyboard), and appearance. The treatment objectives will dictate whether lengthening of the digits by free phalangeal bone grafting and/or distraction of the digit’s phalanx is indicated. The goal is to achieve a length of phalanx that will afford improvement in function.

### Surgical Technique

There are three main ways to augment the existing digit or ray in a child. The digit may be lengthened by a bone graft (most commonly a *free phalangeal transfer* from the foot), lengthened by *distraction,* or have composite parts of another digit transferred to it (“ *on- top plasty* ”).

If there are digits available but their length or mobility prevents opposition or effective function, the option of *transposition* of one or more of these digits to improve function can be considered.

When the digits are absent, the digit(s) can be constructed by *free vascularized toe transfer.*

#### Free Phalangeal Transfer

In the adult, placement of bone grafts on the terminal part of the skeleton of a digit results in early and complete graft resorption. Resorption, however, is not invariably the case in the child. The preferred choice of graft is a toe phalanx encompassed by cortex and periosteum, and it also provides a growth plate compared with the unicortical iliac crest graft. Some authors have reported poor results in terms of growth using this technique, whereas others have reported growth and improved function. Radocha and colleagues have defined three important conditions for success and growth following transfer: (1) Transfer must be done before the age of 15 months, (2) periosteum must be included in the transfer, and (3) collateral ligaments and tendon insertions must be repaired.

The authors agree that the earlier the transfer is completed, the better the chance of any subsequent growth. The inclusion of periosteum allows more rapid revascularization of the free transfer, probably through inosculation, which may help conserve the bone. The role of load across the physis is uncertain, but Radocha and colleagues found unequivocal evidence that attachment of ligaments and tendons to the transferred bone was important.

The best growth results in our children have been seen in those cases in which the phalanx was affixed to the proximal skeleton using chondrodesis, presumably increasing the amount of force transduced across the growth plate. Using the phalanx as a hemiarthroplasty at the MCP level, however, has produced mobile and stable digits. For stability, there must be some of long flexor and extensor tendon remnants at the level of the nubbin and the phalanx must be secured by direct soft tissue repair. Once consolidated, the phalanges have been shown to be robust enough to tolerate distraction lengthening.

The phalanges should not be harvested from the second toes unless microvascular toe transfer has been excluded as an option. Free phalangeal transfers are not a simple surgery. While a successful transfer may grow and add function, this result is not a guarantee as wound complications and skin necrosis can result from oversizing the graft. In addition, the surviving phalanx can be unstable and prone to angular deformity. Regarding the foot, the donor site can be troublesome and a source of morbidity unless properly managed. Overall, the results of free phalangeal transfers appear modest, and ample discussion with the family is necessary prior to surgery. For the most part, our practice prefers vascularized toe transfers compared with free phalangeal transfer because of the greater growth, mobility, and stability. The ideal recipient for free phalangeal transfer is a digital ray with a suitable skin envelope preferably with a small remnant of proximal phalanx and with evidence of extrinsic tendon attachments to the digit. Free phalangeal transfer is especially useful in the thumb in cases of monodactylic symbrachydactyly. In these cases, the fingers can be constructed from two toe transfers (see later), and the free phalangeal transfer augments the length of an otherwise competent thumb.

The requisite in the hand is a mobile, flexible, adequate skin envelope, which is common in transverse absence or symbrachydactyly. The transfers should be planned in patients who can expect to have some functional improvement and are not indicated in patients who will have no increase in prehensile function. The absence of extrinsic tendon function is a relative contraindication. The parents must be aware of the potential esthetic side effects for the foot. Finally, the procedure is relatively contraindicated in the older child, and we do not offer this treatment in children older than 24 months of age.

The donor site in the foot can be an issue with instability and deformity associated with collapse of the toe. There are contrasting results from longer- term outcome studies. Methods for treating the toe donor site have included suturing the extensor and flexor tendons together in the gap left following removal of the phalanx with or without a temporary pin to maintain length. Our experience with soft tissue reconstruction alone has been disappointing. In an attempt to minimize the donor deficit, we have inserted a cylindrical apophyseal bone graft from the iliac crest, which has improved the appearance at the foot donor site.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: FREE PHALANGEAL TRANSFER

**Preparation of the hand.** Under tourniquet, the empty skin nubbins may be approached by either dorsal or volar longitudinal incision. Skin may be closed using a Z- plasty when additional length is required and excess girth is available. The nubbin is opened and some fat is removed to accommodate the toe phalanx. Gentle traction on the nubbin with a skin hook reveals the extent of the cavity created for the transfer. The metacarpal head is evaluated and usually has a thin layer of articular cartilage overlying the epiphyses of the metacarpal. The metacarpal head is capped with a coalescence of the flexor and extensor tendons (Fig. 36.78). The authors’ preference is to incise this in a cruciate manner and to raise the dorsal and volar capsule with the extensor and flexor tendons. The radial and ulnar parts of the capsule are preserved as collateral ligaments. Each of these elements will subsequently be repaired to the phalangeal transfer.

![[Fig.36.78.png]]
> **Fig. 36.78** The flexor and extensor tendons are identified and separated over the metacarpal head.

In children with a remnant of proximal phalanx, the procedure is simpler because the tendons do not require exploration. The end of the residual phalanx is prepared for chondrodesis with the free toe phalanx.

**Harvesting the graft.** The toe phalanx is approached through a zigzag incision on the dorsum of the toe. The harvested phalanx is usually the proximal phalanx of the third or fourth toe. The second toe may be preferred in those cases in which microsurgical toe transfer is not being considered. After the dorsal exposure, the extensor apparatus is split in the midline of the digit and the toe phalanx dissected from its surrounding attachments, leaving the periosteum intact. The fibrous flexor sheath is divided laterally on the volar surface, taking care not to injure the flexor tendon. The distal capsular attachments are incised adjacent to the phalanx. The phalanx is harvested with the volar plate and capsular attachments (Fig. 36.79). Frequently, a small cap of the distal portion of the proximal phalanx is retained within the toe pending the length of graft required. The donor defect may be closed in a variety of ways. Some have advocated simple longitudinal Kirschner wire fixation, whereas others have recommended suturing the flexor and extensor tendons together to serve as a spacer. We prefer harvesting a cylindrical graft from the iliac crest that is capped with apophyseal cartilage using the method of Bourke and Kay (see Fig. 36.79). The graft is placed into the toe with the apophyseal cartilage directed proximally. The tubular cancellous bone abuts the small fragment of distal toe phalanx that was retained within the foot. The graft and toe construct are fixed with a longitudinal Kirschner wire. The extensor tendons and skin are repaired. This pin is removed 4 to 6 weeks later.

![[Fig.36.79.png]]
> **Fig. 36.79** A, A bone graft, including the apophysis, is harvested from the iliac crest with a tubular osteotome and is used to stabilize the toe donor site. B, The proximal phalanx has been harvested from the third toe (with the second toe preserved for possible future microvascular transfer). The tubular bone graft to replace the phalanx has been fixed with a longitudinal wire.

**Placement of the graft.** The graft is transferred to the hand. The volar capsular attachments and flexor tendon within the hand are sutured to the volar plate on the free phalangeal graft. The graft is then transfixed with a longitudinal Kirschner wire passed from proximal to distal and exiting through the apex of the skin envelope (Fig. 36.80). This wire is then withdrawn, the phalangeal graft then aligned with the metacarpal head, and the wire then advanced to fix the graft in place. The wire should be placed with as little trauma and heat dissipation as possible to avoid physeal plate injury. The collateral ligaments are repaired to the lateral margins of the capsule at the metacarpal head. The dorsal capsule and extensor tendon are repaired to the dorsal capsule of the free phalangeal graft. The skin is closed with a Z- plasty or directly with absorbable sutures and the hand immobilized in an above- elbow cast. Postoperatively, the Kirschner wires in the foot and the hand are removed 4 to 6 weeks after surgery. Subsequently, gentle passive range of motion is initiated by the hand therapist and parents.

> **Fig. 36.80** Toe proximal phalanx in place with Kirschner wire fixation and extensor tendon attachment.

**Complications.** Complications include skin necrosis and failure of growth of the transferred phalanx. Skin necrosis occurs when the transferred phalanx is too large for the skin envelope. Absent growth is more likely in the older child, when the tendons or ligaments are not repaired, or when the periosteum is not included in the transfer. Scarring may occur in the foot, especially when a longitudinal incision is used. Contraction may result in dorsal displacement of the toes that may become unstable. In addition, the donor defect may be worse in cases in which the extensor and flexor tendons have been sutured together as a “spacer.” This technique may lead to unstable toes that angulate and override adjacent digits.

#### Distraction of Digits

Diaphyseal osteotomy of one of the skeletal elements of the short ray and distraction using an external fixator can lengthen the digit.The procedure can only be used where the phalanx or metacarpal is long enough to accommodate a distraction frame. The bone generated in the distraction gap may be adequate for stability or augmented prior to removal of the frame with bone graft. A limited gain in length can be achieved, which may improve prehension particularly in the short thumb that is unable to reach the ulnar digit(s). The process is time consuming and requires a cooperative family and patient.

#### On- Top Plasty

Transposing entire digits or components thereof to the distal part of a short digit has been used effectively in reconstruction of the short thumb and in trauma. On- top plasty is an effective method to lengthen digits. The limiting factor is often the length of the neurovascular pedicle. Overlengthening can jeopardize the circulation and must be avoided. On- top plasty is reserved for selected cases only.

#### Transposition of Rays

This technique involves transposition of rays to allow prehension. In certain hands, the thumb lacks enough excursion to reach the fingers. Transposition of a finger or fingers into a more radial position can allow pinch or grasp. Transposition may be combined with microvascular toe transfer to enhance hand function (Fig. 36.81).

#### Microvascular Toe Transfer for Hypoplastic Digits

Single- stage microvascular toe- to- hand transfer is not a new concept. The technique has primarily been used in posttraumatic cases in adults. Before the 1990s, relatively few reports dealt with children. Lister reviewed 12 toe transfers for congenital deficiency of the thumb and reported good return of sensibility but poor return of IP joint motion. Kay and Wiberg extended the indications to include reconstruction of the congenital hand with absent thumb or finger.

**Choice of technique.** For adults, many variations of toe transfers have been developed, including the wraparound free flap, the twisted toe transfer, the double toe transfer, the great toe transfer, and the trimmed toe transfer. The donor defect must be minimized, especially in children. For this reason, we are reluctant to transfer anything other than the second toe. This limits the reconstruction to two digits, although fortunately the commonest indication in children is symbrachydactyly, which is invariably unilateral. In bilateral traumatic injuries the decisions about sacrificing more toes for hand function are more difficult. The second toes can be transferred in separate operations or in the same operation (synchronous bilateral second toe transfer). In very selected cases when either single or bilateral second toe transfer is not appropriate or possible, transfer of the great toe may be considered with caution. The esthetic defect is greater and there may be functional consequences. Rarely, a wraparound free flap or partial toe transfer may be used, but only in the older child since physeal growth is not a property of such transfers.

**Indications.** The indications for digital reconstruction include transverse deficiencies in the thumb and/or fingers (Fig. 36.82).

![[Fig.36.82.png]]
> **Fig. 36.82** A simple grouping of common types of congenital transverse deficiencies in the hand based on the authors’ experience of the reconstructive options available and the probable functional outcome. Group 1a: The absence is at a proximal level through the base of the metacarpal or at the carpometacarpal level. There is no circumduction in the thumb ray, and here bilateral second toe transfers will allow pincer grip between the toe pulps. It is likely that a choice will have to be made between small-or large-object grasp because the range of motion in the reconstructed toes is unlikely to allow both. Group 2a: The absences are at the distal level of the metacarpals, and the basal joint of the thumb is competent. Reconstruction at this level with one toe on the first ray and one toe on the fourth ray will allow good small-and large-object grasp with competent pulp-to-pulp pinch by virtue of the basal motion in the thumb. Group 2b: Distal metacarpal loss with a poorly competent basal joint but the presence of a relatively normal fifth ray. The mobility present in all the joints of the fifth ray, including the basal joint, means that any reconstruction on the thumb ray will probably allow both small-and large-object grasp. Single toe transfer to the thumb ray is therefore indicated, and the optional transfer of a further toe to the ring finger ray is unlikely to result in great improvement. One further option in this situation is microvascular pollicization of the fifth ray and a double toe transfer to the hand on the third and fourth rays. This has the advantage of producing a fuller hand but retaining and restoring mobility in the first ray. Group 3a: Proximal metacarpal level in the hand with a competent and strong first ray. Here, bilateral second toe transfer on the fourth and fifth rays will produce a strong and competent hand even if relatively little motion is restored in the transferred digits. Group 3b: Distal metacarpal absence with the presence of a metacarpophalangeal joint in the thumb. If the patient is seen early in life, free phalangeal transfer in the thumb will convert the patient’s hand to essentially a group 3a hand and allow bilateral second toe reconstruction in the hand rays. Group 3c: This common type has a competent fifth finger but requires one toe to reconstruct a competent thumb, and a further toe may then be used on the ring ray to add power and volume to the hand. Groups 4a and 4b: This type of transverse absence is most commonly seen in ring constriction syndrome where function of the hand is already excellent. Little or no functional improvement will result from microsurgical toe transfers, but there are potentially considerable cosmetic benefits from creating a fuller hand. Although function in the hand itself is already good, good function in the reconstructed digits can be expected as a result of the relatively normal structures present proximally in the hand.

Longitudinal deficiency has been a contraindication because of the deficient proximal structures, such as tendons and nerves. Transfer of an adjacent digit usually reconstructs longitudinal deficiency. In the monodactylous cleft hand, however, we have found available extrinsic tendons and nerves as recipients, and while proximal joints and intrinsic muscles may be deficient, a satisfactory post can be created for opposition to a mobile thumb.

Reconstruction of the thumb. Traumatic or congenital transverse thumb deficiency is the ideal indication for microsurgical toe transfer. The most important determinant of useful outcome is the presence of a stable and mobile CMC joint. The recipient structures vary according to the etiology of the absence. The proximal structures are virtually normal in ring constriction syndrome. Useful recipient structures are also found in symbrachydactyly or transverse deficiency and are more likely to be present when there is a considerable metacarpal remnant. The lack of IP joint motion after transfer reflects the limited excursion of the extrinsic tendons.

Reconstruction of the fingers. Reconstruction of the fingers is not as common as thumb reconstruction. Reconstruction may be indicated after trauma or congenital absence when there is a reasonable thumb present. In such cases, bilateral second toe transfer creates a competent three- digit hand with powerful pinch and grasp. The presence of existing MCP joints enhances surgical outcome. However, absent MCP joints may still allow satisfactory function. Symbrachydactyly with a thumb and a small finger that is short, deviated, and unstable may be a candidate for bilateral second toe transfer to create a more complete hand. Such cases occupy the borderline between the functional and esthetic indication for toe transfer. In the absence of proximal phalangeal remnants, one option is to reconstruct an MCP joint with the toe by suturing the collateral ligaments and volar plate to the metacarpal head for stability. An alternative option is to perform chondrodesis of the base of the toe proximal phalanx to the head of the finger metacarpal, accepting less movement in return for more stability. The last option is to excise the metacarpal head and transfer the MTP joint from the foot, but this joint can be laterally unstable, is bulky, and tends to hyperextend, which can lead to an intercalated instability given the absence of functioning intrinsic muscles. The decision is dependent on surgeon preference and on the hand dissection as this reveals the neurovascular recipients, the recipient motors available, and the adequacy of the skin envelope.

The hand with no digits. Transverse arrest or severe symbrachydactyly without digits requires reconstruction of the thumb and fingers to create any prehensile function. These cases are challenging, and the outcome depends on the level of absence and the competence of the CMC joint of the thumb. A substantial thumb metacarpal remnant may be enhanced by bony augmentation. Even a free phalangeal transfer may be attempted to create an articulated thumb, although this should be approached with caution. Successful transfer may change the reconstruction from a hand that requires thumb and finger reconstruction to one that requires only finger restoration.

If thumb augmentation is unlikely to produce a competent digit, the surgeon may consider one transfer to the thumb and one to the finger rays (Fig. 36.83). Poor or absent CMC joint function requires placement of the digits facing each other. The majority of the movement will originate from the IP joints of the thumb and finger. Postoperative motion is often poor and limits the functional benefits of such transfer. If surgery is selected, a decision must be made to position the transfers to touch in maximum flexion or to place them more widely apart. The former allows small object grasp, whereas the latter allows larger object acquisition. In our experience, small object grasp is preferred to allow the child to hold fine objects such as paper, buttons, and shoelaces.

B, Useful large-object grasp with considerable power. C, Small-object pinch as a result of limited circumduction at the thumb basal joint.

In the presence of a reasonable CMC joint, a second toe is placed on the thumb and another placed on the third or fourth ray. The transfer may create a composite joint, using one articular surface from the toe and one from the finger. The preferred approach, however, is to perform a chondrodesis or arthrodesis between the base of the proximal phalanx in the toe and the shaft of the metacarpal in the thumb and finger. This technique has the additional benefit of stability and precise orientation of the digit. The presence of a CMC joint that allows circumduction decreases the necessity of precise toe alignment, and the function may be better.

Finally, a decision is required regarding the length of the digit to be created. The initial temptation is to create long digits; however, long digits may look bizarre in a two- digit hand (Fig. 36.84). This altered appearance may be disturbing for the child compared with shorter digits that allow the hand to appear more compact and natural. The physis of the transferred digit is retained for growth, which may require some resection of the thumb metacarpal to coordinate the length of the thumb and finger.

**Contraindications.** Contraindications to toe transfer in children are diminishing over time. There are few contraindications from an anatomic viewpoint. Absence of recipient nerves is a contraindication to surgery. We warn parents that if no recipient nerve is found, surgery will be aborted. This is a rare occurrence; even though the median nerve is commonly absent, there is usually an ulnar nerve and dorsal cutaneous nerves including radial sensory nerves. An exception to this generalization is found when the digital metacarpal remnants become progressively shorter from radial to ulnar. In these cases, we have found an increased frequency of absence of both median and ulnar nerves. The absence of adequate donor tendons is also a contraindication.

As discussed above, in the transverse absence group, motors are usually present, although the tendons may be conjoined with limited excursion. Nevertheless, limited IP excursion of a transferred toe may still provide power and an adequate functional result depending on the type of grasp being pursued. An extremely proximal transverse absence (e.g., the distal carpal row or radiocarpal level) will produce a disappointing result, and prosthetic alternatives should be considered. Absence or deformities of the toes precludes surgery.

The most considerable contraindications are the psychosocial considerations within the family and competence within the surgical unit. Careful assessment and counseling of the family and child are essential to ensure an adequate understanding of the goals, expectations, and consequences of surgery. The family and child must accept the donor defect and be able to manage the consequences of a successful or an unsuccessful procedure.

The age of the child is a relative contraindication. Absolute rules about indications and contraindications in respect to age of the child do not exist. We have found no benefit in performing the procedure before 1 year of age, and an analysis of long- term outcomes suggests some disadvantages in terms of ultimate range of motion. There are benefits in completing the surgery before school age, and this reconstruction is rarely accomplished in a single stage. After the age of 6, the child becomes involved in the choice of surgery, which may pose additional problems as there is enough autonomy to refuse surgery but not enough competence to make a reasonable decision based on future function.

**Presurgical assessment.** CT angiography may be useful to determine the presence of donor vessels, particularly in the proximal transverse absences, the monodactylous hand, or traumatic amputations. Preoperative assessment by a pediatric anesthesiologist is required because the procedure is lengthy and the child must be healthy. Ideally, the same surgeon and anesthetist should collaborate on all cases to maximize the team’s experience.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: MICROVASCULAR TOE TRANSFER

Hand preparation. The hand is explored under tourniquet control with exposure of the dorsal venous recipients, dorsal nerves and extensor tendons, site of osteosynthesis and arterial recipients, palmar nerves (if present), and flexor tendons. The dorsal exposure is via either an esthetic transverse incision at the wrist crease or a longitudinal extensile incision on the dorsum of the hand. The extensor tendons, veins, and the dorsal branches of the radial or ulnar nerve are identified and marked. Proximal excursion of the extensor tendons is confirmed via traction. The tendon with the greatest excursion is chosen as the donor tendon. When more than one tendon is required, either the extensor indicis proprius or extensor digiti quinti is used in an attempt to achieve some independent function. The venous anastomosis is planned on the dorsum of the hand, and the arterial anastomosis is planned for the palmar surface (anatomic snuffbox or at the base of first web dorsally, depending on pedicle length and recipient diameter).

The distal skin incisions are planned to result in a transverse scar on the palmar surface and a “V”- shaped scar on the dorsum. This shape allows insetting of a tongue of dorsal foot skin. The level of bony union is identified and marked. The proximal palmar exposure is accomplished via a zigzag incision. The median and/or ulnar nerves are identified at the level of the carpal canal along with the flexor tendons. The ulnar artery, the superficial palmar arch, and the radial artery within the anatomic snuffbox or at the base of the first web space are examined and identified for later use.

Retrieving the second toe (points relative to children). The skin pattern on the toe is designed with reference to the defect in the hand, usually incorporating a “V”- shaped flap of dorsal skin. The harvest begins under tourniquet control using the entire skin incision, including a dorsal linear incision running between the first and second metatarsals. A single dorsal vein is then isolated. In the child, the major veins lie in a distinctly deeper layer of subcutaneous fat than the smaller superficial veins. The vascular anatomy of the toe is identified in a retrograde distal- to- proximal manner. An assistant separates the first and second toes, and the fibular digital artery of the great toe is identified (Fig. 36.85). The artery is traced to its common origin with the second toe vessel. From this bifurcation, both the plantar and dorsal metatarsal arteries may be traced in a proximal direction. This technique facilitates early selection of the artery for anastomosis. In two- thirds of cases, there is a sizeable first dorsal metatarsal artery for anastomosis and the plantar artery and vessel to the great toe can be divided. The proximal dissection of the dorsal artery is then straightforward. In one- third of cases, the plantar artery must be used for anastomosis and the dissection is more difficult, but in these young, flexible patients it is considerably easier than the corresponding situation in the adult. Plantar dissection is facilitated by release of the transverse metatarsal ligament and use of a spreader retractor to widely and forcefully separate the first and second metatarsals. Care must be taken with the dissection of the plantar artery in the region of the first MTP joint where the plantar artery is tethered medially by the transverse branch to this joint. The deep peroneal branch of the anterior tibial nerve is harvested and usually lies in close proximity to the vein and first dorsal metatarsal artery. Both plantar digital nerves of the second toe are then dissected in a proximal direction and separated from the adjacent component of the common digital nerve.

The flexor tendons are exposed at the proximal edge of the flexor sheath just proximal to the MTP joint. The tendons are retracted distally with the ankle flexed, allowing division deep within the sole of the foot. The bone transection is then performed through or proximal to the MTP joint depending on requirement. The tourniquet is deflated, perfusion of the toe is confirmed, and hemostasis is achieved. The vascular pedicle is only divided immediately before transfer to the hand.

Osteosynthesis. After the toe is separated from the foot, the bone is prepared on the back table. When the osteosynthesis is planned at the base of the phalanx, the epiphyseal cartilage is progressively shaved away until the pink blush of subchondral bone is encountered. In some cases, a composite joint can be formed between the articular surface of the base of the proximal phalanx of the toe and the articular surface of the metacarpal. Longitudinal Kirschner wires are preferred. The wires cause little damage to the growing epiphyses, are quick to apply, and are adequate to achieve union in these young hands. Crossed Kirschner wires interfere with the adjacent toes, risk damage to the digital vessels, and do not allow correction of rotation after preliminary placement. Interosseous wire and plates are not used because of the proximal growing epiphyses unless osteosynthesis is performed through the metatarsal neck or shaft.

Tendon repairs. The toe is brought to the hand, and after osteosynthesis the extensor tendons are repaired. The tendons are passed deep to dorsal skin and fat and are repaired by weave at the wrist level. Two extensor tendon repairs are preferred. After extensor tendon repair, any extensor tendon that passes to an absent ray is divided to prevent a tethering or quadriga effect since the proximal extensor muscles may share a common belly.

Selection of the flexor tendon is more difficult. In symbrachydactyly or transverse absence, inadequate excursion is common. Unless individual flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) and flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendons are present in the hand, only the toe FDP is repaired. An overlapping repair or a weave is performed. The toe FDS tendon is cut short, and again any tendon anlage passing to an absent ray is divided to prevent the quadriga effect from the conjoined proximal muscle mass.

Nerve repairs. As many nerves as possible are repaired using the microscope. The nerve repair should be as proximal as possible to ensure good recipient nerve quality in the hand. Both digital nerves can be repaired to the same recipient in a Y- shaped manner, or dorsal nerves can be used where recipients are sparse. Particular care with coaptation is required given the critical role sensory recovery will have in the ultimate functional result. Documentation of the position of the nerve repair is important given the potential for revision surgery for tenolysis at a later date.

Vessels. A single artery and a single vein are repaired. Other authors have recommended repair of more vessels; however, this confers neither a theoretical nor a practical advantage in our opinion as failure rate is exceedingly low. Vein grafts are usually not needed. A warm pack is applied to the limb and the tourniquet is deflated. The transferred digit is observed for 10 or 15 minutes to ensure vascular patency.

Skin repair. The skin is loosely closed with absorbable sutures at wide intervals to allow blood to ooze from the wounds. No attempt is made to close the wound completely and tension is avoided. Most children will require scar revision surgery, so the use of split- skin grafts is encouraged rather than tight closure. The exception to this tenet is the dorsum of the hand. Considerable swelling may create shear forces on the vein as it passes beneath the distal edge of the dorsal palm skin. Therefore skin closure should be complete. The hand is then gently wrapped without compression and a plaster slab applied with the elbow at 90 degrees.

**Postoperative care.** The child is admitted to the critical care unit or similar level of intensive nursing in a temperature- controlled room for 24 to 48 hours after surgery. The child’s vital signs and fluid balance are monitored to ensure adequate intravascular volume is maintained without overfilling. The circulation of the toes is monitored regularly by observation. The authors have not found more intensive monitoring with Doppler probe necessary as experienced observation of color, temperature, and capillary return suffices. The child is kept comfortable and relatively sedated initially. Regional local anesthetic infusions or long- acting blocks are used for analgesia. The child is discharged to home 5 to 7 days after surgery. Dressings are changed

6 weeks after surgery, usually under general anesthesia. The Kirschner wires are removed and the digits gently manipulated. A thermoplastic splint is fabricated for nighttime and during challenging physical activity. Parents are instructed in passive and active motion and to protect the insensate digit from harm, especially self- inflicted. The parents are warned that during reinnervation the child will experience uncomfortable dysesthesias that are normal and transient.

**Additional indications and technical alternatives.** Another indication for microvascular toe transfer is traumatic loss of digits. Toe transfers have been reported in children for the reconstruction of traumatic defects, including the metacarpal hand. In some cases, these defects are bilateral, and the use of a single toe from each foot will not produce adequate reconstruction of function. The use of transfers from the great toe (wraparound flap or trimmed toe transfer) and the use of monobloc transfer of the second and third toes have been necessary in some cases.

Transfers from the great toe. Transfers from the great toe are favored in adults for certain aspects of hand reconstruction.In the congenital hand, great toe transfer is seldom indicated because reconstruction can usually be accomplished using the second toe. In some traumatic defects, however, great toe transfer may be necessary. Considerable esthetic morbidity results from harvesting the entire great toe. Parents are often reluctant to allow such harvest. The wraparound free flap from the great toe contains no epiphysis and is usually unsuitable in the growing child. The wrap- around free flap may be used in the older child or in distal digital injuries with soft tissue avulsion.

The metacarpal hand. Loss of most or all of the digits may be a result of trauma. The metacarpals may be spared, and the thumb may or may not escape injury. Adequate function can be expected when the thumb has been amputated distal to the middle of the proximal phalanx and if a portion of the proximal phalanges of the fingers is present. Any more proximal loss usually results in poor function. Such hands are best reconstructed using some form of free toe transfer.

**Results.** The results of toe transfer in children have been reported. In general, the procedure has been demonstrated to be reliable with a low microvascular failure rate, with good sensory recovery and functional improvement. Growth uniformly continued in the transferred toes in the reported series.

Kay and Wiberg initially reported on 66 toe transfers in 40 children. All but three children achieved motion in their transferred toes. Mean total range of active motion was 60 degrees and was independent of the number of tendons repaired. The passive range was always greater than the active range. Grasp function was useful and satisfactory in both small and large object grasp in the majority of cases (Fig. 36.86). Sensibility recovery showed two- point discriminations between 4 and 5 mm. These results have been corroborated by other reports.

Additional attention has focused on the psychological aspects of surgery. The vast majority of parents (84%) and children (88%) thought the hand was improved after surgery. Only one parent and one child thought the hand looked worse. Scarring was not a dominant complaint of the parents and was virtually ignored by the children. Most parents (80%) believe the hand was incorporated into function most of the time. No adverse effect of toe transfer from the psychosocial viewpoint was found. The reconstructive surgery appears to have a positive response on the child and family.

**Secondary surgery.** Microsurgery rarely offers a one- stage reconstruction, and secondary procedures are usually necessary. The need for adjustment of the skin, bone, and tendon transfers is common. Procedures include pulp plasty, scar revision, excision of skin graft, and tenolysis. Opposition transfers and rotation osteotomies may also be needed. Secondary surgery is usually deferred for at least 18 months to allow scar maturation, joint motion, and the incorporation of the transfer into use.

#### Expected Outcomes

The outcomes of digital reconstruction depend on the surgery and the extent of hypoplasia in the hand. Augmentation of existing digits with free phalangeal grafts may improve pinch and grasp, but growth is limited, and the stability of the graft is variable. The addition of digits to the hand by vascularized toe transfer may add or assist in the development of pinch and grasp in a hand with limited function. In general, these procedures should not be undertaken unless the surgery is expected to add at least one new prehension pattern. In rare cases, appearance may be a relative indication and a satisfactory outcome will depend on the child’s and peers’ perceptions.

## CLINODACTYLY

Clinodactyly describes a digit with a congenital angular deformity in the radioulnar (coronal) plane distal to the MCP joint (Fig. 36.87). Minor angulation, particularly of the small finger, is common to the point of normalcy. Noteworthy clinodactyly has been defined as angulation greater than 10 degrees. The anomaly arises because one (or more) of the phalanges is either triangular or trapezoidal in shape, and therefore the alignment of the adjacent IP joint is deviated away from perpendicular to the normal longitudinal axis of the finger. The abnormal shape generally progresses with age in the child as a result of ongoing asymmetric longitudinal growth of the phalanx. Most clinodactyly occurs as a congenital anomaly; however, a similar deformity may arise after trauma or secondary to an underlying bony tumor.

### Epidemiology

The true prevalence of clinodactyly is difficult to ascertain. Clinodactyly is often an incidental finding rather than considerable compliant. The literature states that clinodactyly is present in at least 1% of the population. Clinodactyly presents most commonly as an isolated radial inclination of the small finger, owing to the middle phalanx adopting a triangular or trapezoidal shape. This form of clinodactyly may be inherited as an autosomal dominant trait and is often bilateral. Expression of the trait is variable, with males more likely to express the phenotype. Clinodactyly of the index finger with radial deviation with or without brachydactyly has also been recognized as a less common entity. Clinodactyly is also seen as a part of many syndromes and complex hand anomalies, and in these settings may involve other digits, including the thumb.

Severe clinodactyly may be obvious in the neonate or infant; however, most cases are recognized later as the angulation develops with growth. In small finger clinodactyly, the reason for presentation is ordinarily the appearance rather than the function. The small finger may reside under the ring finger with flexion, but this rarely obstructs grip considerably. Thumb or central digit clinodactyly may cause functional impairment secondary to altered flexion of the fingers or limitation of pinch with malalignment of the opposing digits.

The radiographic appearance varies with the age of the patient and degree of bony involvement. In the skeletally mature patient, a triangular or trapezoidal bone will be apparent (see Fig. 36.87). In the skeletally immature patient, the basal secondary center of ossification may be malformed, curving around the base of the phalanx on the short side of the phalanx and extending along the diaphysis along the shortened side of the bone (Fig. 36.88). The most severe form of longitudinal epiphyseal bracket forms a “C”- shape as the bracket unites the proximal and distal growth centers.

Clinodactyly can be classified according to etiology: congenital or acquired. Congenital clinodactyly is the result of congenital abnormality of the growth plate anatomy. Acquired clinodactyly can occur as a result of injury to the growth plate (eccentric partial fusion and consequent deformity with growth) or as the result of an underlying tumor deforming the joint surface. Other classifications are based on severity, and the authors find the most useful classification is that proposed by Cooney (Table 36.7).

### PATHOANATOMY

The longitudinal growth of tubular bones, such as the phalanges, is generated at the epiphyseal growth plate, with axial alignment maintained by factors intrinsic and extrinsic to the growth plate. An abnormal growth plate secondary to a congenital anomaly or postnatal insult will produce progressive deformation with growth. The growth plate anomaly associated with clinodactyly is a “C”- shaped physeal plate extending to some extent along one side of the bone, forming a bracket that restricts longitudinal growth on one side of the bone. This was described by Jones in his description of triangular or “delta” phalanges in severe clinodactyly.

The pathologic physis has been described variously as a longitudinal epiphyseal bracket (LEB) or longitudinally bracketed diaphysis (LBD). When the bracket completely bridges between the proximal and distal physes, no longitudinal growth is possible on this side of the bone, and a short, triangular bone, the “delta phalanx,” is produced. If the bracket is incomplete, some longitudinal growth occurs and the phalanx is trapezoidal. The epiphyseal bracket affects the middle phalanx most commonly and this bone is the last bone to ossify in the digital skeleton. The epiphyseal bracket may also occur in the proximal phalanx as seen in the thumb in Apert syndrome or within the extra phalanx in hyperphalangism. More complex variants of the anomaly exist, with dual or “kissing delta phalanx” occurring in specific syndromic anomalies such as Rubinstein- Taybi syndrome and CenaniLenz syndactyly. Acquired causes of clinodactyly include trauma to the growth plate from injury, frostbite, or inflammatory arthritis with asymmetric physeal closure. Bone tumors can also produce a secondary angular deformity, though this is usually related to an asymmetric deformity of the joint surface produced by eccentric expansion of tumors rather than tethering of the growth plate.

Although the skeletal anomaly generates the angulation of the finger, the underlying soft tissues are also affected. Severe angulation leads to soft tissue shortening on the concave side of the digit and possible displacement of the extensor mechanism. This soft tissue deformation requires consideration during surgical correction.

### Management

The majority of patients with isolated clinodactyly of the small finger present with an esthetic rather than a functional problem. Surgery should be avoided in these cases because any improvement in appearance occurs at the risk of scarring and stiffness. Surgical correction is indicated for considerable clinodactyly with shortening and angulation of at least 20 degrees, particularly where the radial digits or thumb are involved and angular deformity interferes with pinch.

Splinting for clinodactyly is fruitless. Correction of the skeletal anomaly requires either epiphysiolysis with resection of the longitudinal epiphyseal bracket or an osteotomy of the deformed phalanx to realign the digit.

Epiphysiolysis by division or resection of the abnormal longitudinal bracket has been performed in clinodactyly to eliminate the ongoing deformation of the phalanx and allow the normal portion of the epiphyseal growth plate to generate “catch- up” longitudinal growth on the shortened side and produce angular correction (Fig. 36.89). While reported initially in 1964, the procedure of resection of the bracket and interposition of a fat graft was described by Vickers in 1987.The procedure is performed through a midlateral incision on the short side of the phalanx. The cartilaginous or bony bracket is resected until metaphyseal bone is exposed from dorsal to volar and the normal portion of the transverse growth plate is defined. The defect created is filled with fat, harvested from the forearm, to fill the dead space and prevent fusion across the growth plate. The procedure is limited to patients with open growth plates and is best performed early, although Vickers and other authors have performed the procedure with good results on children with only 1 to 2 years of growth remaining.Reported results vary but improvement of at least half of the angular deformity can be expected. Complications can include premature epiphyseal closure but secondary epiphysiolysis does not seem to be of further benefit. Bracket resection does not preclude the use of corrective osteotomy for residual angulation and requires minimal splinting or postoperative immobilization.

Realignment of the digit may be achieved with either a closing, opening, or a reverse wedge phalangeal osteotomy. The closing wedge osteotomy (Fig. 36.90) is attractive because of its relative simplicity and reliability; however, in the shortened digit, a further loss of length may be unacceptable. An increase in length of the phalanx is possible with an opening wedge osteotomy. The defect created by the osteotomy is stabilized with Kirschner wires and in the younger child may heal without need for bone graft. In the older child with a preoperative deformity of greater than 45 degrees, a bone graft is recommended to limit the risk of nonunion and recurrent deformity. The reverse wedge osteotomy is technically challenging in the small tubular bones of the child’s hand and requires access to both sides of the digit. Corrective osteotomy is indicated in moderate deviation that interferes with function. If possible, correction is best deferred until skeletal maturity to allow ample time for bone growth. In the skeletally immature patient, osteotomy is technically challenging, with risks of incomplete correction, excessive correction, or physeal injury. In severe clinodactyly or thumb clinodactyly, earlier surgery may be required. Corrective osteotomy tries to achieve a single- stage correction. The soft tissue deficit on the concave side may be addressed with a Z- plasty. In severe angulations, a Z- plasty may be inadequate and a rotational flap or step advancement flap described by Evans and James may be required. For the severe types, realignment of the extensor mechanism may also be necessary to balance the corrected digit. Clinodactyly in the triphalangeal thumb or hyperphalangism of the fingers may be addressed by excision of the extra malformed phalanx. Ligament reconstruction or arthrodesis of the adjacent joints is obligatory to restore stability.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: CLINODACTYLY

The preferred approach for mild to moderate clinodactyly is to avoid surgery. Surgery is uniformly rejected in the common mild small finger clinodactyly. When the angulation is considerable and the phalanx is a “delta phalanx,” early epiphyseal bracket resection and fat grafting may be performed. When the patient presents with substantial angulation and is skeletally mature, the decision is based on the configuration of the phalanx. The trapezoidal phalanx tolerates the shortening of a closing wedge osteotomy, whereas a triangular bone will benefit from an opening wedge osteotomy. A reverse wedge osteotomy is technically demanding in small triangular phalanges, and an opening wedge osteotomy is preferred with or without the addition of bone graft. In patients with thumb clinodactyly, a primary corrective osteotomy is performed to produce a well- aligned thumb column rather than rely on gradual correction with growth after bracket resection.

The expected outcome of surgery following osteotomy is angular correction; however, this may be complicated by stiffness secondary to scar and adhesions. Therefore the relative risks and benefits must be taken into consideration. The outcomes of epiphysiolysis vary according to the extent of growth potential remaining in the digit at the time of surgery.

## KIRNER DEFORMITY

Kirner deformity is characterized by progressive palmar- radial curvature of the distal phalanx of the digit and usually affects the small finger. The deformity was initially described by Kirner in 1927 in the German literature and subsequently by Thomas in 1936 in the *Lancet.* Most of the literature regarding Kirner deformity consists of isolated reports or small series. The condition, however, presents with a characteristic appearance and is common enough to be encountered by most pediatric hand surgeons.

### Epidemiology

The incidence has been estimated to be 0.25% of the population, and females are affected twice as frequently as males. The deformity may be inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with incomplete penetrance. The homozygous state is responsible for expression of the deformity in multiple digits and both hands. The deformity occurs sporadically in the majority of cases. Song proposed a classification based on age of onset as either early- onset (congenital deformity present at birth and associated with a family history) or late- onset (deformity developing in late childhood or adolescence and sporadic in incidence). In the more common late- onset presentation, there is a history of swelling over the dorsum of the distal phalanx base of the small finger. There may be some discomfort associated with this stage, but usually the swelling is painless. The deformity then ensues with the development of the palmar- radial curvature of the distal fingertip (Fig. 36.91), which is marked by the clubbed or beaked appearance of the fingernail. Once established, the deformity is stable. Joint motion at the distal IP joint is preserved.

![[Fig.36.91.png]]
> **Fig. 36.91** Clinical appearance of Kirner deformity of the right small finger.

While Kirner deformity is usually seen in isolation, it has also been noted in cases of Cornelia de Lange syndrome, Silver syndrome, Turner syndrome, and Down syndrome. The differential diagnosis in the unilateral deformity is a delayed presentation of distal phalangeal trauma, particularly a physeal fracture with palmar angulation, or a burn or frostbite injury to the physis. A thorough history coupled with the physical and x- ray findings usually enable confident diagnosis.

### PATHOANATOMY

A number of theories of etiology have been proposed to be responsible for Kirner deformity. Early observations attributed the deformity to an acquired etiology such as osteochondritis of either the epiphysis or entire distal phalanx or focal epiphysiolysis with the angulation due to the effect of the strong flexor tendon on the compromised phalanx. Despite these reports, there is general consensus that the deformity is due to a congenital cause given the epidemiology of the condition. An abnormal distal insertion of the FDP tendon along the phalanx has been linked to the deformity. Radiographic and more recently magnetic resonance imaging evidence of congenital physeal deformity has also been proposed as the basis of the deformity.Fairbank and coworkers described an L- shaped basal physis accounting for restriction of palmar growth and progressive deformity.

Kirner deformity is characterized by distortion and widening of the physeal plate along with curvature of the diaphysis of the distal phalanx (Fig. 36.92). Radiographs show an abnormality of the epiphysis with the palmar lip of the epiphysis relatively lengthened and the metaphyseal margin of the physis sclerotic and ragged. Physeal plate closure is delayed and begins in the dorsal half of the physis. Histology of the phalanx at this stage demonstrates lysis at the junction of the metaphysis and physis. Later, the diaphyseal curvature is well established, and in the mature form of the deformity, the physis has completely fused and normal bone density is restored throughout the deformed phalanx.

### Management

Kirner deformity has little effect on function. Musicians and keyboard operators may identify specific problems related to small finger position. The indication for surgery is primarily restoration of the finger appearance. The patient must be counseled carefully about the potential risks of surgery and expected results.

Splinting the small finger into extension has been reported as effective in relieving discomfort in the early swelling stage of the deformity, although no benefit in prevention or correction of deformity. If the deformity warrants correction, surgery is required. Benatar has reported the correction of the deformity by early release of the abnormal component of the long flexor tendon insertion. If the epiphysis remains open, another option is to perform a dorsal hemiepiphysiodesis or resect a dorsal wedge including the dorsal growth plate to prevent progression of the deformity. For the mature deformity, corrective osteotomies have been used to improve the alignment of the phalanx and corresponding nail growth (Fig. 36.93). The deformity has been addressed with a dorsal closing wedge osteotomy, although this has risk of nail bed injury given that the deformity is most marked in the subungual portion of the phalanx. Alternatively, either a single or multiple opening wedge osteotomies of the volar cortex can be performed to unfold the phalanx. The technique of distraction lengthening has also been used to correct the deformity. Gamo and coworkers described a percutaneous approach to avoid devascularizing the small fragments of bone. The opening wedge osteotomies are performed in two stages with initial percutaneous predrilling with a 1.0- mm Kirschner wire under fluoroscopic control that allows accurate positioning of the planned osteotomy. The osteotomy is then completed percutaneously with a 1.8- mm Kirschner wire, hinging the osteotomy open on the dorsal periosteum.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: KIRNER DEFORMITY

Splinting can be attempted in the early stages of the deformity for symptom relief. Although, there may be a role for early surgery as recommended by Benatar, we do not routinely divide the distal part of the flexor tendon insertion to eliminate the deforming force on the developing bone. Corrective surgery is usually deferred until the patient is able to participate in the decision- making process, as surgery is primarily for appearance rather than function. The authors’ preference is to use volar osteotomies to correct the phalangeal curvature. The nail plate is removed and a radial dorsolateral incision preserving the paronychial fold is used to expose the distal phalanx. An alternative exposure is a direct palmar approach. Stabilization of the base of the phalanx with a temporary Kirschner wire across the distal IP joint prior to osteotomy is a useful step as it will facilitate subsequent reduction and fixation of the osteotomized phalanx but limiting dorsal rotation and subluxation of the proximal fragment. The osteotomies are planned distal to the physis (if the physis is open) and the flexor tendon insertion is preserved by careful retraction if a proximal osteotomy is required. Two or three osteotomies are used, depending on the size of the phalanx and extent of deformity. The volar periosteum and any physeal bracket (if present) is incised, and the dorsal periosteum is preserved to act as a hinge for the correction and to protect the overlying nail matrix. The osteotomies are performed with a sharp osteotome. The curvature of the phalanx is corrected, and fixation is accomplished with an axial Kirschner wire. The Kirschner wire is left in place until the osteotomies have healed. The digit is protected with a splint during the healing process. This is a technically demanding procedure in the small distal phalanx with risk of nail bed injury, devascularization of the small intercalary segments, and nonunion.

## MACRODACTYLY

*Macrodactyly* is the congenital condition of abnormally enlarged digits noted at birth or developing postnatal with disproportionate growth. Macrodactyly is uncommon with an incidence of only 0.9% in Flatt’s series of upper limb congenital anomalies. Despite being a relatively rare condition, the impact of substantial overgrowth on the appearance and function of the affected hand along with the psychological wellbeing of the child is substantial. The overgrowth can seem uncontrollable and the appearance of the digits bizarre and stigmatizing. Such overgrowth can affect the upper or lower extremity including the soft tissue and skeletal elements of the digit(s) that are diffusely enlarged. Almost all cases of macrodactyly are associated with *lipomatosis of nerve* (LN), the massive fibroadipose enlargement of the digital nerve within the enlarged digit and extending proximally to involve the affected nerve trunk (Fig. 36.94).

![[Fig.36.94.png]]
> **Fig. 36.94** A and B, Macrodactyly of the fifth finger associated with lipofibromatous change in the ulnar digital nerve along with a normal radial digital nerve.

Macrodactyly is remarkable for not only its clinical features but also the extent of the associated nomenclature. Both macrodactyly and the associated LN have had many labels including *nerve territory- oriented macrodactyly, megalodactyly, digital gigantism, macrodystrophia lipomatosa, macrodactylia fibrolipomatosis* and *lipomatosis overgrowth* to describe the enlarged digit and *fibrolipomatous hamartoma, lipofibromatous hamartoma, fibrolipoma, lipofibroma,* and *interfibrillar lipoma of the nerve* to describe the nerve pathology. This excess of terminology contributes to confusion and a uniform nomenclature is advocated, hence macrodactyly is the preferred term for the enlarged digit and LN for the associated nerve pathology.

The extent of overgrowth in terms of the number and specific digits involved, extent of involvement of the proximal limb, and the rate and degree of enlargement varies between individuals. In addition to macrodactyly, distinct patterns of overgrowth of digits or the extremity may be seen in a number of syndromes (Box 36.2).

### Classification

Macrodactyly has been classified on the basis of rate of growth with Barsky proposing division of cases into a *static* type, where the enlarged digit grew commensurate with the growth of the child, and the *progressive* type, where the affected digit’s growth outstrips the normal digital rate of growth. Flatt also proposed a classification based on the pathologic basis of the presentation distinguishing gigantism with lipofibromatosis (type I), gigantism with neurofibromatosis (type II), and gigantism with digital hyperostosis (type III). Upton later added type IV, gigantism with hemihypertropy. The clinical usefulness of these systems is limited in practice by the variation in phenotype that is characteristic of the condition. There is value, however, in distinguishing the cases of overgrowth associated with specific syndromes because of the associated anomalies that may be expected and the importance of tumor surveillance in some of these syndromic patients.

### Etiology

The advances in the understanding of the molecular biology of the condition are likely to illuminate the underlying pathology in macrodactyly and other forms of overgrowth. It is understood that macrodactyly forms part of the PIK3CA- related overgrowth spectrum (PROS) (see Box 36.2). Phosphatidylinositol 4,5- biphosphate 3- kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) is the gene that codes for the production a component of the phosphatidylinositol 3- kinase (PI3K) protein that forms part of the PI3K- AKT- mTOR cell signaling pathway. Somatic mosaic gain- in- function mutations in PIK3CA in the developing limb bud can lead to uncontrolled growth of the affected part with the individual phenotype dependent on the timing and the tissue localization of the mutation. The PIK3CA mutation leading to macrodactyly leads to tumor- like growth, but it is a benign condition without potential for malignant transformation. The mutation is postzygotic and a sporadic condition without evidence of inheritance. Other overgrowth conditions in the PROS (see Box 36.2) have overlapping phenotypes with macrodactyly and have mutations in PIK3CA gene that can be identified with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay or genome sequencing in affected tissue. Future classifications are likely to be based on genomic analysis, with implications for natural history and perhaps nonsurgical management.

### PATHOANATOMY

Macrodactyly is usually unilateral and may affect more than one digit. Multiple digit enlargement is two to three times more common than a single enlarged digit. The index finger is the most frequently affected digit, particularly in combination with the thumb or long finger. The enlargement of the individual digit is not uniform. The overgrowth affects the distal digit more than the proximal digit and the palmar soft tissue more than the dorsal soft tissue. The joints are typically hyperextended, particularly the distal IP joint (DIPJ). There is also often angular deformity in coronal plane; radial digits usually deviate in a radial direction. In contrast, affected ulnar digits deviate in an ulnar direction. Where two digits are involved, the deviation is usually divergent (Fig. 36.95). Enlarged thumbs are typically positioned in abduction and extension. Overgrowth of the perionychial soft tissue is often marked and, combined with the enlargement of the nail complex, leads to chronic paronychia. In addition to clinodactyly, other anatomic anomalies are seen including syndactyly, which is seen in 10% of cases (Fig. 36.96). Histology of the digital tissues shows thickening of the skin with decreased sweat gland density and abundant subcutaneous fat with increased fibrous stroma. Lipomatosis of the digital nerve is seen, and the digital arteries are typically enlarged. The bony structure shows wide medullary canals, irregular trabeculae, and thickening of the periosteum.

Overgrowth with neurofibromatosis is different as the nerve trunk is replaced by a plexiform neurofibroma with increased fibrovascular stroma within the nerve fascicles rather than lipomatosis of the nerve.

Macrodactyly may also be part of a broader overgrowth anomaly affecting the proximal limb in addition to the enlarged digit. The proximal overgrowth may be *segmental gigantism,* affecting only a part of one limb; *macrodystrophia lipomatosa,* which is associated with disproportionate and progressive adipose hypertrophy; *hemihypertrophy,* affecting one side of the body (associated with neurofibromatosis or Klippel- Trenaunay syndrome); or *crossed gigantism,* affecting regions on both sides of the body. Sometimes the hemihypertrophy can be subtle, but if a patient has macrodactyly of all digits, the remainder of the limb should carefully be examined for enlargement.

### Management

Macrodactyly is a disfiguring condition. Patients usually present in childhood with obvious disproportionate digital growth. On occasion, the patient may present later with a compression neuropathy secondary to pathologic enlargement of affected nerve(s). Children with macrodactyly can be subject to teasing with social consequences. The hand is often concealed and ignored. Indications for surgery include both esthetic and functional rationale. The enlarged digit(s) can obstruct prehension and has poor sensation.

Macrodactyly is difficult to treat. The progressive and diffuse nature of the condition precludes complete correction to normal. The expectations of the parents, and sometimes the child, must be carefully managed prior to surgery. In particular, the inability to establish a normal digit and the requirement of multiple surgeries should be established at the outset of treatment. Available surgical options either limit ongoing growth, reduce and realign the digit, or remove the affected digit (Table 36.8). Procedures may be combined at the same time or staged according to the patient’s needs as a treatment program is tailored to the individual. In addition, proximal nerve decompression may be needed in cases with concurrent compressive neuropathy (see Fig. 36.98).

#### Limiting Digital Growth

Proposed methods to limit growth include addressing the trophic effect of the lipomatous nerves by either stripping the branches of the digital nerves or resecting of the entire digital nerve. The resection of a digital nerve may also be done with a radical debulking of the larger side of the finger. The sensory deficit following ablation is often less than expected as the remaining nerve is adequate to supply the entire skin envelope. However, caution is advised for the radial side of the index and the ulnar side of the thumb as loss of sensibility would interfere with pinch.

Longitudinal growth may be arrested by epiphysiodesis of the phalanges. This may be accomplished by various methods including using a bur or drill to ablate the physis or direct resection of the epiphyseal plate or physeal stapling in larger bones. Epiphysiodesis is performed when the digit reaches the length of the corresponding digit in the same sex parent. Digital deviation can be corrected simultaneously by including the physis within a metaphyseal closing wedge osteotomy (Fig. 36.99). Progressive deviation can also be managed by hemiepiphysiodesis on the convex side of the digit, although not as reliably as corrective osteotomy. Percutaneous Kirschner wires are often required to stabilize the phalanx after physeal resection, particularly if combined with a wedge osteotomy. Complications of physeal arrest include joint stiffness, excessive bone formation at the site of the physis, or secondary angulation after incomplete physeal destruction. Physeal arrest does not reduce soft tissue growth or the transverse (appositional) growth of the skeleton.

#### Reducing and Realigning the Digit

Debulking the soft tissues reduces the size of the enlarged digit (Fig. 36.100). Usually, one side of the digit is debulked at a time with the convex side of the digit approached initially and at least a 3- month interval between procedures on the same digit. The digit is approached from a midlateral approach. A wedge of skin, fat, and potentially digital nerve is resected. The digital artery should be carefully preserved, particularly when the other side of the digit is scheduled for debulking. Debulking the nail complex requires particular attention to the importance in appearance in the digit. The lateral nail fold on the side of the digit being debulked is raised as a dorsally based flap. Resection of a lateral wedge of the sterile and germinal matrix and underlying lateral margin of the distal phalanx is performed to reduce the width of the nail. Resection of the volar tuft and often the distal tip of the distal phalanx can further reduce the dimension of the fingertip. The nail complex is then repaired. In addition, a distal phalanx epiphysiodesis, DIPJ arthrodesis, or flexion osteotomy of the distal phalangeal shaft may be required to address the skeletal deformity of the distal fingertip.

![[Fig.36.100.png]]
> **Fig. 36.100** A and B, Reduction of the index finger by using a combination of angulation osteotomy, resection of skin and fat, debulking of the enlarged digital nerve, and narrowing of the nail bed.

Skeletal reduction can be achieved by either narrowing or shortening. Narrowing the skeleton can be achieved by resecting the sides of the phalanges, particularly of the distal phalanx, or a central longitudinal ostectomy in either the sagittal or coronal planes. These longitudinal ostectomies are technically challenging and can be complicated by hematoma and/or joint stiffness. These ostectomies are less commonly performed and consequently the excess girth of the skeleton remains an issue for most patients. Skeletal shortening can be achieved by several techniques. An angulated digit can be shortened and realigned via corrective ostectomy. Arthrodesis shortens the digital skeleton and is often applicable at the DIPJ level where motion is less critical. These single level segmental ostectomies will have a moderate effect on digit length. For more considerable shortening, numerous techniques have been described that combine distal phalangeal and soft tissue resection to shorten the digit with repositioning of the nail complex. Barsky’s procedure involves shortening the digit by filleting the distal phalanx, excising the skin over the dorsum of the middle phalanx, and placing the nail on the end of the middle phalanx. Flatt modified this technique by preserving the distal part of the distal phalanx and fusing this to a shortened middle phalanx to reduce the skeleton. Considerable bony shortening (greater than 3 cm) may require concomitant FDP shortening. The excess palmar skin produced by the shortening can be excised at a second stage. The use of a palmar pedicle maintains a reliable blood supply and good sensation in the distal finger. In contrast, Tsuge’s procedure preserves the nail on a dorsal skin pedicle including the dorsal cortex of the distal phalanx (Fig. 36.101). The digit is shortened with excision of the pulp and the palmar part of the distal phalanx. The nail is fitted into a recess on the dorsum of the middle phalanx. A fold of skin is produced on the dorsum of the digit, which can be revised with a second procedure. The dorsal pedicle, however, has proven to be unreliable in the fingers but has been used in the toes with success. Rosenberg’s technique transposed the nail farther by raising the nail unit as an island flap on the digital neurovascular pedicle. After excision of the distal phalanx and pulp, the nail unit was repositioned on the dorsum of the middle phalanx. Excision of the entire middle phalanx has also been reported with an IP joint created between the distal and proximal phalanges. The amount of resection, however, is limited by the capacity for telescoping of the excessive soft tissue. An alternative to transposing the nail unit onto a shortened skeleton is to reduce the nail and shorten the digit further with segmental ostectomies along the length of the digit. Akinci and coworkers reported good results in a series of older children and adults who had undergone singlestage combined skeletal shortening and correction together with soft tissue debulking of the convex side of the digit. In a more complicated procedure published in the Japanese literature, Fujita and colleagues raised a palmar flap including the radial neurovascular bundle along the length of the digit, which left the ulnar neurovascular bundle, skeleton, and flexor tendon on the dorsal flap. The ulnar one- third and distal end of the palmar flap and the radial one- third and the tip of the distal phalanx and nail were excised to match each other.

![[Fig.36.101.png]]
> **Fig. 36.101** Tsuge method for reducing the length of a macrodactylous finger. A, The incision is midlateral to the end of the finger. The volar part of the distal phalanx and the dorsal part of the middle phalanx are removed. B, Excess volar skin and soft tissues are removed from the end of the finger. C, The dorsal part of the distal phalanx and nail are recessed to fit the middle phalanx. The remaining excess dorsal soft tissue can be removed later. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

The thumb can be addressed using similar techniques. The techniques of soft tissue debulking are similar, and shortening can be performed via MCP arthrodesis. Millesi devised a more complicated pattern of reduction osteotomies that preserves joint motion. The nail width and length are reduced, but the central nail is excised. This technique often results in ridging of the nail.

The number and variety of techniques described is testament to the difficulty in achieving a result acceptable to the surgeon and patient, hence the motivation for further refinement of the indications and results of surgery for this condition.

#### Amputation

The ultimate reduction procedure is amputation. This procedure is an option for macrodactyly isolated to a single digit that is progressively growing at an uncontrollable rate (Fig. 36.102). Amputation should be considered early in these cases to limit the trauma and avoid the multiple procedures. Disarticulation through the DIP joint can be considered for distal macrodactyly, but the absence of a nail can make the digit conspicuous. For a border finger, ray amputation is effective and creates an adequate first web space in the case of the index finger. For a central digit, transmetacarpal amputation allows for transposition of the adjacent digit to restore competency to the hand. In cases where both the index and middle fingers are amputated, second toe transfer for digit reconstruction to create a four- digit hand has been reported. While there is concern that the overgrowth phenomenon will recur within the transferred digit due to the influence of the abnormal proximal nerve, this has not occurred. Amputation of the thumb should be avoided unless the overgrowth is unyielding to preclude any function. In early amputation cases, possible digit substitution with toes transfer from the foot functions quite well. Alternatively, an adjacent digit can be pollicized into the thumb position (Fig. 36.103).

#### Special Cases

**Hyperostoses.** Loss of motion can be addressed with ostectomy of the osteochondral masses, which may include part of the volar plate. Recurrence is universal and arthrodesis is often required after the digit attains adult length. Implant arthroplasty of the MCP joints has been used in very select cases.

**Hemihypertrophy.** In macrodactyly associated with hemihypertrophy, contracture of the digit(s) due to the presence of abnormal extrinsic and intrinsic musculature is common. Contracture release, debulking of the hypertrophic intrinsic musculature, and soft tissue rebalancing has been performed in a limited number of cases. Skeletal correction may also be required.

### Nonoperative Treatment

The advances in understanding the mechanism of overgrowth in macrodactyly with the PI3K- AKT- mTOR pathway of intracellular signaling implicated (see above) has revealed pharmaceutical options. Inhibitors of the upregulation of this pathway have been used in oncology, and there is prospect that similar inhibitors may limit overgrowth. A clinical study has been performed in a group of patients with congenital lipomatous (fatty) overgrowth, vascular malformations, epidermal nevi, and scoliosis/skeletal/spinal anomalies (CLOVES) and PROS conditions treated with a specific PIK3CA inhibitor with encouraging results. The results are encouraging; however, many questions remain about pharmaceutical therapy.

### AUTHORS’ PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: MACRODACTYLY

Macrodactyly is often a devastating congenital difference for the child and family. Macrodactyly represents a social stigma, anxiety for parents, and a technical challenge for the surgeon. Multiple procedures are usually required to lessen the enlargement and the end result may be unsatisfactory. In contrast, the authors believe that ray amputation is a worthwhile procedure and should be considered early before multiple procedures to salvage a deformed digit with limited function. Most parents with young children find this a difficult decision and prefer digit- preserving surgery. Experienced surgeons, however, will take the time and convey the trials and tribulations with reduction surgery. The cost to the family and child of multiple operations during childhood must be weighed and the ultimate outcome of a stiff scarred enlarged digit discussed. There is no doubt that the challenge of macrodactyly reduction can appeal to enthusiastic and skillful surgeons, but this enthusiasm must be tempered with realism.

The surgical plan is based on digital involvement, size of the digit(s) at presentation, estimated adult length of the digit, degree of motion, and overall alignment. For digital conservation in young children, improving the appearance before school age is indicated. Debulking of subcutaneous fat with or without digital nerve resection is performed via a midlateral approach (see Fig. 36.100). The decision regarding digital nerve resection is dependent on the digit involved and relative progression of growth. The standard plan is debulking one side of the digit at a time with at least a 3- month interlude before debulking the other side. Debulking can be combined with corrective osteotomy for deviated digits.

![[Fig.36.100.png]]
> **Fig. 36.100** A and B, Reduction of the index finger by using a combination of angulation osteotomy, resection of skin and fat, debulking of the enlarged digital nerve, and narrowing of the nail bed.

When the digit has reached adult length, longitudinal growth can be stopped with epiphysiodesis of all the phalanges. A midlateral approach is performed with physeal plate ablation using a blade, curette, cautery, or drill. Fluoroscopy is necessary to locate the position of the physes and subsequent ablation. The physeal plate resection can be configured as a closing wedge osteotomy to correct digital alignment (see Fig. 36.99). The skeleton is stabilized with Kirschner wires at each level. The soft tissues can again be debulked at this procedure.

![[Fig.36.99.png]]
> **Fig. 36.99** A and B, Excision of the epiphyseal plate and partial removal of the bone by osteotomy can stop growth and correct angulation all in one maneuver, as suggested by Tsuge. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

If a digit exceeds adult length, a shortening procedure is recommended. The procedure used depends on the location and extent of excess. Minimal shortening can be accomplished by reduction of the distal fingertip pulp and nail using the method of Akinci. Excessive length requires distal phalanx excision and repositioning of the nail to the dorsum via a nail island flap. For the thumb, MCP joint resection and arthrodesis are preferred. These techniques address digital length and alignment, but excessive soft tissue and nail contribute to persistent disfigurement. This deformity can be addressed with debulking, nail bed excision, and longitudinal removal of bone. The procedures are extremely difficult and limited by the finger’s vascularity, joint stability, and flexor tendon integrity. Recurrence is common and functional deterioration with continued growth is prevalent. At this point, patients often consider amputation, as removal of the digit may actually improve hand function.

### Expected Outcomes

Outcomes in macrodactyly are very dependent on the initial condition of the hand. No surgical procedure results in normal appearance. Final appearance and function depend on the extent of resection, residual deformity, sensory impairment (secondary to compression neuropathy and/or nerve resection), and residual stiffness. Surgery has inherent risks including hypertrophic scarring (particularly after syndactyly release) and neuroma formation.
