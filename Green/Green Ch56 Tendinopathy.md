Tendinopathy is defined as painful conditions affecting tendons and includes both inflammatory and degenerative processes. True acute inflammation of a tendon is termed tendinitis, with associated inflammatory cells and proteins. Tendinosis is thought to be a chronic and degenerative tendon lesion with disorganization of collagen bundles, increased tenocyte nuclei, and lipid vacuoles.

Several factors are thought to contribute to tendon pathology. Increased age has been shown to correlate with decreased cellular function in tendons, particularly after the third decade of life. Body weight and nutrition have also been proposed as potential impacts on tendon health. Fluoroquinolone use, while more commonly associated with tendon rupture, has also been implicated in tenosynovitis in the wrist and fingers.

In the hand and wrist, tendinopathies include diseases due to stenosing or narrowing of tendon sheaths, such as de Quervain tenosynovitis or trigger digit, as well as true inflammatory diseases of the tendon, such as proliferative tenosynovitis due to inflammatory arthropathies (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis or gout). Tendon entrapment in fibroosseous canals, such as de Quervain disease in the first dorsal extensor compartment, occurs near joints where tendons bend or turn. Repetitive motion of the tendon can lead to synovial hypertrophy and thickening, along with fibrosis of the retinacular sheath overlying the tendon. In contrast, inflammatory disease of the tendon can occur with deposition of crystals or inflammatory synovitis from autoimmune disease and presents with increased tissue volume, often limiting excursion. This chapter will address these various etiologies and types of tendon disease.

## STENOSING TENOSYNOVITIS

### Trigger Digits

Trigger finger is defined by catching or locking as the patient attempts to flex and extend the finger or thumb, due to volume mismatch between the flexor tendon and flexor sheath. Flexion of the digit, especially in power grip, causes high angular loads at the distal edge of the first annular (A1) pulley (Fig. 56.1). Biologic studies of the tendon and pulley have shown that both undergo pathologic change with the development of triggering. The tendon itself develops a spindleshaped thickening, often just distal to the edge of the A1 pulley due to the frictional forces with motion. Ultrasound studies of trigger digits have confirmed the pathologic thickening of the tendon. In addition, the A1 pulley that provides excursion to the digit during grip undergoes pathologic change. Specifically, the normal A1 pulley has an inner layer composed of dense connective tissue with thick collagen bundles and an outer layer made up of loose connective tissue with vascular ingrowth. In trigger digits, the A1 pulley develops three layers: an inner thin sheet of irregular connective tissue and small collagen fibers, a middle layer of dense connective tissue and chondrometaplasia, and an outer layer of loose connective tissue similar to normal pulleys. Thus the volume differential in triggering is derived from changes in both the tendon and pulley.

#### Clinical Presentation

The ring finger is most commonly affected, followed by the thumb.Small studies have suggested a female prevalence, approximately 2:1,and the most common age at presentation is between 50 and 70 years.Trigger digits are present in approximately 2% of the population but occur in 20% of diabetic patients. Patients typically complain of catching or locking of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint but sometimes simply note pain only over the A1 pulley at the level of the metacarpal head in the volar palm.

A locked trigger finger is differentiated from other diagnoses such as Dupuytren contracture, which presents with a thickened volar cord that prevents extension, or dislocation of the PIP joint, which can be identified on radiographs. Other rare mimickers of trigger digits include a tumor of the tendon or tendon sheath, loose bodies in the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, or true locking of the MCP joint.

#### Classification

The Quinnell classification of triggering severity is the most commonly used (Table 56.1). Others have extended or modified this classification, but it is still most frequently cited in this form.

**TABLE 56.1 Quinnell Classification for Trigger Digits**

| Grade | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Grade 1 (pretriggering) | Pain; history of catching, but not demonstrable on physical examination; tenderness over the A1 pulley |
| Grade II (active) | Demonstrable catching, but the patient can actively extend the digit |
| Grade III (passive) | Demonstrable catching and locking requiring passive extension (grade IIIA) or inability to actively flex (grade IIIB) |
| Grade IV (contracture) | Demonstrable locking with a fixed flexion contracture of the PIP joint |

#### Risk Factors for Trigger Digits

As stated above, female sex and increased age have been shown to be associated with increased prevalence of trigger finger. Diabetics have been observed to develop trigger finger at a higher rate than the general population, although the etiology is not well understood. A database study in Taiwan evaluated data from 41,781 cases and noted that 1.07% developed trigger digit over 4 years, with risk factors including female sex, age between 50 and 59 years, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and use of hormone antagonist medications.

#### Treatment: Conservative

**Corticosteroid injections.** Corticosteroid injection is the most frequently described nonsurgical treatment of trigger digit. The theory behind injection is that the antiinflammatory effect of corticosteroid decreases synovitis and swelling and thus improves the volume discrepancy between tendon and sheath to allow motion without locking or clicking. Two randomized controlled trials examining the effect of corticosteroid versus placebo showed a success rate of 60% with steroid compared with 15% to 20% with placebo injection.

Studies of the effectiveness of corticosteroid injections for trigger fingers have shown resolution rates of approximately 45% after a single injection. In a study of 366 first-time corticosteroid injections for trigger digits, Wojahn et al. showed that women had a higher chance of resolution of triggering at 56%, compared with men at 35%. The authors also noted that patients experiencing sustained symptomatic relief past 2 years are likely to have permanent resolution of triggering.

In diabetics, corticosteroid injections have been noted to be less effective in resolving trigger digits. Baumgarten and colleagues performed a prospective randomized trial comparing corticosteroid injections in 30 diabetics (35 digits) to 29 nondiabetic patients (29 digits). The cohort with diabetes was randomized into a corticosteroid group (20 digits) and a placebo group (15 digits). The authors noted that 25 of 29 nondiabetic trigger digits resolved compared with 12 of 19 in the diabetic group given corticosteroids and 8 of 15 in the diabetic placebo group. Nephropathy and neuropathy were significantly associated with the need for surgical treatment in the diabetic group. Other studies have confirmed higher rates of surgery in diabetic patients, especially in those who are insulin-dependent.

Another consideration in diabetics undergoing corticosteroid injections is elevation of blood glucose. This was reported by Wang and Hutchinson, who noted a trend of increase in blood glucose over 5 days after injection in 18 patients, with the highest level occurring within 24 hours. Other studies have confirmed this effect. Kim et al. reported that hemoglobin A1C levels greater than 7% correlated with higher and longer blood glucose elevation in diabetic patients. A study of both upper and lower extremity corticosteroid injections noted greater glucose elevations in those receiving lower extremity injections but also noted a similar correlation with hemoglobin A1C and higher glucose levels.

Risks for failure of initial injection include insulin-dependent diabetes, younger age, involvement of other digits prior to presentation, and a history of other upper extremity tendinopathies. In a study of the long-term effectiveness of repeated corticosteroid injections for trigger digit, Dardas et al. evaluated 292 repeat injections in a cohort that included 22% diabetic patients. They showed that second injections were successful in 111 of 285 digits (39%), with 86 receiving a further injection and 108 going on to surgical release. Similarly, 39% (24 of 62) third injections were successful.

Several studies have considered cost of corticosteroid injections compared with immediate surgery. Kerrigan and Stanwix evaluated four strategies, taking into account Medicare reimbursement rates, and reported that management of trigger digit with two corticosteroid injections prior to surgery was the least costly option. Another study of diabetic trigger digits, which included surgical release in the clinic under local anesthesia, concluded that immediate release was the least costly strategy.

The location in which the corticosteroid injectate is placed has also been a matter of debate, as some authors have advocated for subcutaneous injection of steroid compared with injection placed within the flexor tendon sheath. Taras et al studied 95 patients with 107 trigger digits with a prospective study comparing subcutaneous to intrasheath injection, using radiopaque dye and radiographs to identify injectate placement. The authors reported 47% of the intrasheath group has a good response, defined as resolution of triggering and pain, and 70% of the all-subcutaneous injection group had a good response. The authors concluded that injection into the tendon sheath was not necessary to obtain a clinical effect. A more recent prospective study of subcutaneous steroid placement for trigger digit also noted between 74% and 98% of patients with resolution of triggering.

Complications of corticosteroid injections for trigger digit are rare, but they include skin and subcutaneous discoloration and atrophy; pain at the injection site, flare of symptoms; and pulley or tendon rupture. Infection has been reported as well. Corticosteroids have been reported to cause subcutaneous tissue atrophy and hypopigmentation. A study comparing surgery to corticosteroid injection in 165 patients reported that 11 patients experienced a flare of symptoms, or increased pain and swelling following injection, which resolved in a few days.

Pulley and tendon ruptures are more severe complications, rarely reported after steroid injection. Authors have reported multiple pulley ruptures in both the fingers and thumb, requiring reconstruction. Similarly, a case report of flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) rupture after seven corticosteroid injections in 14 months indicates an accumulating risk after multiple injections, but other reports have noted rupture after one or two injections.

The incidence of infection after corticosteroid injection for trigger digit is low, with minimal reports in the literature. Calif et al. reported a mycobacterial infection after steroid injection for a trigger digit, which resolved after repeated radical debridements and antibiotics. Other case reports include an acute horseshoe abscess of the hand after trigger thumb injection, and a case series of eight hand infections after corticosteroid injections included one trigger digit requiring surgery.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: CORTICOSTEROID INJECTION OF TRIGGER DIGITS

When injecting trigger digits, an intrasheath injection technique is preferred. Because of noted pain with high volume injections, a small amount of 0.25 cc of 1% lidocaine combined with 0.75 cc of corticosteroid is used. Water-soluble betamethasone is preferred; however, triamcinolone is a viable option. The 1 cc volume of injectate is drawn up in a 3 cc syringe. A 25-gauge 5/8-inch needle is preferred and injected at the crease at the base of the finger at the distal end of the A1 pulley, with the bevel of the needle directed in a proximal direction (Fig. 56.2). The needle is placed into the tendon sheath and then withdrawn slowly with forward pressure on the syringe. When the needle is within the space between the tendon and sheath, the syringe is able to be deployed. A bandage is placed, and the patient is advised that the effect of injection might not be seen for 2 to 3 days.

**Splinting.** The use of splints to limit the motion of affected joints has been suggested to “alter the biomechanics of the tendons” while encouraging maximal gliding. While some studies have reported immobilization of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) and PIP joints, the most common type of trigger digit splint is used to immobilize the MCP joint (Fig. 56.3). Authors have variously described placing the joint at 0 to 15 degrees of MCP flexion with the interphalangeal joints free. In a prospective study of 55 digits in 38 patients treated with 6 weeks of MCP splinting that placed the joint at 0 degrees, the authors noted 73% resolved or improved, with 14 digits requiring injection or surgery.

![[Fig.56.3.png]]
> **Fig. 56.3** Custom orthotic for prevention of MCP flexion to treat trigger digit. (Image courtesy of Sean Clancy, OTD, OTR/L, CHT.)

In a prospective study of 28 participants treated with thermoplastic ring splints that held the MCP joint in extension, Colbourn et al. reported that 93% felt that triggering was improved, and 54% had resolution of triggering at 10 weeks. Another study of night splinting for 6 weeks, performed in 34 patients, noted that triggering symptoms resolved in 18 of 34 (55%). A systematic review of splinting for trigger digit reported that immobilization of a single joint was associated with improvement in triggering but recommended more stringent randomized trials with use of standardized outcomes measures.

#### Treatment: Surgical

The standard surgical treatment for trigger digit is division of the A1 pulley. Multiple techniques, including open and percutaneous releases, have been described, with variations within these options.

**Open surgical A1 pulley release.** Open visualization and division of the A1 pulley has been a surgical standard in the treatment of trigger digits, with reported good outcomes and minimal complications.Various authors have described different types of skin incisions including transverse, longitudinal, and oblique approaches. Kloeters and colleagues compared three incisions in 30 patients with 32 trigger fingers. The incision was either (1) transverse in the distal palmar crease, (2) transverse and 2 mm distal to the distal palmar crease, or (3) longitudinal over the MCP joint without crossing the distal palmar crease. All patients showed a significant decrease in Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) scores at 3 and 12 months postsurgery compared with their baseline value. The authors measured scar volume parameters and speed of DASH decrease and noted the longitudinal incision group had the least scarring and fastest DASH reduction. Thus the longitudinal incision was recommended. A prospective randomized trial of transverse versus longitudinal incisions performed in 67 patients with 1 year follow-up noted no significant differences in the patientrated scar assessment scores or the DASH scores between groups.

Open surgery has typically been performed using local anesthetic, with or without sedation. The benefit of local alone is that the patient can move the finger actively and confirm no residual triggering. The use of wide awake, local anesthesia with no tourniquet (WALANT) has become popular for A1 pulley release, with demonstrated high patient satisfaction and lowered costs. A study of varying surgical settings and anesthesia types showed that trigger finger release under local anesthesia in a procedure room setting was the most cost-effective option compared with the use of monitored anesthesia care and the operating suite.

**Percutaneous trigger finger release.** The concept of using a needle or scalpel to divide the A1 pulley without an open incision was conceived to allow the procedure to be done in an office setting (Video 56.1). Eastwood et al. described the use of a 21-gauge needle to release the A1 pulley in 35 fingers, noting complete relief of triggering in 32 of 35 (91%) with the remaining intermittent triggering. Another study described use of a No. 11 scalpel blade to release the pulley, performed in 171 patients with 92 having 6-month follow-up. In 128 digits, 120 (94%) achieved successful release with 8 fingers requiring open surgery. There were no digital nerve injuries, bowstringing, or infections noted.

#### Expected Outcomes

The outcomes of open surgery have consistently been reported as good to excellent, with a low rate of reoperation. In a large study of 305 trigger digits in 276 patients treated with open A1 pulley release, Lange-Riess et al. reported no recurrence of triggering in 254 digits at 14-year follow-up, with full range of motion in 98%. The authors reported complications in 9 patients, including two superficial infections, one delayed wound healing, and 6 transient digital nerve lesions, all of which resolved without further surgery. A large U.S. insurance database study evaluated 209,634 patients undergoing trigger finger release over a 7-year period, noting the middle and ring fingers as the most commonly treated. The rate of surgical revision was 0.4% at 1 year and 0.6% at 3 years, with risk factors for revision including Dupuytren disease, obesity, smoking, diabetes, and age <65 years.

A comparative study of open and percutaneous trigger thumb releases, performed in 107 patients with 126 trigger thumbs, showed good short-and long-term outcomes at 3 months and 2 years in both groups, with significantly better pain and satisfaction early in the percutaneous group but reversal of this finding at 2 years with better outcomes in the open release group. At 2 years, 19% of digits had pain in the percutaneous group compared with 7% in the open group, and recurrence of triggering was present in 9% of the percutaneous group and 3% of the open group. Another study with a mean of 2.5 years of follow-up compared 70 open releases to 266 percutaneous releases and noted high satisfaction rates for both, averaging 97%, with mild residual pain and stiffness equivalent in both groups.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: SURGICAL INTERVENTION FOR TRIGGER DIGITS

Open trigger digit release is the preferred technique. The procedure is performed using local anesthesia with or without sedation. A small amount (approximately 2 cc) of 1% lidocaine without epinephrine is injected, avoiding any concerns for finger ischemia. A 1-to 1.5-cm incision is designed in a vertical or oblique direction at the base of the digit for the index through small fingers, in line with the small skin creases (Fig. 56.4). In the thumb, a 1 cm transverse incision at the base of the thumb is preferred.

![[Fig.56.4.png]]
> **Fig. 56.4** Oblique incision at the base of the finger for A1 pulley release.

After skin incision and blunt dissection, the digital neurovascular structures are retracted out of harm’s way. The A1 pulley is sharply divided with a scalpel. The patient is then asked to flex the digit to confirm that no residual triggering occurs. If there is persistent triggering, one slip of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon is excised. The wound is closed with nonabsorbable sutures. A small dressing is applied and secured with cast padding or woven gauze.

Postoperatively, the patient is encouraged to use the finger, avoiding heavy gripping for 2 weeks. The sutures are removed at 10 days. Trigger digit incisions can form a thickened and painful scar. Therefore the patient is instructed to start scar massage once the skin is fully closed. The patient is also told to expect soreness for 4 to 6 weeks after surgery. If the patient has a PIP flexion contracture, early therapy referral can lessen the contracture.

**Complications of surgery** Persistent triggering. Persistence of triggering has been variably reported but is rare in most series. In a retrospective review of 1598 trigger finger releases over 10 years, Bruijnzeel et al. noted that 10 (0.6%) had persistent postoperative triggering and 4 had recurrence of triggering (0.3%). A large database study noted that the revision rate at 1 year and 3 years was 0.4% and 0.6%, respectively, but no specification of the reason for revision was available.

Persistence of trigger digit after division of the A1 pulley can occur due to enlargement of the flexor tendon with stenosis at the level of the A2 pulley, especially in patients with long-standing trigger digits.Although some authors have advocated combined release of the A1 and A2 pulleys for primary trigger release, most authorities have recommended reduction in the volume of the flexor tendons as the solution for persistent triggering. Seradge and Kleinert described “flexor reduction tenoplasty,” with excision and sutured closure of bulbous enlargement of the FDP tendon. Others have advocated resection of the ulnar slip of the FDS tendon or the entire FDS tendon.

Infection. Infection after trigger digit surgery is low in most series. Ng et al. retrospectively evaluated 780 patients with 999 digits and noted 26 patients with infection (3%), which included stitch abscesses as well as superficial and deep infections. Risk factors include shorter time between corticosteroid injection and surgery, tobacco use, use of epinephrine in the local anesthetic, and use of antibiotics. Others have noted similar risk with regard to close timing of corticosteroid injection, with findings of greater incidence of deep infection in those receiving steroid injections within 90 days of surgery.

Stiffness or contracture. PIP joint stiffness or contracture is often present upon initial presentation in long-standing cases of trigger digit. This contracture is theorized to occur due to pathologic changes or shortening of the FDS tendon. Preoperative flexion contracture is significantly correlated with higher risk of postoperative contracture,and the patient should be made aware of this risk prior to surgery. Yang et al. described the addition of finger splinting after percutaneous A1 pulley release in a comparative study in 65 patients with PIP contractures and noted significantly improved PIP joint extension in the splinted group. Favre and Kinnen resected the FDS in 39 digits with PIP contracture in combination with A1 pulley release and noted that 72% achieved full extension after surgery.

Nerve injury. Iatrogenic digital nerve injuries are rare. The radial digital nerve of the thumb is at higher risk because of its superficial location, as close as 1.29-mm deep to the dermis, and oblique course at the level of the MCP joint. Nerve injuries have also been described in other digits. Sreedharan et al. described a case of a neuroma of the radial digital nerve to the middle finger, which was treated with excision and primary nerve repair.

**Adult trigger digits are distinct from pediatric trigger digits and congenital trigger thumb.** There are important differences between trigger digits in children and those in adults, including trigger thumbs. Congenital or developmental trigger thumb presents in early childhood with a thumb interphalangeal (IP) joint in fixed flexion, with a palpable nodule at the MCP joint crease characterized as Notta’s nodule. These are treated either with passive stretching, splinting,or surgical release of the A1 pulley. This entity will be addressed in detail in Chapter 37.

In addition, pediatric trigger digits other than the thumb are notable for underlying anatomic abnormalities or conditions. Specifically, aberrant lumbrical muscles or decussation of the FDS tendon proximal to the A1 pulley have been described. Trigger digits are associated with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, mucopolysaccharidosis, and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

### De Quervain Tenosynovitis

De Quervain tendinopathy is defined as tenosynovitis of the first dorsal extensor compartment tendons (the abductor pollicis longus [APL] and extensor pollicis brevis [EPB]), as they pass through the fibroosseous sheath at the wrist joint (Fig. 56.5). Similar to trigger digits and other tendinopathies, inflammatory cells are absent in histopathological specimens, which show thickening of the tendon sheath and mucopolysaccharide deposition. Although de Quervain, a Swiss physician, receives the eponymous credit for the condition from his report of five cases in 1912, a surgeon from St. Louis published about this condition in 1898.

> **Fig. 56.5** Release for de Quervain disease. A, The first dorsal compartment is approached by a short transverse skin incision. B, The annular ligament is incised with a scalpel from the snuffbox to the musculotendinous junctions. (A and B, Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

#### Anatomy

The first dorsal compartment tendons cross the wrist at the radial styloid, under a retinacular sheath with a mean length of 21.2 mm. In patients with first dorsal compartment tenosynovitis, the retinacular sheath is nearly three times thicker, based on ultrasound-guided studies. Finkelstein was the first to report septation of the compartment in 3 of 24 patients with de Quervain tenosynovitis. Jackson et al. noted that the most common anatomic pattern of tendon anatomy was composed of two APL tendon slips and one EPB slip in the first dorsal compartment. A septum between the APL and EPB tendons was seen in 40% of cadaveric wrists. Studies have shown a high incidence of subcompartmental septae in patients undergoing surgery for de Quervain tendinopathy.

#### Risk Factors

Risk factors for de Quervain tendinopathy include female sex and increased age. A study in a working population showed a significantly increased risk from repeated or sustained wrist bending activities and twisting motions. De Quervain has also been described during pregnancy and in the postpartum period. A recent study of factors associated with surgery for de Quervain in a population of 200 patients showed that Medicaid insurance status and psychiatric illness history correlated with surgical treatment.

#### Clinical Presentation

Patients present with wrist pain, localized to the dorsoradial side of the wrist, and sometimes complain of triggering of the extensor tendons.

The onset of pain is gradual and not associated with trauma. On examination, the Eichhoff maneuver, performed by clasping the thumb in the fist and passively ulnar deviating the wrist, and the Finkelstein test, performed by grasping the patient’s thumb and ulnar deviating the thumb and the wrist, both are positive when they elicit severe pain. Palpation reveals tenderness and sometimes swelling 1 to 2 cm proximal to the tip of the radial styloid.

Imaging is not typically required, although recent studies of the use of ultrasound have shown increased thickness of the extensor sheath and ability to visualize a septum between the APL and EPB at the wrist.

#### Treatment: Conservative

**Splinting.** The use of splints to decrease tendon excursion and immobilize the wrist has been a standard treatment over time. A forearm-based thumb spica splint decreases motion of the involved APL and EPB tendons and is thought to rest the area. A prospective randomized study of 83 patients with de Quervain tendinopathy compared full-time to as-needed splint wear and showed no differences in pain intensity or treatment satisfaction between groups. However, there are few studies that examined splinting alone. Most have combined splinting with corticosteroid injections and noted overall good outcomes. One comparative study showed improved outcomes with the combination of splinting and corticosteroid injection compared with injection alone.

**Corticosteroid injection.** Treatment with injection of corticosteroid into the first dorsal compartment is intended to deliver medication locally to decrease volume at the tendon-sheath interface (Fig. 56.6). In a cohort of 300 patients stratified by symptom severity, Lane et al. noted that 76% were completely relieved of symptoms after corticosteroid injection, with another 7% improved. The importance of injecting into the tendon sheath has been emphasized, and Sawaizumi et al. noted that their outcomes improved after changing to a two-injection technique addressing both the APL and EPB tendon sheaths. A systematic review of corticosteroid injection for de Quervain tenosynovitis showed that it was more efficacious than splinting, with a number needed to treat of two, indicating that for every two persons treated, one achieves benefit.

![[Fig.56.6.png]]
> **Fig. 56.6** Corticosteroid injection for de Quervain tenosynovitis. The needle is placed into the first dorsal compartment tendon sheath and the injected fluid is observed to fill the sheath, often exiting with a visible pop proximally.

Because of the superficial location of the first dorsal compartment, there is a risk of skin thinning, depigmentation, and soft tissue atrophy after corticosteroid injection in this area. There are multiple case reports of linear hypopigmentation and atrophy after steroid injection for de Quervain tenosynovitis (Fig. 56.7). In general, these skin lesions resolve over time, but can occasionally persist. The occurrence of soft tissue complications after corticosteroid injection has been reported up to 31%.

![[Fig.56.7.png]]
> **Fig. 56.7** Example of skin atrophy and discoloration from multiple corticosteroid injections.

Ultrasound-guided injection has been proposed for greater accuracy of injection placement. Hajder et al. performed injections using ultrasound in 71 wrists in 62 patients with de Quervain disease and reported 68 of 71 with complete or significant relief after one injection. However, local depigmentation was seen in 4 patients. A prospective randomized comparison between blind and ultrasound-guided injection in 44 patients showed that pain and Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) scores improved in both cohorts, with no significant differences between the groups. There were no differences in skin hypopigmentation or severity of skin atrophy between groups. However, in a similar randomized injection comparison of 154 patients, Roh et al. noted a significantly higher incidence of skin discoloration in the blind injection group compared with the ultrasound-guided group (11 versus 3, *P* = 0.02).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: INJECTION OF THE FIRST DORSAL EXTENSOR COMPARTMENT

A small volume of water-soluble corticosteroid, such as betamethasone, with a total injection volume of 1 cc is preferred. A combination of 0.25 cc of 1% lidocaine and 0.75 cc of betamethasone is instilled into the extensor tendon sheath using the tip of the radial styloid as an anatomic marker, with the needle directed in a proximal direction. When successful, the injectate fills the tendon sheath and is often accompanied by a pop and visualization of the fluid exiting the proximal edge of the retinaculum.

**Therapy.** Beyond splinting, there are minimal data on other therapeutic modalities for de Quervain. One study of 9 patients treated with 8 weeks of eccentric exercises at the wrist showed significantly improved pain visual analog scale (VAS) and quick-DASH scores at 12 weeks. A randomized trial in 30 patients comparing splinting plus therapeutic ultrasound to splinting alone showed significantly greater improvement in quick-DASH scores in the ultrasound group, suggesting that this modality may be additive in improving function.

**Treatment in pregnancy and postpartum.** De Quervain tenosynovitis in pregnancy or during lactation can be observed. The tenosynovitis generally resolves after discontinuing lactation. Conservative measures have been described, including splinting and corticosteroid injections. Avci et al. performed a randomized prospective study in 18 patients with de Quervain who were either pregnant or breastfeeding, comparing splinting to corticosteroid injections. The authors noted that 9 patients treated with injection had complete relief, with one recurrence. In contrast, none of the splinted patients achieved complete pain relief until lactation ended.

#### Treatment: Surgical

**Open surgical release.** Fritz de Quervain, a Swiss surgeon, published the first description of surgery to release the tendon sheath over the first dorsal extensor compartment, done under cocaine anesthesia.Multiple surgical incisions have been described. A transverse skin incision placed within Langer’s lines is thought to be more cosmeticand was the common teaching in older literature. Robson et al. noted in a cadaveric anatomic study that the superficial branches of the radial nerve crossed directly underlying this incision in all specimens.Longitudinal incisions have been proposed for safety and effectiveness; other incisions include lazy “S” and oblique designs. A comparative study of transverse versus longitudinal scars in 48 patients showed a higher incidence of hypertrophic scar formation and postoperative superficial radial nerve numbness in the transverse incision group.

Many believe that patients with separate APL and EPB tendon compartments have a higher risk of requiring surgery for de Quervain tenosynovitis. In a retrospective study of 33 patients undergoing surgery, Lee et al. noted separate compartments in 18 of 33 (55%). Another study of 79 patients (85 wrists) showed that 61% of patients had a septated first dorsal compartment at surgery. A systematic literature review of 470 surgically treated patients with de Quervain disease showed a significant difference in the number of tendon compartments with 59% of de Quervain patients having two compartments.

**Endoscopic/percutaneous release.** Endoscopic release of the first dorsal compartment has been described. This procedure is performed using a 2-mm skin incision and a 1.9-mm arthroscope. A study in 50 wrists comparing endoscopic to open release showed no differences in functional outcomes as measured by DASH and pain VAS scores; however, the open group had more scar tenderness and superficial radial nerve injuries. Other authors have described percutaneous release under ultrasound guidance, using medium-gauge needles. A cadaveric feasibility study performed in 48 wrists showed 25 complete releases, 21 partial releases, and 2 missed releases.

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: SURGICAL RELEASE FOR DE QUERVAIN

Surgery is indicated in patients that fail splinting and corticosteroid injections, with three as the maximum number of injections. Surgery can be done under local anesthesia alone or with sedation.

A 1.5 cm longitudinal incision is preferred that is placed just proximal to the radial styloid, avoiding the prominent radial styloid, which leaves a scar vulnerable to impact. After infiltration of 3 to 5 cc of local anesthetic, the skin is incised and blunt dissection performed, protecting the superficial branches of the radial nerve. The branches are not individually dissected, but rather mobilized along with the superficial fat. The first dorsal extensor tendon sheath is exposed and sharply divided in a longitudinal direction, with the location of division slightly dorsal with the wrist positioned in neutral (Fig. 56.8). This preserves a portion of the volar and dorsal tendon sheath as volar and less commonly dorsal subluxation of the APL and EPB has been described.

![[Fig.56.8.png]]
> **Fig. 56.8** Surgical exposure of the first dorsal compartment through a longitudinal incision, with the two leaves of the tendon sheath retracted and visible.

The presence or absence of a separate compartment for the EPB tendon is determined to ensure a complete release. If there is a large septum between compartments, this septum is trimmed. The wound is irrigated and the skin closed using running suture. Steri-Strips and 2-octyl cyanoacrylate glue are applied. The patient is then placed into a thumb spica splint with the thumb IP joint free.

The splint is maintained for 10 days with immediate active digital range of motion. The patient is transitioned into a removable thumb spica splint for 2 weeks. The patient begins using the hand with a 5-lb lifting restriction for 4 weeks total.

#### Expected Outcomes

The outcomes of de Quervain tendon sheath release have been reported as good to excellent in medium-and long-term follow-up. A retrospective study of 43 patients undergoing first dorsal compartment release noted symptom resolution in 39 (91%) with two recurrences.Scheller et al. reported pain relief and return to function in 94 consecutive patients with a mean follow-up of 16 years, with 4 patients experiencing temporary radial sensory nerve irritation.

In 22 patients treated with ultrasound-guided percutaneous release, Croutzet et al. noted significant decrease in quick-DASH, with no reported complications. Lapegue and colleagues reported on 35 patients who underwent ultrasound-guided needle release and noted a high patient satisfaction, although all releases were partial.

**Complications** Tendon subluxation. Both volar and dorsal subluxation of the APL and EPB tendons after release of the first dorsal extensor sheath have been described. White and Weiland described a single case of painful volar tendon subluxation requiring reconstruction of the sheath using the extensor retinaculum. The authors recommended incising the tendon sheath over its dorsal margin to prevent this complication. Other authors have addressed this complication with reconstruction using a distally based strip of the brachioradialis tendon. Collins recommended excision of the prominent radial ridge, with periosteal flap coverage as an alternative treatment in volar tendon subluxation.Matzon et al. noted a 9% occurrence of tendon instability in a large prospective series of 130 patients undergoing surgery. There was perching of the tendons with passive wrist flexion, but only one patient had frank tendon dislocation, implying that true symptomatic tendon subluxation is rare.

Dorsal tendon subluxation has also been reported. One case report attributed the dorsal tendon instability to forceful weightlifting soon after surgery. The subluxation was remedied with reconstruction using the remaining palmar retinaculum.

Injury/irritation of the superficial radial nerve. One or more branches of the superficial branch of the radial nerve courses over the radial styloid, superficial to the first dorsal compartment tendon sheath, placing these branches in jeopardy during surgical release of the first dorsal extensor compartment. Kim et al. proposed the use of preoperative ultrasonography to determine the anatomic location of the superficial radial nerve. In a study of 94 consecutive patients treated with surgery, 4 (4%) had transient lesions of the superficial radial nerve. Another study of 22 releases in 21 patients using longitudinal incisions for extensor sheath release demonstrated that 6 patients sustained superficial radial nerve injury. Treatment of persistent pain or posttraumatic injuries to the superficial radial nerve includes neurolysis, nerve wrapping with vein or allograft material, neuroma excision if present, or burying the nerve into muscle.

Recurrence of de Quervain. Recurrence of symptoms after surgical first dorsal extensor compartment release is a rare but a described complication. Mangukiya et al. reported on 46 cases in 40 patients and noted that 6 patients had recurrent symptoms after 1 to 2 months of complete pain relief, which suggests reformation of the compressive tendon sheath. In Scheller et al.’s 16-year follow-up study in 94 patients, none had recurrent symptoms.

Another potential etiology of recurrent symptoms is incomplete release of the first dorsal extensor sheath. This complication has been noted as a potential outcome in several series, but the incidence is not specifically reported in outcomes studies.

### Intersection Syndrome

Intersection syndrome refers to tenosynovitis due to friction where the tendons of the first dorsal extensor compartment (the APL and EPB) cross the tendons of the second dorsal compartment (the extensor carpi radialis longus [ECRL] and extensor carpi radialis brevis [ECRB]), approximately 4 cm proximal to the wrist joint. While this etiology is most commonly proposed, others have postulated that intersection syndrome is due to stenosing tenosynovitis within the sheath of the second dorsal compartment. This syndrome has been reported in association with athletic activities that use repetitive wrist motion, including rowing, weightlifting, and tennis.

Patients present with sharp pain proximal to the wrist, located dorsally, often with localized swelling at the intersection of the first and second dorsal compartments. Often, there is audible crepitus or squeaking with wrist motion as the tendons slide past each other.Intersection syndrome is differentiated from de Quervain tenosynovitis by the location of the proximal tenderness on examination, as the Finkelstein or Eichhoff test may be positive.

Imaging is not necessary for diagnosis, but ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings have both been described. On ultrasound, thickening of the tendons and fluid within the extensor sheaths have been noted. MRI findings include peritendinous edema and intrasubstance tendon signal suggestive of tendinosis.

Conservative treatment includes rest/activity modification, splinting, therapy, and corticosteroid injection. As overuse phenomenon with repetitive microtrauma is a contributory factor, reduction or total rest from the inciting activity is recommended. Thumb spica splinting for 2 to 4 weeks has been helpful in resolving symptoms.Therapeutic taping of the forearm has been described in 5 patients as isolated treatment with successful pain relief maintained at 1 year follow-up. Corticosteroid injection into the region of tendinosis or swelling has resolved symptoms in the majority of cases failing other nonoperative treatment. Single case reports have noted resolution of symptoms within 10 to 14 days.

For patients with persistent symptoms despite conservative management, surgical treatment has been described. Grundberg and Reagan presented a series of 13 patients who underwent surgical decompression of the second compartment and tenosynovectomy, with symptom relief in all patients at a mean of 10 months. Other authors have recommended concomitant opening the APL and EPB fascial sheaths to decompress the muscle bellies.

### Extensor Pollicis Longus Stenosing Tenosynovitis

Stenosing tenosynovitis of the extensor pollicis longus (EPL) tendon is rare, generally occurring in the tight sheath adjacent to the Lister tubercle, where the tendon courses at an oblique angle. It is theorized that a volume mismatch due to either thickened overlying retinaculum of the third extensor compartment or swelling of the tendon causes painful synovitis. There are multiple case reports of triggering of the thumb due to EPL stenosing tenosynovitis, with snapping noted as the thumb extends.

Several authors have noted that tenosynovitis occurs after trauma.Lanzetta et al. presented a case report of a patient who presented with intense dorsal thumb pain after a fall onto the wrist, who went on to develop triggering. Surgical exploration showed a thickened area of EPL tendon that was treated by decompression of the retinacular sheath. In an anatomic study, Ferreres et al. proposed that a fall with the wrist fully extended may cause direct trauma to the tendon via compression between the third metacarpal and the Lister tubercle.EPL rupture after minimally displaced distal radius fractures is secondary to hematoma forming in the third compartment, creating dysvascularity of the tendon leading to attritional rupture.

Treatment of EPL tenosynovitis begins with a high index of suspicion to avoid later tendon rupture. Conservative management includes immobilization and a single trial injection of corticosteroid, as proposed by McMahon and Posner. Because of the potential risk to the tendon, these authors recommended use of a small volume of watersoluble corticosteroid. If nonoperative management is unsuccessful, surgical release of the tendon sheath over the third dorsal compartment is warranted. Kardashian et al. recommended division of the septum between the third and fourth dorsal compartments to decompress the EPL tendon. Froimson and Wolfe recommended decompression of the third dorsal compartment and transposition of the EPL radial to the Lister tubercle.

### Tenosynovitis of the Fourth and Fifth Extensor Compartments

In the absence of inflammatory disease, tenosynovitis of the fourth and fifth extensor compartment tendons is rare. Cooper et al. described proliferative tenosynovitis in 11 patients without trauma or autoimmune disease, who presented with a painful mass over the dorsal wrist. All patients failed conservative management with splinting and rest. Inflammatory arthritis workup was negative in all patients, and surgical exploration revealed hypertrophic tenosynovium, which required tenosynovectomy. Pathology showed minimal inflammatory reaction. Importantly, the authors noted no tendon invasion at surgery.

A case report detailed isolated tenosynovitis to the extensor indicis proprius (EIP), with pain when flexing the wrist and making a fist. Surgical exploration revealed a hypertrophied EIP muscle belly extending into the fourth dorsal compartment that required release. Van Heest et al. presented a case series of 7 athletes (8 wrists) with symptomatic extensor tenosynovitis and retinacular thickening due to repetitive wrist hyperextension. Two athletes were treated with corticosteroid injections. Five patients (6 wrists) required surgery with pathologic findings of extensor retinaculum thickening and concomitant extensor tendon thickening. Treatment consisted of partial resection of the distal extensor retinaculum. All patients had complete pain relief and full return to sports.

### Extensor Carpi Ulnaris Tendinitis

#### Anatomy

The extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) muscle originates from the lateral epicondyle and along the posterior border of the ulna, with transformation to a tendinous structure at the junction of the middle and distal third of the ulna. The tendon enters the sixth dorsal extensor compartment at the level of the ulnar head. This fibroosseous compartment is made up of the ECU subsheath, which is a thin structure composed of mixed loose and tight collagen fibers, and the groove of the ulnar sulcus running longitudinally over the head of the ulna. Taleisnik et al. described reinforcement of the ECU subsheath by “linea juguta,” which are longitudinal fibers originating from the base of the ulnar styloid and extending proximally in a dorsal and medial direction to coalesce with the extensor retinaculum. This anatomic arrangement lends stability to the ECU tendon to prevent subluxation with wrist rotation.

#### Presentation and Pathology

Repetitive motion of the wrist, particularly in association with sports activities, has been described as the primary inciting cause of tenosynovitis of the ECU; however, tendon changes attributed to the fibroosseous sheath have also been noted. Another described etiology of ECU tendinitis is direct irritation from an enlarged ulnar styloid.Patients with ECU tendinitis present with dorsoulnar wrist pain, particularly with supination and with wrist flexion and ulnar deviation, as these positions place the ECU tendon on stretch.

On examination, there is tenderness over the course of the ECU tendon with or without swelling or prominence. Several provocative examination maneuvers have been described. Pain with resisted extension and ulnar deviation is a classic test, but it occurs with other potential etiologies, such as the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) and lunotriquetral ligament. The ECU synergy test was described to better isolate the ECU, and is performed with the patient’s arm positioned with the elbow flexed at 90 degrees and the forearm fully supinated. The examiner holds the thumb and middle finger with one hand and palpates the ECU tendon with the other, then radially abducts the thumb against resistance. A positive maneuver re-creates the patient’s pain at the dorsoulnar wrist and infers ECU tendinitis. Another provocative maneuver is the carpal supination test, which is performed with the examiner grasping the hand and passively supinating the hand and forearm in an attempt to reproduce the patent’s pain.

Plain radiographs should be obtained to rule out other sources of ulnar-sided wrist pathology such as ulnar abutment. MRI findings of tendinosis include diffuse increased signal within the tendon and fluid in the tendon sheath. The presence of longitudinal fissures in the tendon may represent tendon pathology, but these may also represent normal anatomy as shown by Erickson et al. in a cadaveric dissection study showing that 64% of specimens had longitudinal splits in the ECU tendon. Ultrasound can show fluid around the tendon as well as tendon thickening.

#### ECU Subluxation/Instability

ECU subluxation or dislocation is part of the differential diagnosis. The examiner must rule out instability by examining stability of the tendon with passive supination while holding the wrist in flexion. The initial treatment of ECU instability is often with immobilization of the wrist and/or forearm to prevent rotation. Surgical repair or reconstruction of the tendon sheath is necessary for recalcitrant cases. (Please see Chapter 14 for more detail.)

#### Treatment

Initial treatment of ECU tendinitis is splinting the wrist in neutral or slight flexion and ulnar deviation to lessen any tendon strain for 2 to 4 weeks’ duration depending on the symptom severity. Directed therapy and taping may also be beneficial. In addition, corticosteroid injection has been advocated by some authors to treat synovitis of the tendon.

If conservative management fails, surgical treatment includes release of the tendon sheath and synovectomy, with debridement of torn or devitalized tendon substance. Graham recommended opening the sixth compartment, leaving an ulnar-based flap of retinaculum available if reconstruction of the ECU sheath is necessary (Fig. 56.9). Allende and Le Viet reported the results of 28 patients with ECU pathology, 17 of whom had tenosynovitis or tendinopathy and were treated with debridement. Twelve patients gleaned an excellent or good result at a mean of 23 months.

### Flexor Carpi Radialis Tendinopathy

The flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon travels in its own synovial sheath and fibroosseous tunnel adjacent to the carpal tunnel. The crest of the trapezium and the transverse carpal ligament comprise the volar border, and the trapezial body resides dorsal. The tendon occupies 90% of the space within the fibroosseous tunnel and thus is vulnerable to overuse with repetitive wrist motion and subsequent resultant synovitis. FCR tendinopathy is uncommon, and case reports have shown that patients present with swelling along the tendon at the wrist level, with pain during wrist extension and flexion. Fitton et al. noted that this tendinopathy was more common in women than in men, and pain with resisted wrist flexion is the provocative maneuver consistent with FCR tendinopathy.

Conservative management includes wrist splinting and corticosteroid injection into the FCR sheath. Weeks reported that 4 patients with FCR tendinitis who were treated with corticosteroid injection alone had complete relief of pain at 2-year follow-up. In a study from the Mayo Clinic, 6 of 10 patients were treated with corticosteroid injections and noted at least temporary relief of symptoms.

Surgical release of the FCR tendon sheath is indicated if conservative management fails to relieve symptoms. Release is performed via a longitudinal incision over the FCR tendon proximal to the wrist, curving slightly in an ulnar direction over the thenar eminence for exposure of the trapezium and release of the tunnel. Alternatively, Brink et al. proposed a “blind” tenolysis performed by use of a small beaver blade along the edge of the trapezial crest. Gabel et al. reported the results of FCR open release in 10 patients at a mean follow-up of 44 months and noted good to excellent results with respect to pain and function in 8 of 10.

## PROLIFERATIVE TENOSYNOVITIS

### Rheumatoid Arthritis

The advent of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) has led to clinical control of joint disease in systemic rheumatoid arthritis. The hand surgeon, however, may still encounter patients with tenosynovitis secondary to rheumatoid arthritis. The inflammatory process, which is a hallmark of rheumatoid arthritis, often affects the synovium that lines joint spaces and the tenosynovium that encapsulates tendons. Dorsal extensor tenosynovitis is most common, but tenosynovitis can also affect the flexor tendons.

Dorsal extensor tenosynovitis most commonly affects the fourth dorsal compartment tenosynovium. Synovial inflammation arising from the prestyloid recess of the ulna is also common. The invasive pannus of rheumatoid arthritis synovial inflammation directly causes extensor tendon attrition and rupture. Prominence of the ulnar head, known as caput ulna syndrome (secondary to destruction of the capsular restraints of the distal radioulnar joint) can lead to tendon rupture over the prominent ulnar head. On the flexor side, tenosynovitis occurs within the carpal tunnel or along the flexor tendons in the palm.

Patients present with swelling along the affected tendon sheath, most commonly as a dorsal wrist mass, and pain in the region. The enlarged tenosynovium feels irregular and diffuse, which differentiates the tenosynovitis from the well-defined ganglia or other wrist masses. Occasionally, the hand surgeon is the first physician to assess a patient who presents with inflammatory arthritis, and an appropriate workup is mandatory. Radiographs of the hand and wrist are scrutinized for characteristic findings of rheumatoid arthritis, such as periarticular erosions and cystic change. In patients with known rheumatoid arthritis, numbness in the hand can indicate carpal tunnel syndrome due to tenosynovitis within the flexor tendons passing through the carpal canal.

Conservative management in rheumatoid tenosynovitis of the hand and wrist requires rheumatologic support for systemic pharmacologic management. Splinting has an adjunctive role to relieve pain. Corticosteroid injections can decrease the inflammatory process and reduce swelling and pain. Targeted injection into the tenosynovial inflammation is more effective than the administration of systemic corticosteroids for isolated tenosynovitis.

Surgical treatment for tenosynovitis is to remove the inflamed tenosynovium while preserving normal structures. Tenosynovectomy has been shown to decrease pain and improve disease outcomes.Underlying bone and joint deformities need to be addressed. In-depth information is provided within the chapter that discusses rheumatoid arthritis (Chapter 55).

### Proliferative Tenosynovitis in Other Inflammatory Conditions

#### Crystalline Arthropathies

Gout and pseudogout are crystal deposition diseases that can cause tenosynovitis, joint inflammation, and skin issues. Gout, characterized by negatively bifringent crystals that form in response to hyperuricemia, can present as a liquid deposit along the tendon sheath or as tophaceous deposits. Gout has been characterized as the great imitator, as it can present in multiple sites and can be mistaken for septic arthritis, neoplasm, or inflammatory arthritis.

The initial treatment is to optimize medical treatment with colchicine, nonsteroidal antiinflamatory drugs (NSAIDs), and oral or intraarticular steroids in the acute phase. Persistent tenosynovitis requires surgical tenosynovectomy. In patients with carpal tunnel symptoms who have a history of gout, the physician should consider the possibility of gouty tophi within the carpal tunnel. Rich et al. noted gouty tophi within the carpal tunnel in 15 of 2649 carpal tunnel releases with an incidence 0.6%. Carpal tunnel due to gout is treated with carpal tunnel release and tenosynovectomy. When tophi are adherent to or within the substance of tendons, excision may require partial tendon resection. Debulking rather than complete eradication of tophaceous material is the goal (Fig. 56.10).

Pseudogout, or calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease, most commonly affects the wrist. Presenting signs and symptoms include pain, stiffness, joint inflammation, or dorsal wrist synovitis.The deposition of calcium pyrophosphate crystals causes characteristic radiographic findings such as calcification of the TFCC, as well as arthritic change at the scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) joint and widening of the scapholunate (SL) interval. Some patients with pseudogout are asymptomatic while others experience either acute joint or soft tissue inflammation and/or progressive arthropathy.

Conservative treatment is similar to that of gout, with maximization of medical care. Splinting and corticosteroid injections are useful for acute inflammation. Rheumatologic management includes methotrexate or biologic agents with specific inhibition of interleukin-1.Proliferative dorsal wrist synovitis should be treated with tenosynovectomy to prevent tendon rupture. Joint surgery is necessary in patients with chronic arthropathy who have failed conservative management. Options include STT fusion or resection STT arthroplasty, partial wrist fusion, proximal row carpectomy, or formal wrist arthrodesis.

#### Amyloidosis

Amyloidosis is a rare group of diseases characterized by extracellular deposition of amyloid, a fibrillar substance derived from precursor proteins. The most common subtype is acquired systemic immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis, occurring in 1 per 100,000 persons. The systematic nature of the amyloid deposition most commonly impacts the kidney, followed by cardiac dysfunction and peripheral neuropathy. In the hand and wrist, amyloidosis is manifested by trigger digits caused by deposition of amyloid on the tendons, carpal tunnel due to the compressive effect of amyloid deposits, and arthropathy characterized by subchondral cysts and soft tissue swelling.

Systemic treatment of several amyloidosis subtypes includes combination chemotherapy aimed at decreasing production of the abnormal proteins, as well as stem cell and organ transplantation. In the hand, Young and Holtmann described trigger digits and carpal tunnel compression with green-brown infiltration of the tendons treated with tenosynovectomy as well as standard A1 pulley and carpal tunnel release. Amyloid arthropathy is treated with surgical synovectomy for those failing conservative management.

#### Sarcoidosis

Sarcoidosis is a systemic idiopathic disease that causes formation of noncaseating granulomas typically deposited in the lungs, lymph nodes, and skin. Sarcoidosis can manifest in the hands as proliferative tenosynovitis or nodular inflammation of the digits (dactylitis). Other presentations of sarcoidosis include palmar plaque formation.

Treatment of sarcoidosis includes pharmacologic management with systemic corticosteroid therapy and methotrexate. Al-Ani et al. described granulomatous tenosynovitis affecting both flexor and extensor tendons, treated by excision of nodules and tenosynovectomy.Another case report described a patient with finger flexion contracture that was treated with unsuccessful A1 pulley release. Subsequent exploration of the FDP muscle revealed a sarcoid granuloma requiring longitudinal excision that alleviated the contracture and restored motion.

#### Calcific Tendinitis/Acute Calcific Periarthritis

Calcific tendinitis and periarthritis describe a self-limited phenomenon that presents with acute pain and swelling, usually without trauma, with associated radiographic findings of amorphous calcium deposition in the painful region (Fig. 56.11). While common in the shoulder, this has been described in the hand and wrist.

> **Fig. 56.11** Fluffy calcifications at the level of the A1 pulley appeared 1 week after the onset of excruciating pain along the flexor sheath that mimicked acute septic flexor tenosynovitis in this 38-year-old woman with no history of penetrating trauma. Kim and Park described 30 patients, 13 with calcific peritendinitis and 17 with periarthritis. Twelve of 13 with peritendinitis had calcium deposition in the flexor carpi ulnaris tendon.
