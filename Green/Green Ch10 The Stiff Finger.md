This chapter is intended to provide guidance regarding the treatment of a stiff digit following trauma, primarily with a focus on the metacarpophalangeal (MP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints. There are numerous resources, including several sections in this book, dedicated to preventing stiffness and maximizing motion after specific injuries such as fractures, tendon lacerations, or burns. This chapter will not replace this specific guidance. Instead, this discussion is dedicated to the treatment of a stiff digit caused by trauma, including the timing, methods, potential pitfalls, and expectations of treatment.

Curtis’s elegant work from 1954 and Smith’s elucidation of intrinsic tightness cannot be improved upon. The detailed, granular record provided by Curtis is an accurate reflection of the complexity and variability of every patient. Reading this work removes any illusions about surgical formulas and reinforces the need for careful analysis and strategies that are designed for each individual. If the reader concentrated on those two masterpieces, little more would be required for a conceptual grasp of the digit’s anatomy, pathologic findings, and reliable strategies for treatment.

### ANATOMY

### Metacarpophalangeal Joint

The MP joint is a multiaxial condyloid joint that permits motion in the sagittal plane (flexion and extension) and the coronal plane (abduction and adduction), and a slight degree of circumduction. The joint consists of a capsule, two proper collateral ligaments, two accessory ligaments, and a volar plate (Fig. 10.1). The joint capsule is loose and is attached to the crest surrounding the articular surface of the head of the metacarpal and at the base of the proximal phalanx. The redundant capsule allows extensive motion of the proximal phalanx on the metacarpal head and also allows distraction and a minimal amount of rotation.

![[Fig.10.1.png]]
> **Fig. 10.1** Ligamentous anatomy of the metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint. (A), The MP joint has an accessory and a proper collateral ligament on the radial and ulnar aspect of the joint. A capsule covers the dorsal aspect of the joint. In extension, the proper collateral ligament is lax, as is the dorsal capsule (B), whereas in flexion, the proper collateral ligament and dorsal capsule are taut (C). (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

The collateral ligaments are situated on the radial and ulnar aspects of the joint and are quite substantial, 1.5 to 3 mm thick and 4 to 8 mm wide. The collateral ligament arises from the depression in the subcapital area of the dorsal metacarpal head, courses distally toward the volar proximal phalanx, and has a length of 12 to 14 mm. The accessory ligament courses volar to the origin of the proper collateral ligament on the metacarpal head, fans out to blend in with the collateral ligament, and attaches to the proximal phalanx and the volar plate.

The volar plate is the structure that ultimately restrains hyperextension, which is commonly seen at the MP joint in the ulnar- most digits. The volar plate is composed of crisscrossing fibers that collapse from a fully extended position to a fully flexed position, so its longitudinal length is diminished by a third to half with full flexion, which is notably different than the volar plate structure of the PIP joint. The shape of the metacarpal head is that of a cam with a volar flare. As the proximal phalanx is flexed, the cam and volar flare displace and lengthen the collateral ligaments and thus place them in the position of greatest tension, MP joint flexion, which is also the position of greatest stability.Although the contours of the cam vary slightly from digit to digit, an understanding of this cam effect is essential to understanding MP joint pathologic conditions.

### Proximal Interphalangeal Joint

The PIP joint is a hinge or ginglymus joint, the configuration of which facilitates a wide arc of motion in the sagittal plane (flexion and extension) while resisting motion in the coronal plane. The stability of the joint is afforded by the geometry of the articular surfaces as well as the collateral ligaments and volar plate (Fig. 10.2). The PIP joint is supported laterally by two layers of soft tissues. The superficial layer is thin and made of the transverse and oblique fibers of the retinacular ligament of Landsmeer. The deeper and stouter layer is made of the collateral ligaments, which arise from the small recess in the proximal phalanx head and insert into the volar third of the middle phalanx and the volar plate. The accessory ligaments follow a similar path as their MP counterparts but run more obliquely and insert onto the sides of the volar plate. Unlike the MP joint, there is no cam or volar flare of the proximal phalanx head, and the tension of the collateral ligaments is uniform throughout the arc of motion. The volar plate of the PIP joint is also different from that of the MP joint (Fig. 10.3). There are two distinct regions of the volar plate of the PIP joint. The distal portion is the articular portion, which is fibrocartilaginous and quadrangular in shape, and the proximal portion is thin, compared with that of the MP joint, and membranous. The distal lateral portions of the quadrangularly shaped volar plate form a confluence that anchors the collateral ligament and volar plate to the middle phalanx. On either side of the volar plate, there is an expansion that extends proximally and onto the volar margins of the proximal phalanx. These proximal extensions are volar to the transverse digital artery at the level of the proximal phalanx neck and are variously referred to as checkreins, check ligaments, or swallowtail extensions. The PIP volar plate, unlike that of the MP joint, does not contract with flexion but slides proximally and distally with flexion and extension of the joint. Thus, any limitation or adhesions of the proximal volar plate or contracture of the checkrein ligaments can result in substantial limitation to PIP extension. Moreover, because the checkrein structure limits hyperextension much more effectively than does the looser arrangement of fibers at the MP joint, the interphalangeal joints typically hyperextend much less than the MP joints. Finally, the dorsal joint capsule is closely connected to the central tendon, which provides a dorsal stabilizing force. The relationship is so intimate that the undersurface of the central tendon undergoes fibrocartilaginous metaplasia and serves as an additional articulating structure for the proximal phalangeal head in flexion.

### Distal Interphalangeal Joint

The distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint is also a hinge or ginglymus joint, similar to the PIP joint (Fig. 10.4). The capsule surrounding the DIP joint is reinforced laterally by collateral ligaments that insert into the sides of the head of the middle phalanx and run in a distal and volar direction to insert into the volar lateral tubercle of the distal phalanx. The accessory ligaments are more volar and extend from the sides of the middle phalanx head to the sides of the volar plate. The volar plate serves as an accessory insertion point for the flexor digitorum profundus. Proximally, the plate extends and is attached to the neck of the middle phalanx, but unlike the PIP joint, it does not have lateral volar extensions. Not having checkrein ligaments, the volar plate of the DIP joint can hyperextend. The dorsal aspect of the joint has no reinforcing ligament, but the terminal portion of the extensor mechanism attaches from the dorsal edge of one collateral ligament to the other and blends into the capsular fibers and the periosteum of the distal phalanx.

### Interosseous and Hypothenar Muscles

The anatomy of the hand does not change with time. Although our understanding of the meaning and fine details of that anatomy does, the classic description of the intrinsic system of the hand by the late Richard J. Smith, MD, in previous editions of this textbook, remains, in my opinion, the gold standard. In tribute to Dr. Smith’s enduring contribution to hand surgery, I have preserved his description of the anatomy of the intrinsic system here, in his words.

There are **seven interosseous muscles**, four dorsal and three volar (Fig. 10.5). The dorsal interossei are abductors. Because the anatomic axis of the hand coincides with the axis of the third metacarpal, the dorsal interossei lie to the radial side of the index and middle ﬁngers and the ulnar side of the middle and ring ﬁngers. The little ﬁnger is abducted by the abductor digiti quinti, which functions like an interosseous muscle. The volar interossei are adductors and lie to the ulnar side of the index ﬁnger and the radial side of the ring and little ﬁngers. The middle ﬁnger therefore has two dorsal interossei (abductors) and no volar interossei (adductors) because the central axis of the hand lies within it.,

A, The palmar interosseous muscles are located on the ulnar side of the index metacarpal and on the radial aspect of the ring and small metacarpals and extend to the lateral bands of their respective digits. The middle finger has no palmar interosseous muscles. B, The dorsal interosseous muscles are bipennate muscles originating from the adjacent metacarpals of each web space. Each **dorsal interosseous muscle**, with the exception of the third, has two muscle heads. The **superﬁcial head** arises most dorsally from the shaft of the contiguous metacarpals and is inserted deeply by **a medial tendon**onto the lateral tubercle of the base of the proximal phalanx. The superﬁcial head abducts and weakly ﬂexes the proximal phalanx. It has no direct effect on the middle or distal phalanges. The **deep head** of each dorsal interosseous muscle forms **a lateral tendon, or lateral band**, at the level of the MP joint. The deep head ﬂexes and weakly abducts the proximal phalanx while extending the middle and distal phalanges. At the level of the middle of the proximal phalanx, **transverse ﬁbers** arch dorsally from each lateral band to join each other over the dorsum of the ﬁnger, ﬂexing the proximal phalanx. More distally, **oblique ﬁbers (spiral ﬁbers)** from the lateral bands sweep over the distal third of the proximal phalanx to insert onto the lateral tubercles at the base of the middle phalanx. The oblique ﬁbers extend the middle phalanx (PIP joint). More distally, the lateral bands are joined by the **lateral slips** of the extensor tendon to form the **conjoined lateral band**. The two conjoined lateral bands to each ﬁnger unite at the distal third of the middle phalanx to form **the ter-****minal tendon**. The terminal tendon inserts at the base of the distal phalanx to extend it (Figs. 10.6 and 10.7).

The **ﬂexor digiti quinti brevis** is structurally and functionally similar to the deep head of the dorsal interossei, forming the ulnar lateral band of the little ﬁnger.

The **three volar interossei** arise from adjacent surfaces of contiguous metacarpal shafts. Unlike the dorsal interossei, each volar interosseous muscle has only one muscle head and none of them insert onto the proximal phalanx. The volar interossei form the ulnar lateral band of the index ﬁnger and the radial lateral band of the ring and little ﬁngers. As with the lateral bands of the dorsal interossei, transverse ﬁbers arch over the dorsum of the proximal phalanx and join those of the opposite lateral band to ﬂex the proximal phalanx. They all send oblique or spiral ﬁbers that insert onto the base of the middle phalanx at its lateral tubercle. All lateral bands are joined by the lateral slips of the extensor tendon to form a conjoined lateral band and ﬁnally a terminal tendon that extends to the distal phalanx.

Of the **hypothenar muscles**, the **abductor digiti quinti** and **ﬂexor dig-****iti quinti brevis** are similar in both structure and function to the superﬁcial and deep heads of the dorsal interossei, respectively, and arise from the ﬁfth metacarpal. The abductor inserts onto the ulnar lateral tubercle at the base of the proximal phalanx of the little ﬁnger. The ﬂexor digiti quinti forms the ulnar lateral band. A third muscle, the **opponens digiti quinti**, lies deepest. It arises from the pisohamate ligament and the hook of the hamate and inserts onto the ulnar side of the diaphysis of the ﬁfth metacarpal, which it ﬂexes and supinates.

The lumbrical muscles arise from the ﬂexor digitorum profundus tendons in the palm. The lumbricals to the index and middle ﬁngers arise from the radial side of the profundus tendons (Fig. 10.8). The lumbricals of the ring and little ﬁngers arise from the profundus tendons and typically have a head arising from both the radial and ulnar aspect. The tendon of each lumbrical passes volar to the deep transverse metacarpal ligament and joins the radial lateral band at the middle of the proximal phalanx. The lumbricals ﬂex the MP joints and extend the PIP and DIP joints. When a lumbrical muscle contracts, it pulls the profundus tendon distally and the lateral band proximally and thus, lumbrical contraction decreases the force of ﬂexion of the ﬂexor digitorum profundus on the distal phalanx, and it can more effectively extend the interphalangeal joints., By contrast, when the ﬂexor profundus contracts and the lumbrical contracts as well, interphalangeal joint ﬂexion may be limited (see Fig. 10.8).

### Extrinsic Extensors

The *extrinsic extensors* join the dorsal aponeurosis at the level of the MP joint (Fig. 10.9). The four extensor digitorum communis tendons, the extensor indicis proprius, and the extensor digiti quinti (minimi) proprius tendons are the only extensors of the proximal phalanges. These tendons are loosely tethered to the dorsal joint capsule and the base of the proximal phalanx by a short leash of articular fibers that help hold the extensors at the midline of the MP joint. Normally, the articular fibers have little effect on MP joint extension. In the region of the MP joint, a sling of sagittal (transverse) fibers, the *sagittal bands,* pass toward the palm from the extensors to insert onto the volar plate and the volar base of the proximal phalanx. The sagittal bands lie deep to the lateral tendons of all the interossei and are superficial to the medial tendon of the dorsal interossei and the joint capsule. When the extensor communis muscle contracts, the extensor tendons lift the proximal phalanx into extension through the sling of sagittal bands. Hyperextension of the proximal phalanx is restricted by the volar plate and the normal tone of the intrinsic muscles of the fingers. Any change in the tendon length can result in a decrease in motion/extension at one or both joints. As tension increases on the extrinsic extensors, pull begins at the MP joint and is then transmitted to the PIP joint. The attachments of the extensor tendon to the MP volar plate preclude further proximal movement of the extensor tendon, and in the event of MP hyperextension, the central tendon cannot fully extend the PIP joint, resulting in an extensor lag. Blocking MP hyperextension will allow adequate proximal movement of the tendon to fully extend the PIP joint. In addition, any skeletal shortening, as may occur with a proximal phalanx fracture, can result in tendon imbalance and resultant PIP joint lag.

![[Fig.10.9.png]]
> **Fig. 10.9** Dorsal (A) and lateral (B) extensor tendon anatomy. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

On dissecting the dorsum of the finger, one might confuse the sagittal bands with the transverse fibers. Both structures are directed transversely, and both lie in approximately the same plane at the dorsum of the finger. However, the sagittal bands arise from the extensor tendon at the MP joint and insert into the volar plate and the base of the proximal phalanx to assist in extending the proximal phalanx. The transverse fibers, more distally, arch dorsally from the lateral bands. They are not inserted onto bone, and they flex the proximal phalanx.

Distal to the MP joint, the extensor tendon divides into three slips (see Fig. 10.9). The *central slip* inserts onto the base of the middle phalanx and extends it. The two *lateral slips* diverge beneath the oblique fibers of the dorsal aponeurosis and join the lateral bands at the distal end of the proximal phalanx. The combined tendon distal to the juncture of the lateral slip and the lateral band is called the *conjoined lateral band.* The two conjoined lateral bands of each finger join each other proximal to the DIP joint and form the terminal tendon. The conjoined lateral bands are held dorsally by the *triangular ligament* that passes between them. Thus the borders of the triangular ligament include the medial and lateral tubercles at the base of the middle phalanx and the conjoined lateral bands at its sides. The apex of the triangle is the juncture of the conjoined lateral bands, where they form the terminal tendon. Laterally, the *transverse retinacular ligament* prevents the bands from bowstringing dorsally. These ligaments extend from the lateral sides of the conjoined bands to the fibroosseous tunnel of the flexor apparatus.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGIC FINDINGS

The initial response to nearly any injury to the hand or finger is edema. The injured hand and digit are bathed in a macrophage- and protein- rich fluid that not only encompasses the injured structure but also surrounds and bathes adjacent uninjured structures. Accumulation of edema fluid or hematoma within the layers of tendons, sheaths, and capsular structures of the joint or within the synovial spaces physically limits mobility. The swollen hand assumes a characteristic posture that maximizes the capacity of the joint and comfort of the hand; for the MP joint, this is full extension, and for the PIP joint, this is flexion (Fig. 10.10).

![[Fig.10.10.png]]
> **Fig. 10.10** The anatomy of the extended metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint differs from that of the fully flexed MP joint. In extension, the bone contact surface area is minimal. The collateral ligaments are loose, intracapsular fluid space is at a maximum, and the joint is relatively unstable. The proximal phalanx rotates, abducts, and adducts on the metacarpal. In full flexion, the metacarpal condylar surface is broad, and the contact area is maximal between the metacarpal and proximal phalanx. The collateral ligaments are tight secondary to a cam effect and the necessity of the collaterals to pass around the metacarpal head prominences. The intracapsular fluid space is minimal, and the joint is highly stable because of the bone and ligament configurations in the flexed position. (Copyright © Elizabeth Martin.)

In contrast to the MP joint, the capacity of the interphalangeal joints is affected little by joint position and the hydrostatic effect of edema, although skin tension does have some limiting effect. A PIP joint contracture is the result of an imbalance between the flexor and extensor torque. The posture of resting MP extension following injury has a reciprocal influence on the PIP joint by tipping the finely tuned balance between the extrinsic flexors and extensors in favor of a flexed position. Without intervention, this will predictably progress to a fixed contracture.

## DIGIT FUNCTION AND MOBILITY

The functional demands of the hand obviously vary depending on activity, task, and occupation. However, the basic functions of pinch, grasp, and grip require motion, stability, and strength in the digits. For effective pinch, a stable, well- positioned thumb is crucial. Extremes of movement of the thumb interphalangeal or MP joint are less important for effective pinch than is their stability in a functional position. Some movement of the carpometacarpal (CMC) joint of the thumb may be advantageous but is not crucial. Meeting the thumb at the tip requires MP motion of the index and long fingers, with less motion needed at their PIP joints. In contrast, power grip requires the composite motion of all the MP and PIP joints, especially those of the ulnar three digits. Therefore before embarking on complex and challenging operative attempts to improve mobility of a given digit or joint, a full assessment of need and impact must be undertaken.

The highest priority of function combined with the highest probability of success is seen with attempts to improve *flexion* at the MP and PIP joints following trauma. Improvement of active and passive flexion of the joints is more likely than improvement of extension (e.g., patients with fixed contractures resulting from intrinsic tightness are excellent candidates for improvement following surgery).

Musicians present a unique and interesting challenge, because different instruments place vastly different demands on the fingers and hands and require an individualistic analysis. Pianists, who play with both hands, can adapt so that little PIP motion is needed, as long as the joint is pain- free. In contrast, musicians who play string instruments need both strength and mobility, especially of the fingering or fretting hand, even including the DIP joint.

## TREATMENT

### Evaluation

#### Status of the Articular Surface

If a joint shows signs of posttraumatic arthritis or articular malalignment, there is minimal chance of improvement with capsular release. Therefore, if the joint is in poor condition, contracture release surgery should be avoided. Although implant arthroplasty following posttraumatic stiffness has been successful in the hip, knee, and elbow, this has not been the case for the MP or PIP joints. The reported motion gain is minimal, and implant failure is quite common. Although some limited success has been reported for distraction arthroplasty, at this time a general recommendation regarding this procedure cannot be made.

When evaluating a joint, one must differentiate between a contracture, meaning the joint is fixed, and a lag, meaning the passive motion is better than active motion. For example, a PIP joint may have a flexion contracture where the joint lacks full extension passively and actively or an extension lag, where the joint cannot be actively extended but has full passive extension.

#### Capsular Contracture, Extrinsic Tendon Adherence, and Intrinsic Function

The examination of the stiff joint requires a fundamental understanding of the interdependence and relative roles of the joint capsule, extrinsic tendon adherence, and intrinsic function. It is vital to distinguish between active and passive motion and to determine the position of the adjacent joint in which these parameters are measured.

If the joint cannot be moved at all, it is difficult to tell whether the problem is due to joint incongruity, capsular contracture, tendon adhesions, or a combination of factors. If active motion and passive motion are equal but diminished, the joint capsule or articular surface is likely to be a primary limiting factor. Tendon adherence or dysfunction may be present as well but cannot be determined until the joint is fully or maximally mobilized. If passive motion exceeds active motion, the musculotendinous unit is incompetent, adherent, or both. The adequacy of a motor unit or tendon can only be fully assessed once the joint contracture is resolved.

Evaluation of active and passive motion should be performed with the wrist in extension as well as flexion, as this will help evaluate the extrinsic tendon adherence. To test for extrinsic extensor tendon tightness, the wrist and the MP joints are flexed, and passive flexion of the interphalangeal joints is assessed. In the normal state there is easy passive flexion of the interphalangeal joints. If extrinsic extensor adhesions or tightness is present, flexion of the interphalangeal joints will be difficult or limited. If the passive movement of the IP joints improves with the wrist in extension, this indicates extrinsic extensor tendon tightness or adhesions (Fig. 10.11). Similarly, if interphalangeal extension is less with the wrist and MP joints extended, extrinsic flexor tightness may be the problem.

![[Fig.10.11.png]]
> **Fig. 10.11** Clinical demonstration of extrinsic extensor tightness. A, The MP joint passively flexes with the wrist in extension. B, As the wrist is moved to neutral, the MP joint extends to a greater degree than adjacent digits and the PIP/DIP joints have decreased flexion. C, As the wrist is moved into flexion, the MP joint hyperextends, and the PIP/DIP joints extend.

Because the intrinsic system spans both the MP and PIP joints, testing requires fixing the position of the one joint when testing the other. The *Bunnell intrinsic tightness test* examines the effect of MP joint position on PIP joint flexion (Fig. 10.12). The test is considered positive when there is less flexion of the PIP joint when the MP joint is held extended than when the MP joint is flexed. A positive intrinsic tightness test indicates that there is some element of intrinsic muscle tightness contributing to the finger stiffness.

![[Fig.10.12.png]]
> **Fig. 10.12** Intrinsic tightness test. In most cases of intrinsic tightness, there is less flexion of the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIP) when the metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint is extended (A, C) than when the MP joint is flexed (B, D). (A and B, from Smith RJ. Intrinsic muscles of the fingers: function, dysfunction and surgical reconstruction. Instr Course Lect. 1975;24:200–220, with permission.)

When performing the Bunnell intrinsic tightness test with the finger in a neutral radioulnar position, it is not possible to determine whether the tightness is due to a pathologic condition of the interosseous muscles, the lumbrical, or a combination. It is useful to test for tightness of the PIP joint with the MP joint in ulnar or radial deviation. If the tightness is symmetric, the lumbrical is not the only muscle involved. If there is more tightness with ulnar deviation, however, a component of lumbrical tightness can be suspected. Additional information on lumbrical tightness can also be obtained if the Bunnell test is normal. Limited active finger flexion may be present in the absence of a passive joint contracture as a result of lumbrical tightness alone because the lumbrical fibers are most palmar. Thus lumbrical tightness can be evaluated by passively flexing not just the PIP joint, as in the Bunnell test, but also the distal joint by making a passive hook grip. If this lumbrical tightness test is positive and the Bunnell test is normal, a specific contracture of the lumbrical muscle may be present.

Too much flexor torque, as can occur with pulley rupture and subsequent volar displacement of the flexor tendon, will result in a PIP flexion contracture and will recur after correction of the contracture if the underlying problem (pulley insufficiency) is not addressed. In addition to correction of the underlying contracture, correction of the underlying cause (extensor insufficiency or flexor pulley insufficiency) will be required to maintain correction and prevent recurrence.

### Imaging

While imaging does not typically play a major role in treating the stiff digit, radiographs are useful to determine osseous abnormalities, cartilage loss/arthritis, or malunions as these conditions can affect treatment as well as outcome. Occasionally, advanced imaging, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or ultrasound, can be helpful to determine status of flexor and extensor tendons, pulley deficiency that may result in bowstringing or other changes that should be considered when treating the stiff digit (Fig. 10.13).

### Nonoperative Intervention

Edema can often be diminished and mobilization promoted by applying low- load, prolonged stress during active exercise to encourage functional use of the hand in light and moderate activities. Most MP and PIP joint contractures can be successfully managed nonoperatively.As long as there is improvement, nonoperative treatment should continue. Only in the occasional case when a plateau in active and passive motion has been reached and the result is functionally unacceptable should surgical treatment be considered.

Four types of orthoses are available to a surgeon or therapist treating joint contractures in the hand. *Static orthotic devices* are used to maintain the hand and digits in one position. They may be worn continuously to support healing tissue or may be removed periodically to exercise the injured hand. They are effective in minimizing external stress; when they are used as blocking orthoses, they may confine movement within a specified range. *Serial static orthoses* are applied with the tissue at its maximum excursion and are worn for a period of time. They are removed after the tissues have accommodated to it, and additional stretching force is applied with a new orthosis fitted at the new maximum excursion. *Dynamic orthotics* apply elastic traction, typically either with springs or with rubber bands, and thus apply load while permitting motion. Another type uses the principle of threepoint application of force (Fig. 10.14). Dynamic splinting is the treatment of choice when passive motion is responsive to manual stretch, pain is tolerable, and inflammation has subsided. *Static progressive orthoses* are similar to dynamic orthoses, except they use inelastic components to apply the torque (as opposed to the elastic springs or rubber bands in the dynamic orthotics). The inelastic components place the tissue under tension at a tolerable length without allowing motion. With time, the tissue relaxes, and increased tension can be applied, ultimately resulting in increased length of the tissue and improvement or reversal of the contracture.

#### Nonoperative Treatment of Metacarpophalangeal Joint Contractures

It is much easier to prevent an MP contracture than to treat it after it occurs. Splinting the MP joint in the flexed position results in diminished intrasynovial space and increased collateral ligament and capsular tension so that the length of the collateral ligaments is effectively maintained. Thus the best treatment for reducing the risk for extension contracture of the MP joint is aggressive control of edema, splinting (or occasionally even pinning) of the joint in flexion, and controlled mobilization once the soft tissues and skeletal structures are stabilized. The converse problem of flexion contracture is much less common at the MP joint. When flexion contractures do occur, however, they are typically secondary to external abnormalities such as intrinsic muscle tightness, extensor tendon adhesions, or other nonarticular causes.

When confronted with a contracture of the MP joint that suggests fibrosis of the collateral ligament or capsule, hand therapy plus an orthosis is the first line of treatment. A combination of dynamic daytime and static progressive nighttime orthotics, when used by a diligent patient and implemented by a knowledgeable therapist, can dramatically improve the contracture. Weeks and colleagues evaluated the nonsurgical management of 212 patients with 789 stiff joints of the hand, 336 of which were MP joint contractures, using exercises and dynamic splinting. A total of 173 patients, or 82%, responded favorably within 2 weeks of starting therapy with no further treatment necessary. Of the MP joints, the index finger MP joint gained an average of 35 degrees (42 degrees before treatment, 77 degrees after treatment), the long finger MP joint gained an average of 40 degrees (43 degrees before treatment, 83 degrees after treatment), the ring finger MP joint gained an average of 11 degrees (39 degrees before treatment, 50 degrees after treatment), and the small finger MP joint gained an average of 42 degrees (33 degrees before treatment, 75 degrees after treatment). Provided that the interphalangeal joints are supple, it is unusual for a patient whose MP joints can flex as much as 60 degrees to request or benefit from surgical release. The radial digits tolerate stiffness (limited flexion) better than the ulnar digits, as the latter are most important for grip strength.

#### Nonoperative Treatment of Proximal Interphalangeal Joint Contractures

Flexion contractures of the PIP joint can result from a variety of causes, including scarring of the volar surface of the finger, pulley rupture, contractures of the superficial fascia of the finger (as in Dupuytren disease), contraction of the flexor tendon sheath within the finger, adherent flexor tendons or contracted flexor muscles, adherence of the retinacular ligaments of Landsmeer to the collateral ligaments, contraction of the volar plate, adherence of the collateral ligaments with the finger in the flexed position, or a bony block or exostosis. Initial nonoperative treatment to maximize motion and function utilizes a combination of orthotic devices, exercises to promote tendon gliding, and modalities to decrease edema and sensitivity. Treatment of Dupuytren contractures and flexor tendon injuries and their complications is detailed in Chapters4and6, respectively. Regardless of the cause, the final common denominator of most flexion contractures is a pathologic change in the volar plate complex. The anatomy of the volar plate of the PIP joint is unique in that its fibers are transverse and there is little to no elasticity of the volar plate in flexion or extension. After injury, the checkrein ligaments form thick collagenous bands linking the volar plate and the proximal phalanx and thus restrict extension of the joint. Any injury that causes even slight shortening of these capsular structures can result in fibrosis and contracture despite the most rigid attention to proper splinting and hand therapy protocols.

Orthotic use with dynamic techniques or serial casting is the mainstay of nonoperative treatment of PIP joint contractures. Although there are many orthotic designs, each works on the principle that applied tension alters cell proliferation and the elasticity of contracted tissues. These orthoses vary in design, but the principle of proper use involves slow, steady application of an extension force perpendicular to the position of the flexed middle phalanx (which means constant adjustment of the line of pull as the contracture improves). Valdes and colleagues performed a systematic review of orthotic devices for PIP flexion contractures, identifying two randomized controlled trials and two prospective studies. The best results were obtained when the PIP orthoses were worn for a minimum of 6 hours a day. Pain that limits dynamic splinting to shorter times or that requires more than over- thecounter medication is a reliable indication that the applied load is too large. Traditional dynamic or static orthoses are limited in their ability to reverse PIP contractures because the pressure applied to the dorsal skin over the PIP joint will produce ischemia at 35 g/cm or higher.

Static orthoses are useful at night to maintain the gains achieved in the daytime. It is not advisable to wear dynamic orthoses at night because the position may slip during sleep and pressure sores may develop. As long as there is measurable progress, splinting should continue. As time passes, the force needed to achieve extension diminishes, and flexion deformity of the digit occurs more slowly and less severely. Frequently, several months of orthotic use is needed to achieve maximal correction.

An alternative nonoperative approach to a difficult flexion contracture is *serial casting.* Casts should be applied with sufficient force to achieve some correction but not so much that the digit is painful. Serial casts should be changed every few days as long as progress is being made.

The results of nonoperative management of the PIP joint have been reported by several authors. Weeks and coworkers reported on 453 PIP joint flexion contractures treated by active, passive, and resistive exercises in addition to dynamic and static splinting. Eighty- seven percent of the joints responded to treatment such that no further treatment was necessary. The pretreatment arc of motion of the index PIP joint was 42 degrees, compared with a posttreatment arc of 84 degrees. The long finger arc of motion was 42 degrees preoperatively and 76 degrees postoperatively. The ring and small finger preoperative arc of motion was 40 degrees and 39 degrees, respectively, and the posttreatment arc was 75 and 71 degrees, respectively.

Prosser reported an outcome study of 20 patients with PIP joint flexion contractures who followed a program of dynamic splinting with two different splint types. The average pretreatment contracture was 39 degrees and the final extension after treatment averaged 21 degrees, an 18- degree improvement. Although there was no statistical difference between types of orthoses, there was a statistically significant relationship between splinting duration and final range of motion.

Hunter and colleagues treated 61 flexion contractures of the PIP joint with an average pretreatment range of motion of 24 to 67 degrees. Twenty- nine patients underwent exercises alone, 27 patients underwent serial orthosis use, 5 had thermoplastic static orthotics applied, and 5 had dynamic orthotics applied. The average posttreatment arc of motion was 90 degrees, demonstrating the efficacy of nonoperative treatment. Extensor tendon adhesions resulting from tendon laceration and repair are a common cause of extension contractures and are detailed in Chapter 5. In addition, crush injuries, wounds that heal by secondary intention, or proximal phalanx fractures can result in tendon adhesions to the overlying skin or underlying bone, potentially resulting in loss of PIP flexion. Therapy and focused exercises to promote cutaneous and tendon gliding often result in improvement. Additional therapeutic modalities, including ultrasound and electrical stimulation, can promote scar softening and improvement in tendon excursion. Some cutaneous problems will require surgical treatment with flap coverage, while malunion of the proximal phalanx may require corrective osteotomy. Other factors that may limit flexion include dorsal capsular fibrosis or contracted or adherent intrinsic muscles and tendons.

Although PIP extension contractures are commonly seen, there are few published reports of the results of conservative management. If the contracture is due solely to capsular or intrinsic muscle adhesions, therapy is often beneficial. As with flexion contractures, a combination of static and dynamic orthotic use and stretching exercises are often helpful. The interosseous muscles can be stretched by putting the hand in the intrinsic- minus position of MP extension and interphalangeal joint flexion. Both isolated PIP and combined PIP and DIP joint flexion stretches can be used.

### Intrinsic Muscle Tightness

Patients may describe weakness following trauma or any condition that causes swelling. In addition, some patients with Dupuytren contracture or trigger digits may describe similar weakness. They will often have varying degrees of intrinsic tightness, demonstrated by the Bunnell intrinsic tightness test. This is most common in the central digits with the middle finger often being most affected. Intrinsic contractures often respond to a home exercise program focusing on intrinsic stretching, with resultant improvement in grip and function.

### Operative Intervention

Before developing and implementing an operative approach to joint stiffness, it is essential to confirm that a compliant patient and competent therapist will be permitted to work together and that the postoperative therapy visits are recognized by all concerned parties as an essential part of the treatment plan. If all three conditions (willing patient, capable therapist, and third- party approval when required) are not met, the surgery should not be performed. Ideally, the surgeon should determine the cause of the underlying contracture. In some scenarios, an MRI can be helpful to determine the continuity of the extensor and flexor tendons pulley integrity and tendon bowstringing (see Fig. 10.13). In the event the tendon is disrupted, the need for multistage surgery including tendon reconstruction should be discussed with the patient. In general, it is better to maximize motion and then proceed with tendon reconstruction rather than trying to perform both procedures simultaneously. In the event there is bowstringing of the flexor tendons, pulley reconstruction will be necessary for durable improvement. For high- grade contractures, contracture release and pulley reconstruction are generally performed in stages.

#### Skin and Soft Tissue

If the skin overlying the MP or PIP joint is thin, adherent, and lacks mobility, a separate procedure, including local, regional, or free tissue transfer, to resurface the area should be considered. There is no point in performing tenolysis procedures with capsular releases if the skin and soft tissue are so damaged that there is a risk of delayed wound healing or scar adherence.

#### Anesthesia

Tenolysis procedures are best when the patient can actively move during surgery. This can be accomplished with straight local (with or without tourniquet use) or local with intravenous (IV) sedation as this allows the surgeon to assess active motion intraoperatively. If sedation is used, the anesthesiologist can often titrate the timing and degree of sedation so that the patient is more sedated at the beginning of the case and is lightened later to allow for active motion when needed. If a tourniquet is utilized, it should be deflated for sufficient time to allow reperfusion and adequacy of neuromuscular function.

#### Release of Metacarpophalangeal Joint Extension Contracture

A dorsal approach to a stiff, extended MP joint is best because it allows both extensor tenolysis and dorsal capsulectomy. In the index and small fingers, my preference is to split the extensor tendons in the middle (between extensor digitorum communis [EDC] and extensor indicis proprius [EIP] or extensor digiti quinti [EDQ]), elevate off the capsule, and reflect the extensor tendon and sagittal band to either side. In the middle and ring fingers, the ulnar sagittal band is incised, leaving a strip attached to the extensor tendon, which is used to repair the sagittal band and avoid suturing directly into the extensor tendon, as this may impede motion.

A preliminary extensor tenolysis can be performed at this point by sliding an elevator or an appropriate tenolysis knife (Fig. 10.15), both proximal and distal to the MP joint. A transverse incision is made in the dorsal capsule, and the dorsal capsule is excised, with inclusion of the most dorsal fibers of the collateral ligaments. As the joint is passively flexed, the surgeon must judge if more ligament must be incised. The tenolysis may need to be extended further proximally and distally once the capsulectomy is complete and the range of motion is tested. While there is not an absolute angle of flexion needed, at least 70 degrees of flexion should be the goal. If the remaining collateral ligament is unable to pass over the condyles of the metacarpal head, it is detached from its origin on the metacarpal head. Although satisfactory flexion can be obtained by detaching the ulnar collateral ligament alone, it is often necessary to detach both collateral ligaments to prevent abnormal rotation as flexion occurs or angular deformity when the joint is extended.

![[Fig.10.15.png]]

Once the proper collateral ligaments have been released, an elevator is used to free any adhesions between the volar plate and the volar capsule. Passive motion is assessed, and additional release of the accessory collateral ligament is released as needed. Occasionally, the MP joint will jump or trigger as the joint reaches full extension. In such cases, any remaining accessory collateral ligament should be divided. Following release, the extensor tendons are again examined for excursion and any adhesions. At this point, the patient is asked to actively flex, and extend is assessed to ensure smooth motion and adequate flexion (Fig. 10.16). If regional or general anesthesia is used, a proximal incision for traction on the flexors can be used to assess tendon gliding. If the extensor tendons do not glide freely, tenolysis may need to be extended.

**Decisions regarding when to stop.** The surgeon must decide when the “law of diminishing returns” should be invoked. For instance, there is no value in releasing the MP joint to the point of instability because immobilization or pin fixation of an unstable joint will defeat any gains in motion achieved. Instead, if one has achieved improvement in passive and active flexion from 0 to 60 degrees, excising more tissue may risk destabilizing the joint and the necessary smooth articulating movement.

The use of pin fixation is not necessarily a poor choice, especially in severe cases of global trauma, but if used, the timing of pin removal for activation of the mobilization program has to be carefully considered and individualized, with removal and initiation of therapy as early as 5 to 7 days following the original procedure.

**Closure and postoperative management.** Once the release is complete, repair the extensor mechanism and skin in layers. The degree of flexion can be retested, being careful not to rupture the repaired extensor mechanism. The hand is placed in a bulky dressing, with dorsal and volar plaster splints holding the MP joints at 70 degrees of flexion and the PIP joints in extension.

The hand should be elevated for 24 to 36 hours and mobilization initiated in 2 to 3 days, if possible. The use of removable static and dynamic orthoses is dictated by individual circumstance. Oral steroids can effectively reduce swelling and pain in the immediate postoperative period in many cases and should be strongly considered if progress appears to have stalled.

**Expectations and complications.** In 1974, Buch described the anatomic characteristics of the MP joint and ligaments and detailed the technique of capsulotomy and collateral ligament release.Buch’s technique uses a dorsal exposure to divide the sagittal band off the extensor tendon and expose the capsule. A capsulotomy is performed, and the volar pouch is recreated with an elevator. The collateral ligaments are detached from their attachments to the metacarpal head, and the joint is flexed to 90 degrees. If there was compromised soft tissue over the MP joint, skin grafts or flaps were used in conjunction with the capsular release. Meticulous closure is performed, using nonabsorbable sutures to repair the sagittal band, to maintain centralization of the extensor tendon. Postoperatively, the MP joints are splinted in flexion. Mobilization is started 5 to 7 days after surgery in those patients who did not require skin grafts (2 weeks when skin grafts were used) and all digits gained at least 30 degrees of flexion (range 30–80). Patients requiring skin grafts had less overall improvement in MP flexion.

Young and colleagues described a technique that involved the use of Kirschner wire fixation of the joint in flexion for 2 weeks after the operation. They described both volar and dorsal approaches. Their dorsal technique is similar to Buch’s, with the exception that the extensor tendon is split longitudinally, and the volar half of the collateral ligaments and the accessory collateral ligaments are preserved to maintain joint stability. The joint is pinned with a transarticular Kirschner wire in 90 degrees of flexion for 2 weeks, after which time it is removed, and therapy is initiated. Their volar technique is used if the dorsal skin is moderately deficient but adequate to preclude skin grafting, flexor tenolysis is required, or later extensor tendon reconstructive surgery is planned. The volar technique uses a 2- cm longitudinal incision between the metacarpal heads. The lateral bands are traced proximally to the MP joint. The insertions of the collateral ligaments are detached, and a segment is excised. In comparing the dorsal and volar approaches, Weeks and associates reported that 44% of the joints treated via the volar approach gained more than 50 degrees of active motion compared with just 16% treated via the dorsal approach.

Gould and Nicholson evaluated 105 MP joint capsulotomies performed in 37 patients with a dorsal approach similar to Buch’s technique. The average gain in flexion was 21 degrees. When only fracture and crush injuries were considered, the average gain in flexion was 18 degrees even though 90 degrees of flexion was obtained at the time of surgery. They concluded that although their results have modified their expectations of capsulotomy of the MP joint, the functional gains that allow better prehension are still substantial. It is the author’s opinion that most patients do not maintain the degree of improvement obtained in the operating room, despite intensive and persistent rehabilitation programs.

Complications include wound separation and lack of skin pliability, especially in the first 2 weeks after release. It may be necessary to suspend rehabilitation for a few days until the skin and wound better tolerate flexion. At 10 to 12 days following operation the wounds can better tolerate mobilization and direct pressure, but this must be weighed against the increased scarring that occurs over this time that may limit final motion.

#### Release of Flexion Contractures of the Proximal Interphalangeal Joint

The primary challenge in the treatment of fixed flexion contracture of the PIP joint, assuming healthy articular cartilage, is the integrity and function of the extensor mechanism. If there is attenuation of or injury to the extensor, release of the volar contracture tissue alone will not restore active extension. Instead, a plan to release the joint, including the volar plate and collateral ligaments, and reconstruction of the extensor mechanism must be considered. The MP joint should also be evaluated, and if hyperextension is present, consideration can be given to blocking the MP hyperextension, such as with relative motion flexion splinting, to allow the full extension force to be directed to PIP joint.

Surgical approaches to the PIP joint have been well described in the literature. All use either the volar zigzag Bruner incision or a midaxial approach. The use of local anesthesia with or without intravenous sedation is preferred to allow the patient to actively flex and extend the joint after release of contractures. This enables immediate evaluation of the efficacy of the procedure, and the ability to perform any fine- tuning or additional releases if full extension is not achieved. If local anesthesia with sedation is not possible, an arm block or general anesthesia can be used, but in this case, passive extension can be evaluated while active extension cannot.

**Volar surgical approach.** A standard Bruner (or midlateral incision with transverse extensions) incision is made from the distal phalangeal crease to the palmar digital crease, and the skin flaps are elevated while care is taken not to devitalize the corner. The radial and ulnar neurovascular bundles are identified and protected (Fig. 10.17). The flexor tendon sheath is opened on a lateral hinge from the distal aspect of the A2 pulley to the proximal aspect of the A4 pulley, and the flexor tendons are carefully retracted. While it may not be possible, one should make an attempt to preserve the vincula if feasible. The checkrein ligaments are identified (Fig. 10.18). An anatomic landmark for identifying the checkrein ligaments is the transverse digital artery, which passes underneath the checkrein ligament 3 mm proximal to the joint. The checkrein ligaments are released while the transverse digital arteries are spared. An attempt at passive extension of the joint is then made. If full passive extension is not achieved, a capsular release is performed to release the volar plate complex from the proximal phalanx and accessory collateral ligaments from the volar plate. If the contracture still persists, the rest of the collateral ligament is detached from the proximal phalanx. Alternatively, Diao and Eaton described total excision of the collateral ligaments through radial and ulnar midlateral incisions. The need for an extensor tenolysis cannot be predicted in the presence of a flexion contracture. Thus active extension always needs to be checked after flexion contracture release, and the patient must be advised preoperatively of the possible need for extensor tenolysis.

Careful attention to hemostasis is essential as any hematoma will encourage the formation of a new contracture, and the swelling will impair postoperative rehabilitation. The vascularity of the digit must be carefully monitored during closure and postoperative positioning because overstretching of neurovascular tissues may result in spasm and an ischemic digit. The finger should be splinted in the maximum safe extension position for 3 to 5 days. Kirschner wire fixation of the joint in extension is occasionally used to maintain the release while the soft tissues accommodate to the new position. If there is excessive pressure to maintain extension on the joint, placement of a Kirschner wire can result in cartilage necrosis or cartilage overgrowth on the distal end of the proximal phalanx.

After the dressings are removed, a light dressing is applied, and active motion exercises are begun. Dynamic and static resting orthotics are fabricated and used for 3 to 4 months or until the correction plateaus.

**Expectations and outcomes of proximal interphalangeal joint flexion contracture release.** In 1954, Curtis reported on 25 patients with PIP flexion contractures treated by midlateral exposure, capsulectomy, excision of the collateral ligaments, and elevation of the volar plate, followed by Kirschner wire fixation for 5 to 7 days. He reported that the more anatomic structures involved in the limitation of motion will have a worse end result. If the only limiting factor was the capsular ligaments, release of the collateral ligaments gave a good result. Curtis summarized his findings by stating that restoration of complete motion with his technique is not expected but that improvement of function can be expected and typically about half the intraoperative gain is maintained. Using Curtis’s technique, Rhode and Jennings were able to obtain similar results. Sprague was not as enthusiastic in his reports of the Curtis technique but echoed Curtis’s finding that the more structures involved, the worse the outcome. In joints that underwent capsulotomy and excision of the collaterals, volar plate, and extensor mechanism to release adhesions, the results were disheartening. The initial gain intraoperatively was just 30 degrees, 90% of which was lost in the postoperative period. Sprague concluded, and others have agreed, that surgical release of a severely contracted PIP joint yields unpredictable results and should be considered only when both the surgeon and the patient clearly understand its limitations.

Bruser and associates compared the use of a midlateral incision with a palmar incision for contracture releases of the PIP joint in 1999.Their study of 45 joints demonstrated an average of 30 to 50 degrees of increased motion at 1.5 years’ follow- up. The group in whom a midlateral incision had been used had an average improvement of 50 degrees, whereas the group in whom a volar incision had been used had an increase of 30 degrees, which was noted to be statistically significant. Diao and Eaton reported on 30 patients with midlateral incision and complete collateral ligament excision. Twelve of the 30 had isolated collateral ligament excision while the remaining 18 had additional procedures (extensor tenolysis, volar plate release, or flexor sheath release). The average increase in motion was 40 degrees, with less than 5 degrees of coronal PIP stress laxity.

To identify which demographic, preoperative, and intraoperative factors affected the outcome of contracture release, Ghidella and coworkers performed a review of 68 PIP joints that had been released.The average improvement with a minimum of 24 months of follow- up was 7.5 degrees. Based on their analysis, the best candidates for surgical release were younger than 28 years with an uncomplicated contracture and a preoperative maximum flexion of less than 43 degrees. In general, I find it difficult to maintain correction long term and tell patients their long- term result will likely be about 50% of what is initially achieved in the operating room. While there are exceptions, these results have led me to prefer external fixation for treatment of PIP flexion contractures.

**External ﬁxation devices for proximal interphalangeal joint contractures.** External fixation provides a sustained forceful moment and exerts control through the bone, as opposed to pressure on the skin, thus offering a promising way of both stabilizing and mobilizing the PIP joint.

Though several devices have been reported to have successful outcomes, the Digit Widget (Hand Biomechanics Laboratory, Sacramento, CA) is a unique device that uses extension torque rather than a distractive force, is controlled entirely by the patient, and has a reasonably low profile. This device allows a range of motion of the PIP joint during the application of extension torque on the contracted joint via a series of rubber bands that are applied to the device (Fig. 10.19). This will lengthen all the volar structures as the contracture reverses, and the patient will continue to work on active flexion by removing the rubber bands several times a day for exercises. The Digit Widget comes with three different weights of elastics (light = purple, medium = pink, and heavy = light brown/traditional rubber band color). The goal is to use the lightest elastic that will provide improvement. Increasing the weight of the elastic beyond the minimum necessary is not needed and may result in discomfort. The device may be applied for a wide variety of reasons, is simple to apply and maintain, and “burns no bridges” should further reconstructive procedures be required (Case Study 10.1). Craft and colleagues described their outcomes using the Digit Widget in patients prior to Dupuytren fasciectomy in 13 patients (17 digits) and compared with a cohort that was treated with patients treated with checkrein ligament release in conjunction with fasciectomy, reporting significantly greater extension improvement in the group treated with Digit Widget (53.4 vs. 31.4 degrees, *P* < 0.001).

### AUTHOR’S PREFERRED METHOD OF TREATMENT: PROXIMAL INTERPHALANGEAL FLEXION CONTRACTURES

My preference is to use external fixation (Digit Widget, Hand Biomechanics Laboratory, Sacramento, CA) to treat PIP flexion contractures as I have had better long- term correction compared with open release. This is placed under local anesthesia with the aid of fluoroscopy.

A 0.28- inch Kirshner wire is used to localize the PIP joint through the drill guide. Following this, the proximal and distal predrills and subsequently the threaded half pins are placed. The pins are cut, and the frame and Velcro straps are applied. A light elastic is applied, and the patient is instructed to change this twice a day or as needed and perform pin care with half strength peroxide. The patient removes the Velcro wrist strap and is allowed to shower, getting the finger wet with clean water, but not soak the finger. The elastics are removed, and the joint is actively flexed each hour. If the MP joint hyperextends, the MP extension block Velcro strap should be used to allow full distraction force to be directed to the PIP joint. The patient is seen weekly and assessment of the correction is documented, with the goal of 10 to 15 degrees of improvement per week. The lightest weight elastic needed to obtain this amount of weekly correction is used. In the event this is not obtained, a heavier (medium weight) is used. I have found most contractures correct with the lightweight elastic, and it is unusual to need the heaviest elastic. Once full correction is obtained, the device can be removed or left in place for a few additional weeks to allow the soft tissues to continue to “relax.”

#### Release of Posttraumatic Proximal Interphalangeal Joint Extension Contractures

When treating a PIP extension contracture, the goal is to restore passive flexion first. Once achieved, and provided the tissue has equilibrated, efforts to restore active motion are begun. This may require staged surgery, as it is impossible to know whether flexor tenolysis will be needed in the presence of an extension contracture. In general, the first procedure involves release of the dorsal structures to regain passive flexion and the second procedure addresses the volar structures to regain active flexion. While this is not always needed, I find operating on both the dorsal and volar aspects of the finger simultaneously results in substantial swelling, and the intraoperative gains are often lost. Surgery for extension contractures of the PIP joint is also best performed under local anesthesia with or without sedation. This enables testing of the contracture release via the patient’s voluntary motion.

The operative approach for extension contractures can be from a dorsal or dorsolateral approach. The extensor mechanism is exposed, and the transverse retinacular ligament fibers are identified and longitudinally divided on either side of the central tendon. The extensor tendon is then elevated, with care taken to avoid injury to the central slip insertion. This provides exposure of the dorsal capsule and the collateral ligaments. Extensor tenolysis is performed, and the surgeon can continue as far proximally and distally as needed to free the tendon from underlying bone and overlying skin, taking care not to disrupt the central slip insertion into the middle phalanx. The dorsal capsule is excised, and the range of motion of the joint is assessed. If full flexion is not possible, the dorsalmost fibers of the collateral ligaments should be released (Fig. 10.20). If full passive flexion is still not obtained, the rest of the collateral ligaments are released off the proximal phalanx origin and excised. Once passive flexion is satisfactory, the patient is asked to voluntarily flex and extend the digit to determine the status of the flexor tendons and the gliding of the extensors. If the patient does not tolerate local anesthesia with sedation, the procedure can be done under regional or general anesthesia, and a proximal counter incision can be made in the distal forearm to apply traction to the flexor tendons and understand the potential need for later flexor tenolysis.

The results of release of PIP extension contractures were reported by Mansat and Delprat. In a multicenter study involving 10 institutions in France, 246 contracture releases of the PIP joint were performed, of which extension contractures accounted for 45% of cases. Preoperative range of motion of 19 to 34 degrees was improved an average of 28 degrees to a postoperative range of motion of 8 to 62 degrees. No discussion of PIP extension contractures is complete without mentioning the possibility of intrinsic contractures. After surgical release is completed, the Bunnell intrinsic tightness test is performed to determine whether there is residual contracture of the intrinsics. If the test is positive, release of the intrinsics should be performed as described by Dr. Richard J. Smith (following). If the patient is awake, the active hook grip should be tested following release to detect more subtle degrees of interosseous intrinsic tightness or lumbrical tightness that will respond to stretching postoperatively.

**Release of intrinsic contracture after trauma.** The classic description of the surgical treatment of intrinsic contractures of the hand by the late Richard J. Smith, MD, in previous editions of this textbook, remains, in my opinion, the gold standard on this subject. Again, in tribute to Dr. Smith’s enduring contributions to hand surgery, I have included his description of the surgical techniques for release of intrinsic contractures of the hand, in his own words, with a few minor changes.

Posttraumatic interosseous contracture is usually caused by edema, prolonged immobilization, or muscle ischemia. Following severe hand injuries, **hema-****toma** and **edema** ﬂuid can envelop the interosseous muscles, becoming trapped by the ﬁrm dorsal and volar interosseous fascia. Capillary compression and venous stasis add to the congestion. If a compressive bandage or circular cast further impedes venous drainage, the interossei develop a myostatic contracture in their viscid environment. When the edema ﬁnally subsides, ﬁbrosis supervenes.

Several months after injury to the wrist or hand, many patients will have persistent limited ﬂexion of the PIP joints. The surgeon may suspect that the limited motion is due to intraarticular or periarticular adhesions or posttraumatic synovitis, and, frequently, the cause is residual intrinsic contracture. To ﬂex the MP joints, the intrinsics must stretch the collateral ligaments, and with diffuse edema, these ligaments are tight as they are at their maximal length with the MP joint ﬂexed. The PIP joints, however, have no resistance to extension as the collateral ligaments are tight in extension and the tight intrinsic muscles are keeping the PIP joint extended. As overall motion improves, the patients will often complain of weakness of grip or difﬁculty making a tight ﬁst. The Bunnell test will demonstrate intrinsic tightness, which will often respond to intrinsic stretching. In the event the intrinsic tightness persists, surgical release can be considered. It is not necessary to release the entire intrinsic aponeurosis in these patients. Because only PIP joint ﬂexion is limited, just the oblique ﬁbers of the lateral bands are resected. This is known as a distal intrinsic release (Fig. 10.21).

Technique of distal intrinsic release. This procedure can also be performed under local anesthesia, with or without sedation. A longitudinal incision is made along the dorsal midline of the distal half of the proximal phalanx. The lateral bands, oblique fibers, and central and lateral slips of the dorsal aponeurosis are identified. The lateral band and oblique fibers are resected at the distal third of the proximal phalanx, taking care to preserve the central slip, the lateral slips of the extensor tendon, and the transverse fibers of the intrinsic aponeurosis (Fig. 10.22). Postoperatively, the MP joints are splinted in extension for 3 weeks. Active and passive- assisted interphalangeal flexion exercises are begun within 1 day of surgery.

Late posttraumatic interosseous contracture of the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints. Severe, prolonged deep- space edema of the hand will cause interosseous muscle necrosis and fibrosis. With unrelenting contracture of the scarred muscle remnant, deformities develop, resulting in severely compromised hand function. The hand assumes a rigid intrinsic plus position, with the MP joints flexed, the PIP and DIP joints extended, and the thumb adducted against the palm. This precludes many activities as the PIP and DIP joints cannot flex to grasp or hold an object. Although MP flexion and PIP extension are typically considered the “safe” position due to the lengthened collateral ligaments at the MP and PIP joints, the contracted, fibrotic, or necrotic interosseous muscles cause scarring, limiting any passive extension of the MP joint or flexion of the PIP and DIP joints, and they become rigidly fixed. The thumb becomes adducted and the first web space contracts due to the nonfunctional adductor pollicis. The collateral ligaments become a secondary problem as they can become fibrotic and scarred to the adjacent joints, further limiting mobility. Finally, the volar tissues at the MP joint (volar plate and accessory collateral ligaments) and the dorsal capsule at the PIP joint become fibrotic and scarred, further contributing to the fixed position of the joints. This deformity causes serious disability, essentially creating a hand that, at best, functions as an assist hand. Objects cannot be independently grasped due to lack of the PIP and DIP motion, the first web space contracture precludes holding anything, and, at best, weak lateral pinch between the volar thumb and radial index finger is present.

To correct severe intrinsic contractures that involve both the MP and interphalangeal joints, extensive release of the dorsal aponeurosis in the hand is necessary. If the interossei retain their contractility but are fibrotic and tight, they may be released with a muscle slide, releasing the interosseus from the metacarpal shaft and allowing them to be pulled distally. With more severe ischemia, the interossei are necrotic and nonfunctional. Lumbrical function may be preserved, and the interosseous muscle contracture released by transecting the interosseous tendons proximal to the MP joints (Fig. 10.23). This will typically improve the posture, allowing better use of the hand, but overall hand function remains compromised.

![[Fig.10.23.png]]
> **Fig. 10.23** Metacarpophalangeal (MP) and proximal interphalangeal deformity after late posttraumatic interosseous contracture may be relieved by transecting the lateral tendon proximal to the MP joint (top) if the muscles are necrotic. If some function remains, the muscles may be relaxed by performing an interosseous muscle slide (bottom).

Technique of release of severe posttraumatic intrinsic contractures with muscle fibrosis. The initial step is to release the MP joints to gain passive extension. A dorsal transverse incision is made just proximal to the MP joints. The intrinsics and abductor digiti quinti are exposed along each side of the metacarpals, and their tendons are transected at the level of the MP joints. If the MP joints remain flexed, the sagittal bands are retracted distally, and each accessory collateral ligament is divided at its insertion into the volar plate. The volar plate is freed from its attachments to the base of the proximal phalanx. With a blunt probe or elevator, any adhesions between the volar plate and the head of the metacarpal are separated. If it is difficult to maintain extension of the proximal phalanx after soft tissue release, a Kirschner wire is inserted obliquely through the MP joint in maximum extension. With the proximal phalanx extended, it is important to confirm with fluoroscopy that the base of the proximal phalanx articulates properly with the metacarpal head before passing the Kirschner wire across the joint.

Following release of the MP joints, the PIP joints must be addressed. A separate dorsal incision is made along the proximal phalanx. The skin is mobilized to allow visualization of both the radial and ulnar aspect of the proximal phalanx. Inoue described a technique to gain passive flexion by releasing the lateral bands on either side of the central slip, followed by the gentle passive flexion maneuver. In this technique, the passive flexion breaks up adhesions of the extensor tendon and the joint capsule. A dorsal PIP extension block with a Kirschner wire prevents active extension beyond the desired flexed position, and active flexion exercises are initiated immediately postoperatively.

The wire is removed at 2 weeks, and vigorous flexion exercises are initiated with a dynamic flexion orthosis. The results in 10 patients treated in this manner demonstrated an average gain of 47.5 degrees of PIP flexion without complications. This may work in less severe cases, but often there is minimal flexion after release of the intrinsics and additional surgical release is needed. In this case, a tenolysis is performed, separating the central slip from the dorsal capsule, and passive flexion is again checked. If still limited, the next step is a dorsal capsulotomy/capsulectomy through a transverse incision, followed by release/excision of the true and accessory collateral ligaments as needed to gain passive flexion. At this point, passive PIP flexion is possible. There may be scarring of the flexor tendons, which will limit active motion, but if passive motion can be maintained, a staged flexor tenolysis can later be performed to improve active motion. The patient will be seen in 24 to 48 hours and therapy is initiated focusing on edema control and passive and active PIP motion. MP motion is started at the same time if a Kirshner wire was not used, or following removal, which is typically around 3 weeks.

The thumb contracture can be released in conjunction with the fingers but is often addressed in a staged manner. Releasing all fingers and the thumb together can create substantial postoperative edema and result in loss of the surgical gains. To address the thumb and first web space contracture, the skin of the first web space is released first, typically with a four- flap Z- plasty. Additional deeper release is usually required. If it is felt that the intrinsic muscles are functional but scarred, release of the adductor pollicis from the index and middle finger metacarpals is performed through a longitudinal incision in the palm, which will enable sufficient web space for pinch. The muscle is released from each metacarpal, similar to the proximal intrinsic slide, until passive radial abduction of the thumb is achieved.

If the adductor pollicis if felt to be nonfunctional, it can be released from the thumb metacarpal through the initial Z- plasty incision. This is easier and will enable the thumb to be positioned away from the palm, but lateral pinch will not be possible. A Kirschner wire is typically used to maintain the first web space release by pining the thumb metacarpal to the index metacarpal in maximal radial and palmar adduction. It is removed at 3 weeks and an orthosis is used to maintain this position.
